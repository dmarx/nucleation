# LIT-tmp891dy: The Line: AI and the Future of Personhood

Boyle, J. (2024). *The Line: AI and the Future of Personhood*. Cambridge, MA: The MIT Press. Open access, CC BY-NC-SA.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmp91ovi](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp91ovi.md).*

- **Two lines, two tracks.** "Human" and "legal person" differ (p. 5).
  Personhood comes by moral recognition prompted by empathy, or by legal
  grant for convenience. The corporation is the working case of the
  second (pp. 8–9, 121–122, 132).
- **Sentences do not imply sentience.** An LLM is Searle's Chinese Room
  built as technology, so Searle is right about LLMs. He is wrong that no
  machine could be conscious (pp. 84–94, 110–111). After ChatGPT a
  successor to the Turing Test would look to innovation, autonomy in a
  community, and embodied cognition (pp. 114–118).
- **Three routes for AI.** Sock-puppet (the AI runs a corporation),
  custom-designed status, and the unruly AI that refuses its tasks (pp.
  122–128). The inscrutability paradox: an understood AI looks
  programmed, and an opaque one is feared (pp. 54, 246).
- **Corporate rights came without a decision.** Conkling's misquotation
  and the *Santa Clara* headnote put corporations inside the Fourteenth
  Amendment. By 1912 corporations had brought 312 of its 604 Supreme
  Court cases (pp. 147–153). Having a status and what it carries are
  separate questions (p. 133).
- **Animals and chimeras.** The Tommy suits run a capacity argument and a
  rightsholder argument. The second contradicts its own nominalism (p.
  181). Capacity-based ethics beats speciesism, but its lists are
  species-bound (pp. 216–220). Boyle's view: every human is inside the
  line as of right, and capacities extend the concern beyond the species
  (pp. 231–234).
