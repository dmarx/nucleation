# NOTE-tmp1p0ry: Hafer-Hahmann (2026) — The Evolution and Ecology of Host Manipulation in Helminth Parasites: A Phylogenetic Meta‐Analysis

## Contribution

The first phylogenetic meta-analysis to test theoretical drivers of manipulation strength across helminths, rather than only documenting that manipulation occurs.

## Key results

- Abstract: "mature parasites consistently enhanced predation susceptibility, whereas immature stages showed little or inconsistent impact."
- Abstract: "In mixed infections, mature parasites dominated, and manipulation was stronger in the presence of the correct predator."
- Abstract: "Phylogenetic constraints were minimal, in line with repeated independent origins. The ecological and evolutionary drivers and constraints tested here had only weak or inconsistent effects."
- Direct predation measures, mature parasites: d = 1.044 (0.306–1.815), N = 65/32 (Results, behaviours section).
- Publication bias: "In the model on mature parasites including SE as a fixed effect caused a marked improvement in model fit (Table 2B) indicative of publication bias."
- Inclusion criterion (Methods): "I included any behavioural change plausibly increasing parasite transmission via altered predation susceptibility", because separating true manipulation from side effects "is contentious".
- Framing (Introduction): host manipulation as "an extended phenotype—a trait encoded in one organism (in this case the parasite) but expressed elsewhere (the host)".
- Discussion: "Contrary to theoretical expectations that predation pressure should shape the evolution of manipulation ... I found no evidence linking these factors to manipulation strength."

## Assumptions

Behavioural changes that plausibly raise predation count as manipulation (adaptive status is not tested per case); species-level proxies stand in for local predation pressure; SE-as-covariate corrects publication bias.

## Concepts

Host manipulation; extended phenotype; trophic transmission; stage conflict (mature vs immature co-infection); dead-end predation; predation suppression by immature stages; phylogenetic signal.

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** The LIT summary (A19 §2, §8) is accurate: 207 studies, 82 parasite and 80 host taxa, stronger manipulation in mature stages and with the right predator. What it omits matters for the record: (1) scope is **trophically transmitted helminths only**, so neither of CASE-tmp10ykm's examples (Toxoplasma, a protozoan; the ant-manipulating fungi) is in the data; Toxoplasma appears only in the introduction; (2) the driver tests were mostly null; (3) clear publication bias; (4) the stage and predator effects are associations across studies, and the predator effect for immature parasites appears even where it should not.

- **CLAIM-tmpoxhqq** (parasites' selected adaptations control host behaviour through host machinery; not intentional agency): **supports the causal-control-by-adaptation half.** Stage dependence and the dominance of mature parasites in mixed infections are what selection for transmission predicts. It says nothing on intentional agency or on mechanism ("To fully understand host manipulation, we must link its evolutionary dynamics with the underlying mechanisms"). Its "extended phenotype" framing is the record's "selected adaptations controlling another's behaviour" in Dawkins's terms.
- **CASE-tmp10ykm** (Toxoplasma and ant fungi): **leaves open.** The meta-analysis covers helminths, not these taxa; it supports the general phenomenon, not the case's particulars.
- **ARG-tmp1jad4** (found on recheck; it cites this LIT as A's report): **supports with a qualification.** The stage- and predator-dependence premise is now read and confirmed; but the predator effect is weaker than A's gloss (for immature parasites "their manipulation never clearly differed from zero"), drivers predicted by theory were not found, and publication bias inflates mature-stage effects.
- **CLAIM-tmpu69tn** (provenance, control and alignment come apart): **supports weakly**: a trait encoded in one organism and expressed in another's behaviour, against the host's interests, is the "parasitic capture" combination; the paper does not discuss governance.

## Limitations

Helminths only; heterogeneity 50-80%; species-level proxies for predation; publication bias; inclusion of any plausibly transmission-enhancing change risks counting side effects as manipulation.
