# NOTE-1553: Liebman and Mahoney — Expiring budgets and wasteful year-end spending

## Contribution

The authors call it the first economic analysis of wasteful year-end
spending. It gives a precise definition of waste, spending whose marginal
value is below the social cost of funds; the first hard evidence that a
year-end spike exists in US federal procurement and that year-end projects
are of lower quality; an analysis of when rollover helps, which turns on
commitment; and calibrated welfare comparisons of full rollover, grace
periods and multi-year budgets.

## Key insight

An organization that does not know what its spending will be worth later
in the year keeps a buffer. If unused funds expire, the buffer is spent at
the deadline whatever it buys. The waste is not carelessness but the
residue of prudent saving under a use-it-or-lose-it rule, which is why
even a little rollover helps: it "allows agencies to avoid the lowest value
spending at the end of the year while maintaining sufficient precautionary
balances to respond to emergency spending needs" (p. 6).

## Assumptions

- Congress and the agency share preferences over spending; there is no
  principal–agent misuse (fn. 7).
- Value-of-spending shocks αₘ come from a known distribution; returns v(·)
  are concave within each period.
- Precautionary saving requires the period-1 spending function x₁(α₁) to be
  concave: it holds for CARA, for CRRA with γ ≥ 1, and for quadratic v.
- Agencies cannot borrow; there are no mid-year supplemental
  appropriations; agencies cannot already park funds in fungible goods.
- Agencies and their employees "do not have self-control problems" (p. 35).
- Calibration: CRRA value function, log-normal shocks, M = 12, β = 0.996,
  λ = 1.
- Quality is measured on surviving, active projects; the authors argue the
  selection biases the estimate toward zero.

## Key results

- **The budget is exhausted (§2, p. 8).** "The agency always completely
  exhausts its budget by the end of the year."
- **Waste defined (Definition 1, §2.2, p. 9).** "Wasteful spending is
  defined as spending for which the marginal return to the agency is less
  than the social cost of funds".
- **The spike (§1, p. 4).** In FPDS, 2004–2009, $2.6 trillion: "8.7 percent
  of spending occurs in the last week of the year", against about 1.9
  percent in a uniform week, in all major agencies.
- **The quality drop (§1, p. 5; §4.6, p. 26).** "Projects that originate in
  the last week of the fiscal year have 2.2 to 5.6 times higher odds of
  having a lower quality score." OLS gives about one point lower on the
  0–10 rating, and "it is the evaluation by the agency CIO that is
  responsible for the main finding."
- **Rival mechanisms (§4.5, p. 25).** Procrastinating contracting offices
  are ruled out by office fixed effects; reverse causality in CIO ratings
  by splitting on CIO tenure; and priority-ordered projects because "The
  increased variation in volume and quality at the end of the year is
  inconsistent with the priority-based model."
- **Commitment (Propositions 3 and 4, p. 30).** "If Congress cannot commit
  to a budget rule, the agency will never roll over any budget authority,
  and allowing rollover will not produce an efficiency gain." With
  commitment, "it is optimal for Congress to provide a constant budget
  that does not depend on the level of rolled over resources", so sharing
  savings at less than 100 percent is never optimal.
- **DOJ's rollover authority (§1, §5.3).** A difference-in-differences:
  "rollover reduces the volume of year-end spending by 9.5 percentage
  points". The quality evidence is thin: "DOJ has 15 I.T. projects in the
  Dashboard with only 1 occurring in the last week of the year." (p. 32).
- **Grace periods (§5.4, pp. 34–35).** "We find that a one-month grace
  period achieves 41 percent of the welfare gains from full rollover; a
  two-month grace period achieves 66 percent; and a four-month grace period
  90 percent". The first-best, saving and borrowing, gives "approximately
  19 percent" (p. 34).

## Concepts

- **Wasteful year-end spending**: spending with αₘv′(xₘ*) < λ, λ the social
  cost of funds.
- **Quality of spending**: qₘ = αₘv(xₘ)/xₘ, value per dollar.
- **Precautionary incentive**: holding back early spending because future
  value is uncertain and the period-1 spending function is concave.
- **Rollover**: unused budget authority carried into the next year, g(A −
  x); full rollover is g the identity, none is g = 0.
- **Commitment**: whether Congress conditions future budgets on rolled-over
  funds.

## Connections

The authors place the work against the ratchet-effect literature (Freixas,
Guesnerie and Tirole), and §5.2 shows their model does not exhibit a
ratchet. Siemroth ([LIT-2103](../literature.d/LIT-2103.md)) takes their no-misuse precautionary model
and recasts the problem as moral hazard with costly state verification, in
which roll-over substitutes for auditing.

## Limitations

- Quality evidence covers only I.T. projects that survive into the
  Dashboard.
- The DOJ quality effect rests on a single last-week project.
- External validity beyond federal procurement is argued, not shown.
- The welfare numbers depend on functional form (CRRA; a CARA check is in
  an appendix not read).
- No principal–agent misuse, borrowing or supplemental appropriations.
- The authors note the case for one-year budgets: congressional control,
  and "The use-it-or-lose it feature of appropriated funds may push
  projects out the door that would otherwise languish due to bureaucratic
  delays." (p. 36).
- Rollover gains vanish if balances do not stay with the subunit that
  saved them.
