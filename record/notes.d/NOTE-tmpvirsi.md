# NOTE-tmpvirsi: The Self and the Future

## Contribution

The paper sets two arguments about the same case against each other and
shows that each carries conviction. The first is the familiar
third-person case for the psychological view, made sharper by tying it to
what A and B would choose and how they would react. The second is new: a
first-person series that reaches the same outcome by small steps, at none
of which the subject's fear could rationally stop. It then argues that the
standard escapes, "borderline case" and "decide by convention", cannot be
lived with from the first person.

## Key insight

From the inside, a prediction of my future pain survives any prediction
about what will be in my mind beforehand. Amnesia, a new character, someone
else's memories: none stops it being me who is hurt. The body-swap story
looks like "A moves to the other body" only because the experimenter
chose to stop at the neatest point of a series, and the step that makes it
neat is something that happens to the other man.

## Assumptions

- **Memory is causal.** The memories must be suitably caused by the past
  experiences; the imagined process copies information out of one brain and
  into the other ("information-parking", p. 162).
- **Imaginability.** A and B are taken to be alike enough for the exchange
  to be imaginable "in the most restricted sense" (p. 161).
- **Selfish choice.** A and B choose "on selfish grounds" (p. 163).
- **Pain as test.** Physical pain is "absolutely minimally dependent on
  character or belief", so predicted psychological change cannot remove the
  reason to fear it (p. 170).
- **Fear needs involvement.** Some emotions, fear among them, are felt only
  if I will be "involved" in the expected situation (p. 175).

## Key results

- **The first presentation (pp. 163–167).**
  - With the choice of money or torture for the A-body-person and the
    B-body-person, every pattern of choices yields reactions afterwards that
    fit "changing bodies": the B-body-person complains that he chose
    otherwise, "since he has A's memories" (p. 164).
  - "This seems to show that to care about what happens to me in the future
    is not necessarily to care about what happens to this body" (p. 164),
    and that in some sense "I and my body are 'really distinct'", though
    nothing supports existing without a body (pp. 164–165).
  - The wooden leg, the anxiety and the distressing memories confirm it
    (pp. 166–167). Arguments that bodily continuity is necessary "would seem
    to be just mistaken" (p. 167).
- **The second presentation (pp. 167–171).**
  - Torture announced; then forgetting the announcement; then total
    amnesia; then a different set of impressions of my past; then
    impressions matching another living person, copied from his brain.
    "Fear, surely, would still be the proper reaction" (p. 168).
  - The principle behind it: "my undergoing physical pain in the future is
    not excluded by any psychological state I may be in at the time, with
    the platitudinous exception of … unconsciousness" (p. 169). Contrast
    acrophobia, where predicted change does remove the reason (p. 169).
  - The presentation differs from the first in using "you" and in leaving
    out the other man. Neither seems a fault: "My selfish concern is to be
    told what is going to happen to me" (p. 171).
- **The series (pp. 172–174).** (i) amnesia; (ii) plus character change;
  (iii) plus fictitious "memories"; (iv) character and "memories"
  appropriate to a real person B; (v) produced by copying from B's brain,
  B left unchanged; (vi) the same, with the reverse operation done on B.
  - No step from (i) to (v) gives A a reason to stop fearing.
  - "From the point of view of A's expectations and fears, there is less
    difference between (vi) and (v) than there is between (v) and (iv)"
    (p. 173).
  - To say A reappears as the B-body-person in (vi) implies that A "just
    does not exist" in (v), since the A-body-person there is "in character,
    history, everything, exactly the same" (p. 174). Where then to draw the
    line, between (iii) and (iv)?
- **Against indeterminacy (pp. 174–178).**
  - Ordinary indeterminate expectations (one of five will be hurt; a
    nameless horror) keep fear in place, because "one thing that is fixed
    amid the indeterminacy is the belief that it is to me" that it will
    happen (p. 176).
  - Conceptual undecidability whether I am involved leaves fear "neither
    appropriate, nor inappropriate, nor appropriately equivocal"
    (pp. 176–177). Projective imagining "implicitly answers the necessarily
    unanswerable question" (p. 177).
  - The analogy of ambivalent feeling toward a transformed sentimental
    object fails, because it keeps "him and me clearly distinct" (p. 177).
    "There seems to be an obstinate bafflement to mirroring in my
    expectations a situation in which it is conceptually undecidable whether
    I occur" (p. 178).
- **Against conventionalism (p. 178).** Calling the A-body-person "A" in (v)
  but not in (vi) is "the sort of thing indeed appropriate to lawyers",
  but "as a line to deal with a person's fears or expectations about his own
  future, it seems to have no sense at all".
- **The reversal (p. 179).** The "mentalistic" conclusion came from an
  argument "entirely conducted in third-personal terms"; the bodily one
  from "the first-personal issue of what A could expect".
- **The resolution (pp. 179–180).** The first presentation's neatness "is
  basically artificial"; the experimenter "has rather produced the one
  situation out of a range of equally possible situations which we should be
  most disposed to call a change of bodies". The principle that fears reach
  through psychological change "seems positively straightforward … but we
  need to be shown what is wrong with it". So pass the pain to the
  B-body-person: "It would be risky: that there is room for the notion of a
  risk here is itself a major feature of the problem."

## Concepts

- **A-body-person / B-body-person** — whoever one is confronted with, after
  the experiment, when confronted with the body that was A's / B's
  (p. 163).
- **information-parking** — extracting information from a brain into a
  device and replacing it; not "learning again" (p. 162).
- **involvement** — figuring in a future situation as doer or sufferer, or
  being affected by its consequences (p. 175).
- **projective imagination** — thinking of a future situation "as it will
  be for me" (p. 176).

## Bearing on the record

No THEORY document in the record cites it. It is the main source the record
now holds for the view that what a subject should expect follows the body,
and so bears on any future THEORY that treats copying or re-instantiating an
agent as its persistence. It carries no instruction for machine-learning
practice.

## Limitations

What is shown and what is asserted:

- **Shown, given C2:** C3 and C4. The (v)–(vi) argument is the paper's
  strongest step.
- **Argued from cases, contestable:** C2. The principle is defended by
  pain's independence from character and belief, not by an account of what
  makes a future pain mine. Parfit's later reply targets exactly this.
- **Analysed, not proved:** C5. It depends on fear requiring a determinate
  belief that it is to me that something will happen.
- **Explicitly tentative:** C8. Williams says he is "not in the least clear
  which option it would be wise to take", and that he finds that
  "rather disturbing" (p. 179).
- **Not addressed:** fission. In the series there is never more than one
  candidate at a time on A's side.

## Open questions

- What makes a future pain one's own, if not psychological continuity? The
  paper argues that psychological change does not stop it being mine, and
  does not say what does, beyond the body that "has had the leg on it for
  some time".
- Would the first-person argument survive a version in which the body too
  is gradually replaced? The paper keeps the body fixed throughout.
