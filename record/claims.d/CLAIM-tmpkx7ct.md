# CLAIM-tmpkx7ct: If procedural friction is a step the executor cannot pass on its own, a standing limit enforced by a mechanism the executor does not control is friction without delay, so A's objection removes delay and not friction, and the owner's "characterizes" reading survives, at the price of becoming true by definition; the reading with empirical content is "exposes"

## The reply

The owner's claim ([CLAIM-tmpuiaop](CLAIM-tmpuiaop.md)) says procedural friction "exposes (or
perhaps even characterizes) opportunity for intervention". A's objection
([CLAIM-tmpwpvbz](CLAIM-tmpwpvbz.md), from A33 §8 and A35 §7) offers a process with no friction
that is still interruptible: limits set in advance and enforced by a
mechanism fast enough to stop a transition before it forecloses
alternatives. The objection takes friction to be delay.

On [TERM-tmp91lqy](../terms.d/TERM-tmp91lqy.md), friction is a step the executor cannot pass on its own.
A's architecture has such a step: the transition halts on a limit the
executor did not set and cannot lift. It is friction without delay. So the
objection removes delay, not friction, and the "characterizes" reading is
not refuted by it.

## The cost

On that term, an opportunity for someone else to intervene and a step the
executor cannot pass alone are close to the same thing described twice:
an intervention is a condition outside the executor on which the process
waits. "Friction characterizes opportunity for intervention" then holds
almost by definition and says nothing about automation in particular.

What keeps content is the "exposes" reading. Automation, as the owner's
claim describes it, is mostly the removal of steps that wait on people:
approvals, handoffs, reviews. Those steps are not only gates but the
moments at which a decision in progress becomes visible to someone other
than its executor. A standing limit is a gate without that visibility: it
stops only the transitions foreseen when it was set, and shows no one the
rest. So the owner's claim, read through this term, says that automation
can keep the gates and lose the sight, and that is a claim a case could
defeat. A's objection, read the same way, is right that interruption does
not need delay and wrong that it does not need friction.

## Where it sits

- It objects to [CLAIM-tmpwpvbz](CLAIM-tmpwpvbz.md) and leaves [CLAIM-tmpuiaop](CLAIM-tmpuiaop.md) standing, with its
  content located in "exposes".
- [CLAIM-523](CLAIM-523.md)'s legibility is what the visibility of a
  waiting step supplies; its volitional efficacy is what the gate supplies.
  A standing limit gives the second without the first.
- A35 §5's specification lag is why the difference matters: a limit set
  before a failure was conceived cannot stop it, and only a step that shows
  someone the decision in progress gives the new question a chance to be
  asked ([CLAIM-475](CLAIM-475.md), note of 2026-11-15).
