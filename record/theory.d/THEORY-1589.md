# THEORY-1589: Pinter and Greenwald report that four minimal-group inductions produced comparable explicit in-group liking and identification, and the three compared on it comparable allocation favouritism, that only memorizing one group's names produced large implicit self-association with and preference for that group, and that implicit self-association and liking were related but separable

## The account

Explicit favouritism is easy to produce and hard to move; the implicit
measures separated the procedures. Studying a group's names, with no
claim that one belongs to it, tied that group to "I", "me" and "mine"
more strongly than being told one belonged: "the focused study of
individuating information about one group automatically creates and
strengthens links between participants' self-concepts and the studied
group" (p. 95).

## Evidence

From [NOTE-1319](../notes.d/NOTE-1319.md): memorization against the classic procedure gave d up
to 2.16 on the IATs. The identification IAT and the attitude IAT
correlated .66 (Experiment 1) and .48 (Experiment 2); the explicit
"I identify with" and "I like" items correlated .71 in both. Allocation
favouritism correlated with each measure at about .23 to .37.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-300](../questions.d/QUESTION-300.md) asks for "a criterion of the self's extent that can
come apart from caring". The paper measures self–group association apart
from liking: at the level of report the two nearly coincide, implicitly
they come apart somewhat, and neither predicts allocation much better.
[CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md)'s note of 2026-10-29 records the same.
