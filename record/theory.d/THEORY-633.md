# THEORY-633: Experiences have no phenomenal properties: introspection represents physical, quasi-phenomenal properties of sensory states as phenomenal, so what needs explaining is why experiences seem to have phenomenal properties, not how a brain produces them; a weaker view that keeps a deflated phenomenal residue is unstable unless it can say what that residue is

## Source

- Frankish (2016), "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness", *Journal of
  Consciousness Studies* 23(11–12), 11–39, [LIT-458](../literature.d/LIT-458.md), read in full in
  [NOTE-384](../notes.d/NOTE-384.md) from the author's eprint (pages are the eprint's, 1–24).
- Dennett (1996), "Facing Backwards on the Problem of Consciousness",
  *JCS* 3(1), 4–6, [LIT-397](../literature.d/LIT-397.md), read in full in [NOTE-346](../notes.d/NOTE-346.md).
- Frankish (2025), "Closing the Cartesian Bureau de Change", [LIT-103](../literature.d/LIT-103.md), read
  in full in [NOTE-143](../notes.d/NOTE-143.md).

## What was actually shown

**Three options for an apparent anomaly ([LIT-458](../literature.d/LIT-458.md) §1.1).** Radical realism
(real, needs new science), conservative realism (real, explicable with
existing science) and illusionism (not real; explain the appearance).

**Strong and weak illusionism (§1.2).** A quasi-phenomenal property is a
physical property, perhaps gerrymandered, that introspection typically
misrepresents as phenomenal (p. 4). Strong illusionism says experiences
have only these. Weak illusionism keeps real phenomenal properties that
merely seem ineffable, intrinsic, private and infallibly known. Unless it
can name a residue stronger than quasi-phenomenality yet still
conservatively explicable, which Frankish "doubts", only radical realism
and strong illusionism remain ([NOTE-384](../notes.d/NOTE-384.md), C1).

**The arguments (§2).** A presumption of conservatism, stronger when the
pressure comes from introspection. Epiphenomenalism threatens radical
realism under causal closure. Reductive theories explain at most why
subjects would judge their experience qualitative, "a conflation of
phenomenality with the representation of phenomenality" (p. 13), and so
are covertly illusionist. A debunking argument, taken from Chalmers 1996
without his endorsement: our phenomenal claims would be the same without
phenomenal properties. And an abduction from anomalousness plus the
uncheckability of introspection.

**What it keeps (§§1.6–1.7).** Conscious experiences exist in an inclusive
sense, and are "like something" in a functional sense, as introspective
representation; in that sense "there is something it is like to be a
zombie" (p. 10). Everyday phenomenal talk can be read as talk within a
fiction. The audience of the illusion is no inner observer but "numerous
unintelligent subsystems" (§3.3).

**The earlier form ([LIT-397](../literature.d/LIT-397.md)).** Dennett lists what impresses anyone about
their own consciousness (delight, distraction, foreboding, the limit on
items held at once) and finds only functions and dispositions: "Subtract
them away, and nothing is left beyond a weird conviction (in some people)
that there is some ineffable residue" (p. 5). A fundamental posit needs
independent evidence, as mass and charge had; without it, adding
experience to physics is like adding cuteness (p. 6).

**An application ([LIT-103](../literature.d/LIT-103.md)).** If hedonic valence motivates "in virtue of
its feel", there is a private place where value is "presented to the
organism, felt, and reacted to", "a tiny one-dimensional Cartesian bureau
de change", and the hard problem "remains as hard as ever" (p. 20). An
evolutionary account is complete only if "feel" is metaphor or cashed out
functionally: "Any state will do, provided it has the right effects in the
right context – and the effects are all that evolution is sensitive to".

**What could have come out otherwise.** Someone could have named a stable
weak-illusionist residue, or a reductive theory could have explained
phenomenality rather than its representation. Frankish argues neither has
happened; he does not show that neither can.

## What this does not say

- **That there are no experiences, or no pain.** Illusionism denies
  phenomenal properties, not experiences in the inclusive sense. Dennett in
  1980 held that "there are no such things as pains, although of course
  people do feel pain" ([LIT-456](../literature.d/LIT-456.md), [NOTE-371](../notes.d/NOTE-371.md)).
- **That an illusionist theory exists.** The article "will not propose a
  detailed illusionist theory" (p. 1). How introspection represents
  non-existent phenomenal properties, the core of the illusion problem by
  its own account, gets "preliminary remarks" (§3.4).
- **That the weak view is refuted.** The collapse argument is a challenge:
  it holds until someone names the residue ([NOTE-384](../notes.d/NOTE-384.md), Limitations).
- **That the abduction has a weight.** "If there is even a remote
  possibility that we are mistaken ... there is a strong abductive
  inference" (p. 14) does not say how anomalousness is weighed against the
  cost of being wrong about what introspection presents as certain.
- **That Dennett's subtraction is shown.** That nothing is left after the
  functions are subtracted is asserted from the author's introspective
  catalogue ([NOTE-346](../notes.d/NOTE-346.md), C3), and the reply leaves open what independent
  ground for a fundamental posit would satisfy it.
- **That Dennett is a strong illusionist on every reading.** Taylor reads
  his view as realism about a real pattern, with qualia eliminativism
  peripheral ([THEORY-563](THEORY-563.md)). The readings agree only if Taylor's
  "consciousness" is Frankish's inclusive sense.
- **That Veit's account of valence is wrong.** The bureau-de-change charge
  is conditional. If "feel" is metaphor, Frankish has "no objection"; it
  identifies an ambiguity, not an error ([NOTE-143](../notes.d/NOTE-143.md), Limitations).

## Connections

- **Rival.** The hard problem ([THEORY-694](THEORY-694.md)) holds that experience is a
  further explanandum beyond the functions. This account holds that the
  further explanandum is the seeming. Chalmers's meta-problem paper
  ([LIT-425](../literature.d/LIT-425.md), [THEORY-577](THEORY-577.md)) agrees with Frankish that only strong
  illusionism dissolves the hard problem, and rejects it by a Moorean
  argument that the "denying the data" objection of §3.1 anticipates.
- **Qualia.** Dennett's "Quining Qualia" ([THEORY-415](THEORY-415.md)) supplies the four
  marks weak illusionists deflate, and argues the narrower thesis that no
  property of experience has all four.
- **Valence.** [THEORY-101](THEORY-101.md) (Veit's hedonic valence as the origin of
  consciousness) is the account [LIT-103](../literature.d/LIT-103.md) charges with keeping a Cartesian
  feel; that THEORY records Veit's concession that the positive account of
  how valence motivates is owed.
- **AI.** If what makes experience "like something" is introspective
  representation, the question for a machine is whether it has the
  introspective mechanisms, which sharpens rather than settles [THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md)'s
  mimicry point. The application is the record's, not Frankish's.
