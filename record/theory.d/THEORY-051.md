# THEORY-051: Strengthening the self as context, a perspective from which thoughts about oneself are held as thoughts, is a process by which acceptance and commitment therapy reduces suffering

## Source

- Hayes, Luoma, Bond, Masuda and Lillis (2006), [LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md), read in full in
  [NOTE-431](../notes.d/NOTE-431.md) from an early manuscript. Self as context is on p. 8 and the
  component studies on pp. 11–13.
- Barnes-Holmes, Barnes-Holmes, McHugh and Hayes (2004), [LIT-541](../literature.d/LIT-541.md), read in
  full in [NOTE-430](../notes.d/NOTE-430.md). The deictic reading of defusion is on pp. 370–371.

## The claim, and how it differs from the conceptual one

[THEORY-044](THEORY-044.md) states what RFT says the self as context is: a perspective
learned from deictic relations, which cannot itself be found as content.
This account is the separate, empirical claim that making that perspective
stronger helps. The two can come apart. RFT's account of the self could be
right and the process clinically idle, or the exercises could help for a
reason that has nothing to do with deictic relations.

It extends [THEORY-050](THEORY-050.md). That account says inflexibility, including
domination by a "conceptualized self", maintains psychopathology across
diagnoses. This one names the process ACT says works on that part of it.

## What was actually shown

- **What ACT says.** "Self as context is important in part because from
  this standpoint, one can be aware of ones own flow of experiences without
  attachment to them or an investment in what experiences occur: thus
  defusion and acceptance is fostered. Self as context is fostered in ACT
  by mindfulness exercises, metaphors, and experiential processes"
  ([LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md), p. 8). With acceptance, defusion and contact with the present
  moment, it makes up ACT's "workable behavioral definition of
  mindfulness" (p. 9). On the inflexibility side, the model says the
  conceptualized self gains "regulatory power over behavior", so that it
  can become "more important to defend a verbal view of oneself (e.g.,
  being a victim; never being angry; being broken; etc)" than to act
  workably (p. 7).
- **What RFT adds.** Defusion exercises aim "to transform the relational
  functions of thoughts and feelings from I HERE NOW to I THERE THEN", so
  that "I am a bad person" becomes "I just had the thought that I am a bad
  person" ([LIT-541](../literature.d/LIT-541.md), pp. 370–371). On that reading defusion is a deictic
  operation, and so an exercise of the self as context.
- **The evidence.** The review's component studies are on acceptance,
  defusion and values. Of the rest it says only that "targeted studies are
  underway or completed on all of the other elements" ([LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md), p. 11),
  and reports none for the self as context. The nearest evidence is the
  defusion study in which repeating a negative self-referential word
  lowered its discomfort and believability more than distraction or
  thought control did (N = 8, alternating treatments; [NOTE-431](../notes.d/NOTE-431.md), C4). That
  counts here only if RFT's deictic reading of defusion is right, and the
  2004 paper calls that reading "no more than a heuristic" (p. 369). The
  mediation studies use global process measures, mostly the AAQ, which
  cannot isolate this process. Kashdan and Rottenberg ([LIT-553](../literature.d/LIT-553.md)) argue that
  such measures cannot capture flexibility at all ([NOTE-436](../notes.d/NOTE-436.md), C6).

So the claim is ACT's, stated plainly by its authors. It is plausible on
the conceptual account, and it is untested in anything the record holds.
It is Proposed rather than Deferred because a specific test can be named
and because the defusion evidence points in its direction. That evidence
is borrowed.

## What this does not say

- **Not that the self as context is a self.** On the conceptual account it
  is a perspective, not an entity ([THEORY-044](THEORY-044.md)).
- **Not that weakening identification with self-content is always good.**
  Hofmann and Hayes ([LIT-544](../literature.d/LIT-544.md)) warn that "few processes are always positive
  regardless of context". The meditation study of Lindahl et al. ([LIT-066](../literature.d/LIT-066.md))
  is relevant here because ACT fosters this process by mindfulness
  exercises. In a sample selected for meditation-related difficulties, it
  catalogues distressing changes in the sense of self, among them loss of
  agency, loss of basic self and loosened self–other boundaries
  ([NOTE-036](../notes.d/NOTE-036.md)). That sample is not a clinical ACT population, and the
  exercises differ in dose and aim. It does show that the same direction of
  change can be distressing, so a dose or context condition may be part of
  any true version of this claim.
- **Not that the process is specific to ACT.** The ACT review lists
  decentering (Watkins, Teasdale and Williams) and metacognition (Wells)
  among concepts that overlap its model ([LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md), p. 11). Whether any of
  them is the same process is open.

## Connections

- The minimal and narrative selves in Gallagher ([LIT-453](../literature.d/LIT-453.md)) give a way to say
  what is supposed to change: identification with the narrative self
  loosens while the minimal self is untouched. If the change reached the
  minimal self, [LIT-066](../literature.d/LIT-066.md)'s "loss of basic self" is the form it would take.
  That is the record's reading, and no source tests it.
