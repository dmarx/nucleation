# NOTE-tmpxgail: Fuentes v. Shevin — a hearing before the deprivation

## Contribution

Procedural due process ordinarily requires notice and an opportunity to be
heard before the State, at a private party's instance, seizes property
from its possessor; a later hearing, a bond and damages do not cure the
absence of a prior hearing.

## Key results

- **When** (Part IV, quoting Armstrong v. Manzo): the opportunity to be
  heard "must be granted at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner",
  and so "it must be granted at a time when the deprivation can still be
  prevented."
- **Repair is not prevention** (Part IV): "At a later hearing, an
  individual's possessions can be returned to him if they were unfairly or
  mistakenly taken in the first place. Damages may even be awarded to him
  for the wrongful deprivation. But no later hearing and no damage award
  can undo the fact that the arbitrary taking that was subject to the right
  of procedural due process has already occurred." Quoting Stanley v.
  Illinois: the Court has not "embraced the general proposition that a
  wrong may be done if it can be undone."
- **Bond and damages** (Part IV): they "are hardly a substitute for a prior
  hearing, for they test no more than the strength of the applicant's own
  belief in his rights."
- **Merits irrelevant to the right** (Part V): "The right to be heard does
  not depend upon an advance showing that one will surely prevail at the
  hearing."
- **Temporary deprivation** (Part V): "a temporary, nonfinal deprivation of
  property is nonetheless a 'deprivation'".
- **Exceptions** (Part VI): "extraordinary situations" may justify
  postponing the hearing; "These situations, however, must be truly
  unusual."
- **Holding** (Part VII): the statutes "work a deprivation of property
  without due process of law insofar as they deny the right to a prior
  opportunity to be heard before chattels are taken from their
  possessor", a holding the Court calls "a narrow one". Printed
  repossession clauses were not a waiver, which "must, at the very least,
  be clear."
- **Dissent** (White, J., with Burger, C.J., and Blackmun, J.): "I would not
  construe the Due Process Clause to require the creditors to do more than
  they have done in these cases".

## Assumptions

Constitutional law on state action under the Fourteenth Amendment: officers
seizing goods on a private application. A possessory interest short of
title suffices. Decided 4–3, Powell and Rehnquist, JJ., not participating.

## Concepts

Procedural due process; pre-deprivation hearing; "meaningful time";
prejudgment replevin; bond; temporary and final deprivation; possessory
interest; extraordinary situations; waiver.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpazi8a.** The Court places the wrong in the timing of the
  opportunity, "at a time when the deprivation can still be prevented",
  and holds the right independent of the merits ("does not depend upon an
  advance showing that one will surely prevail"), which is the claim's
  "even where the objection would have failed". It is the legal analogue
  the claim cites from A59. Its scope is a constitutional rule about
  state-assisted seizures, with extraordinary situations excepted, not a
  general moral principle.
- **CLAIM-tmpl0sgt.** The Court refuses to let after-the-fact remedies
  stand in for the prior opportunity: damages may be awarded, but they
  cannot "undo the fact" of the taking, and bond-and-damages liability is
  "hardly a substitute for a prior hearing". That is a legal rejection, for
  hearings, of the identity the claim's defeat condition names, between the
  right to decide whether something happens and compensation after. The
  protection at stake is a hearing, not the holder's consent, so it is an
  analogy.
- **CLAIM-464** and the D.V.D. removals (NOTE-048, LIT-052) turn on the
  same point: an opportunity to be heard before an irreversible act.
- A59's "a later hearing or damages cannot undo the fact of a deprivation"
  is a close paraphrase of Part IV, not a quotation.

## Limitations

State action and a constitutional floor of procedure, not private wrongs.
A 4–3 decision; later doctrine on prejudgment remedies was not checked, so
nothing here says how far it remains authoritative. Pinpoints are by Part.
