# LIT-tmp2q4is: The Perils of Unearned Foreign Income: Aid, Remittances, and Government Survival

Ahmed, F. Z. (2012). The Perils of Unearned Foreign Income: Aid, Remittances, and Government Survival. *American Political Science Review* 106(1): 146–165. doi:10.1017/S0003055411000475. Corrigendum: *American Political Science Review* 108(2): 478 (2014), doi:10.1017/S0003055414000197.

## Key takeaways

*Not read. From the publisher's abstract only, unverified until read; no page of the body was seen.*

- **The claim.** Governments in more autocratic polities "can
  strategically channel unearned government and household income in the
  form of foreign aid and remittances to finance patronage, which extends
  their tenure in political office."
- **The baseline.** Duration models of government turnover, "97 countries
  between 1975 and 2004": unearned foreign income in more autocratic
  countries "reduces the likelihood of government turnover, regime
  collapse, and outbreaks of major political discontent."
- **Identification.** "a natural experiment of oil price–driven aid and
  remittance flows to poor, non–oil producing Muslim autocracies"; the
  instrumental-variables results are said to confirm the baseline.
- **Mechanism.** Governments in autocracies "use aid and remittances
  inflows to reduce their expenditures on welfare goods to fund
  patronage."
- **Not seen**: effect sizes, the measure of autocracy and its
  interaction, first-stage strength, the IV sample, the spending
  variables, robustness, and anything on horizon or withdrawal.

It extends the account of unearned income in THEORY-1459 (Moore) and
THEORY-1460 (Moss et al.) in two ways, if the body bears the abstract
out: it counts remittances, household income, with aid, and its outcome
is the survival of the incumbent rather than the quality of
institutions. That bears on CLAIM-600's last clause, that the unit whose
persistence counts must be the one the support was for: supply that keeps
a government in office while it cuts welfare spending is persistence of
the wrong unit. An incumbent entrenched by outside supply may also be a
case for CLAIM-445 (a protective regime becoming self-maintaining) and
CLAIM-464 (an intervention changing what it would take to correct it);
that suggestion is the reader's, not Ahmed's. Knack (2000), LIT-tmp0gnxg,
filed beside it, measures the institutional side of the same unearned-income
logic.
