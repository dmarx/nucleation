# THEORY-096: Perceptual consciousness is global availability: a content becomes conscious when the activity it drives into a core of long-range hub neurons, prefrontal among them, crosses a threshold and ignites it, so that the network amplifies, sustains and broadcasts that one content to the specialised processors; below threshold it leaves a decaying wave and is not experienced

## Source

- Mashour, Roelfsema, Changeux & Dehaene (2020), *Conscious Processing and
  the Global Neuronal Workspace Hypothesis*, [LIT-631](../literature.d/LIT-631.md), read in full in
  [NOTE-510](../notes.d/NOTE-510.md): Introduction, Central Tenets, Empirical Evidence, The Role
  of Recurrent Activity, The GNW and Other Theories, Table 1 and Figures 1–2.
  The PMC manuscript has no page numbers, so sections are cited by heading.

## What was actually shown

**The mechanism, as the review states it.** "In the conscious state, a
non-linear network ignition associated with recurrent processing amplifies
and sustains a neural representation, allowing the corresponding
information to be globally accessed by local processors" ([LIT-631](../literature.d/LIT-631.md),
abstract). Besides the specialised processors there is a second space of
"GNW neurons" with long-range reciprocal axons, mainly large layer II/III
pyramidal cells with a layer V contribution, forming a "high-efficiency
cortical core" of prefrontal, parietal, cingulate and temporal hubs.
Ignition is "the sudden, coherent, and exclusive activation of a subset of
workspace neurons coding for the current conscious content, with the
remainder of the workspace neurons being inhibited" (Central Tenets). The
theory is "not a localizationist theory": prefrontal cortex is a key hub
because of its density of broadcasting neurons, but "not ... the exclusive
territory for conscious access", and in tasks that need fine detail V1
neurons "become part of the GNW".

**The evidence for ignition.** Across vision, audition and touch, the
first ~200 ms can be preserved on unseen trials, and conscious perception
correlates with later events, the P300 and late prefrontal and parietal
activity; subliminal stimuli give a decaying wave instead (Empirical
Evidence). The strongest single result is van Vugt et al. (2018): monkeys
reported weak stimuli by saccade, and "both perceived and unperceived
stimuli caused activity in V1 and V4, but only the perceived ones elicited
a sudden, strong, and sustained activity in the frontal cortex akin to
ignition", with spontaneous prefrontal ignitions on false-alarm trials.
"The SDT threshold corresponds to the threshold for ignition in the PFC",
and sensitivity to how well the signal propagates there. In mice,
optogenetically silencing feedback from secondary motor to somatosensory
cortex attenuates late amplification and prevents perception (Manita et
al. 2015), one of the few causal results. Spiking simulations give either
self-amplifying ignition or a decaying wave for the same input.

**The step from access to experience.** The review's explanandum is
access: "the term 'consciousness' in this review will be replaced by
conscious access". It takes the step to experience by adopting Dehaene and
Naccache's claim that "global availability of information (...) is what
we subjectively experience as a conscious state" (Introduction), and the
theory "brings into question the very distinction between phenomenal and
access consciousness" (The GNW and Other Theories). Of the proposal that
recurrence within sensory cortex suffices for phenomenal consciousness, it
says that "short of an experimental method for producing conscious
experience in the absence of conscious access, this proposal remains
untested". This account states the theory with that premise, because
without it the theory is about access alone and has no rivals in the
record. The premise is adopted, not shown: the evidence above is evidence
about what reported or accessed contents have that unreported ones lack.

What could have come out otherwise: missed stimuli could have failed in
V1 or V4 rather than en route to prefrontal cortex, or reported and missed
stimuli could have differed by degree in prefrontal cortex rather than by
an all-or-none event that also occurs on false alarms. In van Vugt et
al.'s data, as reported, neither happened.

## What this does not say

- **It does not show that unignited contents are unexperienced.** That is
  the adopted premise. The review's data are contrasts between reported
  and unreported stimuli, and contrasts in level of consciousness.
- **It does not settle the P3.** An earlier negativity (the VAN, ~260 ms)
  survives when stimuli are task-irrelevant yet reported seen, without a
  P3. Whether such stimuli are "truly seen as opposed to being merely
  potentially visible but unattended" is called "(unresolved)".
- **It does not say prefrontal cortex is the seat.** It is one hub of a
  distributed core, and the review places some reverberant loops through
  the thalamus and cerebellar nuclei.
- **Its evidence is second-hand and largely correlational.** It is a
  proponents' review with no new data, and the authors say "it is
  difficult to adjudicate experimentally among these four related
  theories"; anaesthesia, for example, is consistent with all of them.
- **It is not a claim about why access is felt.** It identifies experience
  with availability and does not argue for the identification.

## Rivals

**Overflow ([THEORY-103](THEORY-103.md)).** That account holds, from partial-report
studies as Block reports them ([LIT-646](../literature.d/LIT-646.md), [LIT-640](../literature.d/LIT-640.md)), that subjects
experience more items than the roughly four that working memory holds:
the uncued items are experienced though unaccessed. This account says a
content is experienced only when it ignites the workspace, and ignition is
exclusive, so the uncued items, never broadcast, were not experienced:
at most they were, in the review's phrase about the Pitts stimuli,
"merely potentially visible but unattended". That is the
refrigerator-light reading Block argues against. Both accounts accept the
same capacity data and cannot both be right about whether the surplus is
experienced. Neither yet corrects the other: the review does not discuss
Sperling or overflow, and calls the alternative untested, while Block's
case for overflow is an inference to the best explanation.

**A sensory, not prefrontal, basis ([THEORY-098](THEORY-098.md)).** That account holds
that the neural basis of perceptual experience does not include the
prefrontal machinery of access, and is recurrent activity in sensory
cortex; prefrontal involvement is causal at most. This account makes the
ignition of a long-range core with prefrontal hubs what makes a content
conscious. The two disagree on the same phenomenon and the same
evidence. The review cites Panagiotaropoulos et al. (2012), whose monkeys
passively watched binocular rivalry while prefrontal neurons encoded the
current percept, as "not confounded by working memory or effects related
to reporting". Block (2019), [LIT-637](../literature.d/LIT-637.md), a source of that account,
argues that this is just what a no-report design cannot show, since a
monkey not asked to report still thinks about what it sees, and he offers
inconspicuous rivalry, with no prefrontal difference by fMRI, as the
better test. The review was submitted before Block's paper and does not
answer it. Each would be settled by the first kind of result in
`promote_when`.

## How it bears on the record

- **Report confounds ([THEORY-094](THEORY-094.md)).** That account holds that neither
  report nor no-report paradigms isolate the basis of experience, because
  thought about the percept persists without report. It applies to this
  account's best prefrontal evidence: van Vugt et al.'s monkeys reported,
  and Panagiotaropoulos et al.'s did not report but were not shown to have
  stopped thinking about the percept. So the review's claim that the
  rivalry data are free of confounds is exactly what that account denies.
  It does not say ignition is not the basis; it says the evidence offered
  cannot show it. No relation is declared, since that account is
  methodological.
- **Vishne et al. ([THEORY-102](THEORY-102.md), [LIT-644](../literature.d/LIT-644.md)).** Their intracranial
  data are consistent with the account but not support for it. Prefrontal
  and parietal content appeared as a burst at about 150–600 ms after a
  clearly seen image came on, later than in visual cortex, which is the
  onset ignition the workspace's proponents had predicted for that
  paradigm. But the predicted ignition at offset was not seen, prefrontal
  content did not last while the image stayed on and stayed seen, and a
  stable code for the image's content was carried for its whole duration
  in ventral temporal cortex. Every image was seen, so experience and
  access never came apart, and the met predictions were IIT's as much as
  this account's. The onset-only burst fits the review's own statement
  that sustained prefrontal activity accompanies maintenance for a later
  response, and it sits uneasily with "sustains" if the experience of a
  steady image is continuous. That is why it is not a source.
- **Homeostatic feeling ([THEORY-093](THEORY-093.md), filed alongside)** declares
  this account a rival: it puts what makes a content conscious in its
  identification as the organism's own by interoceptive feeling, and loss
  of consciousness in the brainstem, not in a cortical core.
- **AI consciousness ([THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md)).** The review states the workspace as a
  computational system and closes hoping it can be implemented in a
  device, which is the functionalist premise that account says
  architectural indicators presuppose. Butlin et al.'s workspace
  indicators ([LIT-056](../literature.d/LIT-056.md)) abstract from this mechanism.
- **IIT ([LIT-445](../literature.d/LIT-445.md))** is the review's other named rival, a posterior "hot
  zone" sufficient for experience with prefrontal cortex "not essential";
  no theory in the record states it, so no relation is declared.
