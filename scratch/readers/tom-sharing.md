## Summary
The paper claims that what sets human cognition apart is "shared intentionality": a biological adaptation for collaborative activities with shared goals and joint intentions. Reading intentions is not enough to explain it, because apes, and some children with autism, can read intentions without taking part in such activities.

- **§1** models intentional action as a cybernetic control system (Wiener; Ashby; Powers 1973), made up of goal, action, perceptual monitoring and a hierarchy of subgoals. It takes from Bratman (1989) the idea that an intention is a plan the agent commits to.
- **§2** gives an infant timeline for understanding others: as animate at about 6 months, as pursuing goals at 9 months, and as choosing plans at 14 months.
- **§3** defines shared intentionality. It draws on Gilbert (1989), Searle (1995), Tuomela (1995) and Bratman (1992). It then gives three levels of engagement:
  - dyadic: protoconversation and shared emotion;
  - triadic, at 9–12 months: shared goals and joint perception;
  - collaborative, at 12–15 months: joint intentions, joint attention and role reversal.
  These rest on "dialogic cognitive representations", a "bird's-eye view" of both roles.
- **§4** argues that apes and children with autism understand goal-directed action but do not share psychological states.
- **§5** sets out two hypotheses. The phylogenetic one: collaborative skill was selected, perhaps around 150 kya, within groups or by group-level or cultural-group selection, possibly with strong reciprocity and fairness norms. The ontogenetic one: two lines interweave, intention-reading (shared with apes) and a motivation to share (uniquely human).
- **§6** argues that language and belief–desire theory of mind derive from shared intentionality.

## Contribution
1. It is the first programmatic statement of the shared-intentionality hypothesis. It joins the philosophy of joint action to infant and ape data.
2. It turns the philosophers' conditions into developmental stages and into tests that can be run on apes.
3. It locates human uniqueness in motivation (to share) as much as in cognition (reading intentions).
4. It sketches the route from dyadic joint intention, to collective intentionality and norms, to institutions. It ties morality to Searle's "desire-independent reasons".

## Key results (section, printed page, verbatim)
- Thesis. Abstract, p. 675: "the crucial difference between human cognition and that of other species is the ability to participate with others in collaborative activities with shared goals and intentions: shared intentionality"
- Uniquely human adaptation. Intro, p. 676: "We propose that human beings, and only human beings, are biologically adapted for participating in collaborative activities involving shared goals and socially coordinated action plans (joint intentions)."
- Intention as committed plan. §1, p. 676: "Following Bratman (1989), we propose that an intention is a plan of action the organism chooses and commits itself to in pursuit of a goal."
- The core definition. §3, p. 680: "the goals and intentions of each interactant must include as content something of the goals and intentions of the other"
- Bratman's conditions, "modified slightly". §3, p. 680: "each participant has the goal that we (in mutual knowledge) do X together". The paper misnames Bratman's notion as "joint cooperative activities, as he calls them"; his 1992 title is "Shared Cooperative Activity". It adds role reversal and potential helping to the meshing condition.
- Gilbert's label. §3, p. 680: "thus creating a “shared commitment” (Gilbert 1989)"; and "each interactant has goals with respect to the other’s goals".
- Holistic representation. §3, p. 681: "represent both roles of the collaboration in a single representational format"
- Commitment marks a later stage. §3.1, p. 682: "protoconversations do not involve joint commitments to any shared goals or action plans"
- Commitment is defined behaviourally, by being upset. §3.2, p. 682: "they are not motivated to create shared goals to which they are jointly committed in the same way as humans (such that they would be upset if the other reneged; see sect. 4.1.2 on apes)"
- Apes, collaboration. §4.1.2, p. 685: "There are no published experimental studies – and several unpublished negative results (two of them ours) – in which chimpanzees collaborate by playing different and complementary roles in an activity." Also: "do something with a commitment to do it together and to help each other with their role if needed".
- Morality via collective norms. §3.4, p. 684: "a new kind of social rationality (morality) involving what Searle (1995) calls “desire-independent reasons for action.”"
- Collaboration needs goals about intentions. §5.1, p. 687: "collaborative interactions require interactants to have goals about others’ intentional states"
- Phylogeny, with reciprocity. §5.1, pp. 687–688: "perhaps heralding the emergence of modern humans some 150,000 years ago" (p. 687); and "they are motivationally built for strong reciprocity, in which their behavior is governed by social norms of “fairness”" (p. 688).
- Ontogenetic pathway. §5.2, p. 689: "14-month-olds understand other persons as intentional agents and so share intentions (and attention) and engage with them collaboratively"; and "we might tentatively conclude that this is a very robust, heavily canalized ontogenetic pathway".
- Language derived. §6, p. 690: "Language is not basic; it is derived."
- Against reductive accounts. Note 3, p. 691: "But this is not enough; we might each want the box open and know that the other does also, but still not form a shared goal (perhaps we will compete to see who can open it)." Also: "I want us to have a shared commitment."
- Concession. Response R3, p. 723: "this claim has the least empirical support of any in the article". This refers to infants sharing goals and intentions at 14 months.

## Assumptions
- Intentional action is a feedback control system. Understanding others' actions means modelling that system.
- The philosophers' conditions for shared intention apply to infants and can be tested by behaviour: re-engaging, role reversal, helping, "upset if reneged".
- Ape negatives are informative even though they rest on unpublished nulls and observations.
- The pathway is universal and canalized, but almost no data on cultural variation or individual differences are cited (p. 689).
- "Motivation to share" is a primitive. The response (R5) concedes the term is hard to operationalize.

## Concepts
shared intentionality and "we" intentionality; shared goal and shared commitment; joint intention; joint attention versus joint perception; dyadic, triadic and collaborative engagement; role-reversal imitation; dialogic cognitive representations; the bird's-eye view; collective intentionality; desire-independent reasons; the control-systems model of intentional action; emulation versus imitation; the declarative versus imperative motive; the ratchet effect.

## Bearing on the record

### THEORY-495 (Tomasello 2020, obligation): background and precursor, with three shifts
- **Shared structure.** The two-step structure (a dyadic "we", then a collective, normed "we") is already here. §3.4 and §5.2 run joint intentions at about 1 year, then social norms and collective intentionality "from ages 1 to 5", then morality as Searle's desire-independent reasons.
- **Shift 1: where normativity enters.** In 2005, morality and normativity come only at the collective, internalized-convention step (p. 684). The dyadic joint commitment carries no obligation, only being "upset if the other reneged" (p. 682). By 2020, felt obligation starts inside the dyadic joint commitment.
- **Shift 2: commitment comes apart from shared intention.** In 2005, "shared commitment" is part of every shared goal from about 12–14 months. In 2020 the obligation-specific behaviours (protest, leave-taking) appear at about 3 years, and 2-year-olds do not take leave. So, read together, joint intention and shared goal (2005) come apart from joint commitment with obligation (2020) by about two years.
  - This supports the qualification in THEORY-495 drawn from Bratman's commentary (e62): commitment, not collaboration as such, does the work and is separable.
  - It also sharpens THEORY-495's promote_when, which asks to hold collaboration fixed and vary commitment.
  - The 2005 response concedes the 14-month claim is its least supported (R3, p. 723).
- **Shift 3: reciprocity.** In 2005, strong reciprocity and fairness norms appear as possible motivational substrate and as selection pressure (§5.1, note 7). That contrasts with THEORY-495's §3 argument against reciprocity as a source of obligation. The 2016 and 2020 work turned away from reciprocity. Unlike LIT-504, the 2005 paper has no interdependence or obligate collaborative-foraging hypothesis. Its selection story is within-group coalitions (Wrangham 1980) or group-level selection.
- **What does not change.** 2005 concerns the capacity and motivation to share, not obligation, so it does not test THEORY-495's promote_when.

### Joint-agency readings
- **Bratman (LIT-488 précis, LIT-471): parallel, and partly adopted.**
  - The individual-intention model is Bratman's planning theory (an intention is a committed plan, p. 676). Shared activity is defined through Bratman 1992's conditions, "modified slightly" (p. 680). The construction is individualist: each has goals about the other's goals. That is Bratman's interlocking-intention shape, with no group subject.
  - The modifications add role reversal (the bird's-eye representation) and potential helping, an echo of Bratman's mutual-support commitment.
  - This is a contrast with the record's account of Bratman (sufficient conditions, reductive "in spirit"). The 2005 paper treats the conditions as a description of stages of development and of a species difference, not as a philosophical analysis. It also adds a sui generis motivation to share, which is not in Bratman.
- **Gilbert (LIT-477; LIT-492, On Social Facts 1989, which this paper cites directly): borrowed label, contrasting content.**
  - "Shared commitment" is cited to Gilbert 1989 (p. 680, note 3). But it is built from individual goals about the other's goals. It has none of Gilbert's joint commitment that constitutes a plural subject with rights of rebuke.
  - Its normative mark is affective ("upset if reneged"), not entitlement.
  - Note 3 does argue, Gilbert-style, against reduction to common knowledge of the same goal: we might compete to open the box, and coerced participation is not a shared commitment.
  - So the paper sits between the two philosophers. This is the gap Pettit's dilemma (NOTE-185, e81) and Tomasello's 2020 R4 "graded" bridge later address.
- **Searle (LIT-482, NOTE-388, THEORY-625; LIT-485 and LIT-490 unread): supports the downstream route, contrasts on the primitive.**
  - The paper cites Searle 1995 for institutional facts (money, marriage, government; p. 680, p. 684), for "collective intentionality" as the later achievement, and for morality as "desire-independent reasons for action" (p. 684). So it relies on THEORY-625's structure for the institutional end.
  - Contrast: Searle takes we-intentionality to be a primitive. Here it is ontogenetically constructed: two developmental lines, plus internalization of dialogic representations "in Vygotskian fashion" (p. 689). Only the motivation to share is posited as an adaptation.
- **TERM-080 (senses of collective intention).** The paper's "joint intention" is the participatory shared-intention sense. Its "collective intentionality" (§3.4) is the institutional sense. It uses both and keeps them apart by developmental stage.

### Others
- **CLAIM-096 and QUESTION-007: background only.** The paper's normativity is psychological (motivation to share) and explanatory, and gives no grounding. Its one normative move is borrowed from Searle. It agrees with the deferral: it says nothing about why interdependence obligates.
- **LIT-495 (Alexander) and the new Alexander 1985 reading: closer than THEORY-495 is.** The 2005 paper allows strong reciprocity, fairness norms and between-group competition (Sober & Wilson; Boyd et al.) as part of the substrate. It supplements a "Machiavellian" story with a "Cultural" one. This narrows the 2005 version's contrast with reciprocity-first accounts; the sharper collaboration-first contrast is a 2016/2020 development.
- **LIT-047 and NOTE-034 (morality-as-cooperation): parallel.** Both treat cooperation as the root of norms. This paper gives a cognitive and motivational precondition (shared intentionality) that MAC does not model, and its fairness domain appears only as a possible substrate (note 7).
- **LIT-504 (2016, unread): precursor.** The 2016 chapters' "second-personal" and "objective" moralities expand §3.4 and §5.2 here. The interdependence hypothesis is absent in 2005.

## Limitations
- It is a review and synthesis with no new data. Many key findings were then "in press" or "submitted" from the same lab.
- The ape collaboration claims rest partly on unpublished negative results (p. 685). Chimpanzee collaboration work published later (e.g. Melis et al., cited "in press" in R3) is outside this text.
- The 14-month joint-intention claim is conceded to be the least supported (R3, p. 723). It concerns infant–adult interaction; Brownell et al. note that peers collaborate little at that age.
- Universality and canalization are asserted with almost no cross-cultural data (p. 689).
- Treating children with autism as a model of "intention-reading without sharing" rests on thin evidence (Charman, as conceded in R3).
- "Motivation to share" is underspecified (R5).
- The philosophical sources are used loosely: Bratman 1992 is misnamed, and Gilbert's label is used without Gilbert's normative content.
- It says nothing about obligation or justification.

## Candidate THEORY sentence
Tomasello, Carpenter, Call, Behne and Moll (2005) propose that what is uniquely human in cognition is shared intentionality: a biologically evolved motivation and capacity to take part with others in activities with shared goals and joint intentions, in which each participant's goals include the other's goals. It emerges in human infancy, by about 14 months, from a primate-general understanding of intentional action combined with a uniquely human motivation to share psychological states, and great apes do not engage in it. Language, belief–desire psychology and social norms derive from it.

promote_when: Controlled experiments, in more than one culture, show that infants by about 14 months, but not great apes (including human-reared apes) tested on matched tasks, engage in collaboration that requires complementary roles, re-engage a stopped partner, reverse roles and share attention declaratively; and that individual differences in these skills track both intention-reading and a measured motivation to share. It is refuted if apes show the same collaborative and declarative behaviours under matched conditions, or if human children show them only well after 14 months.
