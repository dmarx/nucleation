# CASE-tmp3bisu: The alarm that learned and the alarm that was disabled: two systems that once warned too often are both quiet in ordinary operation, and only one responds when a new hazard appears

## The case

The owner, at U18, quoting A17 ("What if the subordinate processes are
quiet because they've been conditioned never to object?"): "try to come
up with some CASEs here, I think there are several different interesting
ways to illuminate and illustrate this."

A18's CASE B: "Consider two systems that originally produce excessive
warnings. In System A, the warning process learns to discriminate between
benign and dangerous conditions. It no longer responds to harmless
stimuli, but remains sensitive to genuinely novel hazards. In System B,
the supervising process simply blocks its warnings. During ordinary
operation, both systems behave identically: neither generates false
alarms. A new hazard appears. System A responds. System B does not."

## What it shows

- **Calibration against suppression.** A18: "reducing the influence of an
  unreliable signal is not equivalent to eliminating access to the
  signal", the distinction [CLAIM-349](../claims.d/CLAIM-349.md) states: adaptive regulation keeps
  access and makes authority conditional; overcontrol cuts access.
- **Quiet is not evidence of either.** "a quiet signal may represent
  either successful learning or a loss of the capacity to learn. The
  decisive test therefore concerns **response to changed evidence**, not
  the frequency of past warnings." That is [CLAIM-364](../claims.d/CLAIM-364.md)'s objection, that low
  supervisory cost cannot show non-domination, made into a pair; and it
  has [CLAIM-414](../claims.d/CLAIM-414.md)'s form, agreement in ordinary operation and divergence
  under an intervention.

## What it does not show

A18 cautioned that "neural or motivational processes are not miniature
citizens". The case is about the architecture of a signal's authority,
not about what a suppressed process wants.
