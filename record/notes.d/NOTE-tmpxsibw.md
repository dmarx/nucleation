# NOTE-tmpxsibw: Alsmith & Longo — Where exactly am I?

## Contribution

It asked people to locate themselves on their own bodies, directly, with a
task that let different answers come out on different trials. Before it,
one study narrowed self-location by interview to a single point in the head
(Bertossa et al. 2008), and two asked for judgements about a drawn figure
(Limanowski & Hecht 2011; Starmans & Bloom 2012). After it, there is a
small first-person result: judgements of where "I" am fall on two regions,
the upper face and the upper torso, and the context of the task decides
between them.

## Key insight

Asked to point exactly at themselves, people do not converge on one spot.
They stop at whichever of two candidate regions, face or chest, the pointer
reaches first. The authors read this as ambiguity in the concept of
oneself as a located thing, resolved by context, not as a self spread over
the body.

## Assumptions

- **Pointing at oneself reports self-location.** The instruction was to set
  the pointer "pointing directly at you" (p. 71). That the answer reports
  where the participant takes the self to be, and not a body part judged
  salient or a point that is merely easy to aim at, is assumed. The authors
  move between "the experienced self" (abstract) and "our concept of the
  self as a spatial entity" (p. 74) without saying which the task measures.
- **The egocentre analogy.** The task generalises the binocular
  "egocentre" test of Wells and Hering, in which a rod is turned until it
  points at oneself and the lines from several directions intersect at a
  point (p. 71). Here only where the line meets the body was coded, in one
  plane, so no point inside the body is recovered.
- **Region coding by surface landmarks.** Each response was coded by where
  the pointer's line met the body: lower torso (below the elbow), upper
  torso (elbow to acromion), neck (acromion to chin), lower face (chin to
  tip of the nose), upper face (above the tip of the nose) (p. 72). The
  boundaries were chosen to be visually salient for coding. The upper face
  therefore includes eyes, forehead and crown, and the task cannot tell the
  eyes from the middle of the head.
- **Context variation probes stability.** Height of the pivot and the
  direction of rotation were varied "to add diversity to the task and probe
  possible influences" (p. 72); invariance across them would have meant a
  stable self-location.

## Key results

- **Design (pp. 71–72).** n = 10 (six women, aged 24–48, all right-handed).
  Participants stood before a pole carrying a pointer that rotated in their
  sagittal plane. *Haptic*: blindfolded, they turned the pointer with the
  right hand. *Visual*: the experimenter turned it at roughly constant speed
  and they said when to stop, with corrections allowed. A webcam
  photographed each answer. Two blocks (modality, counterbalanced), each of
  eight mini-blocks at four pivot heights spaced at 1/12 of body height
  around the chin; in each mini-block three trials started pointing
  straight up (Top) and three straight down (Bottom). 96 trials each.
  Participants were told the task "was not a test of the ability to judge
  the direction perpendicular to the pole".
- **Exclusions (p. 72).** Because of delays between key press and photo,
  "A total of 20.7% of trials were therefore excluded from analysis". An
  ANOVA on the proportion excluded found no main effects or interactions
  (all p > .05).
- **Region (p. 73).** Repeated-measures ANOVA, region × modality × start
  (height dropped, no effects): main effect of region F(4, 36) = 9.70,
  p < .0001, "a clearly bimodal distribution of responses, with points
  towards the upper torso and (especially) the upper face predominating".
- **Start location (p. 73).** Start × region F(4, 36) = 24.00, p < .0001.
  Bonferroni-corrected: more upper-face answers from the top than from the
  bottom in the visual condition (t(9) = 5.45, p < .002), a trend in the
  haptic (t(9) = 2.83, p = .079); more upper-torso answers from the bottom
  in haptic (t(9) = 4.13, p < .02) and visual (t(9) = 3.81, p < .02). Read
  off Fig. 2: visual-top about 75% upper face; haptic-top about 39% upper
  face; visual-bottom about 39% and haptic-bottom about 32% upper torso,
  with the remainder spread over neck and lower face; lower torso near
  zero throughout.
- **Modality (p. 73).** Modality × region F(4, 36) = 4.56, p < .005, and a
  three-way interaction F(4, 36) = 3.35, p < .02, read as "a more focused
  bimodality in the visual than the haptic modality", reflecting "reduced
  precision in the haptic modality, rather than any qualitative
  difference".
- **Reanalysis without the lower torso (p. 73).** "Critically, all the
  effects reported above remained significant": region F(3, 27) = 2.90,
  printed "p = .53"; modality × region F(3, 37) = 4.59, p < .02; start ×
  region F(3, 37) = 24.25, p < .001; three-way F(3, 27) = 3.38, p < .05.
  F(3, 27) = 2.90 gives p ≈ .053, so the region effect, the bimodality
  itself, is not significant at .05 once the empty lower-torso region is
  removed; the start interaction is. The denominator 37 is also
  inconsistent with 10 participants.
- **Conclusion (pp. 73–74).** "No single body part is judged as the unique
  seat of the self." Answers are "ambiguous between at least two
  locations, though when forced to judge this ambiguity can be resolved
  according to contextual factors". Against laziness: lazy answers from
  below would have stopped at the lower, not the upper, torso.
- **Why these two regions (p. 74).** The head houses eyes, ears and the
  vestibular organ, which Sherrington put at the top of the proprioceptive
  hierarchy. The torso is "the great continent of the body, relative to
  which all other body parts are mere peninsulas", apt to anchor an
  egocentric frame; the authors cite Blanke's (2012) hypothesis that
  full-body illusions shift trunk-centred visuo-tactile receptive fields,
  and trunk rotation effects in neglect. They offer this against Limanowski
  & Hecht's reading of torso answers as the heart in a folk concept of
  selfhood.
- **The concept (p. 74).** "To the extent that such self-location
  judgements tell us anything about our concept of the self as a spatial
  entity, they tell us that the concept is inherently ambiguous."

## Concepts

- **Egocentre** — the point inside an observer from which visual direction
  is judged, found by having a rod pointed at oneself from several
  directions and intersecting the lines (p. 71); also "cyclopean eye",
  "binoculus".
- **Self-location judgement** — here, where on one's own body a pointer
  aimed "directly at you" meets it.
- **Projected self-location** — the authors' term for judgements made about
  a depicted figure rather than one's own body, as in Starmans & Bloom and
  Limanowski & Hecht (p. 71).

## Connections

It is built on the egocentre literature (Howard & Templeton 1966; Mitson
et al. 1976) and set against three studies of where the self is located:
Bertossa et al. 2008 (head, by interview, also in blind participants),
Limanowski & Hecht 2011 (head and chest on an outline), and Starmans & Bloom
2012 (eyes, by judgements about cartoon characters), read alongside in
[NOTE-tmpc7da4](NOTE-tmpc7da4.md). It calls the last "provocative" and calls both drawing studies
projections onto a depicted subject. Its torso discussion reaches toward
Blanke's account of full-body illusions, the account [THEORY-tmpuehzc](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpuehzc.md) is
filed from. Longo is a co-author of Longo et al. 2008 ([LIT-tmpnoozr](../literature.d/LIT-tmpnoozr.md)) and
signs Ehrsson et al. 2022 ([LIT-tmp3z5v4](../literature.d/LIT-tmp3z5v4.md)).

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-tmpuehzc](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpuehzc.md), the self-location dimension.** Blanke & Metzinger
  ([LIT-tmpnbv1d](../literature.d/LIT-tmpnbv1d.md), [NOTE-tmplqqpe](NOTE-tmplqqpe.md)) define self-location as "a determinate volume
  in space, normally localized within the bodily boundaries as
  represented", within which the origin of the weak first-person
  perspective lies. This study fits a volume and not a point, and it fits
  "within the bodily boundaries": no answer left the body, and almost none
  fell on the lower torso. Two things are new for the record. First, it is
  a measure of self-location other than the walking-back drift, which the
  THEORY's promotion test says cannot measure self-location alone after
  Rohde et al. ([THEORY-tmpfufvf](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpfufvf.md)). Second, that measure is moved by a task
  factor, the start of the pointer, as strongly as by anything else, so it
  inherits the same kind of problem from a different source. The head
  answers fit the weak first-person perspective's origin at the eyes; the
  torso answers do not, which is consistent with Blanke & Metzinger's
  allowance that self-location and the perspective's origin can come apart,
  and does not test it. **It neither meets nor bears against the THEORY's
  promotion test**: it involves no rubber hand, no full-body illusion and no
  one without an agency history. Proposal only: the promotion test could
  say that a pointing measure of self-location, if used, must vary the
  starting direction and report it, since this study shows the start can
  decide the answer.
- **[THEORY-tmpfufvf](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpfufvf.md), [THEORY-tmpxdoe8](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpxdoe8.md), [THEORY-tmpom3z2](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpom3z2.md).** No bearing. Nothing
  is owned, stroked or synchronised, and no interoceptive signal is
  measured.
- **[THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md).** No bearing. Participants acted (in the haptic condition
  they moved the pointer), but agency was neither manipulated nor measured.
- **[TERM-045](../terms.d/TERM-045.md) and [ADR-024](../decisions.d/ADR-024.md).** The "I" pointed at is [TERM-045](../terms.d/TERM-045.md)'s sense 1, the
  subject, as located: the self the `self` topic keeps under [ADR-024](../decisions.d/ADR-024.md). The
  instruction does not separate that self from the body or the person:
  "pointing directly at you" invites any of them.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** A worked case of what a report shows. The answer is a
  report of a judgement under instruction, and it moved with where the
  pointer started. It establishes where people stop when asked to point at
  themselves, and that this is not one fixed place. It does not establish
  where anyone experiences themselves to be, and the authors' own last
  sentence retreats from "the experienced self" to "our concept".
- **[LIT-tmp5a19p](../literature.d/LIT-tmp5a19p.md) (Starmans & Bloom 2012).** The two disagree, and the
  disagreement is partly about method: first person against third, own
  body against cartoon, a surface region that includes the eyes against a
  design that separates eyes from head. Both find the head favoured; only
  this one finds the torso.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Ten participants**, all right-handed, ages 24–48, recruitment not
  stated.
- **A fifth of trials lost** to a camera fault, shown not to differ by
  condition but reducing an already small sample of trials.
- **Statistical slips.** The reanalysis prints "p = .53" for F(3, 27) =
  2.90 (p ≈ .053) and calls it significant, and gives denominators of 37
  in a design with 10 participants. The claim that "all the effects
  reported above remained significant" is false for the region effect.
- **No baseline start.** The pointer started only straight up or straight
  down. A start pointing at the body's middle, or a random start, would
  show whether the two modes survive without a direction of approach.
  The authors' answer to the laziness worry rules out stopping at the
  first contact with the body, not stopping at the first acceptable place.
- **One plane, surface coding.** Only the sagittal plane was sampled and
  only the intersection with the body surface coded. "Upper face" cannot
  separate the eyes from the middle of the head, which is the contrast
  between Starmans & Bloom and Bertossa et al.
- **The visual condition is someone else's movement.** Participants
  stopped a pointer the experimenter moved, so reaction time and the
  experimenter's speed enter the answer.
- **No figures for the means.** The distribution is given only in Fig. 2.

## Open questions

- Do the two modes survive a start at the body's middle, or random starts
  in several planes, so that lines can be intersected as in the egocentre
  task and a point or volume inside the body recovered?
- Does a full-body illusion, which moves self-location in Lenggenhager et
  al.'s drift measure, move the answers on this task, and toward which
  region?
- Is the torso mode a judgement about the self or about the body's centre
  of mass and orientation? A condition asking participants to point at
  their body, not at themselves, would separate them.
