# NOTE-tmph49t5: Team Reasoning and Intentional Cooperation for Mutual Benefit

## Contribution

A new formulation of team reasoning built for cooperation for mutual
benefit, not for a team's collective good. It argues that the economist's
standard device, keeping individual rationality and putting the pro-social
motive into the preferences, cannot say what trust is. It argues that
Bacharach's team reasoning cannot either, and gives a schema that can.

## Key insight

Intending that each of us benefits is not intending the benefit of us as
one entity. "Intending to promote the common interests of team members is
not the same thing as intending to promote the collective interests of the
team" (p. 154). The first is cooperative. The second can ask a member to
lose so that the team gains. A team reasoner of the first kind need not see
her act as a sacrifice for a social end, nor as "adopting a collective goal
that transcends her private interests" (pp. 163–164).

## Assumptions

- **Payoffs measure each player's interests as she judges them**, not
  all-things-considered utility (p. 145). A player who maximizes her payoff
  is "self-interested"; one who does not is "non-selfish". This is what
  makes the paper's distinctions statable.
- **Maximin as the benchmark of benefit.** Whatever a player can guarantee
  herself is not a product of cooperation (p. 157).
- **Pure strategies**; mixed strategies are set aside (notes 1 and 3).

## Key results

- **The games** (pp. 145–150, payoffs to A and B).
  - Trust Game: hold gives (0, 0); send, then keep gives (−1, 5) and
    return gives (2, 2).
  - Market Game: the same, but keep gives (−1, −1), so returning is in B's
    interest too.
  - Confidence Game: return benefits A at B's expense.
  - Dilemma Game, Hi–Lo Game and a three-player Snowdrift Game.
- **Social preferences fail on trust** (§3).
  - Inequality aversion or altruism can make B return. But it ignores that
    A's send preceded it, and second movers are known to respond to the
    options the first mover removed (Falk et al. 2003).
  - Guilt aversion (Battigalli and Dufwenberg) would have B confirm
    expectations in the Confidence Game too, where doing so is being
    conned.
  - Under Rabin's reciprocity, sending is neither kind nor unkind when
    return is expected, since (2, 2) beats (0, 0) for both. So B would keep:
    the practice of trust cannot be common knowledge in equilibrium.
- **Against Bacharach** (§4). His team reasoning:
  - maximizes a team utility, which needs interpersonal comparison and can
    trade members' interests off;
  - binds whether or not others identify, which leaves no room for
    reciprocity;
  - treats individual rationality as the benchmark, a module that switches
    on only when individual choice fails;
  - assumes teams can compute optimal profiles, where real cooperation
    follows existing and perhaps suboptimal conventions (rules of the
    road).

  Bacharach makes group identification psychological and prior to
  rationality: "the question 'Who am I?' (or 'Who are we?') is logically
  prior to rational choice" (p. 153).
- **Mutually beneficial practice** (§5).
  - Condition 1: every player does better than her maximin payoff.
  - Condition 2, for three or more players: every subgroup benefits from
    its complement's participation. This excludes free riding: in the
    Snowdrift Game, (dig, dig, wait) fails it.
- **The Schema of Cooperative Team Reasoning** (pp. 161–162).
  - P1: there is common reason to believe s* is a mutually beneficial
    practice.
  - P2: there is reciprocal reason to believe each player will choose her
    part of s*.
  - P3: there is reciprocal reason to believe each player endorses and acts
    on the schema.
  - So, C: I should choose my part of s*.

  Endorsing the schema is the analogue of Bacharach's group
  identification. P3 works like a contract that binds only when everyone
  has signed.
- **Conclusion** (pp. 163–164). In the Trust Game the schema can lead the
  second mover against her interest, but she does not construe it as a
  sacrifice. In the Market Game it leads to what self-interest also
  recommends, but with the intention of mutual benefit. Contra Adam Smith's
  butcher, market exchange need not express self-interest on either side.

## Concepts

- **self-interested / non-selfish**: acting to maximize, or not, one's
  payoff interpreted as one's own interests as one judges them.
- **social preferences**: preferences that take direct account of others'
  payoffs, beliefs or intentions.
- **team reasoning**: asking "What should we do?" rather than "What should
  I do, given what others will do?". Not reducible to the second by
  redefining payoffs (p. 152).
- **common versus collective interests**: each member's benefit, versus the
  benefit of the team considered as a single entity.
- **mutually beneficial practice**: a strategy profile meeting Conditions 1
  and 2.
- **reciprocal reason to believe**: a chain of reasons to believe about the
  others, saying nothing about what one has reason to believe of oneself.

## Connections

- **Sugden 2003 (LIT-tmpvsmnt)**, unread. This paper revises Sugden's own
  1991 and 1993 papers and develops ideas from 2011, and the 2003 paper
  sits between them. It does not restate the 2003 schema, so it shows what
  Sugden kept and changed, not what the 2003 paper said.
- **Salice and Miyazono (NOTE-tmpaso81)** take team reasoning, via
  Bacharach, as one of the two parts of group identification.
- **The group entity versus a category of members (TERM-tmpko4rw).**
  Sugden's distinction between collective and common interests is the same
  line drawn for interests. The collective interest belongs to the team as
  one entity; the common interests belong to the members severally.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** The paper bears on it at three
  points. These are inferences; Sugden does not discuss the extent of the
  self.
  - The thesis is closest to Bacharach. To identify with a group is to
    take it as one's unit of agency, so that its good is the good one
    pursues. That makes "self-interest on a larger self" a fair gloss on
    Bacharach's team reasoner.
  - Sugden's alternative is a third shape the thesis does not yet name.
    The cooperative team reasoner intends each member's benefit, her own
    included, without a larger self whose interest could outweigh hers.
    It is not the thesis's larger self, nor self-interest as usually read,
    nor sacrifice.
  - Sugden rejects the device the thesis risks. Folding pro-social motives
    into an individual's preferences, he says, "requires, as a matter of
    conceptual necessity, that an individuals' pro-social motivations can
    always be represented as payoffs to her as an individual" (p. 145).
    Doing it by enlarging the individual is the same move.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** Sugden makes the
  objection's point about social preferences: "it is always possible to
  assume that the relevant player has a preference for behaving in exactly
  that way" (p. 148). He asks for non-trivial specifications, as
  CLAIM-tmprudek does for the self.
