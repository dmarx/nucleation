# THEORY-129: In Acropora millepora the natural night-light regime is necessary for spawning on the expected night, and displacing it delays or abolishes both release and a spawning-night programme of G-protein-coupled signalling genes

## Source

Kaniewska, Alon, Karako-Lampert, Hoegh-Guldberg & Levy (2015),
[LIT-710](../literature.d/LIT-710.md), read in [NOTE-557](../notes.d/NOTE-557.md): Results and discussion, Materials and
methods, Figures 1–4 and their supplements (from captions), the decision
letter and the author response.

## What was actually shown

**The cue is necessary, in this setup.** Kaniewska et al. ([LIT-710](../literature.d/LIT-710.md))
held 16 reproductively mature A. millepora colonies from the Heron Island
reef flat in outdoor flow-through tanks under natural sun and moon, from 9
November 2011 to the spawning night of 16 November. Six were left under
ambient conditions. Five received about 5 µmol quanta m⁻² s⁻¹ of artificial
light from 18:15 to midnight and darkness after. Five were shaded from
18:15 to sunrise. The ambient colonies spawned between 21:30 and 22:30, as
the reef did. The other two groups showed "no sign of spawning behavior".
This could have come out otherwise: mature colonies with gametes already
formed could have released them on schedule from an entrainment set
earlier, which is what the months-long entrainment of earlier work
(Willis et al. 1985) would lead one to expect.

**Light quality matters, and the effect is quick.** In a separate 2006
experiment with 90 colonies, six hours of nocturnal light after sunset in
blue, green or white light, at intensities from about 1 to 100 µmol quanta
m⁻² s⁻¹, delayed spawning by 6–8 h or by two nights. Red light did not
delay it, and those colonies spawned at 21:30 with the controls and the
field (one-way ANOVA, Tamhane post hoc, p < 0.01). Both experiments altered
the nights for about a week, so whatever the light acts on responds within
days.

**A transcriptional programme follows the cue.** From 12,384 genes the
authors selected 184 that were highly variable only on the spawning day.
In ambient colonies these changed, mostly upward, just before and during
gamete release. In dark colonies they did not change. In lit colonies they
changed early, around 18:15 and 19:30. Up-regulated genes on the spawning
night (177) were enriched for G-protein-coupled signalling, signal
transduction and respiration. They include two melanopsin-like homologs
and many GPCRs, and qPCR on 12 genes matched RNA-seq with r² = 0.92.
Down-regulated genes (29) were enriched for rhythmic processes. The
programme is in colony tissue, not gametes: its correlation with the
released gametes' transcriptome was near zero.

**So the claim.** In this species at this site, the night-light conditions
of the days before and of the spawning night are necessary for release on
the expected night. Displacing them displaces or abolishes both the release
and a spawning-night signalling programme that is timed with it. The
authors' proposed cascade runs from moonlight or another signal, through
melanopsin-like photoreceptors and neuropeptides acting on GPCRs, to
follicle rupture and gamete release (their Figure 4). The account holds it
as a hypothesis the expression data are consistent with, not as shown.

## What this does not say

- **It does not show that moonlight as such is the cue.** Both added light
  after sunset and darkness all night abolished spawning. What the
  experiment shows is that the natural pattern is needed. It does not show
  which feature: the dark interval after sunset, moonrise, or moonlight
  intensity. The authors' own list of candidate cues includes "the onset of
  darkness".
- **It does not show the cascade causes release.** Nothing was blocked,
  knocked down or applied. The authors say they have "no direct evidence"
  that the melanopsin-like homologs are photopigments.
- **It does not show an endogenous clock.** No colony was held in constant
  conditions to show a free-running rhythm. Rhythmic-process genes go down
  on the spawning night and cryptochromes vary with lunar phase (N = 4,
  August), which is consistent with a clock and does not demonstrate one.
- **It does not show coordination between colonies.** Each colony responded
  to the same external drive. Synchrony across the reef, and across the
  130-odd species that spawn together on the Great Barrier Reef, is the
  expected consequence of a shared cue. It is not measured here, and
  coupling between colonies (for example by a pheromone, which the authors
  mention once on the strength of a tachykinin-like receptor) is not
  tested.
- **"Prevented" may be "delayed".** The fate of the lit and dark colonies
  after 16 November is not reported, and the 2006 experiment found delays
  of up to two nights.
- **Scope.** One species, one site, one spawning night for the
  transcriptome, groups of 5–6, and replication of the RNA-seq libraries
  that the paper does not state.

## Connections

- **[THEORY-126](THEORY-126.md)** (collective emotion) has as its minimal case convergence
  across individuals produced by a shared event, with no mutual awareness
  and no group subject. On this evidence coral mass spawning is a
  non-cognitive instance of that minimal form, collective timing by common
  cause. The parallel is the record's; neither account is about the
  other's phenomenon, and no relation is declared.
- **[THEORY-069](THEORY-069.md)** (HKB) is the record's account of collective timing by
  coupling, where units entrain one another and the collective variable
  undergoes a phase transition. This account is its contrast case: timing
  by a shared external drive with no coupling shown. No relation is
  declared.
- **Reef recovery ([THEORY-128](THEORY-128.md)).** Recovery after bleaching on the same
  reef system runs through recruitment, which depends on successful
  spawning. The two accounts compose; neither rests on the other.
