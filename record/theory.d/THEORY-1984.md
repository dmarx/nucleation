# THEORY-1984: Hohfeld proposes that the word right in judicial reasoning covers several distinct legal relations, which reduce to eight fundamental conceptions, claim and duty, privilege and no-right, power and liability, immunity and disability, each with a correlative held by another party and an opposite, and that a legal power, volitional control over a change in legal relations, is distinct from the physical power to perform the acts that exercise it and from the privilege of performing them

Hohfeld (1913), [LIT-2762](../literature.d/LIT-2762.md).

## The account

"the term 'rights' tends to be used indiscriminately to cover what in a
given case may be a privilege, a power, or an immunity, rather than a right
in the strictest sense" (p. 30). The scheme exhibits the relations as
opposites and correlatives. "X's right that Y should not eat the food
persists even though X's own privilege of doing so has been extinguished"
(pp. 36–37). One must "distinguish carefully between the legal power, the
physical power to do the things necessary for the 'exercise' of the legal
power, and, finally, the privilege of doing these things" (p. 52).

## What it does not show

- Anything about moral rights, demands or commitments; the analysis is of
  positive law, and what rights there ought to be is "justice and policy".
- That inalienability is an immunity: an immunity is freedom from
  another's power; a holder's inability to alienate would be a disability.
