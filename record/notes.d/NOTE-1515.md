# NOTE-1515: Patrick et al. — Need fulfillment in relationship functioning

## Contribution

It extends SDT's need-satisfaction findings from individual well-being to
the quality of a specific relationship, with three designs: a pooled
analysis of eight unpublished samples (Study 1), an actor–partner analysis
of couples (Study 2), and an event-contingent diary of disagreements with a
mediation model (Study 3). It is the relationships-motivation work, after
La Guardia et al. 2000, that tests all three needs inside one relationship.

## Key insight

Within a close relationship the three needs do not weigh alike:
"relatedness consistently emerged as the strongest and, in many cases, as
the only unique predictor" of relationship outcomes, while competence
carried individual well-being (pp. 451, 440–441). And need fulfillment is
said to work through the reasons a person stays: people whose needs are
met "have more intrinsic reasons for being in the relationship, which, in
turn, predicts better relationship well-being" (p. 450).

## Assumptions

- **Need fulfillment = the Basic Need Satisfaction in Relationships Scale**
  (La Guardia et al. 2000): nine 1–7 items, three per need, e.g. "When I
  am with my partner, I feel free to be who I am" (p. 438). α: overall
  .86–.90, autonomy .59–.76, competence .67–.79, relatedness .75–.82
  (Study 1).
- **Correction for attenuation** (Hunter and Schmidt) on every Study 1
  estimate, then Fisher-z inverse-variance pooling across samples (p. 438).
- **Relationship motivation = the Couple Motivation Questionnaire** (Blais
  et al. 1990), 18 reasons weighted −3 to +3 along the autonomy continuum,
  α .76 (p. 447).
- **All self-report, all correlational**, all students (pp. 454, 437).

## Key results

### Study 1: eight samples, N = 1,918, 81% women, mean age 22.6 (pp. 437–441)

- **Overall need fulfillment** (Table 2, p. 440), corrected r: satisfaction
  .72 (k = 8), commitment .57, perceived conflict −.61, defensiveness
  −.51, understanding .34, avoidant attachment −.56, anxious −.50,
  self-esteem .42, positive affect .42, negative affect −.43, vitality .42.
- **Among the subscales** (p. 439): autonomy–relatedness .86,
  competence–relatedness .84 (corrected); autonomy–competence exceeded the
  scales' reliabilities in several samples, so an uncorrected .68 is
  given; corrected it is .96 (95% CI .88 to 1.0) (p. 441).
- **Unique contributions, partial r** (Table 3, p. 440): relatedness with
  satisfaction .65, commitment .59, avoidance −.42; competence with
  self-esteem .39; autonomy with perceived conflict −.21, and near zero
  with positive affect (−.01) and vitality (−.00). Relatedness was a
  stronger predictor than autonomy and competence for every relationship
  outcome (Meng–Rosenthal–Rubin Z from 4.75 to 39.90, p. 441).
- **Repeated with an autonomy–competence composite**: "nearly identical"
  (p. 441). For the nearly 400 men alone, autonomy's unique associations
  with negative affect, both attachment dimensions, satisfaction,
  commitment and defensiveness were not significant (footnote 4, p. 454).

### Study 2: 66 couples (pp. 441–446)

- Within-couple correlations significant for overall fulfillment (.38),
  autonomy (.30), relatedness (.49), not competence (.06) (Table 4, p.
  443).
- **APIM, overall fulfillment** (Table 5, p. 444): actor β with
  satisfaction .53, partner .31; actor with commitment .43, partner .05
  (n.s.); actor with defensiveness −.47, partner −.14.
- **Actor × partner relatedness**: satisfaction t(62) = 3.19, pr = .37;
  conflict −2.22; defensiveness −2.12 (pp. 444–445). Held with actor and
  partner closeness (IOS) controlled (p. 445).

### Study 3: 120 diarists, 84% women, 908 disagreements over 10 days (pp. 446–451)

- Overall fulfillment predicted post-disagreement satisfaction (β = .42)
  and commitment (β = .41); with the three needs entered together,
  relatedness was the only unique predictor (β = .39, .46) (p. 449).
- **Mediation** (Figure 4; Table 7, p. 451): fulfillment → relationship
  motivation β = .62; motivation → satisfaction β = .43 with fulfillment
  controlled; fulfillment's β falls from .42 to .15 (Sobel z = 4.33) for
  satisfaction and from .41 to .02 (z = 6.26) for commitment. With the
  three needs, only relatedness predicts motivation (β = .62).
- Unchanged controlling for perceived resolution, prior discussion, number
  and length of disagreements, recording lapse and closeness (pp. 450–451).

## Concepts

- **Need fulfillment (in a relationship)** — the degree to which one feels
  one's needs are supported when with this partner; relationship-specific,
  not general (p. 438).
- **Relationship motivation** — one's reasons for being in the
  relationship, scored as a relative-autonomy index (p. 447).
- **Actor and partner effects** — one's own and one's partner's predictor
  scores predicting one's own outcome (APIM, p. 442).
- **Autonomy (SDT)** — "a sense of volition, agency, and initiative", not
  "independence or detachment from others" (p. 435).

## Connections

It builds on La Guardia et al. 2000 ([LIT-1979](../literature.d/LIT-1979.md)), whose need-satisfaction
measure it uses and whose finding that relatedness best predicts
attachment it extends (pp. 436–437), and on Knee et al. 2005 and Blais et
al. 1990 for relationship motivation. It places SDT beside self-expansion,
interdependence, communal–exchange and partner-responsiveness accounts
(pp. 452–454), claiming that each lacks either autonomy and competence or
a distinction of qualities of motivation.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-369](../questions.d/QUESTION-369.md) (what shows a need is a need).** This study is of the
  kind the question says cannot settle it: needs are identified by their
  correlation with self-reported well-being and relationship quality. It
  also shows something the question did not ask: in this measure the three
  needs are hardly separable (autonomy–competence .96, autonomy–relatedness
  .86, corrected), so "three needs" is not evidenced here as three
  dimensions. A note on the question should say so.
- **[THEORY-052](../theory.d/THEORY-052.md) (need frustration as transdiagnostic).** No bearing on its
  promote_when: satisfaction, not frustration, is measured, and nothing
  precedes anything.
- **[THEORY-054](../theory.d/THEORY-054.md).** Study 3 reports "a simplex pattern" among the Couple
  Motivation Questionnaire's subscales (p. 447) and then collapses them
  into one weighted index, the practice [THEORY-054](../theory.d/THEORY-054.md)'s note says keeps
  integration unmeasured.
- **RMT, the registration's reason.** The registration filed this as
  relationships motivation theory with La Guardia et al. The paper does not
  use that name; it is one of the studies RMT was later built on, and its
  mutuality finding (C3) is the "mutuality of autonomy support" Ryan et al.
  2022 name as RMT's concern. Ryan et al. report no RMT meta-analysis.
- **[QUESTION-131](../questions.d/QUESTION-131.md).** No bearing: the paper has no notion of frustration
  among incompatible demands.
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- **Correlational, cross-sectional at the person level, self-report**, and
  mostly women students (Studies 1 and 3: 81% and 84%) (p. 454).
- **Corrected correlations reach the ceiling.** Autonomy–competence .96
  with a CI to 1.0 means the two subscales measure one thing in these data;
  the partial correlations dividing their variance are then not
  interpretable as unique effects of distinct needs.
- **Content overlap.** Relatedness items ("I feel loved and cared about")
  and satisfaction items ("My relationship with my partner makes me
  happy"), and the motivation items ("Because I love the many fun and
  exciting times I share with my partner"), share content with the
  outcomes; the paper argues against overlap but does not test it.
- **Mediation with the mediator and predictor measured together** cannot
  show the order the model assumes.
- **One-tailed Z tests in Study 3**: Z = 1.80 reported as p < .05 (p. 449).
- **Two confidence intervals in Table 3 do not bracket their estimates as
  printed** (competence with perceived conflict −.12, "−.19 to .05", and
  with defensiveness the same; relatedness with self-esteem .04, "−.20 to
  .09"), likely sign or typesetting errors.
- **The communal–exchange paragraph inverts its definitions** (p. 453):
  "The former [communal] carries the expectation of immediate repayment …
  The latter [exchange] concerns … mutual responsiveness", the reverse of
  Mills and Clark, after which the paragraph uses the usual sense.

## Open questions

- Does a need measure that separates autonomy from competence in close
  relationships exist, and do the unique effects survive it?
- Do changes in partner need support precede changes in relationship
  quality, in a longitudinal or experimental design?
