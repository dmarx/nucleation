# LIT-tmpwkj6a: The Intimate Contest for Self-Command

Thomas C. Schelling (1980), *The Public Interest*, Summer 1980, pp. 94–118.
Read from the publisher's free archive (National Affairs). No DOI.

## Key takeaways

- **Two selves, alternating in command.** "People behave sometimes as if they
  had two selves", and "The two are in continual contest for control." The
  example is the boy who chose to sleep with one blanket too few: the warm
  boy at bedtime always made the decision, and the cold boy at night always
  cursed it. Who is in charge depends on timing, on whether the decision is
  "synchronized with the command cycle".
- **Self-management is managing another.** "Treating your sometime self as
  though it were somebody else is a ubiquitous and familiar technique of
  self-management." Mittens on one's own hands at night, wax in one's own
  ears, a string on one's own finger and a friend who polices one's calories
  are all the techniques used on children, patients and recruits, turned
  inward. The proposed discipline is "egonomics", and its strategic part
  deals with conscious behaviour.
- **Time preference alone fits poorly.** Explaining a lapse as a high
  discount rate would need one of "100 percent per hour or per minute". The
  essay suggests the lapser is "not thinking of the future" and "shuts his
  eyes to avoid it", rather than valuing it lower.
- **Which self is authentic has no general answer.** In the case of a person
  who asks in advance to be allowed to die and may later beg to live:
  "which is the authentic 'I'?" Some pairs are easy to judge (the vicious
  drunk), and some are not (the dieter starving to be "movie thin" who now
  and then has a good meal).
- **The tactics.** Removing the option, avoiding cues, precautionary rules
  against precursors, bright lines ("zero is a more enforceable limit"),
  public commitment, monitoring like parole, mandatory delays, small
  deadlines for large tasks, and self-administered rewards and penalties.
  Deceit, the most common interpersonal tactic, is "peculiarly unavailable"
  against oneself.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, as the reading that stands in
for Schelling's 1984 Ely Lecture ([LIT-tmpalcrf](LIT-tmpalcrf.md)). That lecture is on JSTOR
only. This essay is shorter, earlier and freely published, and it is
Schelling's own statement of the same programme.

It sits beside Ainslie's précis ([LIT-tmpne8q4](LIT-tmpne8q4.md)). The two describe the same
divided chooser. Ainslie gives a mechanism, hyperbolic discounting and
bargaining among interests. Schelling gives the tactics and a taxonomy of
habits, and expressly declines a theory of the self. Schelling's "zero is a
more enforceable limit" is Ainslie's "bright line", and his New Year's
resolution, which "raises the stakes" so that "one's personal constitution
is violated" by failure, is Ainslie's personal rule staked on a precedent.
Neither text cites the other.

Its authenticity question is the one [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md) asks in the language of
higher-order attitudes. Schelling asks it of time-slices: which self's
instruction should a friend obey? He gives cases on both sides and no
criterion. Its self-administered reward and penalty scheme is, in
self-determination theory's terms, controlled regulation that one imposes on
oneself ([LIT-558](LIT-558.md)). Schelling reports of it: "I tried it and found that it
could" work. See [NOTE-tmpacu07](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpacu07.md).

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing here belongs in the
anthology.
