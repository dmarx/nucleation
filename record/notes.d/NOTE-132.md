# NOTE-132: Moral disciplining: foundations of puritanical morality

## Contribution

The authors ask why many societies condemn apparently harmless pleasures — lust, gluttony, alcohol, drugs, music and dance — and exalt temperance, modesty and piety, which existing theories treat as outside the cooperative function of morality. They argue that puritanical norms arise from lay beliefs that cooperation requires self-control and that indulgence weakens it, so restraining pleasure is moralised as a way of protecting cooperation. They derive testable predictions for each behaviour in the "puritanical constellation" and use the theory to explain why puritanism declines in rich societies.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1.3 (preprint p. 9) states the thesis: puritanism does target cooperation and reduces to concerns with harm or fairness, via folk-psychological beliefs about self-control.
- §2.1 (p. 10) criticises Moral Foundations Theory's Purity/Sanctity-as-pathogen-avoidance account, questioning whether puritanical violations actually trigger disgust.
- §3 (pp. 14–27): people perceive that reciprocal and reputation-based cooperation require delaying gratification, and hold lay theories about what modifies state and trait self-control.
- §4 (p. 27) gives falsifiable predictions for each behaviour: moralisation tracks perceived social harm, and perceived effects on self-control mediate it.
- §5 (p. 37) explains the decline of puritanism in rich WEIRD societies (World Values Survey; Inglehart) as a cost-benefit shift; §7 (p. 42) concludes with reintegrating puritanism into "morality as cooperation".
