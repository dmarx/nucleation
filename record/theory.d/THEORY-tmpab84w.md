# THEORY-tmpab84w: Attributions of mental states to institutions, such as the knowledge that the collective knowledge doctrine of corporate criminal law attributes to a corporation no member of which held it, work largely by shaping the institutions to which they are made rather than by describing states they already had: the attribution holds the institution to a norm, the institution builds structures to meet it, and it becomes more predictable from the intentional stance, whether or not institutions really have minds

## Source

Tollefsen (2025), [LIT-tmpl99yn](../literature.d/LIT-tmpl99yn.md), read in full in [NOTE-tmpcx9bc](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpcx9bc.md). Her book
*Groups as Agents* ([LIT-483](../literature.d/LIT-483.md)) is unread.

## What was actually shown

Tollefsen ([LIT-tmpl99yn](../literature.d/LIT-tmpl99yn.md)) argues it in a short paper, in three steps.

- **A shared assumption.** Fictionalists, summativists and functionalists
  about group attitudes all treat folk psychology as causal explanation
  of pre-existing states (pp. 2–4). That leaves the usefulness of
  institutional attributions unexplained for the fictionalist, and makes
  their truth depend on a metaphysics of group minds for the others.
- **A different function.** On the regulative view (McGeer, Zawidzki,
  Andrews), ascription is a norm-governed practice that makes its targets
  more predictable by shaping them. Extended to institutions, "We are
  shaping them to be more and more agent-like" (p. 5). The view is
  assumed, not defended (p. 5).
- **Two illustrations.** In *United States v. Bank of New England*, three
  tellers each handled checks under the reporting threshold and a
  supervisor knew the rule; no one knew both, yet the bank was held to
  know. Tollefsen reports that banks then installed software, compliance
  procedures and training, so that "The attribution of knowledge, in this
  case, produced a reliable process by which the institution can identify
  violations of the rule" (p. 7). Corporate narratives and public
  counter-narratives likewise set expectations that shape later conduct
  (pp. 7–8).

## What this does not say

- **Not that institutions lack minds.** The paper "can, however, remain
  agnostic" (p. 9); what is regulated may be only individual behaviour.
  Her earlier book argued, on interpretivism, that organizations are
  agents (p. 8).
- **Not that all attribution is shaping.** "Largely" is the record's word
  for the paper's claim about the practice's primary aim; detection is
  not denied.
- **Not that shaping is good.** Philip Morris's narrative shaped the firm
  in its own interest, and the shaping of minds by law and narrative "is
  not always a positive thing" (p. 9).
- **Not documented.** No evidence is given that the ruling caused the
  banks' systems. The court sentence quoted for the case is referenced to
  a 1974 district court decision, not the Bank of New England appeal; the
  record has not checked it.
- **Not a theory of moral responsibility.** Responsibility enters only as
  legal liability.
