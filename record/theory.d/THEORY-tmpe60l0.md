# THEORY-tmpe60l0: At NASA, as the Columbia Accident Investigation Board found, the Challenger and Columbia accidents had the same organizational cause: evidence that the design was not performing as expected was repeatedly reinterpreted as acceptable, each decision seeming correct in itself and each survived anomaly justifying the next flight, while budget and schedule pressure, consensus sign-off, a hierarchy that kept dissent from rising and a safety organization dependent on the program it oversaw sustained the pattern for twenty years and undid post-Challenger reforms

## Source

Columbia Accident Investigation Board (2003), chapter 8, [LIT-tmpimphc](../literature.d/LIT-tmpimphc.md), read
in [NOTE-tmpqb4at](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpqb4at.md). Vaughan's book, [LIT-tmp189we](../literature.d/LIT-tmp189we.md), on which the chapter draws
for its central claim, is unread.

## What was actually shown

The Board ([LIT-tmpimphc](../literature.d/LIT-tmpimphc.md)) compares two decision histories.

- **The same trajectory.** For O-ring erosion before Challenger and foam
  shedding before Columbia, the first decision accepted the deviation
  rather than eliminating it. Later anomalies that did not cause disaster
  were treated as data justifying further flights, the experience base
  expanding "as an elastic waistband", and were classed "in-family".
- **Seen from inside.** "Taken one at a time, each decision seemed
  correct." The Board rejects the reading that NASA knowingly gambled as
  one "only possible in hindsight".
- **What sustained it.** Strict budgets imposed by political sponsors; a
  shift toward a business with schedule and cost goals raised to the level
  of safety; consensus sign-off that "renders no one accountable"; a
  reversed burden of proof, under which engineers had to show a flight was
  unsafe; dissent that did not surface; and safety staff dependent on the
  program they supervised.
- **Why "history is cause".** Post-Challenger reforms were undone by
  management over time, and the institutional causes of Challenger "have
  not been fixed".

The case could have come out otherwise: Columbia's foam decisions might
have followed a different path from Challenger's O-ring decisions, or the
post-Challenger reforms might have held. The Board found neither.

## What this does not say

- **Not that individuals are excused.** The Board says individuals "always
  must assume responsibility for their actions", and that replacing
  personnel would not fix the problem.
- **Not that NASA chose to gamble.** The deviation was normal from inside.
- **Not that all constraints were NASA's own.** They "were not entirely of
  NASA's own making".
- **Not a general theory.** It is two cases in one agency; the general
  account is Vaughan's, and the record has not read it.
