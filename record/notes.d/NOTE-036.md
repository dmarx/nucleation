# NOTE-036: Lindahl et al. 2017, varieties of contemplative experience

## Contribution

Meditation is promoted for health, drawing on Buddhist sources, but those sources also describe many other effects. The Varieties of Contemplative Experience (VCE) study looks at under-reported experiences: challenging, distressing or impairing ones, or ones needing extra support. It combined qualitative interviews with Western practitioners and experts in the Theravāda, Zen and Tibetan traditions with a follow-up survey on causality, impairment and practice variables. Thematic analysis produced a taxonomy of 59 experiences in 7 domains and 26 categories of influencing factors in 4 domains (practitioner, practice, relationships, health behaviours). Interpretation, valence and severity varied widely even when the phenomenology was similar.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- Participants (Methods): practitioners had to report a challenging meditation-related experience. People whose symptoms predated meditation, who had mixed or non-Buddhist practice, or whose symptoms had other explanations were excluded. Sampling was purposive "deviant case" sampling.
- Participant flow and sample (Results): 73 interviewed and 13 excluded, leaving 60 practitioners (20 per tradition, mean age 48.9, 85% US, 94.5% White, highly educated). 88% completed the follow-up questionnaire.
- Phenomenology (Table 4): the 7 domains. The most frequent categories include fear, anxiety, panic or paranoia (82%), positive affect (75%), somatic energy (63%), and re-experiencing traumatic memories (43%). Also listed are loss of sense of agency (25%) and loss of sense of basic self (25%).
- Duration and impairment (Fig. 1): 88% said effects carried into daily life. Median symptom duration was 1–3 years (range days to more than 10 years), and 73% reported moderate-to-severe impairment in at least one domain.
- Limitations: the sample is non-representative by design, so the 100% rate of challenging experiences is an artifact of sampling. The results do not generalize to Asian or Asian-American Buddhists, to mindfulness-based interventions, or to minors.

## Open questions

- A reference taxonomy of meditation-induced changes to self-model, agency and perception, useful for the consciousness and identity clusters (the "sense of self" domain) and for adverse-effects debates.
- Frequencies are within a sample selected for difficulties. A deeper read should not treat them as prevalence and should check the causality-assessment scoring.
- Check the 26 influencing-factor categories (not skimmed in detail), since they carry the practical claims.
