# NOTE-366: Block — Troubles with Functionalism

## Contribution

The paper turns the functionalist's own argument against physicalism back
on functionalism. Physicalism is chauvinist because no physical property is
shared by all realisations of a machine table. Functionalism is liberal
because it "tacks down" mental states only at the periphery, so anything
with the right input–output-and-state structure counts. It supplies the
standard counterexample: the homunculi-headed robot and its nation-of-China
variant. It also supplies the standard critique of Putnam's stipulation that
a pain-feeler has no parts with pain-feeler organisation, and the first
statement of the liberalism/chauvinism dilemma for any specification of
inputs and outputs.

## Key insight

Ask what a functional description pins down: only the inputs and outputs,
with everything inside quantified away. Then any arrangement of anything
that preserves the transitions, including a nation of people with radios,
satisfies the description. Whether that is a reductio depends on what you
think grounds the intuition. Block's answer is that the intuition is not
anti-nesting prejudice. If you are made, below the level of your synapses,
of tiny people, you lose nothing. If a system built to mimic you shares
neither your psychology nor your neurophysiology, you have no reason to
credit it with your qualia. What matters "is how the mentality of the parts
contributes to the functioning of the whole" (p. 292), and in the end not
the parts' mentality at all (n. 19).

## Assumptions

- **Target (§1.1).** Functional-state identity theses about mental state
  *types*, restricted to "narrow" states (p. 277). Token functionalism is
  not attacked (p. 288).
- **Two functionalisms (pp. 268–275).** *Functionalism* identifies a mental
  state with its Ramsey functional correlate relative to commonsense
  psychology. *Psychofunctionalism* uses scientific psychology, and may
  specify inputs and outputs as neural signals. Psychofunctional
  equivalence entails Functional equivalence, which entails behavioural
  equivalence (p. 275).
- **Functional equivalence** is relative to an input–output specification
  and a level of abstraction. Block concedes it is vague, and says
  physicalism is vague in the same way (pp. 271–273).
- **Possibility (§1.3).** The China system is taken to be nomologically
  possible for an hour. If it is not, metaphysical possibility suffices,
  given that true identities of rigid designators are necessary (Kripke).
  Block argues the Absent Qualia Argument does not inherit Kripke's
  conceivability problems, because it asks you to imagine one thing (a
  homunculus-head) and then notes a doubt about it (pp. 285–289).
- **Time scale is irrelevant** (pp. 280–281): slow minds are conceivable,
  and to insist on time scale "seems crudely behavioristic".
- **Empirical premise (§1.6).** Mentality depends "crucially on
  psychological and/or neurophysiological processes and structures"
  (p. 295). The argument is therefore "partly empirical" (p. 293).
- **Neuron count.** "There are only about a billion neurons in the brain"
  (p. 278). This sizes the China system to China's population. It is low
  by about two orders of magnitude; [NOTE-131](NOTE-131.md) records Schwitzgebel's figure
  of about 10^11.

## Key results

All are informal arguments: thought experiments and burden-of-proof
arguments. There are no theorems and no data, except the cited
McCulloch–Pitts replacement result (n. 19, citing Minsky 1967 p. 45).

- **Homunculi-headed robots (§1.2, pp. 277–285).**
  - The little-men robot: lights wired to sense neurons, buttons to motor
    neurons, a state card on a bulletin board. Each "G-man" executes one
    quadruple of a machine table that describes you.
  - The nation of China: a billion people with two-way radios, satellites
    displaying the state, run "for an hour". "Surely such a system is not
    physically impossible."
  - Objections answered. It would malfunction under floods: the describer
    fixes what counts as inputs, as with a computer. It would be too slow:
    time scale is irrelevant.
  - The same construction defeats computational-state functionalism and
    non-machine (Ramsey) functionalism. "The more intelligence exercised by
    the little men … the less inclined we are to ascribe to the simulation
    the mental properties they are simulating" (p. 285).
- **"What if I turned out to have little men in my head?" (§1.4).** Either
  privileged access tells me I have qualia whatever is in my head or it
  does not. On either answer the doubt about homunculus-heads stands; to
  argue otherwise "beg[s] the question against the doubter" (p. 290).
- **Putnam's proposal (§1.5, pp. 290–293; n. 16).**
  - Putnam's condition, quoted from Putnam 1967 pp. 434–439 (the 1975
    reprint), is "the ad hoc device of stipulating them away". Its stated
    purpose is to rule out "swarms of bees as single pain feelers".
  - The all-parts reading, that no pain-feeler has *all* its parts
    sentient-organised, does not exclude the robot, which has a mechanical
    body.
  - The no-sentient-proper-parts reading excludes "pregnant women and
    people with sentient parasites".
  - The "crucial role" reading excludes parts that both have the
    organisation and play a crucial role in giving the whole its
    organisation. It fails on the **elementary-particle people**. Tiny
    intelligent beings in spaceships mimic our elementary particles, and a
    human colonist comes over a few years to be composed of their "matter".
    "Would you be any less capable of feeling pain … ? I think not": the
    synapses' electrochemistry is unaffected (pp. 291–292).
  - Note 16 adds that reformulating Putnam to keep the particle people but
    exclude the robot would be "a most ad hoc maneuver". Split-brain and
    hemispherectomy evidence suggests each hemisphere may have a sentient
    being's functional organisation, so a Putnamian condition might rule
    out all of us. Individual neurons might share an insect's
    organisation, if inputs are neural impulses.
- **The diagnosis (pp. 292–296; nn. 17, 19).**
  - A first guess is that the robot has "too much internal mental
    structure". The particle-people case shows this is wrong.
  - The difference is that the particle change makes no difference to
    psychological or neurological processing, whereas the robot need share
    neither with us.
  - Two empirical facts back the doubt. We know brain-headed systems have
    qualia. Homunculus-heads were designed to mimic us, so the best
    explanation of their winces is the design, not pain (pp. 293–294).
  - The doubt does not depend on the homunculi's minds. Each can be
    replaced by a McCulloch–Pitts "and" neuron (n. 19).
  - The intermediate case of a billion little men each simulating one of
    your neurons is one where "intuition seems to founder" (n. 17).
- **Behaviour is not enough (pp. 294–295).** A finite list-searcher over
  all "smart speakable strings" would pass an hour-long Turing test and
  "clearly has no mental states at all".
- **Against Functionalism specifically (pp. 296–300).** Paralytics and
  brains in vats break the platitudes. The *Problem of Differentiation*:
  distinct sensations can share all platitudes. The *Problem of Truth*:
  platitudes are often false.
- **Psychofunctionalism (§2).**
  - The intuition that a homunculi-headed *Psychofunctional* simulation
    lacks mentality should be disregarded for states in psychology's
    domain. It "could have nonqualitative mental states" (p. 301), for
    example remembering that P by storing a sentence-like object
    (p. 306).
  - Qualia may not be in psychology's domain. Psychophysics would give the
    same results on a system with inverted or absent qualia (pp. 306–307).
  - The two arguments for Psychofunctionalism fail. "It is the business of
    psychology" would make protons and antiprotons identical under a
    Ramsey-sentence physics (pp. 301–303). Inference to the best
    explanation needs a minimally adequate candidate (pp. 303–304).
  - Pain may be a composite of a quale and a functional state, which would
    explain why theories of pain split (pp. 309–310).
- **Chauvinism and liberalism (§3).**
  - Human Psychofunctionalism is chauvinist about Martians who are
    Functionally but not Psychofunctionally like us. Cross-world psychology
    might rescue it, but nobody can now say whether it is possible
    (pp. 310–314).
  - Inputs and outputs described only as inputs and outputs are "wildly
    liberal". A sheik could make Bolivia's economy functionally equivalent
    to himself, and "if there are any fixed points … one of them is that
    the economy of Bolivia could not have mental states" (p. 315).
  - Physical descriptions of inputs and outputs are chauvinist. If brains
    can be input and output devices (EEG Morse code), multiple
    realisability applies to the periphery too (pp. 316–317).
  - Conclusion: a burden-of-proof argument, not a conclusive one (p. 318).

## Concepts

- **Liberalism / chauvinism.** A theory is liberal insofar as it falsely
  attributes mental properties, and chauvinist insofar as it falsely denies
  them (p. 311).
- **Functionalism vs Psychofunctionalism.** Ramsey functional correlates
  relative to commonsense versus scientific psychology (p. 269).
- **Ramsey functional correlate.** A property-abstract over the Ramsey
  sentence of a psychological theory, with mental terms replaced by bound
  variables (pp. 269–271, n. 10).
- **Homunculi-headed robot / nation of China.** A realisation of a person's
  machine table whose state transitions are carried out by persons
  (pp. 277–279).
- **Absent Qualia Argument.** If Q = S_Q and something can be in S_Q with
  its Q in doubt, the identity is in doubt (p. 281).
- **Elementary-particle people.** Persons composing your matter below the
  level of any psychological or neural difference (pp. 291–292).
- **Weak / strong equivalence.** Same outputs for all inputs, versus same
  mediating processes in a branch of science (p. 274, after Fodor).

**Composition and nesting.** Block is explicit that the robot's parts are
minded and that nesting is not what grounds the doubt. "A natural first
guess … is that they have too much internal mental structure … the example
of the elementary-particle people … suggests this first guess is wrong"
(p. 292). So the paper separates three questions: whether a whole can have
minded parts (yes, at the particle level), whether parts must lack the
whole's organisation (Putnam's condition, rejected), and whether a
functional simulation has qualia (doubtful, for reasons other than nesting).

## Connections

- **Putnam ([LIT-396](../literature.d/LIT-396.md), read from an excerpt in [NOTE-335](NOTE-335.md)).** Block's quotation
  of condition (3) agrees verbatim with the 1975 reprint, as far as the
  quotation runs. He reads "(if they count as such)" where the excerpt has
  "(if they can count as such)". He cites Putnam 1967 under the reprint
  title "The nature of mental states", noting it was "originally published
  under the title Psychological Predicates".
- **Schwitzgebel ([LIT-159](../literature.d/LIT-159.md), [NOTE-131](NOTE-131.md)).** It is the source of Schwitzgebel's
  C4 counterexample and of the China brain he enlists for neurochauvinism.
  Schwitzgebel's paraphrase of the counterexample (conscious organisms "each
  choosing to play the role of one neuron") moves it from Block's
  particle level to the neuron level, where Block reports that intuition
  founders (n. 17). Block's design argument, that the best explanation of
  the robot's winces is that it was built to mimic us, has no purchase on
  the US, which was not built to mimic anything. Block's Bolivia "fixed
  point" is the kind of commonsense datum Schwitzgebel discounts.
- **Lycan ([LIT-419](../literature.d/LIT-419.md)).** Lycan reads earlier drafts (n. 25). Lycan 1974
  is answered on the one-state-at-a-time problem (p. 282). Lycan 1981 is

## Bearing on the record

- For the bridge, the paper is the place where three questions that
  Schwitzgebel's §2 treats together come apart. Can a conscious whole have
  minded parts? Yes, per Block's particle case. Is Putnam's condition
  motivated? No. Does functional organisation realised by people bring
  qualia with it? Doubtful, but the ground is mimicry and dissimilarity,
  not nesting. A THEORY drawing the bridge should cite Block for the first
  two and keep the third separate.
- Agency and cognition from parts: Block grants a Psychofunctional
  homunculi-head nonqualitative states (C6). On the question whether
  agency built from parts brings experience with it, Block's answer is "it
  brings belief, and maybe not experience". That is the distinction the
  record's group-agency works (List and Pettit, Huebner) need, and it was
  drawn here first.
- No ML instruction. It does not belong in the anthology.

## Limitations

- Almost every step rests on intuitions about thought experiments, as Block
  says (burden-of-proof framing, pp. 296, 318).
- The particle-people case refutes only the "crucial role" version of
  Putnam's condition. It leaves open a version keyed to the level at which
  parts carry psychological processing, which is the level Schwitzgebel's
  antheads and the US occupy.
- The empirical premises are dated: about a billion neurons (p. 278), and
  right-hemisphere language figures from *Psychology Today* (n. 16).
- The "design" argument (p. 294) applies only to systems built to mimic
  something. The paper does not discuss naturally arising group systems.

## Open questions

- Does any reformulation of Putnam's condition keyed to the psychological
  level survive both the particle people and the hemispheres (n. 16)?
- If the doubt about the China system rests on dissimilarity to our
  psychology (C3), how much similarity in information processing would
  dissolve it? A Psychofunctional China system dissolves it for beliefs by
  Block's own §2.1.
- Is the n. 17 case (a billion people each simulating one neuron) one where
  intuition founders, or one where it favours consciousness? This is the
  case nearest to Schwitzgebel's antheads.
