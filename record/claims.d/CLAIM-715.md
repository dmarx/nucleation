# CLAIM-715: Friction that makes the people in a process think before accepting an output differs from friction that makes the institution answerable to their objections, so a system can make individuals think harder while leaving the institution's authority untouched, and the added scrutiny can then serve as evidence that the individuals owned decisions whose conditions they could not change

## The claim

A23 §7 of the exchange
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b)
separates two things often grouped as "designed friction".

- **Cognitive friction: make people think.** Interface interventions that
  keep people from accepting recommendations too readily, such as forming
  a judgment before the AI's is shown. A cites Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos
  (2021), which the record has read ([LIT-2283](../literature.d/LIT-2283.md)).
- **Political friction: make institutions answerable.** "An employee can
  refuse an unsafe instruction without retaliation. An applicant can
  obtain independent review of a consequential denial. An affected group
  can challenge the variables used by a scoring system. A regulator can
  suspend a deployment". "These aren't merely prompts encouraging better
  thought. They're structures that constrain the exercise of power."

A worker trained to question recommendations sees one is wrong and wants
to override it, but overriding would violate policy or hurt her rating:
"Cognitive friction has succeeded. Political friction is absent."

**The perverse case.** An organization adds verification prompts and
"then points to those prompts as evidence that users were adequately
informed and personally responsible for their decisions." If users lack
control over the system's operating conditions, "the prompts can also make
it easier to attribute failures to them." A19 §2 states the same for a
clinician whose verification time is not budgeted: "The system has converted professional responsibility into an unbudgeted safety mechanism", and the human is "**both an essential safety component and a
convenient repository of blame**".

## How it sits with the record

- **[CLAIM-349](CLAIM-349.md)** (Active, the owner's): access to a signal, its authority
  over action and action on it are separable. Cognitive friction works on
  access; political friction on authority. The claim rests on it for that
  split.
- **[CLAIM-707](CLAIM-707.md)** (same exchange): blame lands where failure becomes
  visible; [CLAIM-702](CLAIM-702.md) adds that credit goes to the system. The perverse case is one way that
  happens, and the claim rests on it for that part.
- **[LIT-2283](../literature.d/LIT-2283.md)** grounds only the cognitive half: forcing functions exist and
  can reduce overreliance. It says nothing about authority.
- [CLAIM-740](CLAIM-740.md)'s interruption right is political friction stated as an
  entitlement of the affected party; [CLAIM-710](CLAIM-710.md) is the owner's thesis
  both refine.
- **[CLAIM-712](CLAIM-712.md)**, from the first share of the same exchange, is distinct:
  traceability does not settle fair attribution, shown by two hospitals
  with equally complete audit trails ([CASE-277](../cases.d/CASE-277.md), argued in [ARG-112](../arguments.d/ARG-112.md)).
  That claim is about what a record of the decision leaves out; this one is
  about a requirement to scrutinize that gives no authority to act and is
  then cited as ownership. The two complement each other: a verification
  prompt is one way a complete trail comes to show the last human approval
  and not the conditions of judgment. The first filing mapped A23 §7 into
  [CLAIM-710](CLAIM-710.md)'s body and did not file it.

## What it does not say

- Not that cognitive friction is useless. A23 §7: "The extra cognitive
  friction may improve decision quality."
- Not that scrutiny prompts are always used to shift blame. It is "a
  possible" mechanism, needing users without "the authority, time, and
  protection needed to act on their evaluations."
