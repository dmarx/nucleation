# THEORY-125: A group can itself be in an emotional state that is not its members' states in aggregation, when its distributed routines integrate the capacities an emotion requires into one action-oriented representation; because emotions are functional kinds such a state need not be felt, and no actual case has yet been shown

## Source

- Huebner (2010), [LIT-702](../literature.d/LIT-702.md), read in full in [NOTE-553](../notes.d/NOTE-553.md), from the
  author's manuscript. Page numbers are the manuscript's, not the
  journal's 89–118.

## What was actually shown

All of it is argument. There are no data of the author's own, and the
paper says it is conditional on collective representation being possible,
which it defends elsewhere (n. 4).

**The test.** Huebner, [LIT-702](../literature.d/LIT-702.md), separates holism, on which
individuals' emotions depend on their social relations, from collectivism,
on which groups themselves are in emotional states (pp. 3–4, after Pettit).
Collective guilt in Jaspers's, May's and Tollefsen's sense establishes only
the first. What would establish the second is an emotion "that could not be
exhaustively characterized in terms of intentional states of individuals in
aggregation" (p. 4).

**What an emotion requires.** Emotions are representations with a formal
object and a particular one, indicative content and imperative force:
"action-oriented representations that reflexively reorient behavior". For
fear, at least: a capacity to perceive things as dangerous; attention aimed
at the threat and at planning; systems producing and monitoring
system-wide preparatory change; and coordination of these into one state
that guides coping (p. 10). Nothing in that description mentions neurons,
so a distributed system that integrates routines this way represents as
fear does.

**It need not be felt.** Huebner concedes that a ship's crew cannot be
conscious: it is too spatially distributed and too loosely integrated
(p. 14). He argues instead that some individual emotions are non-conscious
in a strong sense, with no felt emotion, only scattered felt bodily effects
(§5, after Sizer on moods), so a collective emotion need not be felt
either. The argument rests on describing a case, and the neuroscience it
cites shows unconscious processing of fear stimuli, which is weaker
([NOTE-553](../notes.d/NOTE-553.md), Limitations).

**Emotions are functional kinds.** Against the objection that groups lack
mechanisms homologous to ours, he argues that demanding homology rules out
artificial and alien emotion by fiat and forces Griffiths's eliminativism
about emotion even within humans. Kinds are fixed at the computational
level: "Emotions, like vodkas, require only the right sort of functional
organization" (§6, p. 22).

**The case fails, and the replacement is not evidence.** The USS Palau
crew, tracing Hutchins's report of a loss of steam pressure, detects
danger and copes, but training had removed the agitation. Huebner withdraws
it: it is "a collective detection of danger in the absence of a fear
response" (p. 23). He then offers the closing weeks of the 2008
McCain–Palin campaign as "an intuitively plausible case", noting that
"until the relevant anthropological data is collected, this appeal will be
insufficient to conclusively establish the existence of collective
emotions" (p. 23). There, he argues, members were agitated but their
emotions varied, hopeful, angry, saddened, so the campaign's fear was not an
aggregate of members' fear (p. 25).

So the paper concludes that "there are no principled philosophical reasons to
deny that emotional states can be extended to some sorts of collectivities",
and that where a group
lacks one it is because it is "not organized in the right way" (p. 25).
That is a possibility claim with a research programme attached. No group
has been shown to be in an emotional state.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say any actual group has an emotion.** The account's last
  clause says so, and the Palau failure is the only case worked through.
- **It does not say a group's emotion is felt.** The account sides with the
  view that collective emotions need no phenomenology. So does Gilbert's
  plural-subject account ([LIT-701](../literature.d/LIT-701.md), Deferred and unread), which Huebner
  reports as holding that feelings are "inessential, though common
  concomitants" of emotion, so that collective emotions "require no specific
  phenomenology" (Gilbert 2002, 119, as reported on p. 2). The two locate
  the emotion in different bearers: for Gilbert a plural subject
  constituted by members' joint commitment to feel "as a body"; for Huebner
  an integrated computational system, possibly including its technology.
  Huebner argues the plural subject's emotion reduces to individual
  commitments plus aggregation rules (p. 4), and rejects Gilbert's route to
  unfelt emotion because her psychoanalytic examples show inaccessible
  emotions, not unfelt ones (p. 15). Gilbert's reply is not considered, and
  what is said of her here rests on Huebner's report. Schmid ([LIT-697](../literature.d/LIT-697.md),
  Deferred) is, by his abstract, the view that groups do feel, as plural
  pre-reflective self-awareness.
- **It does not say convergence of members' emotions is collective
  emotion, or that it is not.** The convergence account, [THEORY-126](THEORY-126.md),
  answers a different question: whether and how members' affect converges
  on one object. This account asks whether the group bears a state of its
  own. The two are compatible. Huebner's own surviving case is one in which
  members' emotions diverged, so a genuinely collective emotion neither
  requires convergence nor follows from it, and convergence can occur in a
  group that bears no emotion of its own. No relation is declared between
  them. In List's taxonomy of collective attitudes, [LIT-700](../literature.d/LIT-700.md), the
  difference has a name: a converged emotion is an aggregate attitude, or a
  common one when members are mutually aware, and a genuinely collective
  emotion would be a corporate attitude, the only kind that needs a group
  agent. List says only that there can be "aggregate fears and hopes,
  common fears and hopes, and corporate fears and hopes" (§1); the placement
  of these accounts in his kinds is the record's.
- **It does not say a group's emotion would have the rationality profile of
  its judgments.** List's corporate attitudes must be rational, and the
  reason a group's judgments come apart from its members' is a theorem
  about deductively closed judgments ([THEORY-127](THEORY-127.md)). Huebner's test, a
  state not characterisable as members' states in aggregation, is the
  informal analogue of that theorem's failure of proposition-wise
  supervenience, and the Palau crew, routines each sensitive to one
  constraint and integrated on a chart, has the shape of List and Pettit's
  distributed premise-based procedure. But an emotion's coherence
  conditions are not deductive closure, and neither paper shows that the
  theorem carries over to emotions. The parallel is the record's.

## Connections

- **[THEORY-043](THEORY-043.md)** (minds of minded agents) agrees that functional states
  survive composition and narrows the dispute to experience. This account
  adds emotion, as a functional state, to what survives, and takes the
  same line on experience by conceding that the crew is not conscious
  because it is too distributed and too loosely integrated. That is an
  architectural verdict asserted, not computed, like List's low-Φ verdict
  in [LIT-401](../literature.d/LIT-401.md), so it does not meet [THEORY-043](THEORY-043.md)'s `promote_when`. Huebner's
  footnote 22 states the conditional Schwitzgebel ([LIT-159](../literature.d/LIT-159.md)) argues for: on
  representational or higher-order theories, collectivities "can be
  conscious where they have the right functional organization". This
  paper is the record's first read statement of the macrocognition
  programme; the book ([LIT-426](../literature.d/LIT-426.md)) is unread, and [THEORY-043](THEORY-043.md) names it as the
  work most likely to move that account.
- **Emotion theory.** The account needs emotion kinds fixed by function,
  with formal objects taken from Lazarus's core-relational themes, and
  action-orientation in Griffiths and Scarantino's sense. That is close to
  the appraisal account, [THEORY-055](THEORY-055.md), on which emotions are states of action
  readiness that differ by mode. Barrett's constructionist account,
  [THEORY-057](THEORY-057.md), would deny that fear is a kind with a shared mechanism at any
  level, and on it whether a group's state counts as fear would turn on how
  perceivers categorise it. Huebner does not cite Barrett. [THEORY-045](THEORY-045.md) is
  about felt affect, the experience of a body's regulatory state, and this
  account claims nothing about it: a group's emotion, on this account, is
  exactly an emotion without that.
