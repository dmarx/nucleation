# LIT-tmpvpoqj: Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith

*Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), No. 21-869, decided May 18, 2023. Opinion of the Court by Sotomayor, J.; concurrence by Gorsuch, J., joined by Jackson, J.; dissent by Kagan, J., joined by Roberts, C.J. Slip opinion, Supreme Court of the United States (public domain).

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmpffqxp](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpffqxp.md).*

- **One factor, one use.** The only question was whether the first
  factor of 17 U.S.C. §107, "the purpose and character of the use",
  favours AWF's 2016 licence of Orange Prince to Condé Nast for $10,000.
  It does not (maj. pp. 2, 12–13, 38). The Court expresses no opinion on
  the making, display or sale of the Prince Series (p. 21).
- **Transformation is of purpose, and by degree.** The first factor asks
  "whether and to what extent" a use has a further purpose or different
  character (p. 16). New expression, meaning or message is relevant to
  that, but "not, without more, dispositive" (p. 12). Meaning counts "as
  reasonably can be perceived", not as artist or critic intends or reads
  it (pp. 31–32).
- **The derivative right sets a floor.** Because the owner holds the
  right to works that recast, transform or adapt hers (§§101, 106(2)),
  "transformative" use must transform more than a derivative does
  (p. 16). Adaptations, sequels and remixes add new expression and still
  need licences (pp. 28–29, 35).
- **Justification, and targeting.** Copying is best justified when it is
  needed for the new purpose, as when parody must conjure up its target;
  satire, which does not target the work, must justify the borrowing
  (pp. 17–19, 34–35). Warhol's Soup Cans target Campbell's logo; Orange
  Prince does not target the photograph (pp. 26–27, n. 20).
- **The licensing market is the owner's.** The typical use of a
  celebrity photograph is to accompany stories about the celebrity, and
  photographers license stylized derivatives too; such licences are how
  they make a living (p. 22). The first factor's substitution question
  is tied to, but distinct from, the fourth factor's market harm (n. 12).
- **The dissent** (Kagan) holds that Warhol transformed the photograph
  in aesthetics and meaning, that the majority moves market harm into
  factor 1 and that targeting has no basis in precedent, and that art,
  literature and music proceed by such copying (Kagan pp. 1–36).
