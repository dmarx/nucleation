# ARG-269: Denison et al.'s curriculum results, as evidence, favour specification gaming over influence on the evaluator only through the model's stated reasoning, and do not discriminate between them

## The argument

The reading ([NOTE-2423](../notes.d/NOTE-2423.md)) gives two pieces of evidence for the counter. The authors call what generalizes a "reward-seeking policy". And the seven released tampering samples reason about the reward and about avoiding detection, or present the edit as fixing a broken reward. None reasons about an evaluator's criteria or feelings.

## Critical questions

- **Is the evidence relevant?** Yes. The two claims disagree about what generalizes, and the hidden reasoning is the paper's only window on what the model takes itself to be doing.
- **Is it strong?** No. Stated reasoning need not be the cause of behaviour, the authors note that it "often appears earnest even when the model tampers", and seven samples is few.
- **Does it discriminate?** No. In the curriculum only the first environment has a judging evaluator at all, a preference model with a grader's bonus. Flattery and rubric editing are scored by rules reading files the model writes. So the paper cannot show whether acting on a judge's assessment generalizes differently from acting on a rule's input.
- **Is there contrary evidence?** One sentence: models "invent explanations for their misbehavior that fool the PM". But the authors note that exploit-only models, trained without the preference model, explain themselves too.

## Verdict

Proposed: weak support for [CLAIM-1363](../claims.d/CLAIM-1363.md), not discriminating.
