# NOTE-550: List & Pettit — Group Agency and Supervenience

## Contribution

It turns "can a group be an agent over and above its members?" into a
question about the form of a supervenience relation, and answers it with
judgment-aggregation theorems. Individualism is kept: fix what the members
judge and do, and the constitution, and the group's judgments are fixed. But
the dependence must be holistic. A rational group agent's judgment on one
proposition generally depends on members' judgments on other propositions,
and can depend on nobody's judgment on that proposition.

## Key insight

Majority voting on every question, applied to rational individuals, can give
an irrational group (the discursive dilemma). The paper's point is that this
is not a defect of majority voting. Any rule that settles each question by
looking only at what people think about that question fails in the same
way, given enough interconnected questions. So a group that reliably reasons
must route its judgments through some structure: premises first, then
conclusions by logic, perhaps with different members settling different
premises. That structure is what makes the group an agent, and it is also
what makes the group's view able to differ from what its members, one by one,
think.

## Assumptions

- **Four conditions of agency (§2, p. 3).** Representational and
  goal-seeking states; theoretical rationality in forming them
  (completeness, consistency, deductive closure); action on them as
  practical rationality requires; and robustness across a class of relevant
  possible conditions. Stated as near-consensus, not defended. Function, not
  structure, matters, apart from two stipulations: no control from outside,
  and no "pre-emptive rigging" (p. 4, citing Peacocke and Block).
- **Judgments, not degrees of belief (pp. 4–5).** Judgments are categorical
  and cover fact and value. Rationality constraints on plans are said to
  track those on value judgments, so little generality is lost.
- **Supervenience (p. 5).** B-facts supervene on A-facts iff fixing the
  A-facts fixes the B-facts. Group judgments supervene on individual
  contributions once a constitution, a set of rules for forming group
  judgments from members' inputs, is in place. The constitution is itself
  treated as members' shared dispositions, so it adds nothing
  non-individual (p. 6).
- **Agenda (p. 7).** The court example: P, Q, R and "R iff (P and Q)", closed
  under negation. The results are stated for this agenda and said to hold
  for wider classes (n. 3: Proposition 1 for agendas with a minimal
  inconsistent subset of three or more, 2 for "minimally connected", 3 for
  "strongly connected" agendas).
- **Universal domain (p. 8).** The constitution accepts every profile of
  complete, consistent, deductively closed individual judgments.

## Key results

- **Robust group rationality (p. 9).** For every admissible profile, the
  group's judgments are complete, consistent and deductively closed.
- **Proposition 1 (p. 9).** Robust group rationality is inconsistent with
  *majoritarian supervenience* (the group judgment on each proposition is
  the majority judgment on it). Table 2: individuals (T,T,T,T), (T,F,F,T),
  (F,T,F,T) on (P, Q, R, R↔(P∧Q)); the majority accepts P, Q and the
  biconditional and rejects R.
- **Proposition 2 (p. 11).** Inconsistent with *uniform proposition-wise
  supervenience*: the group judgment on each proposition is the same
  function, for all propositions, of the individual judgments on it, and
  depends on more than one individual. A strengthening of List & Pettit
  (2002), proved by Pauly & van Hees (2003).
- **Proposition 3 (p. 12).** Inconsistent with *proposition-wise
  supervenience*: the function may differ by proposition, provided it
  depends on more than one individual and respects unanimity. Proved by
  Dietrich & List (2005).
- **Proposition 4 (p. 13).** The premise-based procedure (majority vote on
  the premises, conclusion derived) satisfies robust group rationality and
  *set-wise supervenience* (the group's set of judgments is a function of
  the members' sets), but not proposition-wise supervenience. Tables 2 and 3
  have identical R columns and give different group judgments on R. Members'
  judgments on R are insufficient to fix the group's ("weak discontinuity")
  and unnecessary ("strong discontinuity", p. 14, after Pettit 2003).
- **Sequential priority procedure (p. 15).** The generalisation to any
  agenda: propositions in a priority order, majority vote unless logically
  constrained by earlier accepted ones. Proposition 4 carries over.
- **Homogeneous and heterogeneous bases (p. 15).** A base is homogeneous if
  the group judgments are invariant under permutations of the members'
  judgment sets.
- **Proposition 5 (p. 16).** The distributed premise-based procedure, where
  each premise is decided by majority in its own subgroup, satisfies robust
  group rationality and set-wise supervenience, with a heterogeneous base.
  Each member need contribute a single judgment on a single premise, and no
  member's judgment on the conclusion is needed (p. 17). It models
  legislatures with subcommittees.
- **Table 4 (p. 17).** Proposition-wise relations, majoritarian or other,
  uniform or not, are inconsistent with robust group rationality; set-wise
  relations, homogeneous or heterogeneous, are consistent with it.
- **Escape routes (n. 9).** Proposition-wise supervenience can be kept by
  restricting the domain, relaxing completeness or closure, a dictator, a
  constant rule, or shrinking the agenda.
- **Emergent agency (§6, pp. 19–21).** The examples assume members who
  knowingly sustain the group through joint intentions (Tuomela, Bratman,
  Gilbert). Could humans form a group agent as a swarm does, with no member
  conceiving of the group? The authors doubt it: no constitution could
  guarantee robust group rationality whatever members' personal reasons
  are, unless there is central direction and deference, which is itself a
  joint intention (Block's China-body case). Invisible-hand processes yield
  results stable across contexts, but agency needs outputs tailored to
  circumstances, and "no existing theory makes sense of how this could
  happen" (p. 20).

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | A system is an agent if it forms representational and goal-seeking states, is theoretically and practically rational about them, and does so robustly | moderate | stated as consensus, §2; not defended |
| C2 | No proposition-wise aggregation rule (respecting unanimity, non-dictatorial) yields robustly rational group judgments on interconnected agendas | strong | Propositions 1–3; 2 and 3 cited, not proved here |
| C3 | Set-wise supervenience is consistent with robust group rationality | strong | Propositions 4–5, constructive |
| C4 | A group agent's judgments can be discontinuous with its members' judgments on the same proposition, while remaining individualistically grounded | strong (as a consequence of C2–C3) | §5 tables; the "weak" and "strong" discontinuity |
| C5 | Group agency is possible only where members knowingly sustain it, or defer to a director | weak | §6, an argument from the absence of any theory; offered with stated scepticism, not proof |

## Concepts

- **Constitution** — the formal or informal rules by which members' inputs
  determine group judgments (p. 5); a function from admissible profiles to
  group judgments (n. 4).
- **Robust group rationality** — complete, consistent and deductively
  closed group judgments for all admissible profiles (p. 9).
- **Proposition-wise supervenience** — the group judgment on each
  proposition is a function of members' judgments on that proposition alone
  (p. 11).
- **Set-wise supervenience** — the group's set of judgments is a function of
  members' sets of judgments (p. 12).
- **Weak / strong discontinuity** — members' judgments on a proposition do
  not suffice / are not even needed to fix the group's judgment on it
  (p. 14).
- **Homogeneous supervenience base** — the group's judgments are invariant
  under permuting the members' judgment sets (p. 15).

  formal miniature of Huebner's distributed collective representation, and
  "strong discontinuity" is the property Huebner needs for a genuinely

  set-wise survives. By Wimsatt's definition that makes group judgment
  non-aggregative, hence emergent, and compatible with reduction, which is
  List and Pettit's individualism. The strong emergence §6 rejects is a
  different notion. The comparison is the record's.

## Limitations

- Only one necessary condition of agency, theoretical rationality of
  judgments, is studied. Action, desire and robustness are not modelled.
- The results are for categorical judgments under universal domain.
  Restricting the domain, as real groups with shared priors might, reopens
  proposition-wise rules (n. 9).
- Two of the three impossibility results are cited, not proved, and the
  agenda conditions under which they generalise are only named (n. 3).
- §6's scepticism about emergent agency is an argument from the absence of a
  theory. The paper says so.

## Open questions

- Do premise-based procedures describe how real courts, boards and campaigns
  form their positions, or are they idealisations?
- Is there a graded version: how far can a group stray from proposition-wise
  supervenience and still count as reflecting its members?
- Does the distributed procedure extend from judgments to emotions, so that a
  group's fear could be fixed by members' appraisals of different aspects of
  a threat, none of whom fears it?
