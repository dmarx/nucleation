# THEORY-467: Rule-consequentialism that ranks codes by the expected good of teaching them to, and having them accepted by, new generations does not collapse into act-consequentialism, because teaching and internalisation costs favour codes simpler and less demanding than act-by-act optimisation, which therefore sometimes require sub-optimal acts

## Source

Hooker, SEP "Rule Consequentialism", [LIT-198](../literature.d/LIT-198.md), read in full in [NOTE-160](../notes.d/NOTE-160.md) (the
revision as it stood in 2023). SEP entries have no pages, so locations are
sections.

## What was actually shown

An argued survey. Its formulations are stated exactly and its replies
organised by the author who made most of them (Hooker 2000).

- **The objection.** If rules are chosen for their consequences, the best
  rule is "maximise, except where…", with as many exceptions as optimising
  needs. Then rule-consequentialism prescribes what act-consequentialism
  prescribes.
- **The reply (§§6.2, 8).** Codes are ranked by the expected good of "their
  teaching and acceptance", not of compliance. Teaching and internalising a
  code costs more as it gains rules, complexity and demandingness. A code
  with many exception clauses also invites self-serving misapplication and
  undermines others' assurance. So the best code is simpler and less
  demanding than one optimal act by act, and will sometimes require a
  sub-optimal act. The toy example (§6.2): Code A has expected value *n* and
  internalisation cost −1, Code B has *n*+5 and cost −7, so A (*n*−1) beats
  B (*n*−2).
- **Costs measured from new generations (§6.2).** Transition costs are
  computed for new generations, not from a society's existing code, to give
  no weight to unjustified codes and to avoid relativism.
- **Incoherence (§8).** The view is incoherent only if one assumes an
  overriding commitment to maximise, which neither the agent's moral
  psychology nor the theory contains. The agent's basic motive can be "to do
  what is impartially defensible".
- **Rule-worship (§8).** A "prevent disaster" rule, triggered only by "a very
  much larger difference" in expected value, so that it does not re-collapse.

What could have come out otherwise: once teaching costs are counted, the
best code could still match act-by-act optimisation everywhere. The toy
example shows a case where it does not; the general claim rests on the
premises about human nature.

## What this does not say

- **That the human-nature premises are established.** That children start
  partial to themselves and a few others, that each rule carries a cognitive
  cost, and the Sidgwickian limit on impartial concern are called
  "realistic assumptions" without empirical citation ([NOTE-160](../notes.d/NOTE-160.md)).
- **That every formulation escapes collapse.** The reply presupposes ranking
  by teaching and acceptance. It is the teaching-and-acceptance formulation
  that answers collapse; the prevent-disaster rule answers only
  rule-worship ([NOTE-160](../notes.d/NOTE-160.md), corrections).
- **That the incoherence objection "dies".** The headline sentence in §8
  outruns the caveat after it: the reply "seems less viable" if
  rule-consequentialism is argued for from consequentialist first premises.
- **Which acceptance formulation is right.** The ~90% "overwhelming majority"
  threshold is admitted to be a compromise, and the teaching formulation is
  left as a live alternative. The teaching formulation faces the open
  objection that its teachers cannot be described coherently (§9).
- **That the view survives §9.** Demandingness in other possible worlds
  (Mulgan), contingency of justification, the "wrong facts" objection and
  conflicts among rules are left open.
- **Anything about specifying AI behaviour.** The mechanism is the cost of
  teaching children many, complex or demanding rules. An analogy to
  rule-based versus outcome-based specification of AI is the reader's own;
  the entry does not draw it ([NOTE-160](../notes.d/NOTE-160.md)).
