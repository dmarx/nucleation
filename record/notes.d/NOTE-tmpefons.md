# NOTE-tmpefons: Bich, Mossio, Ruiz-Mirazo and Moreno (2016) — Biological regulation

## Contribution

A definition of biological regulation as a distinct form of control, set
against dynamic stability, with five requirements, so that what an organism
adds to closure can be stated.

## Key results

1. **Regulation (pdf p. 2).** "regulation consists in a specific form of
   second-order control, exerted over the core (constitutive) regime of
   production and maintenance of the components".
2. **Constraint (pdf p. 9).** C constrains a process P when, "at a time
   scale characteristic of P, C is locally unaffected by P" and has a causal
   role on it (after Mossio et al. 2013).
3. **Closure as the constitutive regime (pdf p. 10).** In organisms
   "biological constraints realise a circular organisation", citing Moreno
   and Mossio 2015.
4. **Feedback is not closure (pdf p. 14, footnote).** "feedback realises a
   circularity of processes, and it depends, in its basic instances, on the
   action of only one constraint (whose existence does not require that
   circularity). Organisational closure, instead, consists in a circular
   generative relation among constraints, and implies the mutual dependence
   of several constraints".
5. **Five requirements (pdf pp. 17–18).** The regulator R is produced by the
   constitutive regime C; it is dynamically decoupled, operating "at a
   different dynamical scale and under different stoichiometric
   requirements than C"; it is triggered by perturbations; it shifts C
   between regimes; and the new regimes cope with the perturbation.
6. **Not reducible (pdf p. 23).** "This special type of control, which we
   label regulation, cannot be accounted for by other forms of compensation,
   like distributed network responses or simple feedback loops."
7. **Scope (pdf p. 6).** Regulation could be studied "from proto-cells to
   multicellular systems, or even at the ecological sphere"; the paper keeps
   to prokaryotes.
8. **Complexity (pdf p. 24, paraphrase).** Decoupling lets regulation scale,
   and so supports growth in complexity, which a distributed network cannot.

## Assumptions

Closure as the constitutive regime is taken from the book and from Montévil
and Mossio 2015; bacterial chemotaxis and the lac operon are taken as
minimal regulation.

## Concepts

Control; constitutive regime; dynamic stability; regulation; second-order
control; dynamical decoupling; stoichiometric freedom; robustness versus
homeostasis.

## Bearing on the record

- **Connections: how far it stands in for LIT-536.** It restates chapter 1's
  constraint definition with its time scale and adds what organisms have
  beyond closure. It does not apply this to colonies or ecosystems, so it
  is no stand-in for chapter 6, and it has nothing on chapter 3.
- **THEORY-072**: the authors keep differentiation between subsystems at
  distinct time scales as a condition beyond closure (requirement 2), while
  the only time scale inside closure is the one that makes something a
  constraint relative to a process (result 2).
- **CLAIM-395**: consistent; a single-constraint loop is feedback, not
  closure (result 4).
- **NOTE-148** (Baedke): Baedke's added criterion, that organisms can leave
  one closure regime for another and control the passage, is close to this
  paper's regulation, which shifts the constitutive regime between viable
  states; whether colonies or ecosystems meet the requirements is not
  argued.
- **NOTE-093** (Bruckner): the five requirements are a check to run on its
  self-maintaining car and its bacteria.
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- "Dynamically decoupled" is defined by distinct rates and stoichiometric
  freedom, with no measure of how distinct is enough.
- Whether colonies or ecosystems meet the requirements is left open, so the
  paper does not itself answer Baedke.
- §§4 and 6 were read at their conclusions only.
