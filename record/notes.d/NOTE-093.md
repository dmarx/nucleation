# NOTE-093: Bruckner — Welfare Subjects and Autopoiesis

## Contribution

A welfare subject has a good of its own: things can go well or badly for it. Everyone agrees that adult humans are welfare subjects and rocks are not, but what grounds the difference is disputed. The default view makes sentience necessary. A rival view makes teleological organization sufficient. Bruckner defends an intermediate view: every autopoietic entity, one that sustains, maintains and renews itself adaptively to its environment, is a welfare subject. The view explains why sentient animals qualify and why merely teleologically organized artefacts such as car engines do not.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1–2: welfare subjecthood requires that things *matter to* the entity. "Good for" talk about an engine's oil changes is elliptical for serving a human-designed function (following Bradley and Rosati).
- §4 Autopoiesis: the sufficient condition is defined, and the argument is abductive. Autopoiesis accounts for the data on which entities are and are not welfare subjects better than sentience or teleology does. Humans' self-maintaining responses to what "shows up" as mattering are the model (borrowing from Maher 2017).
- §5 Plants: thigmomorphism in bean plants (thicker stems in wind) and phototropism are cases of plants creating themselves in response to their conditions, which reveals interests without any mental machinery. No commitment to plant agency is needed if "behaviour" is taken broadly.
- §6: an engine governor, a thermostat and a heat-seeking missile are "mere input–output machines" and do not qualify (6.1). It is conjectured that all sentient beings are autopoietic, which would explain why sentience is sufficient (6.2). Sentience is not necessary (6.3). A hypothetical self-maintaining car ("Tessler") would be a welfare subject (6.4).
- §7 Objection, absurd implications (following Rosati: welfare facts give reasons): Part One denies that welfare value automatically generates agent-neutral reasons, by analogy with aesthetic value. Part Two grants the reasons but argues that those concerning plants and machines are too weak to be absurd, in line with Bradford 2023 on robot welfare.
- §8–9: a further argument that autopoiesis, not sentience, grounds welfare, from entities that were, will be, or would have been sentient. The paper closes with an open research agenda: whether theories of well-being generalize to insentient autopoietic beings, and what that means for invariabilism.

## Open questions

- It bears directly on AI moral-patienthood debates. It offers a route to welfare subjecthood for machines that does not go through consciousness at all, in contrast to [LIT-111](../literature.d/LIT-111.md) (Birch) and [LIT-135](../literature.d/LIT-135.md) (Seth), which tie it to consciousness or life.
- Check how sharp the line between "autopoiesis" and sophisticated teleological self-regulation really is. The Tessler case suggests that the criterion is graded and could be engineered in.
- The term's biological origin (Maturana and Varela) matters: does Bruckner's looser, behaviour-based use keep what makes autopoiesis a principled boundary?
