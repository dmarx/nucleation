# THEORY-tmp6axop: Narveson argues that creating a person neither benefits nor harms them, that the existence of people with good lives is normally good and a reason to create them though no one is owed it, and that only harm to actual persons can justify coercion over procreation

## The account

"There isn't any 'one' upon which to confer 'life'": before birth the only
candidate is "a bit of germ plasm" (pp. 49–50). On a comparative account of
harm, creating a person neither benefits nor harms them.

Yet good lives are good. Asked whether a child's being happy is a good
reason to have it: "Contrary, perhaps, to what I have been (perhaps
mis)understood to say in those earlier papers, the correct answer is: of
course!" (p. 50). They matter because to parents or "simply benevolent
persons" they "'just do' matter", and because more good people benefit
others (p. 51).

Morality has an enforceable department and a reinforceable one (pp. 54–55).
Others may interfere only if having or not having children would work ill
for "actual persons, alive, breathing, and acting now (or at any rate in
their lifetimes.)" (p. 55), not for "fancied future possible persons".
Possible people are owed nothing (pp. 59–60).

## Evidence

Argument from a comparative account of harm, which is assumed, not defended
(p. 53). The scope of "actual persons" is left unclear: note 2 (p. 53)
allows that "some of those persons are in the future" (NOTE-1318,
Limitations).

## What it bears on

QUESTION-299 names the person-affecting ground through Narveson (LIT-1769);
its note of 2026-10-29 reads this paper in its place: "Happy future lives
are good and a reason to create them, but no one is owed them. Why they
matter is said only through the living's likings and interests, so this
ground collapses into what the living value." The reading bears on how the
question's second and third grounds meet.
