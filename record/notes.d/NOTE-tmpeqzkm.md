# NOTE-tmpeqzkm: Van Lange and Bergmann — Human cooperation is strong among individuals but fragile between groups

## Contribution

A synthesis with a staged model (Fig. 2), and no new data. It puts two
contrasts side by side. One is dyad against larger group (Table 1). The
other is cooperation within groups against cooperation between them. It
separates mistrust, distrust, hostility, aggression and hate as stages, and
keeps in-group favouritism apart from costly competition.

## Key results

1. **The thesis.** "cooperation is often strong in dyads but more fragile
   between groups" (p. 547).
2. **Dyads beat groups, with counter-evidence acknowledged.** "dyads are
   more cooperative than groups in single-trial social dilemmas" (p. 550).
   The reasons are "reciprocity, empathy and perspective-taking, and
   psychological safety" (p. 550). Exclusion costs both partners: "one
   cannot exclude the other person in a dyad without bearing the similar
   cost of cut ties" (p. 551). Large groups "rely on institutions to promote
   and maintain cooperation" (p. 551).
3. **Mistrust from categorization alone.** It "can arise just from
   categorization and unfamiliarity with outgroup members" (p. 552).
4. **Bounded reciprocity.** "Ingroup favouritism primarily stems from the
   heuristic expectation that cooperation is reciprocated and rewarded
   exclusively by ingroup members" (p. 553).
5. **The interindividual–intergroup discontinuity effect.** Individuals
   nested in groups are less cooperative towards outsiders than they would
   be by themselves. The in-group supports their non-cooperation and spreads
   responsibility for it, and reputational checks are weak (p. 553). One
   mechanism among about seven, not the centrepiece.
6. **Favouritism is not harm.** "such ingroup favouritism need not imply
   that people show any harmful behaviour towards outgroups" (p. 553).
7. **Conflicting interests drive escalation, as perceived.** Costly
   competition "becomes more common in situations where the interests of
   both groups conflict" (p. 554). There, "ingroup cooperation can require
   disadvantaging outgroup members" (p. 554). What counts is "people's
   perceptions of their interdependence" (p. 554).
8. **In-group cooperation fuels intergroup conflict.** "the same processes
   that can establish ingroup cooperation can also fuel intergroup conflict"
   (p. 554). Peers punish members who will not invest in the conflict. Peer
   punishment "fails to inhibit self-serving costly competition towards the
   outgroup by a small subset of the ingroup" (p. 554), and retaliation
   then targets the whole group.
9. **Institutions as a precondition for peace.** The review reports, from
   ref. 206, that "a prerequisite for the emergence of peaceful intergroup
   relations is the establishment of more centralized institutions at the
   level of the group that could constrain such behaviours by individual
   group members" (p. 554).
10. **Not inevitable.** Groups "compete, cooperate or coexist
    independently" depending on the situation, expectations, and "the
    ability of groups to prevent proactive aggression against other groups"
    (p. 555).
11. **Group-based morality.** It "is primarily defined in terms of the
    quality of outcomes for one's own group, with little or no regard for
    the quality of outcomes for outgroups" (p. 555).
12. **Remedy, and how it could end.** Cooperative cross-group dyads, with
    reputations spreading, "as long as individuals are not punished for
    friendly relations with outgroup members by their peers" (p. 555).
    Frequent cooperation "might even lead the groups to merge into larger
    collectives that transcend former group boundaries" (p. 555).

## Assumptions

- Cooperation in the economic-game sense: "providing good outcomes for
  others at some cost to oneself" (p. 547). Laboratory games and minimal
  groups are taken to bear on real intergroup relations.
- "Among individuals" is mostly dyads, so the model joins two comparisons:
  group size and the group boundary.
- In-group favouritism is close to a default, and expected reciprocity
  matters more than identity or esteem (p. 553).

## Concepts

- **schema-based mistrust** against **evidence-based distrust** (p. 552);
- **bounded reciprocity** (p. 553);
- **interindividual–intergroup discontinuity** (p. 553);
- **negative interdependence** (p. 554);
- **psychological safety**, in dyads: no exclusion, no subgroups (p. 551);
- **group-based** against **individual-based morality** (p. 555).

## Bearing on the record

Source of [THEORY-tmpmr4g4](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpmr4g4.md).

- **The synthesis's §7** is right in substance, with one refinement. Groups
  cooperate less with each other even when their interests do not conflict:
  categorization and bounded reciprocity are enough. Conflicting interests,
  and above all perceived ones, are what turn lower cooperation into costly
  competition and hostility (pp. 553–554).
- **[CLAIM-tmpjnqe9](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjnqe9.md).** Supports one link: "the same processes that can
  establish ingroup cooperation can also fuel intergroup conflict" (p.
  554). The review is about cooperation and conflict between groups, not
  their persistence. It says nothing on extinction, Nowak's rule or
  impartial concern, beyond the contrast of group-based and
  individual-based morality. That contrast backs the claim's remark that
  [THEORY-468](../theory.d/THEORY-468.md)'s group-loyalty domain can work against the coexistence of
  groups. The review's own success case, groups merging into larger
  collectives, is a second route to fewer groups: by merger, not
  extinction. The claim's note of 2026-11-04 records this.
- **[QUESTION-tmp22a7y](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmp22a7y.md).** No answer. There is no norm that bears harder on
  a dominant group. The nearest it comes is institutions within a group
  that restrain its own aggressive minority (p. 554, ref. 206), and
  intergroup tolerance as risk buffering (refs. 227–229). Power asymmetry
  appears only as one group "more deprived of resources or status" (p.
  554), a source of negative interdependence.
- **[THEORY-1114](../theory.d/THEORY-1114.md) (self-categorization).** A loose fit. The review uses
  categorization and fit, but not metacontrast or a varied frame of
  reference, so it does not bear on that theory's promote_when. Its account
  of favouritism rests on reciprocity rather than identity.
- **[THEORY-1112](../theory.d/THEORY-1112.md) (entitativity).** Indirect. Minimal-group favouritism from
  a shared preference is compatible with a mental-state cue, but the review
  does not separate similarity from shared intention.
- **[CLAIM-493](../claims.d/CLAIM-493.md).** A different route by which a minority sets a group's
  outward stance: unchecked aggression by a few, conformity, and
  retaliation against the whole group (p. 554). That is not
  prototypicality, so it is a rival or complement, not evidence.

## Limitations

- A Perspective. The literature is the authors' selection, and no effect
  sizes are pooled. The stages of Fig. 2 are proposed and not tested
  together.
- The dyad advantage has counter-evidence, which the authors cite (p. 550).
  Extortion and exploitation are also easier in dyads (p. 551).
- The evidence is mostly laboratory games. The applications to war and
  politics come from cited work.
- The cross-group-dyad remedy is untested, and the authors name its
  limit: the partner may be taken as "an exception to the rule" (p. 555).
  [CASE-146](../cases.d/CASE-146.md) is that case.
- The copy read may be a late proof.
