# NOTE-1677: Kadowaki et al. — Merging community assembly into the regime-shift approach for restoration

## Contribution

The paper separates two traditions in the study of alternative stable
states and argues that restoration needs both: regime-shift (RS) work,
which tracks an aggregate state and positive feedbacks through the abiotic
environment under press and pulse perturbations at large scales, and
community-assembly (CA) work, which tracks species composition, competition
and priority effects after pulse perturbations in small systems. What it
adds for restoration is the claim that the intervention must act on the
degraded state's own feedback, by removing or reintroducing species, as
well as on the driver.

## Key insight

Removing the pressure fails when the degraded phase has built a feedback of
its own: hardwoods that keep fire from spreading, sludge that favours a
floating competitor, eroded sites that no longer hold willows. The legacy,
not the pressure, then holds the state, and which species is put back
first can decide whether recovery happens.

## Assumptions

- **The cases are read through the alternative-states lens without a test
  of bistability.** "irreversible" in the Yellowstone case is the cited
  study's word (Wolf et al. 2007); the wetland case is from Martin &
  Kirkman (2009); the Japanese lake mechanism rests partly on "Jun
  Nishihiro, personal communication" (p. 994).
- **The model** (§3, p. 994; details in Appendix S1, not read): a
  two-species Lotka–Volterra competition model with immigration, extra
  mortality from environmental deterioration, and intra- and interspecific
  facilitation that mitigates it. The facilitation is assumed, not
  estimated. "The model itself is not novel".

## Key results

- **The problem (§1, p. 991).** A transition "cannot be easily reversed,
  particularly when human activities have decreased the ecosystem’s
  capacity to return to the original state on its own."
- **Wetlands (§1, p. 992).** Herbaceous communities in longleaf pine
  depression wetlands "are maintained with prescribed fires"; after
  extended suppression, "re-introduction of fire alone does not restore
  previous herbaceous communities because hardwood species impede the
  spread of fire, which further facilitates hardwood dominance".
  Restoration reinstates prescribed fire "as well as mechanical and
  chemical hardwood species removal".
- **Tallgrass prairie (§1, p. 992).** Former agricultural land may be
  restored to high plant diversity by removing clonal species (Baer et al.
  2015), a CA intervention on a biotic feedback.
- **Perturbations (§2.2, p. 992).** "rangeland desertification may result
  from a mixture of intensified stock grazing (press perturbation) and
  drought (pulse perturbation)".
- **Shallow lakes (§2.3, p. 993).** Rooted macrophytes suppress "the
  release of phosphorus into the water column"; phytoplankton causes "the
  release of phosphorus from bottom sediments into the water, further
  facilitating the growth of phytoplankton".
- **The model (§3, p. 994).** With strong facilitation "the system
  exhibits a transition from a high to a low total abundance states with
  hysteresis in response to an increase in mortality (Fig. 2c)"; total
  abundance "changes very slowly before the regime shift" while relative
  abundances change sharply; and "the reintroduction of the species with a
  lower tolerance to environmental stress or a lower intrinsic growth rate
  may fail to restore the community (Fig. 2e)", where the other order
  succeeds (Fig. 2f).
- **Japanese lakes (§4.1, p. 994).** After sewer systems improved water
  quality, "the submerged macrophytes have been outcompeted by water
  chestnut (genus Trapa)"; sludge from the eutrophic phase "constrains the
  germination and seedling establishment of submerged macrophytes". In
  parts of Lake Imbanuma "the removal of organic sludge on bottom
  sediments succeeded in reducing the floating-leaved plants and
  recovering the submerged plants", though it is "a practical challenge"
  at whole-lake scale. No numbers.
- **Yellowstone (§4.2, p. 995).** "Although wolves were experimentally
  reintroduced in 1995, the willows did not initially recover", because
  beaver decline had removed dam sites through erosion "and such
  hydrological shift is irreversible (Wolf et al., 2007)". The authors
  call for wolf reintroduction together with restoring "the hydrological
  regime".
- **Reintroduction as a community tool (§4.2, p. 995).** Assisted
  colonization "can sometimes also serve as an integrated management tool
  to restore a whole ecological community, although unwanted risks of
  reintroduction should always be managed."
- **Function versus composition (§4.3, p. 997).** Alternative community
  states need not be alternative functional states under redundancy:
  wood-decay fungal communities assembled in different orders did not
  differ significantly in decomposition rate (Dickie et al. 2012).

## Concepts

- **Regime-shift (RS) approach**: alternative states of an aggregated
  variable driven by environmental change, held by "abiotic feedbacks".
- **Community-assembly (CA) approach**: alternative compositions from
  different assembly histories under fixed environments, held by "biotic
  feedbacks" such as competition and priority effects.
- **Press and pulse perturbations**: a gradual change that lowers the
  ability to return, against a discrete shock.
- **Biomanipulation**: removal or (re)introduction of a species to steer
  recovery.

## Connections

It stands in for Suding, Gross & Houseman (2004), [LIT-2169](../literature.d/LIT-2169.md), for the
framing only. It cites Suding et al. for sudden transitions (p. 991), for
RS work on environmental change (pp. 991, 992), and for feedbacks as "key
ingredients of alternative stable states and ecological restoration"
(p. 993), and its wetland, lake and Yellowstone cases are the kind of
constraint Suding et al.'s abstract lists (dominance shifts, trophic
interactions, biogeochemistry). It does not restate Suding et al.'s cases,
experiments or models, so none of its results is Suding et al.'s. Its lake
mechanism is Scheffer's ([LIT-726](../literature.d/LIT-726.md), [LIT-720](../literature.d/LIT-720.md)). Schröder, Persson & De Roos
(2005), [LIT-2167](../literature.d/LIT-2167.md), read in [NOTE-1666](NOTE-1666.md), is the evidence standard it
does not meet. In the record it parallels [CLAIM-463](../claims.d/CLAIM-463.md) (Active): restoring
the conditions a capacity was lost under need not restore it where its
maintenance is self-reinforcing, and rebuilding the activity directly can.

## Limitations

A perspective: a toy model and three narrative cases from other studies.
No sample sizes or effect sizes, no test of bistability in any case, and
no case followed after the intervention stopped. The lake case's
mechanism rests in part on a personal communication.

## Open questions

Whether any of the named cases (Martin & Kirkman 2009; Wolf et al. 2007;
the Imbanuma sludge removal) shows, when read, conditions restored without
recovery followed by recovery after the stated biotic manipulation, and
whether that recovery then persists once the manipulation stops.
