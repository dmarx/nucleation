## Reading of 2026-11-16

Read in full from the open-access version of record. The concept is defined in the abstract and
the introduction: the human "may become simply a component—accidentally or intentionally—that
bears the brunt of the moral and legal responsibilities when the overall system malfunctions"
(p. 40). Two cases are argued (Three Mile Island, Air France 447), one counter-case
(Therac-25), and a forward look at self-driving cars (Uber Tempe, Tesla, Google). The paper
limits itself: the concept "is not a way to explain why these gaps occur" and "moral crumple
zones are not a property of every complex and automated system" (p. 50). It concerns "cultural
perceptions of blame and responsibility", not legal liability (p. 52). The exchange reports it
accurately (A15 §5, A17 §§1, 4), adding "professional" to Elish's "moral and legal". A17's
supervisor and nurse illustrations are A's own. Elish says nothing about credit for failures
that human compensation prevented. The Uber discussion predates the NTSB final report
(LIT-tmp3lco2).
