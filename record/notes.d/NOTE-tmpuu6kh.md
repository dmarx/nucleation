# NOTE-tmpuu6kh: Phase Shifts, Herbivory, and the Resilience of Coral Reefs to Climate Change

## Contribution

The first replicated herbivore-exclusion experiment to test
herbivore–algae–coral interactions in the aftermath of mass bleaching, by
the authors' account. Before it, the role of herbivory in reef phase shifts
was argued from Caribbean history and correlation (Hughes 1994; Mumby et
al. 2006, a reserve comparison that did not report corals). It shows by
manipulation that removing large fishes from a recovering, post-bleaching
reef crest produces a macroalgal shift and suppresses coral recovery, and
it decomposes the suppression into recruitment and mortality.

## Key insight

After a mass mortality, a reef does not recover by its survivors regrowing.
It recovers mostly by new colonies settling, and settlement and early
survival need grazed, algae-free surfaces. Large herbivorous fishes keep
those surfaces open. Take them away and the same disturbance that corals
would otherwise recover from becomes the start of an algal reef.

## Assumptions

- **Site.** Pioneer Bay, leeward Orpheus Island, inner Great Barrier Reef
  (18°36′S, 146°29′E). Turbid water (5–8 m visibility), tidal range
  3–3.5 m, fishing banned since 1987. Coral cover and diversity had been
  sharply reduced by the 1998 bleaching. Alcyonaceans and branching
  Acropora were "virtually eliminated" from shallow sites.
- **Treatments.** Four fully meshed roofless cages excluding "all large
  and medium fishes", four partial cages open along 50% of the perimeter
  as cage controls, and four open plots. Each plot was 25 m², 300 m² in
  all. Cages were 4 m tall, so no roof was needed. Mesh was 1 cm² in the
  bottom 2 m and 2 cm² above, scrubbed every 7–10 days. Diadema urchins
  were rare, and the three found in cages were removed.
- **Caging artefacts.** The authors argue against them because the cages
  were roofless and large, algae grew luxuriantly inside (so light was not
  limiting), and the partial cages showed no intermediate reduction in
  recruits.
- **Timeline.** Corals mapped in September 2000 and again in April 2003;
  cover from 16 photographic censuses; mesh removed after 30 months.

## Key results

- **Fish exclusion worked (Figure S1, as reported in text).** Herbivore
  biomass was 0.45 ± 0.08 kg/m² per hour of video in cages, 4.29 ± 2.81 in
  partial cages and 3.12 ± 1.24 in open plots (F = 7.79, p < 0.001). This
  was measured after 28 months with 90 hours of remote video at 1 m²
  quadrats.
- **Macroalgae (Figure 2A).** Mean cover was 4.1% in partial cages and
  1.7% in open plots (maxima 10% and 7%). In cages cover reached up to 91%
  and was 56% ± 21% after 30 months (repeated-measures ANOVA, F = 3.82,
  p < 0.05). Final biomass was 1363 ± 234, 146 ± 49 and 68 ± 28 g wet
  weight per m² (F = 20.8, p < 0.001). Sargassum grew 3 m tall, with more
  than 1000 holdfasts per 25 m² and up to 8.55 kg/m². Coralline algae
  composition also diverged (Figure S3).
- **Coral cover (Figure 2B).** Cages went from 6.0% ± 0.8% to 7.7% ±
  1.0%. Partial cages reached 19.2% ± 2.3% and open plots 20.2% ± 2.2%
  (RM-ANOVA, F = 3.82, p < 0.05). Relative increases were 28%, 68% and
  83%.
- **Recruitment (Figures 3A, 4).** There were 1062 recruits from 26 genera.
  Per 25 m² that was 39 ± 11 in cages, 108 ± 26 in partial cages and 118 ±
  21 in open plots (F = 150.9, p < 0.001). Acropora gave 246 recruits
  (23%), about three times more outside the cages (F = 7.7, p = 0.011).
  Porites had 2 recruits in cages against 45 elsewhere (F = 12.49,
  p = 0.003). Fungia and Euphyllia, normally deep or shaded, were 18% of
  cage recruits against 3% elsewhere. The first two principal components
  explain 63% of the variation among the 12 replicates' recruit
  assemblages. Partial cages and open plots were indistinguishable.
- **Mortality (Figure 3B).** Of the colonies present at the start, 24.2%
  died in cages, 9.8% in partial cages and 11.3% in open plots (RM-ANOVA,
  F = 4.29, p < 0.05). The net number of colonies changed by −72 ± 32
  (−26%), +43 ± 21 (+16%) and +39 ± 24 (+14%) per 25 m².
- **Reversal.** After the mesh came off, macroalgal cover fell from 53% to
  13% in 12 days and to about zero by 30 days. Juvenile fishes in the
  former cages fell 98% in 3 days.
- **Sublethal effects.** Coral tissue thickness (64 Porites cylindrica
  colonies) and reproductive output (90 Montipora digitata fragments: egg
  size, eggs per polyp, reproductive polyps) were measured. The results are
  only in the supplement and were not read here. The summary's "suppressed
  the fecundity" rests on them.

## Concepts

- **resilience** — "the ability of reefs to absorb recurrent disturbances
  … and rebuild coral-dominated systems".
- **phase shift (regime shift)** — a change "to an alternate assemblage
  that is typically characterized by hyperabundances of fleshy seaweeds or
  other opportunistic species". The paper does not require that the
  alternate assemblage be self-stabilising.
- **trophic cascade / top-down control** — changes in food-web structure
  and composition from reducing medium and large fishes.
- **partial cage** — a cage control open on half its perimeter.

## Connections

- **Kaniewska et al. ([LIT-tmpm09g4](../literature.d/LIT-tmpm09g4.md)).** Same reef system, a shared author
  (Hoegh-Guldberg), and the other half of the recovery story: recruitment
  needs larvae, which come from synchronised spawning. Neither paper cites
  the other.
- **Boilard et al. ([LIT-tmpx0l32](../literature.d/LIT-tmpx0l32.md)).** Their model ends traumatic dysbiosis
  in algal "phase shifts". This experiment shows a phase shift following
  bleaching through the loss of herbivory, with no microbial step.
- **Borsboom ([LIT-547](../literature.d/LIT-547.md)) and [THEORY-058](../theory.d/THEORY-058.md).** The record's alternative-stable-
  state account of disorder rests on hysteresis. The rapid reversal here
  shows a shifted state that was not self-maintaining at plot scale. It is
  a contrast in the meaning of "shifted state", not a test of that theory.
- **Wilson & Barker ([LIT-168](../literature.d/LIT-168.md)).** The reef is their example of a group that
  is a living agent but not a Darwinian individual. This paper is evidence
  about what keeps such a group in its state, not about whether it is one
  individual.

- It is the record's first experimental ecology. The vocabulary has no

## Limitations

- **One site, one reef zone, one post-disturbance window, four replicates
  per treatment.**
- **Plot scale.** 25 m² plots draw larvae and grazers from the surrounding
  reef. A real reef-wide loss of herbivores would also change larval
  supply, which the experiment holds fixed.
- **Fish exclusion is not herbivore exclusion.**
- **The supplement was not read**, so the fecundity and tissue claims are
  taken from the summary's wording only.
- **Two small errors in figure references.** The text cites Figure 3B for
  recruitment where Figure 3A shows it, and Figure 1C for the cages, which
  it does not show.

## Open questions

- Does the effect hold at reef scale, where larval supply and herbivore
  populations are themselves affected?
- At what herbivore density does the shift occur, and is there hysteresis
  in the threshold? Plot-scale reversal in 30 days suggests none here.
- How much of the cage effect is herbivory and how much is the loss of
  predators of small fishes?
