# NOTE-tmpx9fbi: Holton — Identification (24.120 lecture XIX)

## Contribution

A teacher's two-page commentary on Frankfurt's "Identification and
Wholeheartedness", with page references, followed by brief notes on "The
Faintest Passion" and on Scanlon. It separates two phenomena the essay
runs together under decisive identification, and raises two objections:
the decision is left unanalysed, and it cannot by itself secure
wholeheartedness. For this record it is the nearest available report of
what the essay says about the stopping point of the regress.

## Key insight

In the 1987/88 essay, as Holton reads it, the stopping point is a
decision, not a further desire, and Frankfurt says nothing about what a
decision is. That moves him off the Hobbesian model of the will and
towards Bratman's, and leaves open whether a decision of the required
kind can be specified at all.

## Assumptions

- **Holton's reading of Frankfurt is the only access.** The handout states
  and questions; it quotes Frankfurt four times.
- **Lecture-note form.** Several points are questions to the class ("Is
  this right?", "What is he talking about here?"), not claims, and are
  reported here as such.

## Key results

- **Consciousness (handout p. 1).** The essay starts by arguing that
  self-consciousness is essential to consciousness. Holton asks whether it
  is meant as necessary or sufficient.
- **Identification is acceptance (handout p. 1).** "Identification here
  should be understood as acceptance, not as essentially linked either to
  endorsement or what one cares about." One can identify with a desire
  without approving of it, or with one taken to be trivial; for this
  Holton cites the "Reply to Watson" in *Contours of Agency*.
- **From higher-order desire to decision (handout p. 1).** "In his earlier
  work Frankfurt had understood this in terms of higher order desires. But
  it was unclear why a higher order desire would be one with which one
  specially identified. Later he comes to understand identification in
  terms of decisions."
- **First case: checking a sum (handout p. 1; Frankfurt pp. 168–169).** One
  might arrive at "a decisive identification with a certain answer"
  (p. 169) that "resounds endlessly". Holton: the point is not a judgement
  that no conflict will be found but a decision that no further checking
  is warranted, which can stand while one thinks one may have reason to
  revise. Yet Frankfurt "does seem to think that the decision ... is one
  that we do think won't be rejected", quoting "he can anticipate that this
  view will be endlessly confirmed by accurate reviews of it" (p. 168), and
  "seems to think that this is necessary for identification. That is what
  the resonance effect amounts to." Holton asks whether one could identify
  without that confidence, and whether the question would "go all the way
  up".
- **Second case: conflicting desires (handout p. 1; Frankfurt p. 170).**
  Where desires conflict, "one makes a decision to embrace one desire rather
  than another", and where it is not just a matter of order, "the rejected
  desire now becomes an 'outlaw'" (p. 170). "Again, resonance doesn't seem
  to be required."
- **The decision is not analysed (handout p. 1).** "Note further that
  Frankfurt hasn't given us an analysis of what such a decision amounts to.
  In particular, he hasn't done it in terms of desires. So we have
  apparently left the Hobbesian model completely. The model looks more like
  Bratman's."
- **Decision does not guarantee wholeheartedness (handout pp. 1–2;
  Frankfurt p. 175).** Frankfurt treats decision as the way one eliminates
  inconsistency and becomes wholehearted, here a property of the person.
  But deciding A over B, B over C and C over A is possible, so further
  constraints on desires are needed. Outlawing desires one by one gives no
  guarantee that those left are consistent, and banishing each loser in
  turn makes the outcome depend on the order of the contests. Frankfurt
  allows "that the agent has identified himself inconsistently" (p. 175),
  but only in the sense that one may wrongly think oneself consistent;
  Holton's worry is prior. He also quotes p. 175: "Nor is it likely to be
  readily apparent whether a decision which the agent intends to be
  wholehearted is actually so."
- **Decision against choice (handout p. 2; Frankfurt p. 172).** Frankfurt
  distinguishes making a decision from making a choice; Holton asks what is
  meant and does not answer.
- **"The Faintest Passion" (handout p. 2).** Wholeheartedness is now a
  property of the person; ambivalence removes it, and "one cannot avoid
  ambivalence by an act of the will". Conflicts among first-order desires,
  "merely impulses", cannot be settled by decision on this model.
- **Scanlon (handout p. 2).** Is identification needed for responsibility
  or for "psychic health"? Negligence counts against the first. Scanlon's
  seemings, assessings and optings; Frankfurt's complaint that this is too
  intellectualized is raised as a question.

## Concepts

- **Identification** — as reported, acceptance of a desire, by decision;
  not approval, and not the same as caring.
- **Resonance** — a decisive identification "resounds endlessly": the
  agent anticipates that accurate reviews will confirm it (pp. 168–169, as
  reported).
- **Outlaw** — a desire rejected by such a decision, which the agent no
  longer counts as hers to act on (p. 170, as reported).
- **Wholeheartedness** — here a property of the person, the absence of
  ambivalence among what one has identified with; sometimes, as at p. 175,
  said of a decision.

## Connections

THEORY-120 states Frankfurt's 1971 hierarchical account from the paper
read first-hand (LIT-692); Holton reports that by 1987/88 Frankfurt had
replaced higher-order desires with decisions as the mark of
identification. THEORY-029 reports, via the IEP (LIT-297), that the essay
ends the regress in a decisive commitment made "without reservation";
Holton's resonance condition, the anticipation that review will confirm
the decision, is the nearest thing in the handout, and he says it is not
needed in the conflict case. His remark that the model "looks more like
Bratman's" points to Bratman's self-governing policies (LIT-596,

## Bearing on the record

- **THEORY-029, partly, at second hand.** Its promote_when asks for a
  first-hand reading of "Identification and Wholeheartedness" to decide
  whether Velleman's unwitting-decision counterexample reaches the 1987/88
  view, and if not, whether that view's conditions can be stated without
  presupposing the agent's participation. This is not first-hand and does
  not run the counterexample. It does report what the stopping point is: a
  decision that Frankfurt does not analyse, carrying in the checking case
  the agent's anticipation that reviews will confirm it. A condition of
  that kind is a condition on the agent's own stance, which is THEORY-029's
  second horn, and the absence of an analysis is consistent with its
  charge that non-alienability is secured by stipulation. Holton's point
  that resonance is not needed in the conflict case suggests the essay's
  conditions are not uniform across its cases, which the reading of the
  essay would have to settle. So it supports the account's dilemma on this
  essay without meeting the promote_when.
- **THEORY-120**: the handout reports that Frankfurt left the higher-order
  account behind for decisions by 1987/88, the move THEORY-029 already
  records.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Two pages of lecture notes.** Much is posed as a question; quotations
  from Frankfurt are few and short.
- **Second hand, edition inferred.** The page numbers are taken to be the
  1988 reprint's because they fall within its pages; the handout does not
  say so.
- **The unwitting-decision case is not discussed,** nor is Velleman.

## Open questions

- Does the essay itself state conditions on decisive identification
  ("without reservation", resonance) that an unwitting decision fails, and
  are they the same in the checking case and the conflict case?
- What is the distinction between making a decision and making a choice at
  p. 172, and does it bear on whether a decision can be unwitting?
