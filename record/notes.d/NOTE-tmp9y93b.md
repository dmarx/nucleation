# NOTE-tmp9y93b: Neumark and Simpson — Place-based policies, read from the NBER working paper

**What was read, and in whose voice.** The text read is the NBER working
paper of 2014, not the 2015 Handbook chapter, which was not read. And this
is a survey: **every empirical result below is Neumark and Simpson's
summary of another study**, none of which was read for this note. A number
here is the authors' report of someone else's estimate.

## Contribution

It sets the theoretical case for place-based policy (agglomeration,
knowledge spillovers, spatial mismatch, networks, multiple equilibria and
the big push) against the quasi-experimental evaluations, sorted by
instrument: enterprise and empowerment zones, discretionary grants to
firms, cluster and university policy, infrastructure (the Tennessee Valley
Authority, the Appalachian Regional Commission, EU Structural Funds) and
locally led community development. Its contribution is the verdict: the
literature mostly measures effects while incentives are in force, so it
cannot say whether any policy leaves a self-sustaining gain, which is the
one thing the strongest theoretical rationale needs.

## Key insight

The big-push argument justifies place-based policy only if a temporary
intervention moves an area to a new equilibrium that holds without it. In
the authors' words a much stronger case would exist if a policy helped "to
jump-start economic development in an area in a way that becomes
self-sustaining" (pp. 73–74, across a page break), and they quote Moretti
(2012) for the test: whether "the publicly financed seed can eventually
generate a privately supported cluster that is large enough to become
self-sustaining" (p. 74). Almost no evaluation runs that test; the one that
does, of the TVA, finds persistence only where a self-reinforcing process
(manufacturing agglomeration) was running, and even then the persistence is
local, with the national gain netting out.

## Assumptions

- **Relative effects.** Evaluations compare treated with control places
  (§4.6). Relocation into the treated area from the controls inflates the
  estimate; positive spillovers deflate it. Aggregate effects need theory.
- **Spatial equilibrium.** Who gains depends on labor mobility and housing
  supply: with perfect mobility and inelastic housing, "landowners benefit
  from a location-specific policy, rather than local residents, with the
  benefits being capitalized into rents" (p. 6).
- **Aggregate gains need heterogeneous agglomeration.** "if there is no
  variation in the elasticity across areas, then there will be no aggregate
  benefits from re-distributing activity geographically" (p. 8).
- **The TVA decomposition** assumes perfect labor mobility at decadal
  frequency, and the higher employment is permanent only if the
  productivity gain is, or "the elasticity of productivity with respect to
  employment density is non-linear" (p. 65, note 52).
- **Scope.** US and Europe only; developing-country zones are excluded.

## Key results

*The authors' summaries of other studies; working-paper pages.*

**Theory**

- **Big push** (§2.1, p. 7). The rationale "may be quite strong, especially
  in a dynamic setting with multiple equilibria", giving "a case for
  place-based policy to jump-start growth in a specific area."
- **Perfect mobility** (§2.6, p. 12). "the only effect is on land prices
  that capitalize the place-based subsidy."
- **Reversion** (§2.7, p. 14). "any local benefits themselves might not be
  long lasting ... whether areas revert to their previous steady state is an
  important question."
- **Theory against practice** (§5, p. 29). The big push is "far removed from
  the multi-faceted set of incentives that place-based policies provide in
  practice."

**Enterprise and empowerment zones**

- **UK** (§4.4, p. 24). "between 50 and 80 percent of enterprise zone
  businesses had relocated into the zones, prompting the British government
  to phase out the program".
- **California** (§5.1.1, p. 31; Table 2). "no evidence that enterprise
  zones affect employment". Zones that worked on claiming credits did worse,
  "likely because these activities focused more on claiming the tax credits
  retroactively than on creating jobs currently" (p. 32).
- **Texas** (Freedman; p. 34). With narrow control rings excluded, "Almost
  none of the estimates are significant ... consistent with the positive
  findings being driven more by relocation"; "a statistically significant
  11 percent increase in median housing values" and "no change in median
  household income".
- **US Empowerment Zones** (Busso et al.; p. 38). "The bulk of the estimated
  welfare gains come from positive effects on house prices in the zones".
  Reynolds and Rohlin: "the value of the wage credit only lasts as long as
  the wage credit is available, whereas other sources of gains may be more
  permanent, such as agglomeration effects from firms moving into the area"
  (p. 38).
- **Hanson; Hanson and Rohlin** (p. 39). "the IV estimates indicate no effect
  on employment"; negative spillovers about equal to the in-zone gains, so
  "Empowerment Zones are, to a first-order approximation, simply creating
  relocation of economic activity."
- **Who gained** (Reynolds and Rohlin 2013; p. 40). The zones were "neutral or
  even harmful to the impoverished residents", gains going to higher-income
  people who likely moved in.
- **France** (Givord et al.; p. 44). In a 300 m ring the results "mirror
  quite closely – albeit with opposite sign – the effects on activity inside
  the ZFUs."
- **Summary** (§5.1.4, p. 46). "it is very hard to make the case that the
  research establishes the effectiveness of enterprise zones in terms of job
  creation or welfare gains".

**Networks**

- **Jobs-Plus** (§5.2, pp. 46–47). The design assumed "saturation can lead to
  tipping points, creating a critical mass of employed residents". Quoting
  Bloom et al.: "most did not take root. What did take root – and grow – was
  the idea of using a small group of residents as extension agents". Gains
  cannot be attributed to the network component.

**Discretionary grants**

- **UK Regional Selective Assistance** (Criscuolo et al.; p. 50). Large
  employment gains, "no evidence that the policy led to increased TFP", and
  "little evidence of displacement".
- **Italy, Law 488** (Bronzini and de Blasio; p. 52). "recipient firms show a
  decrease in investment relative to the control group at five years, i.e.,
  two to three years after the final subsidy payment": investment "brought
  forward", with lower debt at five years read as "substituting public
  funds for private borrowing".
- **Why grants work, and the gap** (§5.3.1, pp. 54–55). Payment is
  "contingent on the job and/or investment targets being met"; but "it would
  be instructive to have further evidence on how long the additional jobs
  created actually survived once the monitoring period expired".
- **Large-plant subsidies** (Greenstone et al.; p. 53). Incumbent productivity
  rose, but "for some counties the estimated spillover effects are negative."

**Clusters and universities**

- **Universities** (§5.4.3, p. 62). Investment in higher education "could
  generate long-term, local effects".

**Infrastructure**

- **TVA set-up** (Kline and Moretti; §5.5, pp. 63–64). Transfers peaked at
  "around 10 percent of average household income" (p. 63); after 1960
  "federal transfers to the program were negligible" (p. 64).
- **TVA by period** (p. 64). Faster manufacturing growth in both periods,
  "around 10-12 percent per decade during 1940-1960, as opposed to 3-3.5
  percent from 1960-2000"; farm employment grew faster to 1960 and much
  more slowly after.
- **TVA mechanism** (p. 64). Agglomeration "continued to make the TVA
  counties an attractive location for new manufacturing activity even in the
  face of depreciation of the initial infrastructure investments following
  the withdrawal of federal funding".
- **TVA direct effect and aggregate** (p. 66). Significant in 1940–60,
  "negative but insignificant" after. The agglomeration elasticity is about
  the same across the density distribution (0.3–0.35, p. 65), so nationally
  the agglomeration effects are around zero, "the positive agglomeration
  benefits which accrued to the TVA region offset by negative effects
  elsewhere." The direct effect's benefits ($22–32.4bn) exceed the transfers
  ($17.3bn).
- **Appalachian Regional Commission** (Glaeser and Gottlieb; p. 66).
  Population rose in 1970–80, with "no statistically significant evidence of
  an effect over a longer period 1970 to 2000".
- **EU Structural Funds** (Becker et al. 2012; p. 69). "no evidence to
  suggest that a minimum level of transfers is necessary". Summary: "Questions
  remain about how long lasting these effects are, with the direct benefits
  of the TVA program appearing to erode over time" (p. 69).

**Community development**

- **Patti Territoriali** (§5.6, p. 72). No effect, possibly through
  "rent-seeking activities, which could even have been heightened by the
  bottom-up, locally-led approach".

**Conclusion**

- **No self-sustaining gains shown** (§6, p. 73). "even the most positive
  evidence on their effectiveness does not establish that they create
  self-sustaining economic gains. That is, at best the evidence (sometimes)
  says that when place-based incentives are in effect, there are increases
  in economic activity".

## Concepts

- **Place-based against people-based policy.**
- **Agglomeration economies** and the agglomeration elasticity; only its
  variation across places allows an aggregate gain.
- **Multiple equilibria and the big push**: a temporary intervention that
  moves an area to a new, self-sustaining equilibrium.
- **Displacement (relocation)**, measured with control rings.
- **Capitalization and incidence**: landowners against renters.
- **Composition effects**: gentrification, gains to arrivals not to
  original residents.
- **Additionality and "bringing forward"** of investment.
- **Conditional payment and claw-back.**

## Connections

- **Kline and Moretti (2014)**, filed in parallel in this batch, is the
  primary study behind every TVA result here.
- **THEORY-863** (Arthur's increasing returns and lock-in) is the formal
  shape of the multiple-equilibria argument the survey tests; the TVA's
  constant agglomeration elasticity is the measured counterpart, and it
  shows none of the non-linearity a lock-in to a new equilibrium needs for
  an aggregate gain.
- **THEORY-885** (Pierson: politics prone to increasing returns) bears on why
  programs persist and on the survey's concern with competition between
  jurisdictions.
- **CLAIM-445** (a protective regime becomes self-maintaining when it shifts
  the evidence used to judge it): the UK phase-out, ended on evidence of
  relocation, is a regime that did not become self-maintaining.
- **THEORY-919** (Juhász: the Napoleonic blockade's temporary protection
  left persistent spinning): the TVA is a parallel case of a temporary large
  push leaving persistent industry, with the caution Juhász's design cannot
  address, that the local persistence may be relocation from elsewhere.
- **THEORY-tmpukudp** (Kenya: impact at exit did not predict sustained
  benefit) and **THEORY-tmph35ra** (Ostrom et al.: predictable aid weakens
  the recipient's own upkeep) are the aid batch's nearest parallels to
  the survey's finding that gains are seen at most while incentives are in
  force.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpc3x0c** (safe withdrawal): supports, with a qualification. The
  TVA's durable part is the part a self-reinforcing process (agglomeration)
  carried after funding stopped; the farm gains, with no such process, and
  wage credits, whose value "only lasts as long as the wage credit is
  available", did not persist. The qualification: locally safe to withdraw is not a net
  gain, since nationally the agglomeration gain was relocation. The claim's
  turnover test matches the survey's demand for post-incentive outcomes,
  which almost no study supplies.
- **CLAIM-tmpw4ai2** (ignition threshold, collapse, hysteresis): mixed. The
  TVA's persistence fits a self-maintaining process, but Becker et al. find
  no minimum transfer, and the TVA's elasticity is constant across density,
  so the evidence here shows no threshold at the scales observed.
- **CLAIM-tmpoh07b** (supply that does the work crowds out capacity): partly
  supports. Law 488's investment was brought forward, with public funds
  substituting for private borrowing and investment below controls after the
  last payment; grants raised jobs but not productivity. There is no
  learning-by-doing evidence on capacity itself.
- **CLAIM-597** (supplied upkeep never becomes optional): supports
  indirectly. Effects appear while incentives are in force; wage credits,
  the ARC's effects and the TVA's direct effect all faded with the support.
  Only what agglomeration carried persisted.
- **CLAIM-tmpqa0qu** (one-way against reciprocal supply): none directly.
- **QUESTION-398** (independence from the founding intervention): directly
  relevant. The survey poses the same test and reports it almost never
  measured, the TVA being the lone positive case.
- No THEORY here is cited for something the survey does not say. No
  instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **Version.** The 2014 working paper, not the 2015 chapter.
- **Secondary throughout.** Every result is the authors' summary of other
  studies; the primaries (Kline and Moretti 2014; Busso et al.; Givord et al.;
  Bronzini and de Blasio; Becker et al. 2010, 2012) would have to be read to
  file their findings as theirs.
- **Short horizons.** Most evaluations cover a decade or less, during the
  incentive period; persistence rests on effectively one study, the TVA,
  and one null, the ARC.
- **Contested findings.** Busso et al. and Ham et al. against Hanson and
  Reynolds–Rohlin on Empowerment Zones; the authors call Ham et al.'s
  estimates "implausibly large" (p. 35). Six first-round Empowerment Zones,
  one TVA.
- **Coproduction barely studied**: Jobs-Plus and the New Deal for
  Communities, with no attribution to components.
- **Scope**: US and Europe only.

## Open questions

Whether any place-based support leaves a self-sustaining gain that is not
relocation: it would take post-incentive outcomes measured over a full
turnover, with aggregate effects, for more than one program.
