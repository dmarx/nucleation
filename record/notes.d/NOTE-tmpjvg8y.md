# NOTE-tmpjvg8y: Boyle — The Public Domain

## Contribution

It gives the public domain an affirmative account. Earlier critics of
intellectual property argued against monopoly. Boyle says they never said
what lay outside it: "The opposite of property, or perhaps we should say
the opposites of property, are much more obscure to us than property
itself." (p. 38). The book names a pattern across copyright, patent,
database and anticircumvention law, the "second enclosure movement"
(ch. 3). It gives the pattern's causes: the confusion of intellectual with
physical property, a fear of new copying technology, and a bias against
open systems. Its remedy is a movement modelled on environmentalism, which
had to "invent" the environment before it could protect it (Preface
p. xv; ch. 10). The Preface states three goals: to explain intellectual
property, to show that policy "is going the wrong way", and to explore
"property's outside" (pp. xiv–xv).

## Key insight

The limits on a right are part of the right. Boyle's figure is the
public road through a private field: fair use is not trespass excused by
need but a road "you never owned in the first place" (p. 66). "The holes
matter as much as the cheese." (p. 69). From this comes the book's main
corollary. Cheaper copying is usually taken to call for stronger rights.
The "Sony Axiom" says the reverse: "cheaper copying makes the limitations
on copyright more rather than less important" (p. 71), because without
them control over copies becomes control "over the very architectures of
our communications technology" (p. 71).

## Assumptions

- **A utilitarian baseline.** Rights are justified as incentives to create
  and distribute nonrival goods (ch. 1). Natural and moral rights are taken
  seriously (pp. 27–35, 227–229). Boyle's answer is that every such scheme
  still limits the right in time, and once it does, "how long" is an
  empirical question (p. 229).
- **The public goods model is qualified, not assumed.** Creation has
  non-market motives. Cheap copying also cheapens distribution.
  Lead time and tied goods can be enough (pp. 3–4). The Westlaw story
  shows that a legally unprotected good can be made excludable by
  passwords, contracts and tied services (pp. 213–216).
- **A presumption against monopoly.** Without evidence either way, the
  burden lies on whoever asks the state to stop copying (p. 207).
- **Definitions.** The public domain is material free of intellectual
  property rights. It includes, on the "granular" view Boyle adopts, the
  reserved spaces inside rights, such as fair use (pp. 38–39). A commons
  is a resource to which a group has access and use rights, perhaps on
  conditions. Boyle counts it a commons only "if the whole society has
  access" (p. 39).
- **Scope.** The law discussed is mostly US law to 2008, with UK and EU
  material in ch. 9. The book was written before streaming, and its
  technology chapters stop at the iPod, Grokster and the DMCA cases.

## Key results

The results are arguments and case studies. Only ch. 9 is a sustained
empirical test.

- **Why intellectual property (ch. 1).** Copyright, patent and trademark
  are presented as decentralized cultural, innovation and commercial
  policy (pp. 4–7). The costs are shown by term. About 85 percent of
  holders did not renew after 28 years (p. 9). Most twentieth-century
  culture is "copyrighted but unavailable", much of it orphan works
  (pp. 9–12). Boyle's "very conservative" alternative is a marked
  copyright of 28 years, renewable once (p. 14).
- **The Jefferson Warning (ch. 2).** From the 1813 letter to McPherson:
  intellectual property is not natural right; there is no entitlement to
  it; it should be short; it is a monopoly; and choosing to have it is only
  the first choice in a long series (pp. 21–22). Macaulay, Madison, Smith
  and Condorcet are shown to share it (pp. 22–32). The droits d'auteur
  tradition still limits the right, because "The moral rights view simply
  proved too much." (p. 33). Romantic authorship justifies fencing "the
  resources of the public domain" by imagining an author "creating
  something entirely new—not recombining the resources of the commons"
  (p. 33, quoting Boyle's 1996 book).
- **The second enclosure (ch. 3).** The commons of the mind differs from
  the grassy commons in two ways. It is nonrival, so overuse is not the
  problem (pp. 47–48). And outputs are inputs, so every extra right raises
  the cost of the next work (p. 48), up to Heller and Eisenberg's
  "tragedy of the anticommons" (p. 49). The older law was "braided": a
  thin layer of rights around a commons (p. 50). Its triggers were placed
  where commercial competitors act. Digital copying lets them be
  "activated by individual footsteps" (pp. 50–52).
- **The Internet Threat (ch. 4).** The story is that "The strength of
  intellectual property rights must vary inversely with the cost of
  copying" (p. 60), so that as copying costs approach zero rights must
  "approach perfect control" (p. 61). Boyle answers with "20/20 downside
  vision": the threats of new technologies are seen, their gains are not
  (p. 63). *Sony* is the counterexample, since video rental came to make
  up "more than 50 percent" of film revenue (p. 64). On fair use he
  concludes "The holes matter as much as the cheese." (p. 69). Then come
  the Sony Axiom (p. 71) and the *Napster* and *Grokster* rulings, which
  he reads as moving from *Sony* toward control of technology
  (pp. 71–82). His test is to ask whether a network like the Internet
  would be approved today. The answer is no: "The logic of control is not
  the logic of the Net." (p. 81).
- **The Farmers' Tale (ch. 5).** An allegory of barbed wire across public
  roads, set against the "Internet Threat" story (pp. 83–87). The DMCA
  forbids cutting digital fences even for fair use. Boyle argues this is
  unconstitutional, by a "red dot" thought experiment: a mark that lets
  owners switch off fair use (pp. 96, 105–109). *Reimerdes* treated DeCSS
  as an epidemic (pp. 100–101). *Skylink* read the act narrowly to protect
  competition (pp. 116–117). Boyle concedes that most feared harms "remain
  as just that: prophecies" (p. 119). He would not put the DMCA in the
  top three bad initiatives (p. 118).
- **Music (ch. 6).** Patent pictures creativity as converging, copyright
  as diverging. Music, more "collective and additive", fits copyright badly
  (pp. 122–124). The case: the received story derives "I Got a Woman"
  from a 1904 hymn. Boyle's research assistant found a 1951 78 rpm record
  by the Bailey Gospel Singers, "I've Got a Savior", with almost the same
  melody (pp. 127, 133–134). Charles likewise turned Clara Ward's "This
  Little Light of Mine" into "This Little Girl of Mine" (p. 134). The
  method is "a lot more like welding, or bricolage, than it is like
  designing out of nothing" (p. 135). "Gold Digger" loops the melody
  through Jamie Foxx and licensed it (pp. 138–140). The Legendary K.O.
  sampled that for "George Bush Doesn't Care About Black People", with
  two video versions (pp. 143–145). The law of sampling runs from *Grand
  Upright* ("Thou shalt not steal", pp. 146–148) to *Bridgeport*'s "Get a
  license or do not sample" (pp. 149–151). Parody that "takes aim" at the
  original is favoured over satire that uses it for another target
  (pp. 151–152). JibJab, having claimed fair use itself, then sent a
  take-down to The Black Lantern (p. 153). Proposals: a compulsory licence
  for derivative work in commerce and stronger fair use outside it
  (p. 158); 28-year renewal, an exemption for samples under five seconds,
  and fair use extended to satire and collage (pp. 158–159).
- **Science and technology (ch. 7).** Software ended up under both
  copyright and patent. Courts kept software copyright narrow and made
  decompilation fair use, and the GPL then used copyright to build a
  commons (pp. 164–168). Patent law went the other way: "by means of a
  computer" turned algorithms, and then business methods, into patentable
  subject matter (pp. 168–171). Synthetic biology faces patents on Boolean
  logic in cells, with no mature "prior art" to limit them (pp. 171–176).
  Boyle advises against using copyright to make a biobricks commons, and
  for putting the parts in the public domain (pp. 176–177).
- **A Creative Commons (ch. 8).** Creative Commons is a private "hack"
  that replaces "all rights reserved" with "some rights reserved"
  (p. 182). Through it "a commons has been made out of private and
  exclusive rights" (p. 184). Open source works socially. With Benkler,
  Boyle argues that the motives do not matter: given varied reserve
  prices, a global network and modular tasks, "it just does not matter why
  they do it. In lots of cases, they will do it." (p. 189). After Carol
  Rose, this is a "comedy of the commons" (p. 192). The worldview behind
  current policy he calls "maximalism", and he denies it is economic
  determinism or conspiracy (pp. 198–199). Legislative change, he says,
  has gone "only in one direction—outward" (p. 200). Privately built
  commons have limits. Orphan works cannot be fixed by contract, and a
  patent pool is "a privatized public domain, a park that only residents
  may enter" (p. 203).
- **An Evidence-Free Zone (ch. 9).** The natural experiment is the EU
  Database Directive (1996) against *Feist* (1991). The US industry grew
  more than twenty-five-fold since 1979 without the right (p. 210). After
  the directive, Europe had a one-time spike, then entry fell back. The
  US added about 900 databases while Britain added about 200 and Germany
  about 300, two thirds of which vanished (p. 211). Most cases concern
  databases that "would have been created anyway" (p. 211). The
  Commission's own review found the impact "unproven" and the EU/US ratio
  falling from 1:2 to 1:3 (pp. 218–219), and kept the directive. On
  weather data, Boyle reports Europe returning about sevenfold on its
  investment and the US thirty-nine-fold (p. 221). On sound recordings,
  the Cambridge study for the Gowers Review put the cost of extension at
  £240–480 million (p. 226). Gowers then recommended no retrospective
  change in the term or scope of rights (p. 227).
- **An Environmentalism for Information (ch. 10).** The bias he names: "Call
  it the openness aversion. Cultural agoraphobia." (p. 231). The test is
  three thought experiments set in 1991: the open network, free software
  and the volunteer encyclopedia (pp. 231–232). Environmentalism joined
  scattered interests through an idea (ecology) and an economics
  (externalities). A movement for the public domain could do the same, and
  its institutions are listed: EFF, Public Knowledge, Creative Commons,
  A2K (pp. 239–245). *Eldred* ends the book on the second enclosure
  "well under way" (pp. 247–248).

## Concepts

- **Public domain.** Material free of intellectual property rights,
  either never ownable or expired. On the granular view, it includes the
  reserved spaces inside rights, such as fair use (pp. 38–39).
- **Commons.** A resource over which a group has access and use rights,
  perhaps on conditions such as share-alike. Boyle requires the whole
  society to have access (p. 39).
- **Jefferson Warning.** Five cautions from the 1813 letter (pp. 21–22).
- **Second enclosure movement.** The extension of property rights over
  the intangible commons, likened to the English enclosures (ch. 3).
- **Internet Threat.** The story that rights must rise as copying costs
  fall (pp. 53, 60–62).
- **20/20 downside vision.** Seeing a technology's threats to rights
  holders clearly and its opportunities not at all (p. 63).
- **Sony Axiom.** Cheaper copying makes copyright's limits more important
  (p. 71).
- **Braided strategy.** A thin layer of rights threaded around a commons
  (p. 50).
- **One-way valve.** Copyright treats collective traditions as unowned
  raw material, then locks the individual works drawn from them (p. 129).
- **Failed sharing.** Losses from sharing that legal barriers prevent,
  "every bit as real" as losses from copying (pp. 182, 202).
- **Maximalism.** The worldview that intellectual property is like
  physical property and that more rights bring more innovation
  (pp. 198–199).
- **Openness aversion.** A systematic undervaluing of open systems and
  nonproprietary production (p. 231).
- **Cultural environmentalism.** A politics that makes the public domain
  visible as the environment movement made "the environment" visible
  (pp. 239–242).

## Connections

The intellectual debts are stated. Lessig and Benkler come first (p. viii).
Ostrom, Hess and Carol Rose are thanked for the governance of the commons
(p. ix). The history draws on Hesse for the droits d'auteur, and on
Litman, Patterson, Rose and Woodmansee. The music chapter relies on
McLeod and Vaidhyanathan, Burkholder and Carroll (pp. 277–279).
Chapter 8 is built on Benkler's "Coase's Penguin" and *The Wealth of
Networks* (pp. 286, 289). Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* ([LIT-737](../literature.d/LIT-737.md))
appears only in the notes, with Carol Rose, as nuance against Hardin's
tragedy (pp. 264, 266). The text never uses Ostrom's design principles.
Of Lessig's books it cites *The Future of Ideas* and *Free Culture* (pp.
278, 293), not *Remix* ([LIT-tmpreyek](../literature.d/LIT-tmpreyek.md)), which appeared the same year. It
does not cite Jenkins ([LIT-1121](../literature.d/LIT-1121.md)).

## Bearing on the record

- **[LIT-tmpreyek](../literature.d/LIT-tmpreyek.md) and [NOTE-tmptp3k0](NOTE-tmptp3k0.md) (Lessig, Remix).** The two books agree
  on the frame and differ in method. Three points of contact:
  1. *The copy trigger.* Lessig's argument that copyright now reaches every
     ordinary use because digital use always copies (*Remix*, pp. 98–103)
     appears in Boyle as triggers that were set for industrial competitors
     and can now be "activated by individual footsteps" (pp. 50–52). Boyle
     adds why the triggers sat where they did: the law was "a form of
     horizontal industry regulation of unfair competition", drafted by the
     affected industries (p. 51).
  2. *Why remix needs the original.* The Lessig NOTE asks whether
     recognizability of the source artifact could be a preservation target
     in its own right. Boyle gives a second, independent witness. Samplers
     do not re-create the riffs *Bridgeport* says they may re-create,
     because "The original beats have a totemic significance" (p. 150).
     Citing Benjamin, he says "cheap copying actually increases the demand
     for authenticity" (p. 150). Both authors assert this. Neither measures
     it.
  3. *A difference of diagnosis.* Lessig ends by blaming campaign money
     ([NOTE-tmptp3k0](NOTE-tmptp3k0.md), *Remix* p. 294). Boyle says the cause is a sincerely
     held worldview, not "economic determinism or industry conspiracy"
     (p. 199). Boyle's remedies for remix overlap with Lessig's (stronger
     fair use for amateur work, a licence for commerce, renewal after
     28 years). Boyle, unlike Lessig, rejects relying on technology to
     evade the law: "A system that can only function well through repeated
     lawbreaking is an unstable and dangerous one." (p. 157).
- **[CLAIM-002](../claims.d/CLAIM-002.md) and [QUESTION-002](../questions.d/QUESTION-002.md) (what survives a retelling).** The music
  chapter is a worked chain for [CLAIM-002](../claims.d/CLAIM-002.md). From "I've Got a Savior" to
  "I Got a Woman" to "Gold Digger" to The Legendary K.O., the melody and
  the phrase pattern carry through ("I've got a savior, Oh what a savior"
  becomes "I got a woman, way over town" becomes "There's a Gold Digger,
  way over town", p. 142). The message inverts at each step: a saviour, a
  generous woman, a mercenary one, then a president. Each stage is
  recognizably descended from an original it no longer resembles in
  function. The last step is identity of the artifact itself, since the
  K.O. looped the recording (p. 146). The chapter also shows the relations
  pulling apart. The received transmission story (a 1904 hymn) was wrong.
  Similarity of melody found the real source (pp. 127, 133). It would be a
  candidate CASE if the chain turns up again.
- **[CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md) (kind versus rendering).** Copyright's line between genre and
  song maps onto [CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md). Genre-defining sequences such as the twelve-bar
  blues or the flattened fifth are left free. Charles's borrowing "is not
  just copying the genre. It is copying the lines of the song within the
  genre." (p. 154). *Bridgeport*'s advice to re-create a riff instead of
  sampling it amounts to an independent utterance of the same kind, with
  no fidelity to the source. Boyle's report that samplers refuse it fits
  [CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md)'s point that kind-sharing is not transport.
- **[CLAIM-034](../claims.d/CLAIM-034.md) (fidelity among other goals).** *Acuff-Rose*, as Boyle reads
  it, makes the goal of a rendering decide its legality. Parody that
  "takes aim" at its original gets favoured treatment as fair use. Satire
  that uses the original to hit another target does not (pp. 151–152). The
  K.O. song fails the parody test for that reason. This is a legal system
  sorting renderings by the goals [CLAIM-034](../claims.d/CLAIM-034.md) lists.
- **[CASE-100](../cases.d/CASE-100.md) and [CLAIM-332](../claims.d/CLAIM-332.md) (distributed uptake).** Boyle's account of the
  K.O. song adds a reason the redub in [CASE-100](../cases.d/CASE-100.md) could reach a film only as
  a line: "Copyright acts as the barbed wire around mass media outlets."
  (p. 158). A mashup whose rights cannot be cleared stays online. For
  [CLAIM-332](../claims.d/CLAIM-332.md), the reserve-price argument (p. 189) explains production by the
  organized pattern (numbers, low overhead, modular tasks) and not by any
  member's motive. That is the kind of explanation [CLAIM-332](../claims.d/CLAIM-332.md) needs.
  But Boyle's examples are of producing a commons, not of changing an
  outside institution. His evidence is the famous successes (Linux, IBM,
  Wikipedia), which is the biased sample [QUESTION-119](../questions.d/QUESTION-119.md) warns of. So the
  book illustrates [CLAIM-332](../claims.d/CLAIM-332.md)'s form but does not test it.
- **[LIT-719](../literature.d/LIT-719.md) and [LIT-737](../literature.d/LIT-737.md) (Ostrom; [THEORY-149](../theory.d/THEORY-149.md)).** [LIT-719](../literature.d/LIT-719.md), the Nobel
  lecture, is Active and read. [LIT-737](../literature.d/LIT-737.md), *Governing the Commons*, is
  Unavailable. The connection is real but thin, and mostly a contrast.
  [THEORY-149](../theory.d/THEORY-149.md) concerns users governing a subtractable resource they depend
  on, with clear boundaries and graduated sanctions. Boyle's commons is
  nonrival, so the tragedy Ostrom's institutions prevent does not arise
  (pp. 47–48). It is open to the whole society (p. 39), which removes the
  first design principle. Two points of contact are worth noting:
  1. The free-software governance Boyle describes resembles self-organised
     rule-making: community judgment of contributions, governance assembled
     by the same distributed method, and forking as exit (pp. 186, 190).
     Boyle does not compare it to Ostrom. Whether [THEORY-149](../theory.d/THEORY-149.md)'s principles
     hold for nonrival commons is open.
  2. The arrangement in the book that most resembles a bounded Ostrom
     commons is the patent pool, which has members and excludes others. It
     is the one Boyle criticises, as "a park that only residents may
     enter" (p. 203).
  The book neither supports nor contradicts [THEORY-149](../theory.d/THEORY-149.md).
- **Does not bear on** [CLAIM-220](../claims.d/CLAIM-220.md) or [QUESTION-023](../questions.d/QUESTION-023.md). It has nothing on
  measuring what survives a rendering, or on insider vocabulary.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice, and not held by the

## Limitations

- **Argument from chosen cases.** Except ch. 9, the evidence is cases
  picked to show the pattern. The music chapter says so: "By itself, it
  proves nothing." (p. 138). The open-source chapter rests on its
  successes.
- **Strong negatives without a survey.** That legislation has moved "only
  in one direction—outward" (p. 200) rests on Boyle's knowledge. The
  openness aversion is called a cognitive bias, but its evidence is thought
  experiments and the same one-way trend (pp. 231–236).
- **Second-hand numbers.** The weather multipliers (p. 221) and the
  database counts (p. 211) are reported from other studies and not
  examined.
- **The music attribution is hedged.** That "Ward" on the 78 label is
  Clara Ward is a guess ("might be", p. 134), and the argument says "If I
  am right" (p. 135). The notes give the record's title as "I Got a
  Savior" (p. 280), the text "I've Got a Savior".
- **Commons theory is thin.** The commons is defined in a paragraph
  (p. 39). Its governance, monitoring and sanctions are not analysed, and
  Ostrom appears only in notes. The comparison with environmentalism is a
  strategic analogy, not a theory of the public domain. Boyle says the
  field lacks "a robust conception of the public domain" (p. 239).
- **Dated.** The law and technology stop in 2008. The sound-recording
  extension he reports as rejected was announced by the European
  Commission "As this book went to press" (p. 229).

## Open questions

- Would sampled music lose its value if the samples were re-created? An
  answer would test C5, and through it *Bridgeport*'s premise.
- Do Ostrom's design principles, built for subtractable resources with
  bounded users, transfer to nonrival commons such as free software or
  Creative Commons pools? Which ones fail? The book does not ask.
- Is the openness aversion a measurable bias? What experiment would show
  that people undervalue open systems relative to their outcomes?
- For the record: Lessig and Boyle now both assert that remix needs the
  recognized original. Should the question of recognizability as a
  preservation target be filed under [QUESTION-002](../questions.d/QUESTION-002.md), with this chain of
  borrowing as its case?
