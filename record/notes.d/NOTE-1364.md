# NOTE-1364: Fiss — The forms of justice

## Contribution

It gives structural reform its defence as adjudication. Where Chayes
(1976) described public law litigation as a new model and left its
legitimacy to be earned, Fiss denies that dispute resolution has any
normative priority, argues that courts have always given meaning to
public values, and locates the hard problem not in the form of the suit
but in the remedy, where the judge's independence is at stake.

## Key insight

A structural suit is "an in rem proceeding where the res is the state
bureaucracy". It addresses a social condition and the organizational
dynamic that produces it, not an incident or a wrongdoer, and its remedy
is a continuing, revisable reconstruction of the organization, "cycle
after cycle of supplemental relief". Because rights and remedies "jointly
constitute the meaning of the public value", the judge cannot hand the
remedy off; but because success depends on actors outside the court, the
remedy pulls the judge into bargaining, and the meaning of the right
bends toward what can be achieved.

## Assumptions

- **Public values exist and have true meanings** that can be "discovered"
  (p. 17); the resurgence of *Carolene Products* is put down to "the frail
  quality of our substantive vision", not doubts about courts.
- **The judge's competence comes from his office**, specifically from two
  features of its process: a dialogue he does not control (he must hear
  grievances, listen widely, respond and justify) and independence from
  the parties and from the body politic (pp. 12–14).
- **Legitimacy of a particular institution does not rest on consent**;
  consent goes to the political system as a whole (p. 38).
- **The setting is the bureaucratic state**: "a vertical one; the market
  has been replaced by the hierarchy, the individual entrepreneur by the
  bureau" (p. 44).

## Key results

- **Against footnote four** (pp. 5–17). The theory of legislative failure
  cannot say why failure is corrected by courts, and rests on the
  supremacy of preferences; courts are "a coordinate source of government
  power", not default institutions.
- **The transformation of form** (pp. 17–28). Five shifts from the
  dispute-resolution model: from incident to social condition; the
  plaintiff split into victim (a group), spokesman (often an institutional
  advocate) and beneficiary (who need not coincide with the victim); the
  defendant split into spokesman, wrongdoer and addressee, with the
  wrongdoer "virtually disappear[ing]"; an active judge who constructs a
  representational framework (notice, litigating amici, special masters);
  and a remedial phase with "no end — well, almost no end".
- **Dispute resolution has no priority** (pp. 28–44). The instrumental
  critique (Horowitz) ignores the value of success and the failures of
  agencies; the historical critique confuses form with function and
  rests on consent theory; Fuller's axiom of individual participation,
  taken as a necessary condition, would make almost all judicial
  norm-creation illegitimate and leaves the individual "at the mercy of
  large aggregations of power".
- **Against the tailoring principle** (pp. 46–50). "The remedy must fit
  the violation" falsely makes the remedy deducible from the violation.
  The structural remedy is instrumental: one of several ways to remove a
  threat, shaped by efficacy and fairness, always revisable. Its specifics
  (report dates, shower temperatures) are "instrumental or remedial
  rights", created by courts in discharge of a constitutional function.
- **The dilemma** (pp. 50–58). Judges have no special competence in
  means–end judgments; the remedy depends on "white flight", the Police
  Benevolent Association, appropriations; so "no judge is likely to decree
  more than he thinks he has the power to accomplish … He will tailor the
  right to fit the remedy." Special masters only relocate the dilemma.
  Fiss's answer is to "live with the dilemma".

## Concepts

- **Structural reform**: adjudication in which a judge, "confronting a
  state bureaucracy over values of constitutional dimension, undertakes to
  restructure the organization".
- **Dispute-resolution model**: triadic, individualistic, incident-focused,
  with a passive umpire.
- **Tailoring principle**: that the remedy must fit the violation.
- **Declaration and actualization**: the two phases, right and remedy, of
  giving a public value meaning.
- **Polycentric** (Fuller, after Polanyi): a problem whose resolution has
  "broad and never-ending repercussions".

## Connections

Fiss answers Chayes's "The Role of the Judge in Public Law Litigation"
(1976) and Fuller's "The Forms and Limits of Adjudication" (1978), and
Horowitz's *The Courts and Social Policy* (1977). Robert Cover, his
colleague, is thanked; Cover's "Violence and the Word" ([LIT-1601](../literature.d/LIT-1601.md),
[NOTE-1384](NOTE-1384.md)) takes the other side of the same remedial problem, that the
judge's word becomes deed only through others in roles. The line "he will
tailor the right to fit the remedy" is the thesis Levinson later names
remedial equilibration ([LIT-1592](../literature.d/LIT-1592.md), unread here). Sabel and Simon's
experimentalist account ([LIT-1587](../literature.d/LIT-1587.md), unread here) is the later development of
Fiss's revisable, cycle-by-cycle remedy.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-176](../cases.d/CASE-176.md) and [CASE-172](../cases.d/CASE-172.md), "Reversal ≠ Repair ≠ Structural reform".** The
  record says A8 used Fiss for this. The formula is A8's, not Fiss's, but
  the distinction is in the paper: the dispute-resolution remedy corrects
  or prevents "a discrete event", while the structural remedy removes "a
  condition", by reorganizing an ongoing institution over many cycles.
  Fiss does not discuss restoring an earlier state; his structural suit
  presumes the existing arrangement is what threatens the value, which is
  the record's point that restoration can be the wrong target.
- **[CLAIM-457](../claims.d/CLAIM-457.md).** Fiss's disappearance of the wrongdoer is a precedent for
  the claim's edge: in a structural suit "the costs and burdens of
  reformation are placed on the organization, not because it has 'done
  wrong' … but because reform is needed". Corrective duty is assigned to
  an addressee without a finding of individual fault.
- **[CLAIM-464](../claims.d/CLAIM-464.md) and [QUESTION-278](../questions.d/QUESTION-278.md).** Fiss's remedy depends on forces outside
  the court that can withdraw (white flight) or withhold (unions,
  legislatures). That is a court's version of the question's correction
  across institutions none of which governs the rest, and a mechanism
  beside [CLAIM-464](../claims.d/CLAIM-464.md)'s by which the cost of a corrective outcome rises while
  the governing standard stays fixed; Fiss adds that the standard itself
  then bends.

## Limitations

- **Normative and conceptual.** The empirical claims about structural
  suits rest on a handful of well-known cases.
- **The independence dilemma is stated, not resolved**; "live with the
  dilemma" is a stance.
- **Written at the turn of the Burger Court**; later doctrine (Lewis v.
  Casey, the Prison Litigation Reform Act) is outside it.
- **Errors and slips.** None found in the argument. The scan's OCR
  corrupts some citations, so none was checked.

## Open questions

- Is there evidence that structural judges in fact narrow the declared
  right to fit what they can achieve, as Part III predicts? Levinson's
  paper is the obvious next reading.
