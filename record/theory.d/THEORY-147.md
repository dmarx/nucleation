# THEORY-147: Deep responsibility is not limited to the moral, and attributability and accountability are independent kinds of it rather than two levels: accountability needs only that the agent acted as she ought not to, was in a position to know it, and that acting so was up to her, while what makes a self a fit target of the reactive attitudes is plausibly that it can perceive, understand and appreciate the world as we do

## Source

Wolf (2015), [LIT-747](../literature.d/LIT-747.md), read in the author's typescript of the 2013
lecture in [NOTE-565](../notes.d/NOTE-565.md). Page references are the typescript's.

## What was actually shown

Wolf argues it in a lecture, in three steps.

- **Depth is not morality.** The contrast of causal with moral
  responsibility blends two distinctions, shallow vs deep and nonmoral vs
  moral (p. 2). Excuses
  work alike for a rude push and a messy home, and love for an artist's
  work is a reactive attitude as much as resentment is (pp. 4–9). On the
  self-disclosure view, which she takes from Watson, one is deeply
  responsible for what expresses one's self, and the self is more than
  one's moral attitudes (pp. 5–6).
- **Attributability and accountability come apart.** On the two-level
  view, accountability needs self-disclosure plus control, "At least,
  regulative control" (p. 14, n. 5). Wolf rejects it (p. 15). What makes
  the older pie-taker accountable is not which self his act discloses but
  that "(1) he acts as he ought not to, (2) he was in a position to know
  that he ought not to so act, and (3) it was up to him whether to act in
  that way or not" (p. 17). Her psychopath, without empathy or conscience
  but able to control himself by prudence, "may be accountable for his
  psychopathic behavior without his psychopathic character being
  attributable to him at all" (p. 18). Whether accountability then needs
  "anything metaphysically robust" is "not obvious" (p. 19).
- **The hunch.** The fit object of reactive attitudes is "an 'intelligent'
  self", one with "the same (or better) powers of perception, reason, and
  imagination that we do" (pp. 21–22). It fits the psychopath, who "can't
  get it", the case of El Greco, and Strawson's participant stance. Any
  depth in responsibility may lie in "the manifestation or disclosing of
  an intelligent self's unimpeded response to the world" (p. 23).

## What this does not say

- **Not the Reason View restated.** The lecture does not use "Reason
  View", "the True and the Good" or "sanity", and does not cite *Freedom
  Within Reason* ([LIT-685](../literature.d/LIT-685.md), unread). Whether the intelligent self is the
  sanity condition under another name is a question for a reading of that
  book. This account does not attribute the link to Wolf.
- **Not a worked-out theory.** Wolf calls the positive view a hunch, and
  is "uncertain" that it recovers depth (p. 23).
- **Not about real psychopaths.** The case is "a possibly fictitious and
  inaccurate conception" (p. 17).
- **Not about groups.** The lecture never mentions collective agents.
- **Not that accountability is easy.** It keeps knowledge and control;
  it doubts only that the control must be metaphysically deep.
