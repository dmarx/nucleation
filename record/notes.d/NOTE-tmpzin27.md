# NOTE-tmpzin27: Eyal (SEP, 2025 revision) — Informed Consent

## Contribution

A structured map of the competing justifications of informed consent and their difficulties — the standard reference statement of the debate.

## Key results

- §2 intro: "The main arguments for informed consent revolve around: protection, autonomy, prevention of abusive conduct, trust, self-ownership, non-domination, and personal integrity."
- §2.2 (autonomy): "grounding informed consent in autonomy turns out to be difficult" — e.g., "violating informed consent requirements can have positive impact on autonomy", and some gross violations (anesthetized patient) violate it "less directly if at all by thwarting the patient’s determination of what actions to perform."
- §2.6 (non-domination): "no one should be under the arbitrary control of another and... informed consent requirements help to prevent such arbitrary control"; there is "a specific informed consent right to withdraw from research at any point", perhaps because "arbitrary control by others, without the protection of a consent requirement, would constitute domination".
- §5.1 (coercion): coercion is usually "a threat to make someone seriously worse off than she is or should be, unless she consents", but the medical ethos treats more cases as coercive than this.
- §8 (status): "Many contemporary philosophers assume that consent has moral “magic” that turns what would have been a wrongful action into a right one", but some rationales (e.g. forward-looking trust) support legal/conventional rather than natural-right readings.
- §10: the "core" of the requirement — when an intervention is "risky, value-laden, high-impact, physically invasive, and/or medically controversial" and refused — commands high priority, while "other parts may turn out to be easily overridable."

## Assumptions

Bioethics focus (clinical and research consent), with comparisons to other domains. No single justification is presupposed; the entry is deliberately pluralist and aporetic.

## Concepts

Informed/voluntary/capacitated consent; the seven rationales; presumed vs hypothetical consent; tacit consent; coercion, undue inducement, "no choice" situations; waiveable vs inalienable consent rights; natural-right vs legal/epistemic/trust status; core vs rim.

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** A's account ("sets out competing justifications of informed consent, among them autonomy, bodily integrity and protection from domination") is accurate. "Bodily integrity" maps to §2.7 personal integrity and §2.5 self-ownership; "protection from domination" is §2.6 non-domination.

- **[LIT-202](../literature.d/LIT-202.md)** (Pettigrew, "Consent and the Formation of Preferences"; consent's moral effectiveness depends on how the preference was formed, keyed to the space for free agency): the SEP entry supplies the background terrain Pettigrew works in. Eyal's autonomy rationale (competence + authenticity of the values that motivate choice) is exactly the ground Pettigrew refines by asking how those values/preferences were formed. Supports [LIT-202](../literature.d/LIT-202.md)'s framing and situates it: Pettigrew is a graded, mechanism-sensitive development of the autonomy rationale Eyal surveys. The SEP entry also independently notes autonomy's difficulties, which is a mild caution to any account (including [LIT-202](../literature.d/LIT-202.md)'s) that rests effectiveness entirely on autonomy. Leaves open which rationale is correct.
