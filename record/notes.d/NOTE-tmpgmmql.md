# NOTE-tmpgmmql: Putnam — Diplomacy and domestic politics: the logic of two-level games

## Contribution

It replaced lists of "domestic influences" on foreign policy, and the
unitary state of state-centric theory, with one framework in which a
negotiator plays a domestic and an international game at once. "It is
fruitless to debate whether domestic politics really determine
international relations, or the reverse. The answer to that question is
clearly 'Both, sometimes.' The more interesting questions are 'When?' and
'How?'" (p. 427). Its answer is the win-set: the set of agreements that
would be ratified at home, whose size and overlap decide whether a deal is
possible and who gains from it.

## Key insight

What a government can concede abroad is bounded by what it can get ratified
at home, and the bound is a resource as well as a constraint. A negotiator
with a narrow win-set can extract more and risks more; a divided
government can sometimes agree to more than a united one, because some of
its factions want what foreigners demand. Domestic and international
politics are therefore not modelled in sequence but together: when
foreign pressure reverberates at home or issues are linked across the
table, "domestic outcomes are not exogenous, and the two levels cannot be
modeled independently" (p. 456).

## Assumptions

- **A chief negotiator is the only formal link** between the levels, at
  first with no policy views of his own; the assumption is relaxed in the
  last section (pp. 435–436, 456).
- **Ratification is generic** — "any decision-process at Level II that is
  required to endorse or implement a Level I agreement, whether formally
  or informally" (p. 436) — and need not be democratic; forms of
  political power are assumed reducible to "some common denominator" for
  counting votes (p. 437).
- **Ratification is up or down**: a Level I agreement cannot be amended at
  Level II without reopening Level I (p. 437).
- **No-agreement is the reversion point** against which ratification
  judges a deal (p. 442).
- **Constituents are myopic** about the other side's Level II, an
  assumption Putnam calls "not unrealistic empirically" (p. 453 n. 73).
- **Metaphor, not theory.** "Metaphors are not theories"; formal solutions
  for two-level games are left as "a difficult challenge" (p. 435).

## Key results

- **The Bonn summit of 1978 (pp. 427–430).** Germany's fiscal stimulus,
  Japan's growth pledge and Carter's commitment to decontrol oil prices
  were possible "only because a powerful minority within each government
  actually favored on domestic grounds the policy being demanded
  internationally" (p. 428). International pressure was "a necessary
  condition", and without "domestic resonance" it would not have
  sufficed (p. 430).
- **The state as a plural noun (p. 432).** State-centric work runs "a
  unitary-actor model run amok"; on nearly all important issues central
  decision-makers disagree. "If the term 'state' is to be used to mean
  'central decision-makers,' we should treat it as a plural noun: not
  'the state, it ...' but 'the state, they ...'" Because state structures
  "vary little from issue to issue or from year to year, such
  explanations are ill-suited for explaining differences across issues or
  across time (unless 'time' is measured in decades or centuries)".
- **The two tables (p. 434).** National leaders sit at both; "any leader
  who fails to satisfy his fellow players at the domestic table risks
  being evicted from his seat".
- **Win-sets (pp. 437–440).** Larger win-sets make agreement more likely;
  the relative size of the win-sets shapes the division of gains, so a
  small domestic win-set is a bargaining advantage (Schelling), up to the
  point of deadlock (Fig. 1).
- **Voluntary and involuntary defection (pp. 438–439).** Reneging by an
  egoist against failing to deliver because ratification failed. The
  Bonn accord "contained no provisions for temporal balance, sequencing,
  or partial conditionality", yet Germany and Japan enacted their parts
  "more than six months before" Carter's decision on decontrol, and the
  promise held "because reneging would have had high political and
  diplomatic costs" (p. 438).
- **Three determinants of win-set size (pp. 442–450).** (1) Level II
  preferences and coalitions: the cost of no-agreement, homogeneous
  ("boundary") against heterogeneous ("factional") conflicts, abstention
  and politicization, and multi-issue packages, including "synergistic
  linkage" that creates options beyond domestic control (pp. 446–448).
  (2) Level II institutions: ratification rules (a two-thirds Senate),
  party discipline, and autonomy. "The greater the autonomy of central
  decision-makers from their Level II constituents, the larger their
  win-set", as with central-bank insulation, yet "the stronger a state is
  in terms of autonomy from domestic pressures, the weaker its relative
  bargaining position internationally" (p. 449). Several levels of
  ratification, as in the European Community, multiply the analysis.
  (3) Level I strategies: side-payments targeted at swing constituents,
  and "generic goodwill" (pp. 450–452).
- **Uncertainty (pp. 452–453).** Negotiators understate their own
  win-sets and doubt the other's; the useful claim is a "kinky" win-set.
- **Restructuring and reverberation (pp. 454–456).** Foreign pressure can
  change domestic minds, positively or as backlash.
- **The chief negotiator (pp. 456–459).** His motives include shifting
  "the balance of power at Level II in favor of domestic policies that he
  prefers for exogenous reasons"; he will "normally give primacy to his
  domestic calculus ... not least because his own incumbency often
  depends on his standing at Level II" (p. 457); he has, in effect, a
  veto (Wilson and Versailles); and his "fixed investment" in a
  supporting coalition constrains which deals he will back (p. 458).
- **Conclusion (p. 460).** Eight features the approach highlights,
  ending with "the divergences of interest between a national leader and
  those on whose behalf he is negotiating".

## Concepts

- **Level I and Level II** — bargaining between negotiators toward a
  tentative agreement, and each side's separate process of ratifying it
  (p. 436).
- **Win-set** — "the set of all possible Level I agreements that would
  'win' ... when simply voted up or down" at Level II (p. 437).
- **Involuntary defection** — failure to deliver on a promise because
  ratification fails, as against reneging (p. 438).
- **Synergistic linkage** — a Level I deal that creates a policy option
  previously beyond domestic control, and so alters feasible outcomes at
  Level II (p. 447).
- **Reverberation** — international pressure that shifts domestic
  opinion, positively or negatively (pp. 454–456).
- **Kinky win-set** — a claim that the proposed deal will be ratified but
  one slightly better for the other side will not (p. 453).

## Connections

Putnam builds on Walton and McKersie's behavioral theory of labor
negotiation, Schelling's power of a bound negotiator, Allison's
bureaucratic politics ("intra-national games, the overlap of which
constitutes international relations", p. 431), Snyder and Diesing on
divided crisis governments, and Shepsle and Weingast on win-sets and the
ex post veto. He lists Tsebelis's "nested games" among related models of
linked games (p. 441 n. 43). In the record it is one of the four accounts
A28 named beside [CLAIM-423](../claims.d/CLAIM-423.md), with Pierson's path dependence ([LIT-1372](../literature.d/LIT-1372.md)),
Allison and Tsebelis.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-423](../claims.d/CLAIM-423.md): met, fully, for Putnam's half of the defeat condition.**
  The claim falls if Putnam already states that components' time
  horizons decide which component holds authority. He does not. Read in
  full, the article locates authority over a deal in three things: the
  distribution of power, preferences and coalitions at Level II; the
  ratification institutions, including how insulated decision-makers are;
  and the negotiators' strategies, with the chief negotiator's own veto
  and coalition investments. Time horizons appear only in passing, and
  never as what allocates authority among a polity's components:
  - State structures are slow, so they cannot explain variation across
    issues or years "unless 'time' is measured in decades or centuries"
    (p. 432). That is a point about which variables can explain fast
    change, not about which component governs which.
  - Electoral calendars shape timing: German expansionists pointed to the
    1980 elections (p. 428), Carter's advisers postponed decontrol until
    after the 1980 election (p. 429), Italy played "we can lose the
    election" in Trieste (p. 459). The leader's incumbency makes him put
    the domestic game first (p. 457).
  - Repeated play lowers the temptation to defect among players "who
    expect to meet again" (p. 438), and offending foreigners "may be costly
    in the long run" (p. 455).
  - The Bonn deal had no "temporal balance, sequencing, or partial
    conditionality" (p. 438).

  The nearest Putnam comes to the claim's mechanism is the autonomy of
  central decision-makers, such as an insulated central bank (p. 449). He
  treats it as an institutional fact that enlarges a win-set, not as
  following from a horizon. So the claim is not a reading of Putnam. Two
  of its pieces are already his, and the claim should credit him: the
  state as a plural of contending decision-makers ("the state, they",
  p. 432), and authority in a deal shifting between factions as foreign
  pressure tips "the balance of power at Level II" (p. 457). Its other
  half, the state's continuity as an actor while the organization behind
  its positions changes, Putnam does not take up: his game is one
  negotiation, and nothing in it runs across successive coalitions.
- **[CLAIM-423](../claims.d/CLAIM-423.md)'s other open items are untouched.** Pierson's 2000 article
  is still unread, and no case shows a horizon, rather than a formal
  rule, deciding authority.
- **[CLAIM-331](../claims.d/CLAIM-331.md).** Putnam's one remark on slowness (p. 432) fits the premise
  that slower variables sit at a higher level of organization (state
  structures against issue politics), and is no argument for it.
- **[LIT-1375](../literature.d/LIT-1375.md)'s summary** ("domestic coalitions set the win-set within
  which an international deal is possible") is accurate, though narrow:
  the win-set also depends on ratification institutions and on the
  negotiators' strategies.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **A framework, not a model.** No equilibrium is derived, and Putnam says
  formal solutions are left for future work (p. 435). The propositions
  hold *ceteris paribus* and are illustrated, not tested; the principal
  case, Bonn 1978, is from his own earlier study.
- **One negotiation at a time.** Level II actors and preferences are taken
  as given within a game, so how coalitions, careers and institutions
  change between negotiations is outside it.
- **Strategic voting at Level II**, and direct ties between Level II
  players across borders, are named and set aside (p. 453 n. 73, p. 459).

## Open questions

- Can two-level games be solved formally, and which propositions survive?
- How do repeated two-level games across successive governments differ
  from one game, and does the slower side gain from being repeated?
- How should cross-table alliances among Level II players be modelled
  (p. 459)?
