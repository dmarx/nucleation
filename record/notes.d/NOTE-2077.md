# NOTE-2077: Locke — Second Treatise, on slavery, the legislative and trust

## Contribution

On the sections read: limits on political power derived from limits on
what individuals can transfer; political authority as a trust revocable by
those who gave it; and the final judgment of breach reserved to the people.

## Key results

- **Self-enslavement** (§23): "a man, not having the power of his own life,
  cannot, by compact, or his own consent, enslave himself to any one";
  "No body can give more power than he has himself". **Forfeiture**: one
  "having by his fault forfeited his own life, by some act that deserves
  death" may be kept by the one he forfeited it to.
- **Limited compact** (§24): once there is "an agreement for a limited power
  on the one side, and obedience on the other, the state of war and slavery
  ceases", for "no man can, by agreement, pass over to another that which he
  hath not in himself, a power over his own life."
- **Property** (§27): labour makes a thing one's own, "at least where there
  is enough, and as good, left in common for others."
- **Majority** (§95): "the majority have a right to act and conclude the
  rest."
- **The legislative** (§135): "for no body can transfer to another more
  power than he has in himself; and no body has an absolute arbitrary power
  over himself"; "Their power, in the utmost bounds of it, is limited to the
  public good of the society."
- **Trust** (§149): "the legislative being only a fiduciary power to act for
  certain ends"; the people "will always have a right to preserve, what they
  have not a power to part with"; but "this power of the people can never
  take place till the government be dissolved."
- **Judgment** (§§168, 240): with no judge on earth the people appeal to
  heaven, "And this judgment they cannot part with"; "The people shall be
  judge; for who shall be judge whether his trustee or deputy acts well ...
  but he who deputes him".

## Assumptions

Theological at root: persons are God's "property, whose workmanship they
are" (§6), and the law of nature is God's will. Inalienability follows
from lacking power over one's own life, not from a theory of standing.
Forfeiture by wrongdoing is allowed.

## Concepts

State and law of nature; property in one's person; the "enough, and as
good" proviso; consent and majority; fiduciary power and trust;
forfeiture; dissolution; the appeal to heaven.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-720](../claims.d/CLAIM-720.md).** The form of the claim's argument is Locke's: the limit
  lies in what the holder can transfer (§§23, 135), and the people's
  judgment of breach is something "they cannot part with" (§168). Two
  qualifications. Locke's protected object is life, and liberty from
  arbitrary power as its safeguard, grounded in God's ownership, not
  "standing" in general. And Locke admits forfeiture "by his fault" (§23),
  which the claim does not provide for: a way to lose what cannot be
  conferred.
- **[CASE-286](../cases.d/CASE-286.md).** §24's "agreement for a limited power on the one side,
  and obedience on the other", against §§23 and 135 on unlimited power, is
  the case's contrast between limited and unlimited delegation.
- **[CLAIM-727](../claims.d/CLAIM-727.md).** §240, "The people shall be judge", against an
  authority that would judge its own breach.
- **[QUESTION-481](../questions.d/QUESTION-481.md).** §95 (majority) and §149 (power reverts only on
  dissolution).
- **[CASE-282](../cases.d/CASE-282.md).** §27's proviso, the labour account A93 names.
- The exchange's A85 is faithful to §§23–24 and 135; it omits forfeiture,
  and its "standing" is not Locke's ground.

## Limitations

Selected sections, from a transcription without a scan; the 1764 wording
may differ from Laslett's. The theology and forfeiture were not traced
further. The property chapter beyond §27 is unread.
