# NOTE-735: Grice — Meaning

## Contribution

A twelve-page paper that names non-natural meaning and gives it an
analysis in terms of the speaker's intentions, not the sign's causal
powers. After it there is a definite proposal for what it is for someone
to mean something by an act on an occasion. It does not cover what makes
an act a promise, a warning or a reproach, and it states its analysis
"roughly" and as "a first shot".

## Key insight

Meaning something is a special way of trying to affect someone: one that
works only if they see that you are trying. The handkerchief, the
photograph and the pound note work whether or not the target sees the
intention, so they mean nothing. The picture, the pointed door and the cut
in the street work only through that recognition, so they do.

## Assumptions

- **Occasion meaning is basic.** "The meaning (in general) of a sign needs
  to be explained in terms of what users of the sign do (or should) mean
  by it on particular occasions". Grice says he is "sympathetic" to this
  and "aware that the point is controversial". The paper does not argue
  it.
- **The informative case first.** The analysis is built on utterances
  meant to induce a belief, then extended to imperatives and to the cut.
- **No psychological machinery.** "I must disclaim any intention of
  peopling all our talking life with armies of complicated psychological
  occurrences." Intentions are attributed by the criteria used for
  non-linguistic action.

## Key results

- **The five tests (pp. 377–378).** For natural meaning:
  - x means that p entails p;
  - no "what is meant by" x;
  - no "somebody meant by x";
  - no restatement with quoted words;
  - a restatement as "the fact that …" is available.
  Non-natural meaning reverses all five.
- **Against the causal theory (pp. 379–381).** A tendency to produce an
  attitude, "dependent on an elaborate process of conditioning", either
  admits the tail coat or is circular. Stevenson's appeal to linguistic
  rules, to keep "Jones is tall" out of what "Jones is an athlete" means,
  "seems to involve us in another circle".
- **The analysis (p. 383–384).** "A must intend to induce by x a belief
  in an audience, and he must also intend his utterance to be recognized
  as so intended. But these intentions are not independent; the
  recognition is intended by A to play its part in inducing the belief".
  Shortly: "A uttered x with the intention of inducing a belief by means of
  the recognition of this intention." Grice adds that A must not regard it
  as "a foregone conclusion that the belief will be induced … whether or
  not the intention behind the utterance is recognized".
- **The deliberate frown (p. 383).** A deliberate frown has the same
  effect as a spontaneous one "only provided the audience takes it as
  intended to convey displeasure". Remove the recognition and "the
  belief-producing tendency of the frown must be regarded as being
  impaired or destroyed". So it means something.
- **The generalisations (p. 385).**
  - (1) "A meantNN something by x" is roughly "A intended the utterance
    of x to produce some effect in an audience by means of the recognition
    of this intention"; what A meant is the intended effect.
  - (2) "x meant something" is roughly "Somebody meantNN something by x",
    with an exception: the red light means stop, but "it would be very
    unnatural to say, 'Somebody (e.g., the Corporation) meantNN by the
    red-light change that the traffic was to stop.'" Still, "there seems
    to be some sort of reference to somebody's intentions".
  - (3) Timeless meaning is "as a first shot" a statement about what
    "people" (vague) intend to effect by x.
- **Reason, not cause (p. 385).** The person who blushes when he hears a
  grunt he knows is meant to make him blush shows that the intended
  effect "must be something which in some sense is within the control of
  the audience". The recognition is "a reason and not merely a cause".
  Of the cut: "one cannot in any straightforward sense 'decide' to be
  offended; but one can refuse to be offended."
- **Primary intention only (p. 386).** If I intend effect E by recognition
  and E to lead to F, F is not part of what I meant.
- **Criteria for intentions (pp. 386–388).** An explicit plan "counts
  fairly heavily" but is not conclusive. Without one, "an utterer is held
  to intend to convey what is normally conveyed (or normally intended to
  be conveyed), and we require a good reason for accepting that a
  particular use diverges from the general usage". Between candidate
  intentions, "we tend to refer to the context … and ask which of the
  alternatives would be relevant to other things he is saying or doing":
  a man calling for a "pump" at a fire "would not want a bicycle pump".

## Concepts

- **Natural meaning.** The evidential sense of "mean": the spots mean
  measles. Factive, and no one means anything by it.
- **meaningNN (non-natural meaning).** The sense of "A meant something by
  x" and of "x means 'the bus is full'".
- **Utterance.** "A neutral word to apply to any candidate for
  meaningNN", with an act–object ambiguity. Gestures, pictures and a
  push all count.
- **Primary intention.** The effect the speaker intends to produce by
  means of recognition. Only it fixes what was meant.

## Connections

Grice argues against C. L. Stevenson's *Ethics and Language* (1944),
chapter iii, the only work cited. The paper is the first link in the line
that [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md) names. Strawson (1964) builds his account of illocutionary
force on it, and Bach and Harnish (1979) build a theory of speech acts on
that. "Logic and Conversation" (1975) turns from what a speaker means to
what a speaker implicates. The rational-speech-act listener of [THEORY-172](../theory.d/THEORY-172.md),
which inverts a model of a speaker, is a probabilistic descendant. Its
papers credit Grice by name ([LIT-816](../literature.d/LIT-816.md), [LIT-821](../literature.d/LIT-821.md)).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md).** The objection says the intention-based line exists and
  "plausibly" individuates teasing and reproach by speaker-internal
  states. This paper is that line's foundation, and it confirms two
  things. First, Grice gives one case near reproach, the cut in the
  street. It counts as meaningNN because the offence depends on the
  recognition of the intention to offend. That is speaker-internal in
  [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md)'s sense. Second, the paper is about meaning, not about act
  types. It says nothing about what separates teasing from reproach when
  the same thing is meant. So it does not yet meet the defeat condition
  of [CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md). Strawson's paper would have to.
- **Two passages the relational side can use.** Neither answers the
  objection, but each puts the audience into the analysis rather than
  leaving it as evidence alone.
  - **The intended effect must be "within the control of the audience".**
    For the cut, "one can refuse to be offended". Whether the act succeeds
    depends on the hearer's response to a reason. That is close to
    Austin's uptake ([QUESTION-073](../questions.d/QUESTION-073.md)), though Grice treats it as a condition
    on what the speaker can intend, not on the act.
  - **Intentions are read by public criteria.** "An utterer is held to
    intend to convey what is normally conveyed", and context decides
    between candidates. This is the intentionalist's own way to make the
    parties' relations evidence of intention. It is the reading [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md)
    gives the rival and that [CASE-069](../cases.d/CASE-069.md) reports Labov's rule can bear.
- **The traffic light.** Grice concedes that conventional signals mean
  without anyone meaning by them, while "there seems to be some sort of
  reference to somebody's intentions". This is where institutional acts
  enter. It is consistent with [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md)'s concession of the judge's
  reprimand to Searle.
- **[THEORY-172](../theory.d/THEORY-172.md).** The rational-speech-act listener infers the speaker's
  intended referent, which matches Grice's analysis only in part. In the
  formulation of [LIT-821](../literature.d/LIT-821.md), the speaker chooses words by their
  informativeness to a literal listener. Nothing in it is an intention
  that the choice be recognised. This is the record's observation, not
  Grice's: [THEORY-172](../theory.d/THEORY-172.md) is Gricean in its inference, not in its analysis of
  meaning.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.
