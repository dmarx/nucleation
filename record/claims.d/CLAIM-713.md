# CLAIM-713: A moral commitment is exploitable as a resource: because a committed party reliably acts on it, whoever controls the circumstances that trigger it can capture what its fulfilment yields and leave its costs with that party, without deception, coercion or any unreasonable choice by the party, and an institution can come to run on what it so avoids paying, with the burden falling hardest on its most conscientious members

## The claim

The exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afa2a8d-2598-83ea-8d1a-d9ceb992a99c). The
owner, at U8: "elaborate on the resource theoretical perspective on moral
commitments". A15 opens by disclaiming: "I'm developing a
*resource-theoretic interpretation*, not describing a single established
academic theory". It names four resource-like properties of a moral
commitment (predictability, credibility, motivational force, legitimacy)
and concludes, §1: "**The exploitable resource is partly the person's
reliable willingness to act on moral reasons.**"

A15 §7:

> A moral commitment becomes a strategic vulnerability when another actor
> can control the circumstances that trigger it while avoiding the costs
> of its fulfillment.

**The institutional form.** A15 §3: a hospital needs 100 units of care,
pays for 85 and relies on employees "unwilling to let patients suffer" for
the rest. "**They've been transferred from a financial obligation of the
institution into a moral burden on its employees.** One could call this an
*implicit moral subsidy*." The loop: the work gets done, so leadership
concludes resources are adequate, so more effort is needed, and "Those
efforts conceal the inadequacy of the system." "**The institution can
become more irresponsible precisely because its employees are
responsible.**"

**Who bears it.** Carried from the continuation's filing of the same
turns (share https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b).
A13 §4: "Consider a hospital that chronically understaffs its emergency
department. The hospital's management knows that nurses will not willingly
abandon patients in urgent need." "No one needs to issue an explicit
threat." "The party that should bear the cost of satisfying an obligation
arranges for another party to bear it instead. And the more conscientious
the second party is, the more effectively the arrangement can function."
A13 §10 names the selection: "the people least willing to compromise their
moral commitments are made to absorb the greatest burdens. Over time, the
institution may reward people who are more comfortable offloading those
burdens." A15 §4 separates "**Moral commitment:**" from "**Moral
capacity:**" and "**Moral burden:**": "the exploiter can draw on the
target's capacity until that capacity is damaged, without necessarily
diminishing the target's sense of obligation". A15 §3's hold-up reading:
the nurse "can't simply stop caring about patients at the end of a
contractual shift", so the employer can truthfully say "*We aren't forcing
you to stay. You're free to leave when your shift ends.*"

**Why the need gives no hold: a candidate, not asserted.** [CLAIM-690](CLAIM-690.md):
with the part able to withdraw or renegotiate, the whole's need of it
becomes the part's hold on its terms. A commitment to patients takes
withdrawal away from the nurse without anyone else taking it, so the
arrangement's dependence on her is as great as ever and the leverage runs
the other way. That inference is the continuation filer's statement of
what A's case shows against [CLAIM-690](CLAIM-690.md). A30 §4 asks it as a question,
and the record keeps it there ([QUESTION-472](../questions.d/QUESTION-472.md)); this claim does not rest on
it.

**When it is exploitation.** A15 §8 grants that "benefiting from someone's
conscientiousness" is not in itself objectionable, since "Every functioning
society relies on people doing things for reasons other than material
self-interest", and offers four dimensions: consent (accepted or imposed
obligations), burden (proportionate or excessive), control (influence over
the conditions of one's obligations or accountability without it) and exit
(refusal possible, or made to look like harming innocents). Exploitation
then "can consist in **appropriating the practical benefits of someone's
moral commitments while denying them control over the conditions under
which those commitments are exercised**."

## Where it sits

- It rests on [CLAIM-703](CLAIM-703.md), from the same exchange: circumstances someone
  else sets can create a genuine obligation. This claim makes the setting
  of such circumstances a standing position, not a single manoeuvre.
- [TERM-123](../terms.d/TERM-123.md): exploitation relative to a declared
  standard. A15 §8's four dimensions are a candidate standard; the claim
  uses the term in that relative sense.
- [CLAIM-690](CLAIM-690.md): who controls the terms sorts a dependence. A30
  §4 of the exchange asked whether a party's own moral commitment can be
  what makes its dependence a vulnerability, "the moral cost of withdrawal"
  as leverage. That question is filed with this claim, [QUESTION-472](../questions.d/QUESTION-472.md).
- [QUESTION-461](../questions.d/QUESTION-461.md) asks when an organization's
  dependence on subordinated contributors increases their authority over
  it. The subsidy is a case where it does not: the dependence is on their
  conscience, which keeps them from withdrawing.
- [CLAIM-614](CLAIM-614.md)'s non-legible harm and [CLAIM-670](CLAIM-670.md)'s arrangement that
  produces the dependence it exploits are neighbours; here the dependence
  is the members' own commitment, which the arrangement did not produce but
  uses. [CASE-288](../cases.d/CASE-288.md) (three hospitals with the same output and budget) is
  a construction from the continuation's filing. The senses of
  responsibility are [TERM-127](../terms.d/TERM-127.md)'s.
- [QUESTION-468](../questions.d/QUESTION-468.md) asks how the record
  identifies exploitation. This claim proposes one mechanism and one
  standard.

## What it does not say

- **That the institution intends it.** A15 §3: "This is a theoretical
  mechanism, not a claim that every understaffed hospital deliberately
  operates this way."
- **That relying on conscientiousness is wrong as such.** A15 §8: "Every
  functioning society relies on people doing things for reasons other than
  material self-interest."
- **That the remedy lies with the members.** A13 §4: "Telling nurses to
  stop caring about patients is not a satisfactory response to
  exploitative staffing practices."
- **That the remedy is less conscientiousness.** A15's close: "**The
  challenge isn't to make people less conscientious. It's to prevent
  conscientiousness from becoming a mechanism through which the powerful
  transfer costs and responsibilities onto those least able to refuse
  them.**"
- **What Robillard and Strawser, Keller or Tronto hold.** A15 names them as
  its foundations; all are registered unread ([LIT-2692](../literature.d/LIT-2692.md), [LIT-2682](../literature.d/LIT-2682.md), [LIT-2670](../literature.d/LIT-2670.md), [LIT-2708](../literature.d/LIT-2708.md)).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Nelkin, read (for Williams)

The SEP entry "Moral Luck" ([LIT-2695](../literature.d/LIT-2695.md)) reports two dispositions this claim's
mechanism would run on. One is Williams's agent-regret, expressed in "the
willingness to compensate a person who was harmed by one’s actions" even
where the harm "was not his fault". The other is Wolf's virtue of "taking
responsibility for one’s actions and their consequences", which "involves a
willingness to give more…that justice requires". Both are presented as
fitting, even admirable. The claim's point is that whoever controls the
triggering circumstances can capture what such a disposition yields. The
entry does not discuss exploitation. Relation: none. The reading names the
virtue whose exploitation the claim describes.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Keller, read

Keller supplies one of the claim's named foundations. His third-party case (p. 24) is an employer who
cuts the time lawyers may spend per client, expecting them to work longer out of obligation to their
clients: "I can increase your workload without having to increase your pay and without losing clients
for the firm." His conclusion names the unpaid cost: those in caring roles may reasonably "expect
greater pay or improved conditions, as compensation for the moral burden that comes with her role"
(p. 26). This supports the claim's mechanism, capture of what a commitment's fulfilment yields with its
costs left on the committed party, at the scale of one firm. Keller's agents are deliberate
strategists; he does not say an institution "can come to run on" what it avoids paying, and his
account is "moral blackmail" (wrongful by definition), not a resource theory. Relation: corroborates
(partial; [THEORY-1917](../theory.d/THEORY-1917.md)).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tronto (2018), read as a stand-in

The claim lists Tronto among A15's foundations. This essay states privileged irresponsibility and
the democratic reallocation of care, but nothing about the exploitation of a party's moral
commitment. The commitment mechanism, in a care setting, is Liedo et al.'s ([LIT-2683](../literature.d/LIT-2683.md), p. 11),
not Tronto's in anything read. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Liedo, Van Grunsven and Marin, read

Corroborates the claim from an independent source in care ethics ([LIT-2683](../literature.d/LIT-2683.md)). In the hospital
case the authors take from Van Wynsberghe, nurses collect toxic urine samples at risk to
themselves, "a situation where the unsatisfactory conditions of performing care are resolved by
exploiting the commitment of PCWs to the value of care, resulting in the prioritization of care for
the patient over their own well-being and safety" (p. 11). The worker's own satisfaction is part of
the mechanism: "this very identification and satisfaction may function as part of an exploitation
dynamics" (p. 6), so that it "contributes to them assuming deficient work conditions" (p. 6). This
is the claim's structure: whoever sets the conditions captures the yield of a commitment and leaves
its costs with the committed party, without deception or coercion. Where the claim speaks of a
resource, Liedo et al. speak of a structural allocation (Tronto's privileged irresponsibility).
They do not argue the claim's last clause, that an institution comes to run on what it avoids
paying, beyond noting that robots can let employers avoid reallocating duties (p. 13).

Relation: **corroborates**.
