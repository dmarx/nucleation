# THEORY-337: In the comparative-question paradigm, judging whether a quantity is higher or lower than a high or low anchor pulls the later estimate of it toward the anchor, with large effects in five preregistered experiments of 128 people each, lasting through dozens of intervening word-recognition trials

## Source

Harris, Blower, Rodgers, Lagator, Page, Burton, Urlichich & Speekenbrink
(2019), [LIT-1067](../literature.d/LIT-1067.md), read in [NOTE-876](../notes.d/NOTE-876.md) from the authors' submitted manuscript
(the version of record was not reachable). Strack & Mussweiler (1997),
[LIT-1064](../literature.d/LIT-1064.md), read in [NOTE-877](../notes.d/NOTE-877.md).

## What was actually shown

**The preregistered replications.** Each experiment asked a comparative
question (is the quantity higher or lower than the anchor), then a
word-recognition task, then an absolute estimate. Anchors were set at the
5th and 95th percentiles of a calibration sample, except in Experiments 3
and 4, where they were chosen to match the critical words. Low against high anchor
means (Table 2): Experiment 1, 12.68 against 14.70°C; Experiment 2,
£10,754 against £19,742; Experiment 3, 10.71 against 15.88°C; Experiment
4, £13,817 against £61,452; Experiment 5, 11.03 against 16.16°C; all
ps < .001, computed d ≈ 0.87, 1.06, 1.39, 0.85 and 1.34. These held
although 70 lexical-decision trials (40 identification trials in
Experiment 5) came between the comparison and the estimate. The authors:
"the robustness of anchoring effects is remarkable".

**The original studies.** In Strack and Mussweiler's three studies
(N = 32, 32 and 67), participants told the anchors "were randomly
selected" still assimilated to them when the estimate was on the dimension
compared (Study 1: a difference of 1.75 z between high and low anchors),
and implausible anchors, such as a 45,000 km Elbe, "were at least as
effective as plausible ones" (Study 3). When the estimate was on another
dimension the difference fell to 0.24 z (Study 1), and when it was about a
very different object the effect reversed to contrast (Study 2).

**What could have come out the other way.** Five preregistered
experiments at about four times the originals' N could have found a small
or absent effect, as they did for the accessibility measure run alongside
it; they found a large one each time.

## What this does not say

- **Why the estimate moves.** Neither paper measures the carrier. Harris
  et al. found no change in word recognition alongside the shift, with a
  task that also missed direct priming ([THEORY-335](THEORY-335.md)). Their leading
  account of the durability is a provisional answer formed at the
  comparison, which "may result from (e.g.) selective accessibility,
  anchoring-and-adjustment, scale distortion…".
- **That anchoring holds outside this paradigm.** Both papers use a
  comparative question about a general-knowledge quantity, with samples
  in Germany (students) and the UK. Nothing here is about anchors met
  in conversation, negotiation or expert judgement, or about anchors not
  explicitly compared.
- **That any anchor works the same.** The moderations by dimension and
  object (Strack and Mussweiler, Studies 1 and 2) rest on n = 8 per cell
  and have not been replicated in the record; Study 2's anchor main effect
  was F < 1, with the effect reversed for the other object.
- **That anchoring is a cost.** No study here scores accuracy against a
  true value in a way that separates harm from help. Whether cue-led
  judgement is a bias needs a standard outside the search ([CLAIM-292](../claims.d/CLAIM-292.md)).
- **How long it lasts.** Seventy trials is a lower bound on the delay the
  effect survives in these designs, not a measured decay.
