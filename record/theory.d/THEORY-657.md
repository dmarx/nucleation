# THEORY-657: If an emergent property is one that the parts lack, then the other marks commonly given for emergence are separate questions about each system and not consequences of it: established condensed-matter cases show novelty without a discontinuity, a discontinuity without novelty, novelty that was predicted first, and novelty with a rigorous reduction

## Source

McKenzie (2025), [LIT-141](../literature.d/LIT-141.md), *Emergence: from physics to biology,
sociology, and computer science*, arXiv v4, 187 pp., read in full in
[NOTE-124](../notes.d/NOTE-124.md). [NOTE-124](../notes.d/NOTE-124.md) files the book as a hub reference and says "No THEORY
follows". Its reason is that the definition is stipulated and the
characteristics' relations are left open, not that the book shows nothing.
This document states only what its counterexamples show, and is Proposed
for that reason.

## What was actually shown

**The definition (§2.1).** A property of a system of many parts, scales or
iterations is emergent iff "the individual parts of the system do not have
this property". An alternative test: the property is absent when the
parts' states are assigned at random, as at high temperature (p. 11).
McKenzie does not claim this definition is better than its rivals (p. 12).

**The marks.** Twelve further characteristics, six called objective
(discontinuities, universality, diversity with limitations, modification
of the parts, order, modularity) and six subjective (self-organisation,
unpredictability, irreducibility, contextuality and downward causation,
intra-stratum closure, complexity). The count is given as twelve in §2 and
thirteen in §22.

**The counterexamples.**

- Novelty without discontinuity: the Kondo effect, "novelty does not
  necessarily imply discontinuity" (p. 58); Fermi liquids; quantum-statistics
  crossovers (p. 61).
- Discontinuity without novelty: the liquid–gas transition, which is
  adiabatically connected (p. 13).
- Novelty without unpredictability: BKT and the hexatic phase were
  predicted first, "unpredictability is not equivalent to novelty" (p. 65),
  as were spin ice and some quantum-statistics properties.
- Novelty with reduction: Butterfield's four rigorous cases, "novelty is
  not sufficient for irreducibility" (§2.9, p. 20).

**What could have come out otherwise.** Any of the marks could have
turned out to travel with novelty in every case examined. Four of them do
not.

## What this does not say

- **That novelty is the right definition.** It is stipulated. On a
  definition that builds in, say, unpredictability, the third
  counterexample is no counterexample.
- **That the marks are unrelated.** The book shows some non-entailments by
  example. It does no systematic logical analysis, and §24 leaves the
  relations open.
- **That most physicists hold weak emergence because of this.** That is the
  author's impression (§22), not an argument.
- **That molecular structure is evidence of strong emergence, or that it is
  not.** §15 argues it is not, against Primas, Cartwright and Hendry, from
  the Born–Oppenheimer treatment and non-BO calculations. That is a separate
  argument, not carried here.
- **That LLM emergent abilities show a phase transition.** The book's
  opening sentence on Wei et al. ("concrete and rigorous") is undercut by
  its own later paragraph: no agreed metric, order parameter or
  finite-size analysis (p. 138).
