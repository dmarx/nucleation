# NOTE-tmp11g34: Retention of Memory through Metamorphosis: Can a Moth Remember What It Learned As a Caterpillar?

## Contribution

It shows in a moth that an associative memory formed in the caterpillar
is still expressed in the adult. It rules out the standard rival, a
"chemical legacy" of odour carried on the pupa, with two manipulations of
the pupal case. And it shows that what survives depends on when it was
learned: an aversion learned at third instar lasts through two larval
molts and is lost in metamorphosis, while one learned at fifth instar is
kept.

## Key insight

Metamorphosis does not erase the animal's past wholesale, and it does not
keep it wholesale either. It keeps what was learned late. The authors
read this through the mushroom bodies: in *Drosophila* the earliest lobe
(γ) is pruned in pupation, and a later one (α′/β′) keeps its projections.
So their result "challenge[s] a broadly-held popular view of lepidopteran
metamorphosis: that the caterpillar is essentially broken down entirely,
and its components reorganized into a butterfly or moth" (p. 5).

## Assumptions

- **Position in a Y maze after ten minutes measures preference.** Each
  animal was scored, blind, as in the odour arm, the ambient-air arm or
  "no choice" (p. 6). The paper does not say how "no choice" animals
  enter the proportions.
- **Shock is an ecologically fair aversive stimulus.** The authors argue
  this from mantids learning to avoid milkweed bugs and hornworms
  sensitized by pinching (p. 5).
- **Chemical legacy means residue on or in the pupa.** Washing tests the
  outside of the pupal case. The inside is addressed by design: a gaseous,
  ephemeral odour and a shock, not an ingested chemical (p. 4).
- **_Manduca_ mushroom bodies develop as _Drosophila_'s do**, scaled to
  five instars instead of three. The mechanism section is explicitly
  conditional on this ("If M. sexta MB development is analogous", p. 5).

## Key results

- **Training** (pp. 5–6). Two to three days after the molt to fifth
  instar, up to ten larvae at a time on conductive agarose: 10 s of ethyl
  acetate (EA), then 10 s of odour with a 16–18 V AC shock. Eight bouts,
  an hour apart, in one day. Larvae were tested the next day, adults
  three days after eclosion. At least three cohorts per treatment; sample
  sizes set by power analysis.
- **Larvae learn** (p. 2). Fifth instars avoided EA only after forward
  pairing: 78%, N = 41, p < 0.001 (two-tailed binomial against 50%).
  Untreated (N = 46, p = 0.32), shock only (N = 43, p = 1.0), odour only
  (N = 29, p = 1.0) and backward pairing (N = 22, p = 0.52) showed nothing.
- **The memory survives larval molts** (p. 2). Larvae trained in early
  third instar avoided EA 10 to 14 days later as late fifth instars: 81%,
  N = 32, p < 0.001. Not different from fifth-instar training
  (χ² = 0.1128, df = 1, p = 0.74).
- **It survives metamorphosis** (pp. 2–4). Adults tested 28 to 35 days
  after larval conditioning. Controls chose at random (N = 31, 23, 28; each
  p = 1.0). Forward-paired adults avoided EA: 77%, N = 27, p = 0.005. The
  authors note 78% of larvae and 77% of adults: "a long-lasting and stable
  memory" (p. 5).
- **Individuals keep their choice** (pp. 2–4). Of forward-paired adults
  that chose ambient air, 80% had chosen it as larvae (N = 15,
  p = 0.035). Control groups' constancy did not differ from 50%.
- **Early learning does not survive metamorphosis** (p. 4). Adults trained
  as third instars did not avoid EA (N = 15, p = 0.5).
- **Not chemical legacy** (p. 4). EA gel applied to naive pupae, four to
  six times from three weeks after pupation, gave no aversion (N = 30,
  p = 0.20). Washing the pupal cases of trained larvae, one and three
  weeks after pupation, did not remove it (N = 31, p = 0.438). Washing
  control pupae changed nothing (N = 25, p = 0.423).
- **The proposed carrier** (p. 5). In *Drosophila* the γ lobe forms in the
  embryo and is pruned in pupation; α′/β′ forms in mid-third instar and
  keeps intact projections; α/β begins at pupation. With five instars,
  *Manduca* third-instar training likely precedes α′/β′ neurogenesis. So
  early memory would sit in γ and be lost, late memory in α′/β′ and be
  kept. The results are "consistent with, but do not provide conclusive
  support for" surviving synaptic connections (p. 5). The test they
  propose is to ablate α′/β′.

## Concepts

- **holometabolous**: undergoing complete metamorphosis, larva to pupa to
  adult.
- **chemical legacy** (Corbet 1985): an adult's response shaped by
  odours from the larval environment met at eclosion, not by memory.
- **Hopkins' host-selection principle**: the idea that adults prefer the
  host they fed on as larvae. The authors call the evidence for it
  "equivocal" (p. 4).
- **constancy**: the proportion of individuals choosing ambient air as
  larvae that also did so as adults.
- **forward and backward pairing**: odour before shock, which conditions,
  and shock before odour, which here did not.
- **mushroom bodies (γ, α′/β′, α/β lobes)**: paired brain structures
  receiving antennal-lobe input, the presumed seat of olfactory memory.

## Connections

- **Alloway 1972 (LIT-tmpvl4oj)**, unread. Cited (ref. 36) among a
  century of studies of larval experience and adult behaviour in beetles,
  moths and butterflies. The paper claims to be the first conclusive
  demonstration in Lepidoptera, not the first in insects. It counts
  *Drosophila* (Tully et al. 1994) and a weevil among controlled earlier
  cases (p. 4).
- **Blackiston, Shomrat and Levin 2015 (LIT-tmpaazd0)** restate the result
  in one sentence and give a different account of α′/β′ remodelling, as
  "partial pruning" rather than intact projections. Blackiston moved to
  Levin's lab.
- **Shomrat and Levin 2013 (LIT-tmpgfqw1)**, the planarian counterpart.
  There the claim is that memory lives outside the brain; here the
  evidence points to neurons that survive inside it.
- **Dias and Ressler (NOTE-tmpeof8o)** also used an odour paired with
  shock. They carried a sensitivity across generations, through sperm.
  This paper carries a learned aversion across one life's metamorphosis,
  and does not test offspring.

## Bearing on the record

- **QUESTION-tmpws54x, what carries it across.** The paper excludes one
  answer and points to another. These are inferences beyond the authors'
  claims.
  - Residue on the pupa is excluded. Washing and scenting were each tested
    the other way round, and neither moved behaviour.
  - The timing result is the most informative fact for the question. A
    memory that survives two larval molts and then fails in metamorphosis
    is not stored in "the animal" as such. It is stored in something that
    the molts keep and metamorphosis replaces. The *Drosophila*
    mushroom-body map supplies a candidate, a lobe that survives, but
    this paper only fits the map; it does not test it.
  - So on this evidence the carrier is a surviving part of the nervous
    system, not a body-wide store. That is the opposite of the planarian
    reading the question also holds.
- **QUESTION-tmpws54x, whether the moth is the one that learned.** The
  moth is the same organism as the caterpillar on any biological view.
  The paper adds a thin psychological connection: one learned aversion,
  held by about three quarters of a group and kept by 12 of 15 tracked
  individuals.
  - That fits THEORY-464's line that the relation that matters comes apart
    from numerical identity. Here identity holds and connectedness is
    partial and depends on when the learning happened. A third-instar
    caterpillar is connected to its moth less than a fifth-instar one,
    though both are the same animal.
  - It does not make the Parfitian case (LIT-469) of full psychological
    continuity through a wholly rebuilt body. The body is not wholly
    rebuilt; the carrier, if the authors are right, is the part that is
    not.
- **The offspring claim** in the question, from the unpublished Kobe
  repetition, is not tested here at all.
- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** No direct bearing. No dated
  note is proposed for it.

## Limitations

- **One species, one odour, one lab, one aversive stimulus.** Adult
  groups are 15 to 31.
- **The "no choice" animals** are scored but their handling in the
  proportions is not stated. The figure captions give "means±SD" for what
  are proportions of binary choices, without saying over what.
- **Internal residue is argued away, not tested.** The authors fault an
  earlier study for possible residue "in the insect haemolymph" (p. 4).
  Their own answer is the gaseous stimulus, not a measurement.
- **The washing test's statistic is unclear.** The text says washing did
  not cause "a loss of odor aversion in adults (N = 31, p = 0.438)" (p. 4).
  If that p were the binomial against 50% used everywhere else, it would
  mean the washed adults showed no significant aversion. The Figure 4
  caption marks some values as differing from random. So the 0.438 is
  presumably a comparison with unwashed adults, but the test is not
  named.
- **The loss after third-instar training** is a non-significant result in
  15 adults. The two adult groups are not compared directly.
- **The mechanism is borrowed.** No *Manduca* brain was examined.
- **Inconsistencies and typos.**
  - Figure 3's caption (p. 3) gives shock only N = 23 and odour only
    N = 28; the text (p. 2) gives odour alone N = 23 and shock alone
    N = 28.
  - Figure 4's caption (p. 4) gives "N = 40 adults, 30 larvae" and
    "N = 39 adults, 31 larvae", while the text gives N = 30 and N = 31 for
    the adults. The caption's labels look swapped.
  - "fifth insta" (Fig. 3 caption, p. 3); "holometablous" and
    "implications. as retention" (p. 5); "thoratic" and "resulted in in a
    change" (p. 6).

## Open questions

- Does ablating the α′/β′ lobe, or its *Manduca* equivalent, abolish the
  adult memory of fifth-instar training and spare nothing else?
- Is the haemolymph free of the odour? A direct measurement would close
  the last chemical route.
- Does an appetitive memory, or one for a second odour, survive the same
  way? One aversion is a narrow sample of what a caterpillar learns.
- Does the aversion reach the moths' offspring? The Kobe repetition claims
  so for swallowtails; it is unpublished.
