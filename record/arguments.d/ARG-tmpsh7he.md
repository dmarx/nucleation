# ARG-tmpsh7he: Han's seductive power and auto-exploitation, Benjamin's default discrimination and Marcuse's administration, which runs through the choice of a society's project and is veiled, show that a complete dependence can be domination or unfreedom on serious accounts without anyone holding present discretion over the dependent, so the domination threshold holds only as a test for domination by an arrangement's controllers

## The inference

1. [THEORY-tmpoxssh](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpoxssh.md), Marcuse read in full: "Domination is transfigured into administration" (ch. 2); the choice of a society's project is "primarily (but only primarily!) the privilege of those groups which have attained control over the productive process" (ch. 8); "the technological veil conceals the reproduction of inequality and enslavement", and "the organizers and administrators themselves become increasingly dependent on the machinery which they organize and administer" (ch. 2). Version 1 read Marcuse through Farr ([THEORY-tmpnydju](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpnydju.md)), and two of the sentences it quoted here are not the book's (see the revision below).
2. [THEORY-tmpclnon](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpclnon.md): neoliberal power "tries to make them dependent", and "There is no person opposite"; [THEORY-tmp5hgen](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp5hgen.md): freedom and constraint coincide in auto-exploitation, though Han calls this "the disappearance of domination".
3. [THEORY-tmpo6ipc](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpo6ipc.md): "default discrimination", subordination by defaults and inherited data with no discretionary act now.
4. So a complete dependence can lack anyone holding present discretion over the dependent and still be domination (Marcuse, Benjamin) or unfreedom (Han): for Han and Benjamin because no one's present discretion is exercised, for Marcuse because the controllers' discretion runs through the choice of a project for the whole, is veiled, and binds them too. Version 1's clause "is not domination, however complete" is false on those accounts.

## Critical questions

- **Do the sources say what the inference needs?** Marcuse and Benjamin, as read, call the constraint domination or subordination; Han denies it is domination and calls it unfreedom. So the readings refute the clause as a claim about domination on two accounts, and as a claim about freedom on all three.
- **Could the claim answer that administrative systems and defaults still have controllers?** For Marcuse, largely yes: the book names them (step 1). For Benjamin and Han, in part: Benjamin's designers exercised discretion once, at design time; administrators exist. But the readings' point is that the constraint does not run through anyone's present discretion. The reply would make "discretion" cover design-time choice, which changes the claim more than narrowing does.
- **Are the sources reliable?** Marcuse is read in full since version 2, from an OCR copy; Han through one chapter and one essay, Benjamin through one chapter.
- **Verdict.** Proposed. The claim is narrowed at version 2 to a test for domination by an arrangement's controllers, and the four readings now bound it.

## Revision of 2026-11-13: Marcuse read in full

Version 1 grounded step 1 in Farr's reading ([THEORY-tmpnydju](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpnydju.md)) and listed
Marcuse among accounts of constraint that no one holds. The book, read
in full after the owner's ruling of 2026-11-13 ([NOTE-tmpf1tpw](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpf1tpw.md)), corrects
that in three ways.

- **Attribution.** "all domination assumes the form of administration" is
  from Eros and Civilization, not this book; its own forms are "Domination
  is transfigured into administration" (ch. 2) and "At its most advanced
  stage, domination functions as administration" (ch. 10). "domination no
  longer requires force or the presence of an authority figure" is
  Farr's wording; the book speaks of the absence of terror ("Technology
  rather than Terror"), not of an authority figure.
- **Controllers.** The "objective order of things" is "itself the result of
  domination" (ch. 6); the independence of the whole is "a spurious one
  inasmuch as it is that of particular powers" (ch. 8); and the project
  for the whole is chosen "primarily" by those who control production.
  So Marcuse does not describe constraint that no one holds.
- **What he adds.** Control is exercised through the choice of a
  society's project, which sets "the range of possibilities open" for
  everyone, not through discretion over particular dependents; it is
  veiled ("the technological veil"); and the controllers are caught in it
  ("a vicious circle which encloses both the Master and the Servant").

Marcuse still bounds [CLAIM-tmpkjv7u](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpkjv7u.md), and the relation stays `bounds`. The
claim's second condition is discretion over the dependent, and its third
is the dependent's means of protection or challenge. Discretion exercised
through a project, veiled, by controllers enclosed in it, meets neither
as stated: there is no particular discretion over this dependent to
protect against, and no visible holder to challenge. Bringing it inside
the test would mean widening "discretion" to the choice of a project,
the reply this argument's second critical question already says changes
the claim more than narrowing does. What changes is the reason Marcuse
lies outside: not that no one holds the constraint, but that those who
hold it do so through the project, out of sight. The claim's own text,
which names "impersonal constraint that no one holds", fits Han and
Benjamin; for Marcuse a dated note on the claim restates it.
