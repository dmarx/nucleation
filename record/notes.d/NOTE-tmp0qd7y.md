# NOTE-tmp0qd7y: Aldé — Two geometrical arguments against similarity structuralism

## Contribution

Earlier objections to phenomenal structuralism appeal to intuitions about
intrinsic character that structuralists reject. This paper plays "the
structuralist game through and through" (abstract). It takes structural
facts about colour that colour science accepts, and argues that the
similarity geometry the structuralist must explain them with does not
contain them. The claim is that structuralism cannot account for them, not
only that it has not yet.

## Key insight

A similarity space records how far apart colours are. It does not record
that orange looks like red and yellow together while red looks like
nothing else, nor that grey lies between black and white as a mixture while
it lies between red and green as neither. Those are facts of composition,
not of distance. Strip the colours and axes from CIELAB and the colourless
solid "encodes only its own shape" (p. 11). If the shape does not mark the
unique hues, structuralism has nothing to explain them with.

## Assumptions

- **Similarity structuralism** is the target: "The phenomenal character of
  a mental quality is fully determined by its similarity relations with the
  other phenomenal characters in the similarity space" (p. 4). Rosenthal's
  discrimination-based quality-space theory is set aside, though the
  arguments "could be adaptable to it" (pp. 4–5). Tsuchiya allows other
  relations in principle but works with similarity in practice (fn. 2).
- **What structuralism implies** (p. 5): a system cannot experience only
  red; losing one character changes others; similarity spaces "must be
  asymmetric", since symmetric points are "structurally indistinguishable
  ... they should feel the same, hence they should be the same point"; and
  a subject's several similarity spaces must all differ in geometry.
  Characters have "neither microstructure ... nor any intrinsic nature".
- **The colour composition claims** (p. 7). 1.1: exactly four hues (red,
  green, blue, yellow) are experienced as pure, and all others as
  combinations of them. 1.2: exactly six colours (the four saturated unique
  hues, white, black) are pure. 2.1: composite hues are weighted
  combinations of two unique hues, and red with green, blue with yellow,
  are "prohibited". 2.2: composite colours combine one or two saturated
  unique hues with white and/or black.
- **CIELAB is a good approximation.** Euclidean distance in CIELAB is
  imprecise for small differences, and CIEDE2000, CAM16-UCS and Oklab are
  better, but CIELAB is close enough to make it "improbable that
  structuralism will find in the other models what it does not already
  find in Euclidean CIELAB" (p. 12).
- **Colour as the test case.** The paper is silent on other modalities,
  but argues that if colour needs a non-structuralist account, others
  likely do too (pp. 2–3).

## Key results

- **The evidence for the composition claims (§3, pp. 7–10).** Leonardo's six
  simple colours; Hering's opponent theory; hue-naming studies in which
  naïve subjects describe orange as red and yellow but cannot describe the
  region around 580 nm without "yellow"; Clark's observation that unique
  colours match every colour in mixture and cannot themselves be matched by
  others. Some cross-cultural and infant evidence is cited against a purely
  linguistic origin. The opponent-process model accounts for the claims,
  though its neural basis for unique hues is "elusive" (p. 9).
- **Against the critics of unique hues (pp. 9–10).** Wright's appeal to
  Bosten & Boehm 2014 (hue scaling with only non-unique hues) is said not
  to generalize, since that design forced reliance on similarity. Conway et
  al.'s Tsimane' study is said to have had 9 of 40 participants misread the
  task. Arstila's citation of Jameson is said to cite an assertion, not an
  argument. "On the whole, the case against unique hues is weak" (p. 10).
- **The unique hues objection (§4.1, pp. 11–13).** (1) Structuralism must
  explain character by the geometry of the similarity space. (2) Exactly
  four hues are experienced as distinct from all others. (3) No geometric
  property of the space distinguishes unique from composite hues. (4)
  Structuralism is wrong. On premise 3: CIELAB shows no such property;
  better metrics are unlikely to; and the colours missing from current
  models (chimerical colours, psychedelic colours, and "olo", the
  saturated teal produced by stimulating M cones alone, Fong et al. 2025)
  would add mainly saturated teals and light blues (Figure 3), which "does
  not seem enough to do the job" (p. 13). Even a distinctive location would
  not explain why composite hues "seem to be composed of unique hues, while
  the converse does not hold" (p. 13).
- **The opposing dimensions objection (§4.2, pp. 13–15).** The black–white
  axis "exhibits the same gradual change from black to white, whereas the
  perpendicular dimensions are opposing dimensions" (p. 14): along red–cyan
  one goes from red to grey and then a different way from grey to cyan.
  Current models are asymmetric, so every axis is geometrically unique,
  but "only the black-white axis is so peculiar in its phenomenology, and
  it is not equally peculiar in its geometrical uniqueness" (p. 15). This
  argument does not need the composition claims, since the difference is
  "an immediately evident qualitative fact" (p. 15).
- **Introspection (§5, pp. 15–18).** Structuralism is built on introspected
  similarity, so it cannot dismiss introspection wholesale. Judgements
  that a hue contains other hues are component analyses within
  introspection's operating range, like noticing citrus notes in a perfume,
  unlike the judgement that experience has an intrinsic character. The
  structuralist reply that orange "seems to contain red and yellow because
  it is similar to them" (p. 17) is called incomplete, since it does not
  explain why we describe hues as "pure" and "mixture" at all. This is
  offered as a third, less decisive argument.
- **Conclusion (§6, p. 18).** "the structure of our color experience is
  incompatible with structuralism". The suggested alternative is to ask how
  far neuroscientific models such as opponent-process theory "might inform
  a metaphysics of mental qualities".

## Concepts

- **Similarity structuralism**: character fully fixed by similarity
  relations, hence by location in, and geometry of, a similarity space.
- **Similarity space**: the structure of a subject's phenomenal characters
  in one modality and their similarities, often a geometric solid in which
  distance is proportional to dissimilarity. Called a quality space by
  others.
- **Unique hues and pure colours**: the four hues, and six colours,
  experienced as unmixed.
- **Opposing dimensions**: axes through grey along which each half shows
  only one of its extremes.
- **Natural Color System**: a system expressing colours as combinations of
  pure colours; not a similarity space, because it is not perceptually
  uniform (Figure 2).

## Connections

The paper takes its definition of structuralism from Clark (1993, 2000)
and names Lyre 2022 ([LIT-tmp3sgbg](../literature.d/LIT-tmp3sgbg.md), [NOTE-tmpg26n5](NOTE-tmpg26n5.md)), Fink et al., Fleming &
Shea, Malach, Lau and Tsuchiya's group as defenders. It sets itself apart
from Negro 2025 ([LIT-tmp86j0r](../literature.d/LIT-tmp86j0r.md), [NOTE-tmp0dy5a](NOTE-tmp0dy5a.md)) and Chalmers 1996, whose critiques
rest on intuition; Negro is thanked in the acknowledgements. Its colour
science runs through Hering, Hardin, Matthen and Cohen. Two internal
slips: the introduction says introspection is treated in "Section 6",
which is §5, and premise 2 of §4.2 is said to draw "from structural fact
3", where the composition claims number only two.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-tmpdga80](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpdga80.md) (constitution by interpretive organization).** The claim
  already records that Aldé bears on it only if its relations are
  similarities, and that A7 lists causal, counterfactual, temporal and
  evaluative ones. The reading confirms the scope: Aldé's arguments are
  against similarity geometry alone, and he says Rosenthal's discrimination
  view would need its own version. But it also gives the claim something
  to meet. The composition facts are facts about how one colour
  experience contains others. If interpretive organization fixes
  character, it must yield unique hues and the opponent structure. Aldé's
  own pointer, to the opponent-process mechanism, is a causal organization,
  so the claim's relational vocabulary may have the resources that a
  similarity space lacks. That is a reading, not something the paper says.
- **[TERM-tmpgqmop](../terms.d/TERM-tmpgqmop.md).** Aldé's "component analysis" in introspection, the
  judgement that a colour contains red and yellow, is an interpretation in
  the term's sense, a distinction with context and consequence. Whether it
  constitutes the composite character or reports it is the claim's
  question in miniature.
- **[CLAIM-136](../claims.d/CLAIM-136.md) (relata without primitive identity).** By analogy only. Aldé
  states that a structuralist must make similarity spaces asymmetric,
  because two symmetric points "should feel the same, hence they should be
  the same point" (p. 5). That is the strict version of [CLAIM-136](../claims.d/CLAIM-136.md) applied
  to phenomenal characters: no difference without a relational difference,
  and so no plurality either. The weak version, which allows two
  structurally indistinguishable positions to be two, is the move a
  phenomenal structuralist would need if colour space had a symmetry, and
  Aldé does not consider it. His arguments are about character, not
  identity, and they do not reach communicative acts.
- **[THEORY-095](../theory.d/THEORY-095.md) (Inverted Earth).** Aldé cites Chalmers's
  "structure-preserving spectrum inversion" as the intuition-based objection he
  means to improve on. His unique-hue facts would also block simple
  inversion, since swapping unique and composite hues would be noticed. He
  does not draw that consequence.
- **THEORY warranted.** [THEORY-tmp4u3l2](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp4u3l2.md) states the paper's thesis. It is a
  second objection to [THEORY-tmpv5rmc](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpv5rmc.md), with a different footing from
  [THEORY-tmp7uasf](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp7uasf.md)'s.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- Premise 3 of the unique hues objection is argued by inspection of
  CIELAB and by extrapolation. No geometric analysis of a uniform colour
  space is reported, though the paper says one "could" be carried out
  (p. 12).
- "Adequately" in the opposing dimensions objection carries the weight.
  The paper grants that the black–white axis is geometrically unique and
  argues only that its uniqueness is "unremarkable".
- The case for unique hues answers the critics' studies and does not add
  data. The paper itself notes that unique-hue loci vary widely across
  subjects and are not opposite in colour space (p. 9), which the
  structuralist could also use.
- The target is similarity structuralism. Structuralisms that use other
  relations (discrimination, causal or computational relations, or the
  internal structure Negro distinguishes) are not shown to fail.
- Colour only.

## Open questions

- Run the analysis the paper calls possible: search CIEDE2000, CAM16-UCS or
  Oklab for any invariant (curvature, a singularity, a symmetry axis) that
  singles out the unique-hue loci, and check it against each observer's
  unique-hue settings.
- Can a structuralism whose relations include composition or containment,
  not only similarity, account for the composition claims? If so, is it
  still structuralism in Lyre's sense?
- Does the opponent-process mechanism's organization fix the composition
  facts, as Aldé suggests? That would move the explanans from a similarity
  space to a causal structure.
