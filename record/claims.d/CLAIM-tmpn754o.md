# CLAIM-tmpn754o: Beside heuristics that select what to do there are governance heuristics, learned priors over which perspective or procedure determines what to do; intuitive expertise and entrenched bias can then be one learned allocation of authority, efficient where the conditions it was learned under still hold and a source of systematic error where they no longer do

## The claim

A12 §9, from CLAIM-443's prior P(g | z, H) ∝ exp(βQ(g, z) + b_H(g, z)):
"Repeated experience can establish which processes ordinarily receive
authority in particular circumstances. This suggests a distinction between
two forms of heuristic learning." An action heuristic settles "What to do";
a governance heuristic "Which perspective or procedure should determine what
to do". "For example, an experienced practitioner may not have memorized an
answer to every possible situation. Instead, they may have learned when
immediate intuition should govern, when it should yield to explicit
analysis and when uncertainty warrants consultation. What becomes learned is
not just a response but an allocation of authority." And: "This potentially
explains both intuitive expertise and entrenched cognitive bias through a
common organizational achievement: the acquisition of durable,
context-sensitive governance dispositions."

A13 §VI adds the history: "However, the learned allocation may continue to
operate when the conditions that originally justified it no longer obtain.
This gives cognitive bias a historical dimension. A bias may be the
consequence of an organizational constraint that was previously useful,
learned under different conditions, socially inherited, or locally
reinforced despite its consequences elsewhere." It limits the claim: "This
does not imply that all heuristics arise through such learning."

A11 §9 names the conditions: Kahneman and Klein identify "environmental
regularity and opportunities for valid feedback as crucial to the
development of reliable intuition" (LIT-tmpyshsf, unread).

## Where the record stands

CLAIM-443 (Active) is the mechanism, "a candidate mechanism for durable
authority and for amortized governance", with its limit, which A12 repeats:
"persistence alone does not demonstrate amortization". What this claim adds
is the reading of that prior as a kind of heuristic, distinct from one over
actions, and the thesis that expertise and bias are the same learned
allocation evaluated in and out of its conditions. CLAIM-283 separates the
precedence a process claims from the authority it is granted; a governance
heuristic is learned on the grant side.

## What it does not say

That every heuristic, or every bias, is a governance heuristic; or that
learned authority is ever amortization without a showing that it replaces
later work.

## Note of 2026-11-24: the prediction that tells it from an action heuristic

The assistant's gap fill (ADR-137): A12 and A13 propose the distinction and
give no test that separates it. One follows from the claim. Take a context
in which several tasks share a cue and intuition was reliable in all of
them. Change the cue–outcome regularity in some of the tasks, so that the
intuitive answer is now wrong there while explicit analysis is still
available and right, and give clear feedback in one of them. An action
heuristic is corrected task by task: errors fall in the task that got
feedback, and nothing changes in the others. A governance heuristic is an
allocation learned on the context, so two things follow: before feedback,
fast intuitive answers persist in the changed tasks, and after it, the
shift toward deliberation generalizes across the tasks that share the cue,
including those where intuition is still right. Response time and markers
of deliberation measure the allocation apart from the choice, which is what
keeps the term from naming every learned response (the claim's
`defeated_if`). The wording is the
assistant's (Claude, in this session); adopting it is the owner's call.
