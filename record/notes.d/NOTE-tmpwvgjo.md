# NOTE-tmpwvgjo: Hindriks & Guala — Institutions, rules, and equilibria

## Contribution

The paper sets three accounts of institutions side by side: institutions
as rules (North, Weber, Hodgson), as equilibria (Lewis, Schotter, Sugden,
Binmore), and as systems of constitutive rules (Searle). It argues that
the first two are each inadequate and jointly adequate. That combination
is credited to Aoki and to Greif & Kingston. The paper's own step is the
second half: Searle's constitutive rules are shown to be regulative rules
re-expressed with a new term. If so, all three accounts are one theory.
The idea is credited to Hindriks's 2005 dissertation (n. 11).

## Key insight

A constitutive rule is what a regulative rule looks like after you name
the status it confers. "If you occupy land first, you may use it
exclusively" becomes "first occupancy makes land your property*" plus
"property* means exclusive use". The name lets one word carry a bundle of
rules, which is useful. It creates nothing that the rule, held in
equilibrium, did not already settle.

## Assumptions

- **Players and games.** One-shot games with rematching each round (n. 7);
  the mixed-strategy equilibrium is set aside. Institutions are confined
  to *coordination* games with several equilibria, in which strategies
  must be *correlated* on a signal.
- **Representation.** A third condition: players represent the
  equilibrium in symbolic form, as rules. It is introduced to exclude
  animal conventions (§4) and is a stipulation about what "institution"
  should cover; the reply calls the human/animal line a working
  hypothesis, not a requirement.
- **Collective acceptance** is read as "whatever set of intentional states
  is needed for institutions". Ordinary preferences, expectations and
  common knowledge may qualify (§5 and n. 14). The paper is neutral on
  whether collective intentions reduce to individual ones.
- **"Counts as"** is interpreted by two biconditionals: X counts as Y iff
  X is collectively accepted as Y, and X is collectively accepted as Y iff
  X is Y (§5).
- **Definitions** must meet Belnap's (1993) criteria, eliminability and
  conservativeness, for the reduction to go through (§6).

## Key results

- **Against pure rules (§2).** A rule that is known and not followed (a
  1799 French ban on women wearing trousers; a 65 mph limit in a 75 mph
  equilibrium) shows that stating a rule does not make an institution.
  What does is an incentive structure that keeps behaviour where it is.
- **Against pure equilibria (§§3–4).**
  - Not every equilibrium is an institution. Mutual defection in a
    prisoner's dilemma needs no coordination. Hare–hare in a stag hunt
    needs no correlation. Both are excluded.
  - Property* is modelled as the correlated equilibrium of hawk-dove
    (Fig. 1: (U,U) = (0,0), mixed cells (2,1) and (1,2), (NU,NU) = (1,1))
    with first occupancy as the correlation device. Expected payoff is
    1.5 each when occupancy is equally likely.
  - *Pararge aegeria* butterflies also play a "bourgeois" correlated
    equilibrium. They have no institution, because they cannot condition
    on new signals or invent new equilibria. Representation is the
    difference.
- **The transformation (§5).**
  - [R] If one is the first to occupy a piece of land, one has the right
    to its exclusive use.
  - [B] If a person first occupies a piece of land, then it is her
    property*.
  - [S] If a piece of land is someone's property*, she has the right to
    use it.
  - [C] = [B] and [S], with the form "if C then X is Y, and if Y then Z".
    This is the *XYZ schema*, the "transformation view".
- **Consistency (§6).** The Y-term is conservative (nothing new follows
  that lacks it) and eliminable (reverse [C] → [B],[S] → [R]). Behaviour
  under [R] is extensionally equivalent to behaviour under [B] and [S].
  So the constitutive-rules theory is "reducible to" the
  rules-in-equilibrium theory.
- **Four virtues (§6).** Two linguistic: institutional terms are what
  ordinary talk uses, and they economise cognition. Two ontological:
  multiple realisability corresponds to multiple equilibria, and the
  theory is parsimonious.
- **Against Searle's necessity claim (§6).** If constitutive rules were
  necessary for institutions, practices of etiquette, and in the reply
  driving on the right, would not be institutions. The authors see no
  ground for denying that they are.
- **Reference (§6).** On Lewis's account of theoretical terms, "if the
  theory that introduced it is true it does name something". So if the
  constitutive rules are correctly transformed from true regulative
  rules, "property" names something and the institution exists.
- **Reply (JIE 11(3):515–522).**
  - Common knowledge is not needed for coordination. Correlated
    equilibrium was chosen to highlight the variety of correlation
    devices, not to require Aumann-style epistemics.
  - Normativity does not require constitutive rules. "No one is allowed
    to use my garden" is a regulative rule with normative force. Rights
    are other people's obligations, so "property" names a bundle of
    regulative rules.
  - Searle's "desire-independent reasons" is a dilemma for him. On the
    weak reading it is a platitude compatible with equilibrium accounts.
    On the strong reading it is an unargued anti-Humean thesis.
  - A theory of institutions should stay neutral among theories of
    normativity. Lewis, Bicchieri and Sugden (expectations and
    resentment), Gilbert (joint commitment), and emotion and reasons
    accounts are listed.

## Concepts

- **Rules-in-equilibrium.** An institution: a correlated equilibrium of a
  coordination game, whose strategies the agents represent as rules.
- **a-rule / o-rule.** An agent-rule summarises and guides one's own
  behaviour; an observer-rule summarises others'. Equilibrium theories are
  observer theories (§4).
- **Base rule / status rule.** A base rule says what it takes to have a
  status (X is Y); a status rule says what having it means (if Y then Z).
- **Transformation view.** Constitutive rules are regulative rules
  transformed by introducing a Y-term (after Hindriks 2005, 2009).
- **Property\*.** The toy proto-institution: exclusive use by the first
  occupant, as a correlated equilibrium of hawk-dove.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md): indirect, and neutral.** The account is about
  institutions, not groups, and it is deliberately neutral about
  collective intentionality (n. 14). It neither needs nor excludes a
  group-level subject. Its one point of contact is methodological: it
  treats an institution as real when a true theory with that term
  explains behaviour. A group-mind claim would need a further argument
  that the group is the bearer of the states, which is exactly what
  [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) says no one has supplied for experience.
- **Group agency** ([LIT-391](../literature.d/LIT-391.md), [LIT-401](../literature.d/LIT-401.md)). List & Pettit's group agents are
  organised collectives; this paper's institutions are equilibrium
  patterns that need no collective agent. The two are compatible and do
  not address each other.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice appears, so nothing here
  points to the Anthology of the SOTA.

## Limitations

- **The reduction is shown once.** C4 is a worked example. C5 claims every
  constitutive rule arises this way, but no case is given where a status
  is created with no regulative rule behind it. Searle's chess appears
  only as his example of a constitutive rule, in the argument that
  etiquette is an institution too.
- **Collective acceptance is made easy to satisfy.** Defining it as
  "whatever set of intentional states is needed for institutions" lets
  the "counts as" biconditionals hold by construction. The reduction then
  inherits that looseness.
- **Equivalence is extensional.** [R] and [B]+[S] are shown to prescribe
  the same behaviour. Whether they confer the same rights is the point
  Searle presses; the paper answers it only in the reply, and only by
  example.
- **No normativity in the paper.** The authors concede this in the reply
  and point to a companion paper (Guala & Hindriks 2014, Philosophical
  Quarterly), not read.
- **Representation is not analysed.** What it takes for agents to
  "represent the equilibrium in symbolic form" is left at Sterelny's
  decoupling of stimulus and response.

## Open questions

- Is there a constitutive rule with no regulative pre-image, such as a
  rule creating a status whose only content is further statuses? If so,
  C5 fails for it.
- Does the transformation preserve deontic content, or only behaviour?
  The reply's bundle-of-obligations claim (C9) is the answer offered, and
  it needs a case where the bundle is large or open-ended.
- Where exactly does the representation condition draw the line between
  human and animal conventions, and can it be tested? The reply proposes
  intervention speed as one test.
