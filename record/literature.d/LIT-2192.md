  quantitative-genetic model. Hatchery fish are generally less fit in the

  relative fitness 0.02–0.37); stocks from local wild broodstock "generally
  perform better than nonlocal stocks, but often worse than wild fish". For
  the authors' Hood River steelhead the decline is fast, inherited and
  cumulative from the first to the second hatchery generation. Of relaxed
  selection, inbreeding and domestication selection, the last is judged
  "The last and most likely explanation", and the model finds that it can
  give a decline of more than 30% per generation on one trait only under

  several traits. Using local wild broodstock mitigates the loss "to some
  extent", and no rise in wild productivity from hatcheries is documented.

# LIT-2192: Fitness of hatchery-reared salmonids in the wild

Araki, H., Berejikian, B. A., Ford, M. J. & Blouin, M. S. (2008). Fitness of hatchery-reared salmonids in the wild. *Evolutionary Applications* 1(2): 342–355. doi:10.1111/j.1752-4571.2008.00026.x.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-1668](../notes.d/NOTE-1668.md). Sections are the paper's.*

- **Fast loss.** Fitness decline "can occur very quickly, sometimes
  following only one or two generations of captive rearing" (Abstract).
- **Local broodstock helps, not enough.** Loss "mitigated to some extent
  by using local, wild fish for broodstock, but we found little evidence to
  suggest that it can be avoided altogether" (Conclusion).
- **Hood River, genetic and cumulative.** Table 1: one hatchery generation
  "(m) 0.70 (f) 0.88" of wild fish; two "(m) 0.32 (f) 0.30"; the second
  against the first "(m) 0.55 (f) 0.55", the contrast in which rearing is
  held equal.
- **Domestication selection over disuse.** Hatchery egg-to-smolt survival
  is "85–95% in hatcheries versus 1–5% in the wild", but relaxed selection
  "seems an unlikely explanation for dramatic declines in fitness unless
  salmon carry an extraordinary standing genetic load" (Mechanisms).
- **The loss can hide itself.** Where hatchery fish had long dominated the
  spawning grounds, the 'wild' comparators were largely hatchery-descended,
  and old local stocks looked as fit as wild ones.
