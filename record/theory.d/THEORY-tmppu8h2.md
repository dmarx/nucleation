# THEORY-tmppu8h2: In hierarchically organized systems, levels are demarcated by sharp discontinuities in characteristic process and relaxation rates, the embedding level being slower than its components, and a level controls another by constraint only while their rates stay separated: when the cycling rates converge, the control asymmetry and the distinction between the levels are lost, whatever the signal amplitude

## Source

Juarrero (2023), LIT-1995, pp. 68, 72, 77 and 183–191; read in NOTE-1532.
The book's own wording of the thesis is reported, Active, in THEORY-1306.

## Where the source asserts it

- "Boundaries between levels of organization and constraint are therefore
  typically marked by sharp discontinuities in order parameters and average
  process and relaxation rates" (p. 183).
- The asymmetry "can be destroyed by either excessively accelerating the
  dynamics of an embedding environment or slowing down the rates of the
  focal-level system beyond its tolerance range" (p. 189, after Allen,
  O'Neill and Hoekstra 1984).
- "Lack of asymmetry in cycling rates between focal level and the context
  in which it is embedded, or between the focal level and its components,
  reflects the absence of control constraint by one on the other,
  regardless of signal amplitude. It also reflects an absence of
  qualitative differences between the two levels of organization" (p. 190).

## What was actually shown


Nothing is derived; the chapter states and illustrates.

**Rate demarcation.** "Boundaries between levels of organization and
constraint are therefore typically marked by sharp discontinuities in order
parameters and average process and relaxation rates", and "overarching
hierarchical levels of organization are usually slower than their
components or realizations. The smaller the 'average rate constants' that
define a set of interdependencies (Salthe 1985, 72), the higher its
organizational level" (p. 183). Earlier: entrained pendulums trace a pattern
"longer and slower than the swings of the individual pendulums" (p. 68), and
rate differences between predator–prey cycles and lifespans "constitute the
boundaries between parts and wholes" (p. 77).

**Why the relation is constraint.** Quoting Salthe: processes at one level
cannot interact energetically with another "because [faster] lower-level
dynamics would have been completed before feedback from the [slower] upper
level arrived" (p. 187). Whole-to-part relations are "rate independent":
governing constraints "inform and influence" components "without
participating in them dynamically" (p. 188).

**Loss on convergence.** The asymmetry "can be destroyed by either
excessively accelerating the dynamics of an embedding environment or
slowing down the rates of the focal-level system beyond its tolerance
range" (p. 189, after Allen, O'Neill and Hoekstra 1984). "Lack of asymmetry
in cycling rates between focal level and the context in which it is
embedded, or between the focal level and its components, reflects the
absence of control constraint by one on the other, regardless of signal
amplitude" (p. 190). A component sped up becomes "invisible" to its
embedding context, the tumour being the example (p. 190).

**Inside a level, coupling is fast.** A coherent dynamic's interactions have
"feedback loops and relaxation times shorter, faster, and more regular" than
the environment's (p. 72).

What could have come out otherwise: a case in which a whole controls parts
that run at its own rate, or a slower context that exerts no control. The
chapter offers none of either and tests neither.

## What this does not say

- **That rate separation suffices for control.** The book has control
  "muted or lost" by an enabling constraint with no change of rates
  (p. 188).
- **That slowness makes a constraint.** That mechanism is CLAIM-478's, from
  LIT-1292 and LIT-582, not this book's.
- **Anything quantitative.** No ratio, threshold or measure is given.
