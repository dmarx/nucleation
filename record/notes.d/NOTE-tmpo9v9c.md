# NOTE-tmpo9v9c: Nicholson — The organism as a system (Weiss)

## Contribution

A historical and philosophical paper. It gathers Paul Weiss's scattered
remarks on "system", written mostly between 1962 and 1978, into one
concept, and argues that this concept diagnoses four doctrines of modern
biology: reductionism, genocentrism, determinism and mechanicism. Its
claim to novelty is synthesis, not discovery: "my aim is to bring together
his philosophical ideas about systems in a clear, digestible way so that
they can be put to effective use in ongoing biological discussions" (§1).
It adds a thesis of its own in §7: the four doctrines' shortcomings are
"mutually reinforcing consequences of a deeply entrenched habit of
thought: the tendency to atomize reality and to treat wholes (or systems)
as derivative composites, rather than as primary, integrated units".

## Key insight

For Weiss a system is defined by invariance of the whole against
variation of the parts, and every other property Nicholson credits to it
follows from that. The definitions he quotes all say so. In 1925 a system
is "any complex which tends to retain its constancy as a whole relative to
the outer world during and not withstanding [sic] the changes taking place
in its parts" (§3). In 1971 it is "a complex unit in space and time so
constituted that its component subunits, by ‘systematic’ cooperation,
preserve its integral configuration of structure and behavior and tend to
restore it after non-destructive disturbances" (§3). In 1973 it "retains a
relatively stationary pattern of structure in space or of sequential
configuration in time in spite of a high degree of variability in the
details of distribution and interrelations among its constituent units of
lower order" (§5). The mutual generation of parts, the triplet example,
which is what brought the paper into this record, is not among Weiss's
definitions. Nicholson introduces it in §4 as the answer to "How does a
system come into being?"

## Assumptions

- **Non-formal by design.** The concept is to be "intuitive,
  non-mathematical", articulated "philosophically, as opposed to
  mathematically" (§1). Its one formal element is Weiss's inequality
  VS ≪ ∑ (va + vb + … + vn) (§5).
- **"System" is an empirical concept.** Weiss, for one, "treats the system
  as an empirical concept … it designates entities that we discover in nature"
  (§6), and the account is "derived empirically from experimental work"
  (§7). No experiment is reported in the paper, and the derivation is not
  shown.
- **The doctrines are characterized by their strongest statements.**
  Reductionism is "an explanatory strategy which prescribes that a whole
  can be fully understood by analytically decomposing it and then
  progressively piecing together all that can be learned from a study of
  its elements and their interactions" (§3), with Jacob and Watson as
  witnesses. Genocentrism is "the belief that genes are central to all
  organismic processes" (§4). Determinism is development as "an
  unfolding of pre-existing capabilities, an acting-out of genetically
  encoded instructions" (Medawar, §5). Mechanicism is "the machine
  conception of the organism" (§6).
- **Weiss is read through Nicholson.** Every Weiss passage is quoted, and
  the reconstruction is Nicholson's: Weiss "never formally presented a
  comprehensive systems theory of the organism" (§1).

## Key results

### What the system concept claims (§§3–6)

Nicholson attributes to systems, in order of appearance:

1. **Interdependence of parts.** "In a system, no part is truly independent
   of all the others (Weiss 1939: 111)" (§3), against an aggregate, whose
   parts can be changed or removed "without altering the condition of the
   others".
2. **The whole partly determines the parts.** "in a system, the character
   of the parts is at least partially determined by the character of the
   whole" (§3). Weiss 1969a: "[i]n the system, the structure of the whole
   determines the operation of the parts; in the machine, the operation of
   the parts determines the outcome" (§6).
3. **The "more" is epistemic.** "The ‘more’ does not refer to any
   ontological, measurable quantity in the system. Instead … it refers to
   the epistemic necessity to supplement the knowledge obtained from the
   study of the parts by the knowledge required to appropriately describe
   the collective behaviour of the parts when these are considered as a
   coordinated unit" (§3).
4. **Self-regulation.** The 1925, 1939 and 1971 definitions above.
5. **Mutual generation, and so no stepwise assembly** (§4): "in a system
   the network of interactions between the parts is causally responsible
   for the generation of each part". The triplet: "a triplet of
   interdependent processes—A, B, and C—each of which provides some of the
   conditions required for the production of the two others. This triplet
   cannot be assembled by simply adding A + B + C in a stepwise manner,
   given that no two members of the set—(A + B) or (B + C) or (C + A)—can
   exist or be maintained without the presence and contributions of the
   third member". Cited to Weiss 1969a: 17–18 and 1973: 76. Nicholson
   names the modern term: "a form of closure (Varela 1979; Rosen 1991;
   Kauffman 2000; Moreno and Mossio 2015)", and n. 18 separates Varela's
   organizational closure, Rosen's closure to efficient causation,
   Kauffman's catalytic closure and Moreno and Mossio's closure of
   constraints. The arch (Fig. 3) gives the corollary: such a structure is
   built over scaffolding later removed, and present cells arise only from
   cells, "omnis organisatio ex organisatione. Only systems beget systems"
   (§4).
6. **Constraint from above, freedom below.** "the component activities
   have many degrees of freedom, but submit to the ordering restraints
   exerted upon them by the integral activity of the ‘whole’" (Weiss 1969a:
   9, §5). Genes supply "‘boundary conditions’ that shape its developmental
   trajectory" (Weiss 1965, §5), and the cell's bounds are "‘boundary
   conditions’ set by the dynamics of the system as a whole" (Weiss 1969a:
   21, §6).
7. **The variance inequality.** For parts a, …, n of S, with va the total
   variance of a's parameters and VS the whole's: "What we would invariably
   find, if we studied any system in this way, is that the sum of the
   individual variances of the parts is far greater than the total variance
   of the whole" (§5). "This is the only mathematical expression we find in
   Weiss's theoretical writings about systems", used "sometimes as a way of
   illustrating how one might go about empirically identifying systems in
   nature" (§5). It is claimed for every level of the hierarchy (§5).
8. **Hierarchy and turnover.** Nested levels (Fig. 4), each more invariant
   than the one below; parts replaced continually by metabolic or
   generational turnover (§§5–6).
9. **Mechanisms as consolidated systems.** Systems show "a tendency
   toward progressive consolidation (Weiss 1970a: 118–127)": "Evolution
   tends to ‘freeze’ as much free systemic activity as possible into fixed
   sequences of reactions (i.e., mechanisms)" (§6).

### The four critiques, and what each rests on

| doctrine | the paper's verdict (§7) | what carries it |
|---|---|---|
| Reductionism (§3) | "analytical decomposition necessarily eliminates information about the organization of the whole that cannot be recovered from an inventory of the parts" | **Argument and illustration.** The chick-embryo homogenate (Fig. 2) and the telephone-directory and museum analogies show that a list of components omits organization. That is uncontested, and it is not the doctrine as §3 defines it, which includes the parts' "interactions". The §3 resolution, that the "more" is the knowledge needed to describe the parts' collective behaviour, is one a reductionist who counts organization among the interactions can accept. The stronger claim, that the order "could never" be retrieved "from our knowledge of the parts alone" (Weiss 1977, §6), is asserted, not argued. |
| Genocentrism (§4) | genes are "indispensable participants in development and heredity, but not autonomous sources of organismic order" | **Mostly argument about causal inference, with uncontested facts.** The logical core: a knockout shows "necessary participation, never exclusive responsibility" (§4); "gene for" talk tracks differences, not formation (the eye example); apportioning a trait between genes and environment is like crediting a gas's volume to temperature and pressure. The facts are standard: DNA is chemically inert and is replicated and expressed by existing machinery; Venter's synthetic genome needed a host cell (§4). "Molecular animism" is a diagnosis of language. The origin-of-life scaffolding is a supposition: "it seems reasonable to suppose" (§4). |
| Determinism (§5) | "macroscopic regularity need not reflect rigid microscopic fixity" | **The only critique with cited evidence.** Weiss's observation, redrawn as Fig. 5, that regional topology is conserved between stages while cell trajectories differ "even between closely related embryos developing under the same conditions"; later work on stochastic gene expression and cell-fate decisions (Elowitz et al. 2002; Raj and van Oudenaarden 2008; Losick and Desplan 2008; Balázsi et al. 2011). The paper shows none of the data: Fig. 5 is a schematic. The analogy with statistical thermodynamics is argument. The counterexample is conceded in n. 30: *C. elegans*, whose 671 embryonic cells can be tracked individually, with the reply that this is "the exception rather than the rule". |
| Mechanicism (§6) | organisms are "self-maintaining, processual wholes whose parts are continually produced, replaced, and coordinated by the systems to which they belong" | **Argument by contrast, with the facts of turnover.** Machines are assembled from pre-existing parts, are explained by reversing assembly, and keep the same material parts; organisms develop from "an originally undifferentiated yet already integrated system" and turn their material over (§6). Turnover and the dynamic cytoskeleton (Weiss 1947; Dumont and Prakash 2014, n. 43) are evidence for the second half. The account of mechanisms as frozen systems dynamics is a hypothesis given no evidence. The claim that mechanicism "provides an ontological basis for the other three" (§6) is conceptual. |

The paper does not itself mark which critiques are argued and which are
evidenced. It calls the concept empirical throughout (§§1, 6, 7).

### The variance inequality, checked

Weiss's inequality is stated loosely: for each part, "the cumulative record
of these deviations" over "a range of physical and chemical parameters" is
summed (§5), so the units of the two sides are not fixed. Read with one
scalar quantity per part, and with the whole's value either the sum or the
average of the parts' values, the variance identity
Var(∑xᵢ) = ∑ Var(xᵢ) + 2∑ᵢ<ⱼ Cov(xᵢ, xⱼ) settles what it can show.

- **Whole measured as an average:** VS = (1/n²)(∑v + 2∑Cov). For n
  independent parts VS = ∑v/n² ≪ ∑v. Any aggregate satisfies the
  inequality, so it cannot tell a system from an aggregate.
- **Whole measured as a sum:** for independent parts VS = ∑v, and the
  inequality fails. It holds only if ∑ᵢ<ⱼ Cov(xᵢ, xⱼ) ≈ −½∑v, that is if the parts'
  fluctuations compensate one another. That is a real mark of
  regulation, and it is what Weiss's words "order in the gross with freedom
  of excursion in the small" (§5) would need.

A numpy check (100 parts, 20,000 samples, seed 0): independent parts gave
VS = 0.010 against ∑v = 100 for the average, and VS = 101 against ∑v = 100
for the sum; parts constrained to a fixed total, plus a small common
fluctuation, gave VS ≈ 25 against ∑v ≈ 99 for the sum, with the identity
met to three decimals. So the
inequality is a criterion only for an extensive whole, where it says the
parts covary negatively. Neither Nicholson nor, as quoted, Weiss says this.
The §5 conclusion, "what makes the whole more than the sum of its parts is
that it varies less", inherits the ambiguity.

## Concepts

- **System** — Weiss's, in the definitions under Key insight; contrasted
  throughout with an aggregate and with a machine.
- **Aggregate** — a whole whose parts can be changed or removed "without
  altering the condition of the others", and whose character is fixed by
  theirs (§3).
- **Molecular ecology** — Weiss's picture of the cell as a community of
  molecular "species" in niches they partly construct, whose order is
  "only the general statistical norm of the[ir] proportions, arrangement,
  and distribution" (Weiss 1949, §5).
- **Macrodeterminacy / microdeterminacy** — predictability of the whole
  process against predictability of its component events. Weiss preferred
  "determinacy" to "determinism" as empirical terms (n. 29).
- **Molecular animism** — Nicholson's label for crediting molecules with
  ‘control’, ‘dictate’, ‘command’, ‘orchestrate’ of what the whole does (§4).
- **Progressive consolidation** — the hardening of repeatedly used
  systemic pathways into mechanisms (§6).
- **Mechanicism** — Nicholson's word for the machine conception of the
  organism, chosen to keep it apart from other senses of "mechanism"
  (n. 38).

## Connections

- **Closure.** Nicholson ties Weiss's triplet to closure (§4, n. 18) and
  cites Moreno and Mossio 2015 (LIT-536, Unfetched), Rosen 1991 (LIT-1669,
  Unavailable), Varela 1979 and Kauffman 2000, the last two not held. He
  places Weiss among the precursors of the "autonomy school" (§7).
- **Developmental systems theory** (Oyama 1985; Griffiths and Gray 1994;
  Oyama et al. 2001): Weiss's 1971 sentence that genes "do not ‘act’ as
  independent autonomous dictators, but … simply ‘interact’ as cooperative
  parts" is called "a remarkable anticipation" (§4). None is held here.
- **Waddington** (nn. 33, 36): the epigenetic landscape and homeorhesis.
- **Bertalanffy** (§2, nn. 9, 40, 41): the same city, the same opponent
  (the machine conception), and a different route, through general systems
  theory. Bertalanffy is quoted crediting Weiss with introducing the system
  concept in 1924.
- **Process ontology** (Dupré and Nicholson 2018, n. 45): §6 ends with
  Weiss's processualism.
- **Nicholson's own earlier critiques** of the machine conception (n. 39)
  and of genocentrism (Nicholson 2025, n. 17).

## Bearing on the record

- **TERM-070 and CLAIM-395: the triplet is a closure, and it is a
  closure of processes, not of constraints.** The exchange's description
  in LIT-tmptd6hn is right that the triplet is the record's closure
  pattern, and needs two qualifications. First, Weiss's members are
  "processes" that supply "conditions required for the production" of the
  others (§4), while Montévil and Mossio (LIT-582) separate constraints,
  which are conserved at the time scale of the process they act on, from
  the processes they constrain. The triplet becomes TERM-070's closure
  only if each "condition" is read as a constraint in that sense; the
  paper does not say so, and n. 18 itself warns that the authors who
  write of closure "interpret" it "in slightly different ways". Second,
  the triplet is stronger than TERM-070's Definition 4: each member
  supplies conditions for both others, and "no two members … can exist or
  be maintained without the presence and contributions of the third". In
  Montévil and Mossio's terms that is a closed set with no closed proper
  subset, a strict closure, and more (every member depends on every other).
  CLAIM-395's lemma that a closed set has at least two members is met with
  room to spare; its time-scale half has nothing to bite on, because
  Weiss gives no time scales.
- **The "Why this is here" of LIT-tmptd6hn needs one correction.** It says
  "Weiss's system is defined by the network of interactions generating
  each part". The definitions Nicholson quotes define a system by the
  invariance of the whole; the generation of parts is a further property,
  stated by Nicholson in §4 as the answer to how a system can "come into
  being". The pairwise point stands as the exchange gave it.
- **CLAIM-480: an interior that becomes a constraint, in the reverse
  direction.** CLAIM-480 eliminates an interior and finds a constraint on
  the boundary. Weiss's "‘boundary conditions’ set by the dynamics of the
  system as a whole" (§6) and the genome as "boundary conditions" (§5) are
  the same relation seen from the whole's side: the whole's dynamics
  constrain the parts. The triplet also illustrates CLAIM-480's last
  clause informally. A three-way dependence ("no two members … can exist or be maintained
  without the presence and contributions of the third member")
  that no pair of members carries is a relation among three that the
  pairwise relations do not state. But the paper gives no formal model, so
  this is an illustration, not a further instance of the computation.
- **CLAIM-477: prior art for the recursion half.** "those constraints are
  themselves realized and maintained by something" is Weiss's triplet and
  his bounds "set by the dynamics of the system as a whole" (§6). The
  paper is an older statement of it, at the level of argument. It supplies
  nothing for the specification–realization half.
- **CLAIM-478: progressive consolidation is the same idea at an
  evolutionary time scale.** The stream that "later becomes constrained by
  the channel it has created" (§6) is a realization at one scale working as
  a constraint at another, which is CLAIM-478's thesis. Weiss's version is
  analogical and gives no mechanism.
- **CLAIM-474: Weiss's definitions sit at the individuation step, and his
  inequality is a candidate criterion of the kind QUESTION-115 and
  CLAIM-157 seek.** Weiss's system is individuated by persistence of
  configuration through turnover, and the variance inequality was meant as
  a way of "empirically identifying systems in nature" (§5). As checked
  above, it discriminates only for an extensive whole, where it detects
  compensating covariance among the parts. That makes it a statistic of the
  same family as CLAIM-157's near-decomposability and QUESTION-115's
  criteria, and a weaker one. Nothing in the paper separates the five
  relationships of CLAIM-474; Nicholson's single "system" covers
  interconnection, dynamics and individuation at once.
- **THEORY-072: neither supports nor undermines its demarcation.** The
  paper never discusses dissipative structures. Weiss's "whirl of fluid"
  appears only as a warning against naming a molecule its "spinner" (§4).
- **THEORY-302: a parallel.** Turvey's coordinative structures, where a
  higher level fixes an equivalence class of states and the parts keep
  their freedom, are Weiss's "many degrees of freedom" under "ordering
  restraints" (§5), stated for motor control. Neither cites the other.
- **THEORY-465** holds that genes are reproducees with little
  reproductive autonomy; the §4 critique of "self-replicating" genes
  points the same way.
- **Should produce:** THEORY-tmp1244l (the variance inequality, checked)
  and THEORY-tmp2moei (macrodeterminacy without microdeterminacy, the one
  evidenced critique, Proposed).
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- **No new data, and the "empirical" concept is not derived here.** The
  paper says the system concept is "derived empirically from experimental
  work" (§7); what it shows is Weiss's statements and illustrations.
- **The figures are schematics**, several redrawn (Figs. 2, 3, 5, 6).
  Fig. 5 is the only evidence offered for the determinism critique, and it
  is a diagram.
- **The doctrines are taken at their most extreme.** Jacob 1973, Watson
  1965, Medawar 1965 and Davidson 2012 are quoted; nobody's current
  defence of a gene-regulatory-network account or of mechanistic
  explanation is engaged. The reductionism critique rebuts composition
  without interactions, not the doctrine as defined in §3.
- **The concept gives no criterion for applying "system"** beyond the
  definitions and the variance inequality, which is ambiguous as stated.
- **The author says what he does not do**: Weiss's field concept is left
  out (n. 8), and "systems thinking is not the only route" to these
  critiques (§7).
- **Weiss's texts were not read here.** What Weiss held is what Nicholson
  quotes, and the quotations are selected for the argument.

## Open questions

- Can Weiss's inequality be restated so that it separates systems from
  aggregates on its own: a fixed extensive quantity, and a test that the
  parts' covariances are negative beyond what a conservation law or a fixed
  total imposes? Measurements of cell-to-cell variability against
  tissue-level variability would then be its test.
- Is there a biological triplet in Weiss's strong sense, a set of processes
  of which no pair can be maintained without the third, documented as such?
  Minimal-cell work would be the place to look.
- How often is developmental macrodeterminacy achieved with variable cell
  trajectories, and how often with invariant lineages as in *C. elegans*?
  A comparative census of lineage variability would answer "the exception
  rather than the rule" (n. 30).
