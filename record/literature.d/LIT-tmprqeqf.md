# LIT-tmprqeqf: “First we invented stories, then they changed us”: The Evolution of Narrative Identity

Dan P. McAdams (2019), "'First we invented stories, then they changed us':
The Evolution of Narrative Identity", *Evolutionary Studies in Imaginative
Culture* 3(1), Spring 2019, pp. 1–18 — DOI-10.26613/esic.3.1.110

## Key takeaways

- **Definition.** "A narrative identity is a person's internalized and
  evolving story of how he or she has become the person he or she is
  becoming (McAdams and McLean 2013)." It reconstructs the past and imagines
  the future to give the self "temporal coherence and some semblance of
  psychosocial unity and purpose". Once a person has one, "the author has
  become the story". There is "no going back" to being only an actor or an
  agent.
- **Three layers of self.** Development runs "from actor to agent to
  author". There is the social actor of traits (self-recognition by age 2),
  the motivated agent of goals and plans (theory of mind, the school years)
  and the autobiographical author (adolescence and young adulthood).
  Autobiographical reasoning is "a critical factor", and by "the early 20s"
  most people have a narrative identity.
- **The story matters.** Stories with agentic protagonists go with
  well-being. In Adler's (2012) psychotherapy study, "the story changes
  first, and then the storyteller gets better". Midlife adults whose stories
  resemble "the redemptive self" score high on generativity. That pattern
  may be "an especially 'American' kind of life story", drawn from a
  culture's master narratives.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, as the read substitute for
McAdams and McLean's "Narrative Identity" ([LIT-tmpa39zo](LIT-tmpa39zo.md)), the canonical short
review. That article is closed at SAGE, with no open copy found. This essay is
by the same first author, five years later, and it **extends** the 2013
article in the LIT scheme's sense: it takes the 2013 definition verbatim and
cites the article for the claim that a narrative identity "provides a life
with unity and purpose". It adds the evolutionary framing, the actor, agent
and author layers, and the cultural section. It lacks the 2013 article's
compact review of measures and findings, which it cites to Adler et al.
(2016) and McAdams and Manczak (2015).

It is the record's first psychological, as against philosophical, statement
of narrative identity. It carries `narrative-unity` and `self` as the owner's
distinction asks, `social-science` because it is personality and
developmental psychology, and `cognition` for autobiographical memory,
episodic future thought and autobiographical reasoning. It also carries
`personhood`, because it adopts Schechtman's (1996, [LIT-468](LIT-468.md)) claim that "we
constitute ourselves as persons" by narrative, and that narrative "situates
the individual as a moral agent".

Where it bears on the record:

- **Against Narrativity** ([LIT-452](LIT-452.md)). Strawson denies the descriptive thesis
  that human beings typically experience their lives as a narrative, and
  says some people ("Episodics") do not. McAdams asserts it as a
  developmental fact. The two are **rivals** in the LIT scheme's sense:
  accounts of the same phenomenon that exclude each other, neither testing
  the other. McAdams does not cite Strawson. His concession is that studies
  "generally assume that nearly everybody 'has' a narrative identity", and
  that some people are more oriented to it than others, as measured by the
  Awareness of Narrative Identity Questionnaire. That concession is the
  nearest he comes to the dispute, and it is where a test would start.
- **Dennett's narrative self** ([LIT-442](LIT-442.md)). Dennett's self is a fiction the
  brain spins. McAdams's narrative identity is "an invented construction",
  not "a veridical testimony", but it is causally real ("then these stories
  change us"). That is the empirical side of the question Dennett leaves
  open.
- **Schechtman** ([LIT-468](LIT-468.md), Deferred). McAdams relies on her for the claim
  that narrative constitutes persons and allows "autonomy and moral agency".
  This adds one more second-hand report of what her book says to the two that [LIT-468](LIT-468.md) already uses (Strawson and the SEP map).
- **Metzinger** ([LIT-tmp7qvyd](LIT-tmp7qvyd.md)). The two selves are of different kinds. The
  précis's self is pre-reflective and present; McAdams's is reflective and
  temporally extended. The précis attributes the experience of an enduring
  self to bodily self-awareness and autobiographical memory, which is where
  McAdams starts.

No anthology topic holds it, and it carries no instruction for
machine-learning practice.
