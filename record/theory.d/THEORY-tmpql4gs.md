# THEORY-tmpql4gs: Stubborn distrust of scientific experts can follow validly from the truster's position: one with little say in collective decisions, who has reason to think the experts' service ethos leaves out their core interests, has a reason to distrust an expert because of the expert's prestige, whatever the expert's competence or goodwill

## Source

Desmond (2022), [LIT-158](../literature.d/LIT-158.md) (*Social Epistemology* 36(5): 586–600), read in
full in [NOTE-113](../notes.d/NOTE-113.md) from the author's accepted manuscript. Page numbers are
the manuscript's.

## What was actually shown

**The target.** "Stubborn" distrust is distrust that resists evidence for
the experts' views, demonstrations of competence and displays of goodwill
(p. 3). "Circumscribed" distrust, resting on defeasible suspicion of bias
or conflicts of interest, is set aside as not irrational anyway (pp. 2–3).
The paper offers an alternative to "irrationality views" of the stubborn
kind, both the charitable one (lack of education, misinformation) and the
uncharitable one ("post-truth" cynicism) (pp. 3–4).

**The definitions.** Social status is "the influence over collective
decision-making that an individual is given by others" (p. 11). Scientists
hold it as prestige, conferred on the expectation of service to the
community (pp. 9–10, citing Henrich and Gil-White; Price and Van Vugt).
Status distrust is distrust in which status, read as an indicator of
distrustworthiness, overrides every other indicator (pp. 11–12).

**The scheme (§6, pp. 16–17).** Premise 1, status vulnerability: in a
conflict of interest, a low-prestige truster will get less support from
third parties than the trustee. Premise 2, service doubt: the truster has
reason to believe the dominant service-for-prestige ethos "does not
integrate their core interests". With two background definitions (trust
entails vulnerability; prestige carries service norms), it follows that
the trustee's norm-governed decisions will conflict with the truster's core
interests, that third parties will not back the truster, and so that the
truster has reason to distrust the candidate trustee "in virtue of their
high degree of prestige".

**Service doubt is the trigger** (p. 18). Status differences alone do not
produce status distrust. Past harm, such as Tuskegee, raises the credence
that high-status groups do not keep service norms.

**What could have come out otherwise.** The scheme could have been invalid,
or valid only with a premise that assumes the conclusion. [NOTE-113](../notes.d/NOTE-113.md) finds it
valid as informal argument, with one weakness: the step to "in virtue of
prestige" leans on the second background definition, so the operative
reasons are the truster's vulnerability and the content of the ethos, and
the "prestige" framing is partly a relabelling.

## What this does not say

- **That status distrust is ever justified in a liberal democracy.** The
  paper leaves when the premises are true "beyond the scope of this paper"
  (pp. 15, 18–19). The one case it calls sound is a self-serving
  totalitarian regime. The abstract's "justifiably" overstates the body
  ([NOTE-113](../notes.d/NOTE-113.md), Corrections).
- **That actual distrusters reason this way.** There is no data. The
  Havasupai case (§5) is illustrative and secondhand, assumes the
  researchers were well-intentioned, and misdates the lawsuit. Whether
  status predicts stubborn distrust over education, ideology and past harm
  is the NOTE's open question, not a result.
- **That it beats the rivals.** The author declines an abductive comparison
  with testimonial injustice and with the empirical predictors of
  conspiracy belief (p. 6).
- **That distrust is widespread.** Premise 1 assumes status distrusters
  are a minority (fn. 9). If they are not, the usual markers of status
  invert.
- **That conspiracy claims are true.** §7 reads them as expressions of
  social position, not descriptions. Taken literally they are unjustified,
  even where the distruster is right to expect harm. That reading is
  interpretive and untested.

## Connections

- Fricker's testimonial injustice ([THEORY-327](THEORY-327.md)) runs the other way, from
  hearer to speaker: a prejudiced hearer deflates a speaker's credibility.
  Desmond's truster discounts a high-status speaker for structural
  reasons. The two are compatible, and Desmond names Fricker as a rival
  explanation he does not assess.
- Keeley's argument that sweeping distrust undermines itself ([LIT-119](../literature.d/LIT-119.md),
  [LIT-203](../literature.d/LIT-203.md)) is held but unread. Whether status distrust is local enough to
  escape it is an open question in [NOTE-113](../notes.d/NOTE-113.md).
