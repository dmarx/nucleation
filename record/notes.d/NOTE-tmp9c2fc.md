# NOTE-tmp9c2fc: Lenggenhager et al. — Video ergo sum: manipulating bodily self-consciousness

## Contribution

Before this, the rubber-hand illusion and somatoparaphrenia had shown that
the ownership and felt position of a body part can be changed. They left the
whole-body self untouched. This report moves the rubber-hand procedure to
the whole body, using live video of the participant's own back shown 2 m
ahead. It is the first experiment to claim a manipulation of "selfhood per
se" (p. 1097), meaning the attribution and location of the entire body, in
healthy people.

## Key insight

If the felt self is located wherever a whole-body representation is
located, and that representation is built by the same visuo-tactile
weighting that makes a rubber hand feel like one's own, then seeing one's
body touched where one feels touch should pull the self toward the seen
body. The authors predicted this from out-of-body experiences of
neurological origin. They designed the experiment to induce such
experiences in healthy people, and report that it induced only part of one.

## Assumptions

- **The two rubber-hand measures transfer to the whole body.** The
  questionnaire is Botvinick and Cohen's, reworded. The rubber hand's
  "proprioceptive drift" becomes a locomotor homing error. The participant
  is walked backwards blindfolded in small steps (about 20 cm each) and
  then walks back to where they stood (SOM, p. 1). That a walking error
  measures *self*-location is assumed, not tested.
- **The asynchronous condition is a control for everything but synchrony.**
  In Study I it was a replay of the training session, "completely random".
  In Study II it was a constant 200 ms lag, chosen because healthy people
  can detect a 200 ms delay in their own movement's feedback (SOM, p. 2).
  The authors say this difference may explain why Study II's
  synchronous–asynchronous contrasts were weaker (SOM, p. 5).
- **Q3 indexes self-identification, and Q1–Q2 index touch referral.** This
  reading is reached after the fact, from the object condition (p. 1098).
- **Participants were "naïve to the purpose of the study"** (SOM, p. 1).
  No check of expectations or demand is reported, and the experimenter who
  displaced each participant and measured the drift is not said to have
  been blind to condition.

The setting is narrow. Participants stood still during 60 s of stroking and
saw a third-person view of a back 2 m ahead in a 25.6° × 17.1° display.
The participants were young adults with no neurological history.

## Key results

- **Study I drift (anterior–posterior).** Synchronous drift was
  24.1 ± 9.0 cm (SEM), t = 2.67, p = 0.02 against the starting position.
  Asynchronous drift was 12.5 ± 8.5 cm, t = 1.45, p = 0.17. The main text
  prints this as "12. ± 8.5 cm", a dropped digit. Synchronous against
  asynchronous gave paired t = 2.92, p = 0.01. Left–right drift was null.
  *Checked:* every t is mean/SEM for n = 14, and every p matches df = 13.
- **Study I questionnaire.** Synchronous scores were Q1 2.6 ± 0.8, Q2
  2.2 ± 0.6 and Q3 2.1 ± 0.6 (labelled SEM). The other items, and every
  item after asynchronous stroking, were negative. That includes Q4, "It
  felt as if my (real) body was drifting towards the front". The main text
  says the synchronous–asynchronous differences on Q1–Q3 had "all P values
  < 0.001". The supporting text says "p<0.01, t<3.24", with t "<" where
  ">" is presumably meant. *Checked:* fourteen scores between −3 and +3
  with mean 2.6 cannot have an SEM above 0.43, and none with mean 2.2 can
  exceed 0.54. So the Q1 and Q2 error bars cannot be SEMs, and are
  probably SDs. The participant ages, "23.1 years ± 4.4SEM", imply an SD
  of 16 years, so that label is probably wrong too. The drift error bars
  are consistent with SEM, since the reported t values need them to be.
- **Study II drift, relative to the motor-control condition.** Synchronous:
  own body 17.1 ± 6.1 cm (p = 0.02), mannequin 25.4 ± 8.4 cm (p = 0.01),
  box 14.3 ± 7.3 cm (p = 0.07), empty room 1.1 cm. Asynchronous drifts were
  1.5 to 11.7 cm, none significant. The 2 × 3 ANOVA gave a Synchrony ×
  Condition interaction, F = 3.70, p = 0.04. Synchronous against
  asynchronous was significant only for the mannequin (t = 4.70,
  p = 0.0004), *not* for the own body. Among the synchronous conditions,
  only mannequin against box differed (t = 2.26, p = 0.04). Own body
  against box is not reported.
- **Study II questionnaire.** Q1 and Q2 were positive after synchronous
  stroking in all three conditions, the box included. Q3 after synchronous
  stroking was own body 2.1 ± 0.5, mannequin 0.6 ± 0.6 and box −0.4 ± 0.6.
  Synchronous against asynchronous on Q3 was significant for the own body
  (p = 0.001) and the mannequin (p = 0.032), and not for the box
  (Table S1). The main text says participants "self-identified with both
  bodily stimuli" (p. 1098). For the mannequin that means a score that
  rose from negative to 0.6, about the midpoint of the scale.
- **No out-of-body experience.** "None of the participants reported
  sensations of overt disembodiment or a change in visuospatial
  perspective" (p. 1097). Some found the experience "irritating". The
  authors conclude that they "induced only some aspects of out-of-body
  experiences or rather the closely related experience of heautoscopy"
  (p. 1098). They add that visual–vestibular disintegration is probably
  needed for the full experience.
- **What could have come out otherwise.** Drift and Q3 could both have
  been indifferent to synchrony. Q3 could have been as high for the box as
  for the bodies. Neither happened for Q3. For drift, the box result is
  intermediate: 14.3 cm against 17.1 cm for the own body, with p = 0.07
  against baseline.

## Concepts

- **Global self-attribution / self-identification.** Experiencing a whole
  body as one's own, here Q3. Contrasted with body-part ownership in the
  rubber-hand illusion. In the authors' terms, Metzinger's, the
  "transparent content of a single, coherent whole-body representation"
  (p. 1097).
- **Global self-localization.** Where the self is felt to be. Operationally
  here, the walking-error drift.
- **Spatial unity.** The normal coincidence of self-location with the
  physical body, which out-of-body experiences break.
- **Heautoscopy.** Seeing a second own body and partly identifying with it,
  with no overt disembodiment. Self-location may alternate between bodies.

## Connections

It adapts Botvinick and Cohen's rubber hand and its questionnaire, and
Tsakiris and Haggard's object control for "cognitive knowledge about
bodies". Both are filed in this batch. It sides with Tsakiris and Haggard
against Armel and Ramachandran, for whom correlation alone suffices. Its
hypothesis comes from Blanke's clinical series of out-of-body experiences
and heautoscopy. Its theory of what is manipulated is Metzinger's *Being
No One* ([LIT-467](../literature.d/LIT-467.md)), cited as (2). Metzinger is a co-author.

**The e-letters (June 2008).** Meyer argued that self-location follows
the *visual* location of the stroking, against the first-person
perspective. Ehrsson and Petkova replied with two problems. First, the
rubber hand fails when the fake arm is rotated, or more than 30–40 cm from
the real one, so projecting touch 2 m onto a third-person body is unlikely.
Second, in their own hands, a mannequin 2 m ahead without video never
induced the illusion in 10 naïve participants. They propose that
participants recognised themselves on what amounted to CCTV: "they were
looking at a video reconstruction of their own body". The questionnaire
cannot tell this apart from felt ownership, they say. They note that
Groenegress and Slater found no reliable illusion with a similar protocol.
They also point out that the paper never says whether participants stopped
feeling touch on their real back. Blanke, Metzinger and Lenggenhager
replied by extending Meyer's analysis to the asynchronous condition and to
a no-stroking baseline that neither study tested. They did not answer the
CCTV reading or the non-replications. They call the paper's self-location
claim one that "has been evaluated by different survey questions and
behavioral measures" across the two labs.

Petkova and Ehrsson 2008 (filed in this batch) belongs to the
first-person-perspective programme that this exchange set against the
third-person one. Its reading, not this one, should say how it bears on
this result.

## Bearing on the record

- **[NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md), C6.** The row says body ownership "can be manipulated by
  multisensory conflict", "moderate for the manipulation, cited". Checked
  against this source, the full-body manipulation is real but small and
  narrower than cited. The questionnaire moves strongly for one's own
  filmed back and barely for a mannequin. The drift measure moves 17–25 cm
  out of 200, is not reported as felt, and separates a body from a box
  only weakly. Its Study II own-body contrast was not significant. There is
  one study, two samples of fourteen and no replication in this source, and
  the alternative reading in the e-letters is not excluded. The *body-part*
  manipulation, the rubber hand, is better supported, but not by this
  source. It rests on Botvinick and Cohen and on Tsakiris and Haggard. So
  C6's "moderate for the manipulation" holds for body parts on other
  evidence. For the whole body, on this source, it should read weak to
  moderate.
- **Metzinger ([LIT-450](../literature.d/LIT-450.md), [LIT-568](../literature.d/LIT-568.md), [THEORY-064](../theory.d/THEORY-064.md)).** The paper is the
  experimental arm of the self-model theory: Metzinger co-authored it, and
  it describes the target as the "transparent content of a single, coherent
  whole-body representation". It shows that reports about that content can
  be shifted by sensory conflict. As [THEORY-064](../theory.d/THEORY-064.md)'s promote_when says, this
  shows the self-model can be manipulated, not that the self is nothing
  more. The transparency claim itself is not tested: nothing here makes the
  representation opaque.
- **Active inference ([LIT-1181](../literature.d/LIT-1181.md), [NOTE-960](NOTE-960.md)).** The result reads naturally as
  precision-weighted cue combination. Vision is the more precise cue for
  where the touching happens, so it captures the felt location of touch,
  and a prior over what bodies look like limits the capture to bodies.
  That is the structure of Friston et al.'s cued-reaching simulation, which
  optimised multisensory precisions. The paper's "rejecting a pure Bayesian
  account" (p. 1098) rejects Armel and Ramachandran's correlation account,
  not Bayesian inference with priors, which the body–box difference fits.
  That reading is mine. One further point: in Friston et al. action fulfils
  proprioceptive predictions, and this paper's self-location measure is an
  action, walking back. Its effect may sit in the motor read-out rather
  than in a felt location, since Q4 says participants did not feel
  themselves drift.
- **[LIT-1186](../literature.d/LIT-1186.md) (attempted out-of-body experiences).** The full-body illusion
  is no evidence of out-of-body perception. Nobody here left their body or
  saw from elsewhere, by the authors' own account.
- **Lush 2020 (filed in this batch).** The questionnaire items state the
  hypothesis outright, expectancy was not measured, and blinding of the
  drift measurement is not reported. Lush's demand-characteristics
  critique of the rubber hand applies here unchanged.
- Filed as [THEORY-tmpahbbc](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpahbbc.md).
- **For the Anthology of the SOTA:** no instruction for ML practice.

## Limitations

- Two samples of fourteen, many uncorrected t-tests, and no preregistration.
  The key Study II contrasts sit at p = 0.04–0.07.
- The asynchronous control differs between the studies. The authors use
  this difference to explain why the own-body contrast was weaker in
  Study II.
- Some error bars are mislabelled (questionnaire SEMs in Study I, ages),
  and the text and the supplement disagree on the Study I questionnaire
  p-values.
- No expectancy or suggestibility control, and no blinding of the drift
  measurement reported.
- Self-location is never measured as experienced. The questionnaire item
  that comes closest (Q4) was negative.
- No out-of-body experience was induced, though inducing one was the
  stated aim.
- Whether participants still felt the touch on their own back is not
  reported.

## Open questions

- Does the drift survive a blinded experimenter and a measure of
  expectancy? Lush's design applied to the full body would settle whether
  the questionnaire effect is demand.
- Is the walking error self-location or a recalibration of locomotor
  homing by visual capture? A test: compare it with a direct report of
  felt location taken at the same moment.
- Does the effect depend on knowing that the display is live video of
  oneself (the CCTV reading)? A test: compare a mannequin seen through the
  same display with one seen in the flesh at 2 m.
