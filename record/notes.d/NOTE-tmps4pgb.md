# NOTE-tmps4pgb: Vissio — Reasoning in Life

## Contribution

An expository paper placing Canguilhem's theory of norms within his
vitalism. It adds a reading, not a result: that Canguilhem's vitalism is
"attitudinal" (in Wolfe's taxonomy), grounded in a philosophy of values
he formed in the 1920s and 1930s under Alain, and that his normativity is
what lets him extend "life" to knowledge, science and society without
reducing them to biology. It proposes the result as a ground for a
semiotics of values (section 5).

## Key insight

For Canguilhem, a norm is not a statistical average and not a fixed
standard: it is a living being's established way of dealing with its
milieu, and health is the capacity to change it. Sickness is not the
absence of norms but a narrowing of that capacity.

## Assumptions

- **Canguilhem as reported.** All of Canguilhem is quoted from his texts,
  chiefly *The Normal and the Pathological* (Vissio's reference 13) and
  *Knowledge of Life* (reference 11).
- **Wolfe's taxonomy of vitalism** (substantival, structural–functional,
  attitudinal) as the frame (section 1).
- **Continuity of the theory of norms** across Canguilhem's work, following
  the scholars cited in section 1.

## Key results

The paper reports and interprets. Its claims, by section:

- **Section 1, what kind of vitalism.** Canguilhem's is "an Existential or
  Attitudinal Vitalism", like Goldstein's: no special living matter, no
  special emergent laws, but a perspective on living beings.
- **Section 2, values.** In the 1920s and 1930s, under Alain, Canguilhem
  opposed fact to value ("war" and "peace" name values, not facts); by the
  1940s "value" had become a biological concept.
- **Section 3, vital norms.** "Even for an amoeba, living means preference
  and exclusion" (Canguilhem, p. 136). "Normative, in philosophy, means
  every judgment which evaluates or qualifies a fact in relation to a
  norm, but this mode of judgment is essentially subordinate to that which
  establishes norms" (p. 126). "Biological pathology exists but there is
  no physical or chemical or mechanical pathology" (p. 127). With
  Goldstein, "disease appears when an organism is changed in such a way
  that, though in its proper, 'normal' milieu, it suffers catastrophic
  reaction" (p. 185). Vissio's gloss: "the sick individual is not devoid of
  any sort of norms, but it finds itself confined and reduced in its
  capacity to change these norms", "a state of restricted ability to
  negotiate norms with the environment". "Canguilhem calls this ability or
  capacity to redefine vital norms 'normativity'" (p. 227): "If biological
  norms exist it is because life, as not only subject to the environment
  but also as an institution of its environment, thereby posits values not
  only in the environment but also in the organism itself. This is what we
  call biological normativity."
- **Section 4, social norms.** In the 1966 *New Reflections*, Canguilhem
  criticizes organic models of society: "in the case of the organism the
  fact of need expresses the existence of a regulatory apparatus"
  (p. 252), whose regulation "expresses the integration of parts within
  the whole" (p. 253); a society has no such apparatus, so "the
  infringement of a social norm or rule is different from sickness and
  there is not a social pathology". "If social norms could be perceived as
  clearly as organic norms, men would be mad not to conform to them. As
  men are not mad [...] social norms are to be invented and not observed"
  (p. 259). Yet "the form and functions of the human body are the
  expression not only of conditions imposed on life by the environment but
  also of socially adopted modes of living" (p. 269).
- **Section 5, conclusion.** Human values arise from a "debate" between
  parts of the social body, which human rationality can arrange, and which
  lets a subject "challenge regimes of injustice".

## Concepts

- **Vital norm** — an established regime of life an organism finds "by
  bargaining with his milieu" (section 3).
- **Normativity** — the capacity to redefine vital norms (Canguilhem,
  p. 227 as cited).
- **Attitudinal vitalism** — vitalism as a stance on living beings, with no
  claim about special matter or forces (Wolfe).
- **Milieu** — the environment as evaluated by a living being.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-018](../claims.d/CLAIM-018.md): partly.** The claim waits on a NOTE that reads Canguilhem's
  book, and its defeat condition is a reading on which normativity "is not
  a capacity to establish new norms under changed conditions". This paper
  is a reading of the book by another, and it says the opposite of the
  defeat condition: normativity is "this ability or capacity to redefine
  vital norms", and disease leaves the organism "confined and reduced in its
  capacity to change these norms" (section 3, after Canguilhem p. 227). Its
  section 4 reports why the analogy should not be pressed: social norms
  have no integrating regulatory apparatus and "are to be invented and not
  observed", so "there is not a social pathology", which is the claim's
  own caveat that regulatory failure in an institution is not literally
  disease. Together with Giroux it gives two independent second-hand
  readings that agree; it does not give the first-hand reading the claim
  names.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Short and expository.** About 4,000 words of text; most of it situates
  Canguilhem historically.
- **Canguilhem at second hand**, with page numbers whose edition is not
  stated beyond the reference entry, which names both the French and the
  English editions.
- **No engagement with critics** of normativism or of Canguilhem's
  individualism, such as Giroux's population argument.

## Open questions

- Does the capacity to change norms belong to anything but an organism?
  Vissio reports Canguilhem's no for society, without discussing other
  collectives.
