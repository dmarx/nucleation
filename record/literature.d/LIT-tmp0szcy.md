# LIT-tmp0szcy: Cultural Trauma and Collective Identity

Alexander, J. C., Eyerman, R., Giesen, B., Smelser, N. J. & Sztompka, P. (2004). *Cultural Trauma and Collective Identity*. Berkeley: University of California Press.

Not read. The summary is drawn from the abstract or from what the work is
standardly cited for. A reading should check it first.

## Why this is here

The sociological account on which a group's trauma belongs to its identity, not to any individual's experience. For [QUESTION-tmp8bttd](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmp8bttd.md) and [LIT-1388](LIT-1388.md) (the political legacy of slavery).

## Read in its place

The work cannot be read here, so the record holds it at second hand.

Eyerman's chapter gives the volume's framework as its authors shared it in
2001, cited as "Alexander et al. 2001": Smelser's definition of cultural
trauma, quoted whole (p. 2); Alexander's "trauma process" and "carrier
groups", and the meaning struggle over the pain, the victim and the
responsible party (p. 3); Sztompka's "traumatic affects" (p. 3); and
Giesen's "temporal map" (p. 6). It applies them to slavery, the case the
LIT's summary names as Eyerman's. Missing still: the published chapters
themselves; Alexander's theory chapter and his Holocaust chapter; the
other cases the LIT's summary names (the Holocaust, Germany after Nazism);
whether the 2004 wording matches the 2001 one; and Eyerman's chapter in
the volume.

Hirst, Cyr and Merck give Alexander's theory chapter in four quotations
with page numbers: the trauma process as a collectivity's coming to feel a
horrendous event has marked its consciousness (p. 1); trauma as "acute
discomfort entering into the core of the collectivity's sense of its own
identity" (p. 10); the speaker, audience and situation triad (p. 12); and
the constraint of unevenly distributed resources (p. 21). They also
paraphrase his Holocaust chapter on Anne Frank and the series *Holocaust*.
Their reference list swaps the two chapters' labels. Missing still: the
theory chapter's full argument; the other authors' chapters (Eyerman,
Giesen, Smelser, Sztompka); and the volume's cases apart from the
Holocaust.
