# THEORY-1923: Presidential adjudication needs a harder look

## Source

Cuéllar, "The New Presidential Adjudication", The Yale Law Journal Forum,
9 February 2026, pp. 597–653 ([LIT-2706](../literature.d/LIT-2706.md)), read in [NOTE-2029](../notes.d/NOTE-2029.md).

## What was actually shown

An argument, not a measurement. Cuéllar defines presidential adjudication by
"distinctions in the agent involved, the object of the action, and its form."
(p. 610). He traces its growth to doctrine (removal, immunity), to norms that
"are quickly eroding." (p. 617), to weakened congressional oversight and to
lost agency capacity, so that the new strain reaches "more people and
organizations, in more specific ways, and in more situations." (p. 604).
Courts can respond with a "harder look" (p. 597) built from statutory limits,
the foreign/domestic line, nonarbitrariness review and a presumption of
regularity that is defeasible on pretext. Civic actors add friction and buy
time.

It corroborates [CLAIM-462](../claims.d/CLAIM-462.md) in one polity and independently of the exchange:
the executive's capacity for effective action grows while the relations that
corrected it weaken, and review stays limited "even as the importance and
scope of this practice grows" (p. 599).

## What this does not say

- Nothing about speed as a mechanism. The "temporal asymmetry" reading in the
  exchange (A25) is A's addition, which A acknowledges.
- Nothing about how an act changes the cost of its own correction
  ([CLAIM-464](../claims.d/CLAIM-464.md)). The closest it comes is harm imposed "in the meantime" before
  courts act (p. 647).
- No evidence of frequency or outcomes; the essay predates the 2026 cases in
  the record.
- Its remedy applies only after "final agency action" (p. 645), so acts taken
  before a reviewable decision exists fall outside it.

## From the second reading (2026-11-17)

The second reading adds a relation: it corroborates the mechanism of
[CLAIM-748](../claims.d/CLAIM-748.md), which does not rest on it. The White House "can
successfully target opponents even when courts shut down an initial line
of attack", with attacks "that might be thwarted by a court eventually but
levy a high cost on those targeted in the meantime" (p. 647). It adds one
limit:

- **That a remedy becomes unreachable.** Its mechanism is cost before
  correction, not a change in what correction can achieve.
