# NOTE-1301: The Minimal Group Paradigm and its maximal impact in research on social categorization

## Contribution

A five-page review in a themed issue on intergroup relations. It sets
out what the minimal group paradigm (MGP) is, what Tajfel's first studies
found, what the critics said, and how the paradigm has been used since
about 2011. It has no data of its own and almost no numbers. For the
record its use is as a second-hand account of Tajfel et al. 1971, and as
a list of the explanations of minimal-group bias that compete with social
identity theory.

## Key insight

The paradigm was built to remove self-interest, and what is left still
favours the in-group. Allocations are made to anonymous others, never to
oneself, so "no utilitarian self-interests can be directly served"
(p. 85). Social identity theory explained the bias by a motive to "derive
meaning and a positive self-concept" from membership (p. 85). The later
self-anchoring work gives a different route to the same bias: the new
in-group is favoured because the person's positive view of themselves is
projected onto it, "rather than a comparison with the respective
outgroup" (p. 86).

## Assumptions

- **Allocation matrices measure motives.** The original design reads
  choices on Tajfel's matrices as parity, maximum joint profit, maximum
  in-group profit or maximum difference (p. 85). Critics said the matrices
  did not measure these independently (p. 86).
- **"Minimal" is a matter of degree.** A plausible rationale
  (overestimator, a painter preferred) differs from an explicitly random
  one (a coin toss). The first allows stereotypes; the second "undermines
  meaning making" (p. 87).
- **A review's selection.** Otten is a principal of the self-anchoring
  and positive–negative asymmetry work she reviews (refs. 18, 19, 23–25,
  34, 35).

## Key results

- **The design** (p. 85). Tajfel and collaborators meant to start with a
  minimal setup and add elements until discrimination appeared. Groups
  were said to rest on artistic preference (Klee versus Kandinsky). Three
  features made it "mere categorization":
  - the categorization is novel and arbitrary, with no history;
  - it is anonymous, with no face-to-face interaction;
  - no utilitarian self-interest is directly served by the allocations.
- **The matrices** (p. 85). Participants distributed resources between an
  anonymous in-group member and an anonymous out-group member. The
  matrices separated parity, maximum joint profit and maximum in-group
  profit, and asked whether participants would give up in-group profit
  for more differentiation. Otten's illustration: choosing 11 for the
  in-group and 9 for the out-group over 12 and 11.
- **The first findings** (p. 85). "Fairness concerns strongly guided
  intergroup allocations", but in-group favouritism emerged reliably.
  "In some instances" the pull to differentiate was stronger than the pull
  to maximize in-group profit. This contradicted realistic conflict
  theory (Sherif 1967). It inspired social identity theory (Tajfel &
  Turner 1979) and self-categorization theory (Turner et al. 1987).
- **Criticism** (p. 86).
  - The matrices were said to confound strategies (Bornstein et al.
    1983). The findings replicated with new matrices.
  - The artificial setting may give category information "unrealistic
    attention" (Diehl 1989).
  - Reciprocity expectations may make the bias utilitarian after all
    (Gaertner & Insko 2000).
  - Positive–negative asymmetry: bias is mostly confined to allocating
    positive resources and positive traits. With punishment or negative
    traits, "'mere categorization' has no longer a reliable impact".
  - Self-anchoring: favouritism may come from projection of the positive
    self onto the minimal in-group (Cadinu & Rothbart 1996; Gramzow and
    colleagues; Otten 2002).

  Otten's verdict: the MGP "has remained a reliable and useful
  instrument".
- **Automatic responses** (pp. 86–87). Right after assignment, people
  have more positive implicit associations with the new in-group. In-group
  faces are encoded faster (Ratner & Amodio 2013), but the face-memory
  bias held for European Canadians and not East Asians (Ng et al. 2016).
  Americans favoured a minimal in-group more than Japanese did, "partly,
  but not fully explained" by self-esteem (Falk et al. 2014). Out-group
  faces were more readily encoded as angry, for minimal as for racial
  groups (Dunham 2011). Products were better built from instructions said
  to come from an in-group member, and the gap shrank when a superordinate
  category was introduced (Greenaway et al. 2015). Preschoolers did not
  trust a reliable out-group informant over an unreliable in-group one
  (MacDonald et al. 2013). Physiological threat arose when a minimal
  group's status was threatened (Scheepers et al. 2009).
- **Two directions of self–group alignment** (p. 87). With an anonymous,
  undefined group, identification followed how far people projected their
  own traits onto it (self-anchoring). Once stereotypes about the group
  were given, projection from group to self (self-stereotyping) predicted
  identification best (van Veelen et al. 2013, 2016).
- **Induction procedures** (p. 87). Pinter and Greenwald (2011) found
  similar explicit favouritism across four procedures, and the largest
  implicit effects when participants only memorized one group's names.
  Otten reports the authors' reading that this links the self-concept to
  the studied group.
- **Self-interest included** (pp. 87–88). Harvey and Bourhis (2012, 2013)
  added a Monopoly game, group wealth and wealth stratification.
  - Poor groups favoured the in-group whether wealth came by chance or
    merit. The rich favoured it strongly only if their wealth was earned.
  - Dividing money between self, in-group and out-group, "participants
    gave most money to themselves, but kept favoring their ingroup over
    the outgroup in their distributions of the remaining resources"
    (p. 87).
- **Conclusion** (p. 88). The paradigm is now used less to understand
  discrimination than to study "a whole range of other interpersonal,
  intra-group and inter-group phenomena".

## Concepts

- **minimal group paradigm**: categorization that is novel, arbitrary and
  anonymous, with no instrumental link between self-interest and the
  allocations or evaluations (p. 85).
- **maximum differentiation**: giving up in-group gain for a larger
  in-group advantage over the out-group.
- **positive–negative asymmetry**: minimal-group bias appears with
  positive outcomes and largely not with negative ones.
- **self-anchoring**: projecting one's own traits onto a group, so that
  the group shares the self's (usually positive) evaluation.
- **self-stereotyping**: projecting the group's traits onto oneself.

## Connections

- **Tajfel et al. 1971 ([LIT-1697](../literature.d/LIT-1697.md))**, Unavailable. This review gives
  its design and the gist of its result, not its experiments or numbers.
- **Robertson et al. ([NOTE-1245](NOTE-1245.md)).** Their preregistered online study
  found minimal-group favouritism only as it rose with identification.
  That fits Otten's emphasis on the self–group link as the driver (C4).
  It is not the same claim: Robertson measured identification, not
  projection.
- **Hornsey 2008 ([NOTE-1253](NOTE-1253.md))** names the same critiques at the same
  level of detail: demand, similarity and Rabbie's interdependence.
  Otten adds positive–negative asymmetry and self-anchoring, which Hornsey
  does not.
- **Self-categorization theory ([THEORY-1114](../theory.d/THEORY-1114.md)).** Van Veelen's
  self-stereotyping is the theory's depersonalization seen from the
  individual. Self-anchoring runs the other way. Otten treats the two as
  complementary pathways chosen by how much is known about the group.
- **Self-esteem ([LIT-1780](../literature.d/LIT-1780.md), unread).** Falk et al.'s cultural result,
  partly explained by self-esteem, is one more piece of the contested
  self-esteem strand.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).** The paper bears on it at
  three points. These are inferences; Otten does not discuss selflessness.
  - The minimal group is the textbook case of acting for a group with no
    personal gain. Its standard explanation, a positive self-concept from
    membership, already has the form of the thesis: a self-interest served
    through the group.
  - Self-anchoring and self-stereotyping are two routes to the same
    identification. Self-anchoring extends the personal self over the
    group: the group is valued because it carries the self. That fits a
    "larger self". Self-stereotyping fits SCT's "changed self", which the
    claim's "What it does not say" flags as the poorer fit. On Otten's
    account, which route operates depends on how much the person knows
    about the group.
  - When the personal self can be paid directly, it is paid first. The
    group is favoured with what is left. The extended self then has an
    order inside it, with the personal self at the front. The thesis says
    nothing yet about that order.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md)).** Projection of one's own
  traits is a criterion of the self's extent that is not a report of
  caring. Otten reports it predicts identification and bias. It is
  measured by trait overlap between self and group, the same kind of
  measure as Aron's circles.
- **Batson's objection ([CLAIM-494](../claims.d/CLAIM-494.md)).** Not addressed.
- **A dated note should be added to [CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)**, perhaps together
  with Pinter and Greenwald's (see the second draft). Proposed text:

  > Note of 2026-10-29: the minimal group, read at second hand. Otten
  > (2016) reports two routes from a new self-category to in-group
  > favouritism: projecting the positive personal self onto the group
  > (self-anchoring), and taking on the group's traits
  > (self-stereotyping). The first is the thesis's larger self and the
  > second its changed self, and which operates depends on how much is
  > known about the group. When participants could also pay themselves,
  > they took the most for themselves and favoured the in-group with the
  > rest, so the extended self, if that is what this is, puts the personal
  > self first.
- **No THEORY filed.** A review with no data, of a paradigm the record
  holds at second hand.
- **No ML instruction.**

## Limitations

- **Not the original.** No sample, no matrix, no pull score, no
  experiment from Tajfel et al. 1971 is given. The reader cannot tell
  from this whether the 1971 paper reported the "maximum differentiation"
  result itself or whether it came later.
- **A short review with few numbers.** No effect size is given for any
  study.
- **The author's own work** carries the positive–negative asymmetry and
  self-anchoring sections.
- **An internal inconsistency.** The text says Falk et al.'s cultural
  difference was "partly, but not fully explained" by self-esteem (p. 86).
  The annotated reference says it "could mostly be traced back to"
  self-esteem (p. 88).
- **Typos.**
  - "MPG-experiments" for "MGP-experiments" (p. 86).
  - "This effects of variations in how minimal social categories are was
    examined" (p. 87).
  - "preferring one painter of the other" for "or the other" (p. 87).
  - "an adopted version" for "adapted" (annotation to ref. 15, p. 88).
  - Ref. 37, Pinter & Greenwald, *Group Process Interg*, is dated 2001;
    its DOI and volume (7) are 2004's (p. 89).

## Open questions

- What did Tajfel et al. 1971 actually report: how large was favouritism,
  how strong was parity, and was maximum differentiation shown there or
  later? Only the original, or a reanalysis of it, can answer.
- Does self-anchoring predict sacrifice for the group when caring about
  the group is held fixed? That would test it as the criterion
  [CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md) asks for.
