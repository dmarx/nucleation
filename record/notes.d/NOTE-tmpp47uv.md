# NOTE-tmpp47uv: Kasser & Ryan — Further examining the American dream

## Contribution

It widens Kasser and Ryan 1993, which compared financial success with
three intrinsic aspirations in late adolescents, to three extrinsic
aspirations (financial success, social recognition, appealing appearance)
and four intrinsic ones (self-acceptance, affiliation, community feeling,
physical fitness), in an adult community sample as well as students. It
gives the factor-analytic case that the two kinds are distinct, and the
pattern of correlations with well-being that goal contents theory was
later built on.

## Key insight

What matters is not how much one aspires but which aspirations are
relatively central. Overall importance is entered first, so that "variance
due to having generally high importance or likelihood ratings is controlled
before considering the relationship of the content of goals to well-being"
(p. 283); only then do intrinsic and extrinsic contents part. Extrinsic
goals "do not provide satisfaction in and of themselves; instead, their
allure usually lies in the presumed admiration that attends them" (p. 280).

## Assumptions

- **Aspiration Index** (Kasser and Ryan 1993, extended): 32 aspirations,
  each rated for importance and for likelihood on 5-point scales; 14
  subscale means; α importance .59–.87 (Study 1), .72–.89 (Study 2) (pp.
  282, 284).
- **Overall scores** are the average "across all domains for each
  dimension" (p. 282), so the intrinsic and extrinsic summaries enter
  after a score built from them.
- **Guiding principles**: a rank-ordering of the seven domains (after
  Braithwaite and Law), α .39–.85 (Study 1), .51–.84 (Study 2) (pp. 282,
  284).
- **Outcomes, all self-report**: self-actualization, vitality, CES-D, HSCL
  anxiety, physical symptoms; in Study 2 also narcissism (NPI) and a
  14-day twice-daily mood and symptom diary (n = 97) (pp. 282, 284).

## Key results

### Study 1: 100 adults, 18–79 years (M = 38), 76% women, 41% return rate (pp. 281–283)

- **Two factors** for both importance and likelihood (Table 2, p. 282):
  intrinsic loadings .60–.77, extrinsic .75–.87 on importance.
- Intrinsic and extrinsic importance r = .20; likelihood r = .41 (p. 282).
- **Table 3** (p. 283), standardized β after overall importance:
  intrinsic with self-actualization .40***, vitality .46***, depression
  −.35**, physical symptoms −.35**; extrinsic with self-actualization
  −.52***, vitality −.60***, depression .29, anxiety .25, physical
  symptoms .46** (in the table's notation ** p < .05, *** p < .01).
  Likelihood: intrinsic with self-actualization .59***, depression
  −.45**; extrinsic −.57***, depression .44**.
- Gender: males with high extrinsic importance had more physical symptoms,
  F(1, 92) = 5.81; females with low intrinsic likelihood more anxiety and
  depression. Income was unrelated (p. 283).

### Study 2: 192 students, 107 women (pp. 283–285)

- **Table 4** (p. 285), after overall importance: intrinsic with
  self-actualization .59***, vitality .31***, narcissism −.31***, physical
  symptoms −.35**; extrinsic with self-actualization −.67***, vitality
  −.34***, depression .30**, narcissism .35***, physical symptoms .43**,
  positive affect −.35*. Likelihood: extrinsic with self-actualization
  −.90***, vitality −.44***, depression .48***, positive affect −.70***.
- **Social desirability** (Marlowe–Crowne) correlated with intrinsic
  importance .19 and likelihood .27, not with extrinsic; entering it first
  left the pattern, "except that depression and physical symptom results
  became marginally significant for importance ratings" (p. 285).
- Women rated intrinsic aspirations higher, men extrinsic importance
  higher (Table 5, p. 286); no gender interactions.

### What the paper does not test

- **Reasons for pursuing goals.** "it would be interesting to examine
  whether the well-being outcomes associated with intrinsic versus
  extrinsic goals differ when the reasons one pursues the goals are
  considered" (p. 286). No regulation measure is in either study.

## Concepts

- **Extrinsic aspirations** — financial success, social recognition,
  appealing appearance: goals that "depend on the contingent reactions of
  others" and are "typically engaged in as means to some other end" (p.
  280).
- **Intrinsic aspirations** — self-acceptance, affiliation, community
  feeling, physical fitness: "expressive of desires congruent with
  actualizing and growth tendencies" (p. 280).
- **Relative centrality** — an aspiration's importance with the overall
  importance of all aspirations controlled (pp. 281–283).
- **Importance vs likelihood** — how much one values a goal vs how likely
  one thinks it is to happen (p. 282).

## Connections

It extends Kasser and Ryan 1993 and cites Deci and Ryan 1985a (LIT-tmp7s2t8)
for the control orientation as "a focus on obtaining rewards or praise from
others" (p. 281). Ryan et al. 2022 (LIT-tmpd9u4s) cite it among goal
contents theory's sources; Bradshaw et al. 2022 there confirm that
relative-centrality scoring, as here, is what produces the negative
extrinsic association.

## Bearing on the record

- **NOTE-439's C4, a correction to the registration.** The registration
  filed this paper for NOTE-439's C4, "Extrinsic aspirations predict lower
  well-being, beyond the reasons for pursuing them". The paper supports the
  first half only. It has no measure of reasons and names the second half
  as a future question (p. 286). NOTE-439's own support for C4 is Carver and
  Baird (pp. 244–245 of LIT-558), which this paper does not include.
- **QUESTION-tmpee04a.** The paper ties extrinsic goals to well-being
  through need satisfaction in its theory, "likely to satisfy basic and
  inherent psychological needs" (p. 280), but measures no need. It is
  evidence that aspiration content correlates with well-being, not that
  needs are needs.
- **THEORY-054.** Its caveat is THEORY-054's distinction in another form:
  goals such as religiosity "may not, on the basis of their surface
  content, be a priori accordant or discordant with integrative and
  actualizing processes … the relationship … likely depends on whether the
  reasons one pursues the goals are more autonomous or controlled" (p. 286).
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- **Self-report, cross-sectional, one culture** (the authors' own list,
  p. 286), with low α for some guiding-principle subscales (.39).
- **The intrinsic and extrinsic Step-2 tests are close to one contrast
  estimated twice.** The overall score is the average across the seven
  domains, four intrinsic and three extrinsic (p. 282). With it held
  fixed, the intrinsic and extrinsic summaries can vary only together and
  in opposite directions. Were the overall score exactly the
  domain-weighted mean of the two, the two Step-2 tests would be one test
  with opposite signs; the unequal significance in Table 3 (depression:
  intrinsic −.35 at p < .05, extrinsic .29 not significant) shows it is not
  exactly that, which the paper does not explain. So "differential
  correlates" of intrinsic and extrinsic goals are largely one finding
  about their balance, not two.
- **A 41% response rate** in Study 1; mostly white, Rochester.
- **Mediating explanations are offered, not tested**: insecurity, less
  self-actualizing activity, or lower attainment (pp. 285–286).
- Results by single aspiration domain are not reported ("for the sake of
  brevity", note 3, p. 287).

## Open questions

- Does the relative centrality of extrinsic aspirations predict well-being
  once the autonomy of the reasons for pursuing them is controlled?
  (Sheldon et al.'s later tests are not held by the record.)
- Does need satisfaction mediate the association, measured rather than
  assumed? Dittmar et al. 2014, as Ryan et al. 2022 report, say yes for
  materialism.
