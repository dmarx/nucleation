# LIT-727: The Corporation as a Moral Person

Peter A. French (1979), "The Corporation as a Moral Person", *American
Philosophical Quarterly* 16(3):207–215 (University of Illinois Press for
North American Philosophical Publications)

## Key takeaways

- **The thesis.** "corporations can be full-fledged moral persons and have
  whatever privileges, rights and duties as are, in the normal course of
  affairs, accorded to moral persons" (p. 207).
- **Legal personhood does not settle it** (pp. 208–210). On the Fiction,
  Aggregate and Reality theories alike, corporate legal personhood is
  "virtually useless for moral purposes". Moral personhood needs the
  attributes of an administrator of rights, not merely a subject of them.
- **A moral person is a non-eliminable subject of accountability** (pp.
  210–211). Ascribing responsibility in the accountability sense says that
  the subject caused the event and that it intended the act. So "If
  corporations are moral persons, they will be non-eliminatable Davidsonian
  agents." A mob fails: it has no identity beyond the sum of its members'.
- **The CID Structure does the work** (pp. 211–214). An organizational or
  responsibility chart gives the "grammar" of corporate decision; recognition
  rules give its "logic". Procedure alone makes an act only procedurally
  corporate. When the act also implements established corporate policy, it
  is done for corporate reasons and is corporate intentional.
- **Corporate and member reasons come apart** (p. 214). A director bribed
  to vote for joining a cartel still produces a corporate decision whose
  reason is the corporation's: to reduce competition. Whether the
  corporation is responsible turns on issues "that may be unrelated" to the
  bribe.
- **The sufficiency premise is not argued.** French says Davidsonian agency
  is necessary and sufficient for moral personhood and that he "cannot
  further argue that position here" (p. 215).

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-05 at the owner's request, as a supplement to the essay on
organizational will. It is an older and stronger claim than Pettit's
([LIT-718](LIT-718.md)). Pettit argues that corporations are fit to be held
responsible, which the record's account of him ([THEORY-139](../theory.d/THEORY-139.md)) keeps
apart from moral standing; French argues that they are moral persons. For §7.1 it offers a ground for corporate endorsement:
policy, not a higher-order attitude, is what makes a reason the
corporation's. The account is [THEORY-131](../theory.d/THEORY-131.md).
