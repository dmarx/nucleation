# NOTE-tmpjiwb7: Rung & Madden — Experimental reductions of delay discounting

## Contribution

It is the first systematic and comprehensive review of attempts to reduce
delay discounting or impulsive choice by manipulating the environment.
Earlier reviews were either not systematic or not comprehensive
(Introduction). Its result is a map: which kinds of manipulation move
discounting, roughly how far, and how little is known about why.

## Key insight

Discounting is not fixed. It can be shifted by how a choice is framed,
by what is cued or imagined, by how the choice is arranged in time, and
most of all by training with delays and bundles. But nearly every study
measures discounting as the outcome and almost none tests what carries
the change. So the literature says that discounting moves, not how.

## Assumptions

- **Discounting as a target.** The review works "under the assumption
  that steeply discounting future outcomes plays a role in the
  development and/or continuation of maladaptive behaviors"
  (Discussion). The introduction states that the evidence "favors (but
  does not establish)" an etiological role.
- **Discounting describes, it does not explain.** Footnote 1: "delay
  discounting is a description of a pattern of choices ...; it is not an
  explanation". Opportunity costs, collection risk, magnitude
  sensitivity and failures of response–outcome coupling can all produce
  it.
- **Inclusion (Method).** PsycINFO and PLoS One searched on 1 March
  2016, plus references, giving 1,233 articles. Excluded were
  pharmacological and neural manipulations, studies without a control or
  baseline, ambiguous measures, manipulations impractical outside the
  laboratory (reward magnitude, sign, income, commodity), unresolvable
  statistical violations, and categories hypothesized to increase
  discounting. 92 papers remained.
- **Effect size (Method).** Hedges' g from between- or within-subject
  designs, with within-subject variances computed assuming r = .5
  between measurements. Values were sometimes read off graphs. These are
  "relative, as opposed to absolute" (Discussion). Mixed-effects models
  were run by category and subcategory. Cross-category comparisons,
  moderators other than category and publication bias were deliberately
  not analysed.

## Key results

- **Overall (Results).** "Overall, the interventions were successful in
  reducing discounting, Q(9) = 224.68, p < .0001", with heterogeneity
  I² = 64%, across nine categories. In the authors' order these are
  clinical interventions, episodic future thinking, framing, perspective
  taking, priming, cueing, context, learning-based approaches and
  environmental enrichment.
- **Clinical interventions (Results, Table 1).** Twelve studies. Overall
  B = 0.23, SE 0.08, z = 2.78, p = .005, I² = 38%. "For most of these
  studies, delay discounting was not a primary target."
- **Mindfulness-based approaches (Results, Table 1).** Two studies,
  subcategory estimate 0.32 (SE 0.17), z = 1.89, p = .059: "the reduction
  in discounting produced by mindfulness interventions only approached
  significance".
  - Hendrickson and Rasmussen (2013), college students, mindful eating
    against a nutrition-information control: g = 0.27 for food
    (significant) and 0.16 for money (n.s.).
  - Morrison et al. (2014; NOTE-tmp0mi8i), college students, ACT against
    waitlist: g = 0.94, with the between-groups comparison at trend level
    (p < .06) and the within-subjects reduction significant.
  - The review asks for "better controls (e.g., sham-therapy in lieu of
    wait-list controls)".
- **Contingency management (Results).** Three smoking studies,
  inconsistent alone, significant together (0.36, z = 2.39). **Other
  substance-use treatments** gave a model-estimated d = .16. "These
  findings offer no simple support for the position that drug abstinence
  would reverse" discounting, and "much needed mediation analyses" are
  asked for.
- **Reward bundling (Results).** "If a single choice determines not just
  the next outcome, but a bundle of the same outcomes ... this should
  reduce impulsive choice". Studies "typically" found reductions
  (z = 4.57): Kirby and Guastello 2001 (five-reward bundles reversed many
  participants' preference); Ainslie and Monterosso 2003 in rats;
  Hofmeyr et al. 2011 in smokers, not nonsmokers. After experience with
  9-reward bundles, rats chose impulsively less even when the outcomes
  were unbundled (Stein, Smits, et al. 2013). Learning-based approaches
  overall: B = 0.62, SE 0.08, z = 7.43, I² = 60%.
- **Mechanism (Theoretical Issues).** "Within most of the categories of
  intervention strategies reviewed above, there is no clear theoretical
  understanding of how the manipulation influences delay discounting.
  Inclusion of formal mediation analyses is rare." Demand
  characteristics are named as the chief threat, especially "when control
  procedures do not equate for expectancy of change (e.g., wait-list
  control groups)".
- **Conclusions.** Brief manipulations (nature cues, episodic future
  thinking, adding delays, date and explicit-zero framing) reliably move
  one-off choices but are not designed for lasting change. The
  "largest and longest-lasting effects were produced by learning-based
  manipulations" (Abstract). EFT could be built into ACT (Summary).

## Concepts

- **Delay discounting** — "the devaluation of an outcome because it is
  delayed", approximately hyperbolic across species (Introduction).
- **Impulsive choice** — choosing the smaller-sooner reward (SSR) over the
  larger-later (LLR); the self-control choice is the LLR.
- **Reward bundling** — making one choice commit the chooser to a series
  of the same outcomes, which by the hyperbolic form favours the LLR.
- **AUC** — area under the indifference-point curve, the theory-neutral
  measure the authors recommend (Methodological Recommendations).

## Connections

It builds on the addiction literature that treats steep discounting as a
"trans-disease process" (Bickel et al. 2012), and on Ainslie's bundling,
tested by Kirby and Guastello and by Madden's own laboratory. It covers
studies published before 1 March 2016. So it cannot include Dixon et al.
(2018; LIT-906) or Ge et al. (2024; LIT-916), the two direct mindfulness
studies CLAIM-156 cites.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-156: partly, and only on the claim's first fork.** The claim's
  defeat condition is that mindfulness's effect on self-control is
  "fully mediated by a change in measured discounting". To test that, a
  study must measure discounting and choice under temptation and ask
  which carries the change. This review shows that no such study
  existed by 2016 for any manipulation: mediation analyses are "rare",
  and the outcome is discounting itself. What it adds is the size of the
  discounting change. Mindfulness and ACT moved measured discounting
  only at trend level (0.32, p = .06, two studies, both in college
  students, one against a waitlist). That is weak evidence that
  mindfulness acts on the valuations. It is consistent with the claim's
  route, which needs no change in discounting, but it does not show
  that route either, since no non-discounting outcome was measured. It
  does not reach LIT-906 or LIT-916, which postdate its search.
- **THEORY-067 and CLAIM-169.** Outside what the claim waits on, the
  bundling subsection is a read summary of evidence for the bundling
  mechanism these entries say is untested. Bundled choice reduced
  impulsive choice in humans and rats, and in rats bundling experience
  carried over to unbundled choice. This review reports it at second
  hand; the bundling studies themselves are unread.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Only part of the review was read** (see the history): the categories
  other than clinical interventions and bundling are known only from
  the abstract, the overall result and the discussion.
- **Category-wise pooling.** Effects were pooled within categories, often
  from few studies (two for mindfulness), with effect sizes partly
  estimated from graphs and an assumed correlation of .5. No publication
  bias assessment.
- **Mostly hypothetical monetary outcomes** in human studies
  (Methodological Recommendations).
- **Demand characteristics.** The authors flag them for manipulations
  that overlap the discounting task and for waitlist controls, which
  covers one of the two mindfulness studies.

## Open questions

- Does mindfulness reduce impulsive action without a matching change in
  discounting? This needs both measured, with mediation tested. That is
  the study CLAIM-156 asks for, and none appears here.
- Do the post-2016 mindfulness studies (LIT-906, LIT-916) change the
  subcategory's estimate?
