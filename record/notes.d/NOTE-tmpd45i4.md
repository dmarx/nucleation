# NOTE-tmpd45i4: Moss, Pettersson and van de Walle — An aid-institutions paradox

## Contribution

It joins two literatures that, in the authors' words, "have too long
ignored each other" (p. 5): the economists' work on tax effort and the
political science of revenue and state building. It frames aid as a
subsidy meant to be temporary and asks why it became permanent in dozens
of cases. It names the moral-hazard mechanism, and it separates harms that
donors could fix by changing practice (§4) from harms of the flow itself,
"regardless of the precise nature of donor practices" (§6).

## Key insight

Aid can undercut the institutions it is meant to build, an aid curse like
the resource curse: when revenue does not depend on how well the state
performs or on what it extracts from its citizens, the state underinvests
in the capacities that would let it do without the aid. The way out is for
the state to learn to tax.

## Assumptions

- **Tax effort as a proxy for capacity**, which the authors call "an
  imperfect indicator" (p. 19).
- **Accountability from the fiscal bargain**, imported from European state
  formation (Tilly, North and Weingast) without testing whether it
  carries to Africa.
- **Aid as fungible unearned income** like oil rents; it carries more
  strings, which they argue cannot stand in for domestic accountability.
- **Harm grows with the aid share and the duration of dependence**:
  surmised, not estimated ("It also seems reasonable to surmise", p. 9).
- **Neo-patrimonial regimes as the background**: aid "enhanced an evolution
  already under way" (p. 16), an amplifier rather than the origin.

## Key results

- **Scale (pp. 2–3).** ODA to sub-Saharan Africa was "11.7 percent of the
  continent's GNI in 2003", excluding Nigeria and South Africa. "Exactly
  half of the region's 46 countries with data for 2003 received in excess
  of 10 percent of GNI in ODA, and 11 received more than 20 percent."
- **Persistence, not tapering (p. 3).** "a core set of roughly three dozen
  countries that have received a tenth of GNI or more in aid for at least
  the last two decades". The Marshall Plan "lasted only a few years and
  never exceeded 3 percent of GDP"; US aid to Korea and Taiwan "tapered off
  within a decade"; today's ratios "have tended not to recede, but to grow
  larger over three decades."
- **Aid in the budget (pp. 9, 18).** "In twelve poor countries, of which ten
  are African, the ratio of ODA to government expenditure was 75 percent or
  more." "It is not uncommon for donors to fund over half of the country's
  public investment budget".
- **Uganda's target (p. 2).** Museveni: "if we can get about 22 percent of
  GDP we should not need to disturb anybody by asking for aid".
- **A temporary subsidy (pp. 5–6).** Aid "is supposed to provide temporary
  financial assistance in order to encourage certain long-term behaviors:
  revenue collection, investment in physical and human capital, and the
  establishment of the institutions of a developmental state". South Korea
  and Botswana were the cases where "the country gradually was weaned off
  aid"; elsewhere aid is a "crutch delaying institutional development".
- **Capacity building has not built capacity (p. 6).** "70 civil service
  reform projects between 1987 and 1997"; "over a quarter of all Bank
  credits to the region is explicitly devoted to capacity building".
- **Soft budget (p. 7).** High aid as a "soft budget constraint"; in Ghana,
  "as donor financing increased, so did disparities between budgeted
  expenditures and actual spending", a "budgetary façade".
- **Donor practice (pp. 8–10).** Parallel structures "cream the best staff
  from the civil service"; "over time, real developmental capacities may
  atrophy within the administration."; per diems turn "training into a rent
  to be distributed". "the larger the relative aid flows, the more these
  problems are likely to be exacerbated"; "the number of distinct projects
  funded by donors has nearly tripled since 1995".
- **The route out (p. 10).** Kaldor: "the key determination of whether a
  state moves from aid dependency to economic self-sufficiency is the
  degree to which the state learns how to tax". Azam et al., in the
  authors' paraphrase (the words are Moss et al.'s): exit depends on "learning-by-doing in the public sector, a process that is greatly
  affected by the level of aid in relation to overall revenue and the
  initial institutional conditions." Bauer: aid "displaces the processes of
  institutional maturation".
- **Figure 1 (p. 11).** 55 low and lower-middle-income countries, 1972–1999:
  "there are no incidences at all of high aid (>10% of GNI) and high tax
  (>18% of GDP). These thresholds are of course arbitrary and the figure
  does not imply any causation".
- **Econometrics (pp. 12–13).** Mixed: Leuthold not significant; Brautigam,
  "71 percent of the African countries with aid/GDP above 10 percent in 1995
  had lower than expected tax effort"; Ghura negative; Teera and Hudson
  insignificant; Remmer partly significant; country cases both ways. Gupta
  et al., 107 countries: "grants have a significant, negative effect on
  revenue while loans have a significant, positive impact", loans having to
  be repaid.
- **Verdict (pp. 13–14).** "Generally, thus, the literature finds a negative
  relationship between aid and revenue collection, but this is not a
  conclusive result." The link may be confounded by low income and
  industrialization, and it may be that "states choose not to seek
  revenues they have the capacity to collect". IMF revenue data were not
  public despite "Repeated attempts" (fn. 3).
- **Accountability (pp. 14–15).** "aid flows themselves, separate from
  particular inefficiencies in the aid system, can affect the evolution of
  state-society relations." "the flow of revenues to the state is not
  affected by government efficiency, so there will be a tendency for
  governments to underinvest in developmental capacity."
- **Conditionality (pp. 15–16).** It "cannot fundamentally replace
  government accountability towards its citizens in an equally legitimate
  way"; "the move to authoritarian and military government in the 1960s did
  not reduce aid".
- **Successful developers (p. 17)** "have typically been solidly extractive
  states, with above average tax effort ratios".
- **Donor-funded NGOs (p. 18)** "are less likely to seek to build up their
  own memberships, or autonomy".
- **How much is too much (p. 19).** "We have studiously avoided this
  difficult question until the end". Berg: harm from "5 percent of GDP";
  Clemens and Radelet: saturation at "15-45 percent of GDP". Debt relief,
  peacekeeping and regional or global public goods "would be unlikely to
  have the kinds of negative institutional effects described here."

## Concepts

- **Aid-institutions paradox, or aid curse**: aid meant to build
  institutions weakens them, after the resource curse and the rentier
  state.
- **Aid as subsidy**: temporary support for behaviours meant to become
  self-financed.
- **Soft budget constraint**; **moral hazard**: revenue decoupled from
  performance.
- **Tax effort**: tax as a share of GDP, conditional on economic structure.
- **Learning-by-doing in the public sector** (Azam et al. 1999).
- **Parallel structures**: donor projects outside the national budget.
- **Saturation point** (Clemens and Radelet).

## Connections

It builds on Moore (2004), filed in this batch, and on Brautigam and Knack
(2004), Knack, Gupta et al. and the fiscal-sociology tradition of
Schumpeter, Tilly, Ardant, and North and Weingast. THEORY-429 (North,
institutions as constraints that reward productive or redistributive
activity) bears on the rent and patronage mechanism. The contrast with
externally accountable governance is Ostrom's user-made rules: LIT-737,
THEORY-149, LIT-719. CLAIM-410 bears on the paper's point that aid moves
accountability from citizens to donors; THEORY-440 touches its
observations on long tenure and weak legislatures (pp. 15–16).

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-597 (supplied maintenance).** Supports it by analogy, with a
  caveat. Organization fed from outside keeps its share only while the flow
  lasts, and in the paper the flow has lasted decades and grown. The paper
  adds what the claim's model lacks: the supply also lowers the
  organization's own regeneration (moral hazard, atrophy, poaching of
  staff), so dependence deepens. No evidence on decay after withdrawal.
- **QUESTION-398 (independence from the founding intervention).** Partly.
  The route to independence is learning to tax, by learning-by-doing that
  depends on the aid share and initial institutions; Korea, Taiwan and
  Botswana are cited as weaned. No measured test and no further
  conditions.
- **QUESTION-399 (a self-maintaining nucleus).** Own revenue is the
  candidate self-regenerating loop: taxation, accountability, legitimacy
  and capacity, more taxation. Aid substitutes for the loop's input and so
  can block its closure. Interpretive and historical.
- **CLAIM-566.** Illustrates it: donor-built capacity can assemble (central
  bank technical assistance, p. 6) and persist under funding without the
  state regenerating it. Not measured separately.
- **CLAIM-516 and CLAIM-558.** Consistent, loosely: capacities produced only
  by donor projects outside the budget have no internal producer, and where
  donors run budgeting and planning "real developmental capacities may
  atrophy". No rates.
- **THEORY-072, CLAIM-273, QUESTION-085.** An illustration of order supplied
  from outside at the scale of a state, nearer a cell held by an external
  gradient than a flame; nothing on the demarcation itself.
- **QUESTION-386 (funding by observed activity).** An inverse case: aid was
  not tied to performance (p. 10, "until very recently, the government's
  performance did not appear to affect whether or not it received aid");
  the shift to performance-based allocation is noted, not evaluated.
- **TERM-105 (resource types).** Lightly: grants against loans with a
  repayment obligation show the form of the supply matters, the obligation
  acting like a cumulative constraint that keeps tax effort up.
- **CLAIM-598.** Lightly: past an unspecified share, more aid may lower the
  chance of institution building by favouring the donor-facing path.
  Qualitative.
- **TERM-117, TERM-070, CLAIM-520.** Nothing direct.

## Limitations

- **A review with no new identification**; its one analysis, Figure 1, is
  bivariate with thresholds it calls arbitrary.
- **Poor data**, partly not public; confounding by income and
  industrialization not excluded; reverse causation, weak states attracting
  aid, not examined.
- **Mixed cross-country evidence**, several studies insignificant and
  country cases both ways.
- **The accountability channel rests on European analogy** and qualitative
  African case literature.
- **Africa-centred and dated 2006**; later work on aid and taxation is not
  covered.
- **Almost nothing on exit**: what happens to services and institutions
  when aid is withdrawn or tapered is not studied beyond the weaned cases
  and the Marshall Plan contrast.
- **No threshold of its own**; it relays Berg and Clemens and Radelet.
