# THEORY-tmptv00n: Caviola et al. report that laypeople judge adding a very happy person to a world as good as adding a very unhappy one is bad, prefer larger happy populations with averages fixed but less so as numbers grow, reversing at one billion against ten billion, and tend to favour the higher average when average and total conflict

## The account

Lay population ethics fits no single theory. People require more happiness
than suffering for a population to be better existing than not, about 75%,
and about 61% when the two are stated to be equally intense (Studies 1a–c).
Yet they judge adding one person at +100 as good as adding one at −100 is
bad, against the intuition of neutrality and the procreation asymmetry
(Studies 2a–b).

With averages fixed they prefer larger happy and smaller unhappy
populations, but the preference for more happy people fades with numbers,
and one billion happy people were preferred to ten billion (Study 3a). With
totals fixed they prefer the higher average (3b). In conflicts averagism
tends to win, more under instructed intuition than reflection (3c–d).

The authors suggest averagism comes from judging a population as if it were
the distribution of experiences in one life, "such as themselves" (General
Discussion). That is not tested.

## Evidence

- Study 2a (N = 157): adding one at +100, M = 5.06; at −100, M = 3.08;
  reverse-scored, no difference (t(156) = 1.23, p = .22). Study 2b (N = 369)
  agreed.
- Study 3a (N = 789, pre-registered): at one billion against ten billion,
  the larger happy population was dispreferred (t(401) = −2.90).
- Study 3c: instructed intuition was more averagist than reflection, F(1,
  457) = 7.42, ηp² = .02.

The reading lists discrepancies in the preprint, among them an abstract N
that does not match the studies and a printed effect size equal to its mean
(NOTE-1267, Limitations). None changes the direction of a result above.

## What it bears on

QUESTION-299 cites this work under how far concern reaches: "It counts
adding happy people as good, against the person-affecting view (Caviola et
al., LIT-1749)." Its note of 2026-10-29 adds that "the preference fades with
numbers: one billion were preferred to ten billion". The reading qualifies
the citation: the finding is axiological, so a person-affecting view of
obligation survives it (NOTE-1267, Connections).
