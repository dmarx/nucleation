# THEORY-tmptxgpp: People interpreting an analogy carry relations from base to target and drop the attributes of objects, and among the relations they prefer those bound into a system by higher-order relations, such as causal or mathematical constraints, over isolated ones (structure-mapping and its systematicity principle)

## Source

Gentner (1983), LIT-tmpfxrm8, pp. 156–168; read in NOTE-tmpz0kel from a
scan of the published article.

## What was actually shown

**The rules** (p. 158): "Discard attributes of objects"; "Try to preserve
relations between objects"; and "(The Systematicity Principle) To decide
which relations are preserved, choose systems of relations". Attributes
are one-place predicates and relations two-or-more-place ones; a
higher-order relation takes propositions as arguments (p. 157). The
principle: "A predicate that belongs to a mappable system of mutually
interconnecting relationships is more likely to be imported into the
target than is an isolated predicate" (p. 163).

**The argument.** In the Rutherford analogy, MORE MASSIVE THAN (sun,
planet) is mapped and HOTTER THAN (sun, planet) is not, because only the
former belongs to the system of relations tied together by the equations
of central-force motion (pp. 162–164). In "heat is like water", a causal
scenario is preferred to a static one because causal knowledge is
represented as a more deeply embedded structure (pp. 164–165). The
analysis of the 3:6::2:4 analogy shows feature overlap is irrelevant
(p. 156).

**The evidence.** Interpretations written for comparisons such as "A
cigarette is like a time bomb" were judged relational or attributional:
"Relational information predominates over attributional information in
analogy interpretations, but not in object descriptions generated by the
same subjects", and aptness ratings went with the amount of relational
information written (pp. 165–166). No numbers are reported; details are
in a conference paper (Gentner 1980b).

**What could have failed.** Interpretations of analogies could have been
as attribute-laden as descriptions of the objects; they were not, by the
one study's report. Nothing in the paper could have shown systematicity
false: no experiment varies a predicate's place in a higher-order system.

## What this does not say

- **That the attribute/relation split is fixed by the world or by logic.**
  "Logically, a relation R(a,b,c) can perfectly well be represented as
  Q(x) … Psychologically, the representation must be chosen to model the
  way people think about the domain" (n. 4, p. 157). The claim is about
  people's representations, and it inherits whatever choices built them.
- **That systematicity determines the mapping.** The text's own statement
  is a preference (p. 163); "determined by" in the abstract overstates it.
- **That a good analogy is a true one.** Acceptance depends on the
  plausibility of the candidate inferences, "a separate matter" (n. 11,
  p. 165).
- **That matching one relation's values is structure-mapping.** An
  alignment of two spaces by a single distance relation, as in
  Gromov–Wasserstein matching (LIT-tmpprsxm), meets the first two rules
  for that one relation and has no higher-order relations, so it has no
  analogue of the systematicity principle (NOTE-tmpz0kel).
- **A processing model.** The order of processing "is clearly variable"
  (n. 10, p. 164), and no algorithm is given here.
