# NOTE-tmp222eb: Self-categorization and social identification: Making sense of us and them

## Contribution

A secondary source with no new results. It puts self-categorization,
social identification and social identity theory in one sequence:
categorize, identify, compare. It then gives a current survey of
multiple categorization, which neither Turner et al. 1994 nor Hornsey
2008 covers. As a stand-in for Turner et al. 1987 it is thin: the
theory's core gets about two pages (pp. 3–5).

## Key insight

Self-categorization is the cognitive step: placing oneself in a
category. Identification is everything one then attaches to it: value,
importance, attachment, embeddedness, behaviour and meaning. These come
apart; someone can rate a group positively without it mattering to them.
Studies that treat "identification" as one thing are often measuring
different things.

## Assumptions

- A textbook framing. The chapter takes SIT and SCT as one "social
  identity perspective" (p. 1), and presents findings at the level of
  "research showed".
- Motive: a "fundamental need to belong" (Baumeister & Leary 1995) is
  given as why people self-categorize at all (p. 3). Turner et al. 1994
  offers no such motive.

## Key results

- **Self-categorization (pp. 3–5).** It is "placing oneself in a social
  category". Category membership and self-categorization can come apart:
  a German citizen may not self-categorize as German. It is hierarchical
  (personal, social, human; the human level "less relevant", fn. 3) and
  situational. Salience depends on accessibility and fit (Oakes 1987).
  - Accessibility is situational (a woman in a room of men) or chronic (a
    feminist campaigner).
  - Comparative fit is the meta-contrast ratio. Normative fit is
    agreement with stereotypical expectation: in the chapter's example,
    all men choose rugby and all women dance.
  - Depersonalization follows: alignment with the prototype
    (self-stereotyping), and group-based perception and behaviour.
- **Social identification (pp. 5–8).** Tajfel's definition, "that part
  of an individual's self-concept which derives from his knowledge of
  his membership ... together with the value and emotional significance
  attached" (1981, p. 255). Its cognitive, evaluative and affective
  components. Leach et al.'s (2008) two dimensions, self-definition and
  self-investment. Ashmore et al.'s (2004) seven components:
  self-categorization, evaluation (private and public regard),
  importance (explicit and implicit), attachment and interdependence,
  social embeddedness, behavioural involvement, and content and meaning.
  The components "need not converge or correlate". A single-item measure
  is still valid (Postmes, Haslam & Jans 2013).
- **Social identity theory (pp. 8–12).**
  - The minimal group paradigm (Tajfel et al. 1971: Klee and Kandinsky;
    adolescent boys; money allocations).
  - Positive distinctiveness. The chapter says the self-esteem hypothesis
    "has received extensive empirical support" (Aberson, Healy & Romero
    2000; Rubin & Hewstone 1998). It adds that experiments manipulating
    identification increase bias, while correlational studies find only
    weak links.
  - In-group favouritism versus out-group derogation: with negative
    outcomes, minimal groups do not discriminate (Mummendey et al. 1992),
    so mere categorization does not produce out-group harm.
  - Status, permeability, stability and legitimacy select between social
    mobility, social creativity and social competition. High-status
    groups show more bias (meta-analyses). Mobility and creativity can
    justify the system.
- **Other motives (pp. 12–13).** Optimal distinctiveness (Brewer 1991),
  uncertainty reduction (Hogg 2000, 2007), terror management.
- **Multiple categorization (pp. 13–20).**
  - Crossed categorization: mostly an additive pattern, though six other
    patterns occur (Crisp & Hewstone 1999).
  - The common in-group identity model: recategorize "German" as
    "European" and bias falls (Gaertner & Dovidio 2000). It is criticized
    for erasing minority identities. Recategorization into one group
    reduced disadvantaged members' collective-action intentions, and dual
    identity did not (Ufkes et al. 2016).
  - In-group projection: the majority's attributes become the default of
    the common category (Mummendey & Wenzel 1999, derived from SCT).
  - Social identity complexity: higher complexity correlates with
    tolerance (Brewer & Pierce 2005; Schmid et al. 2009, 2013).
  - Identity inclusiveness structures (van Dommelen et al. 2015).
- **Conclusion (pp. 20–21).** Self-categorization and identification "are
  not sufficient to explain negative intergroup attitudes and
  behaviour"; socio-structural and historical context is needed.

## Concepts

- **self-categorization**: placing oneself in a social category and
  thinking of oneself as a member (p. 3).
- **situational versus chronic accessibility** (p. 4).
- **social identification**: perceiving one's group and membership as
  meaningful, desired and important; multidimensional (p. 5).
- **private versus public regard** (p. 6).
- **social mobility / social creativity / social competition** (p. 11).
- **dual identity**: holding a superordinate and a subgroup identity at
  once (p. 15).
- **in-group projection**: taking the in-group's attributes as the
  default of the common category (p. 16).
- **social identity complexity**: overlap and similarity among one's
  in-groups as one represents them (p. 17).

## Connections

- **Hornsey 2008 (NOTE-tmp3dav9).** On the self-esteem hypothesis the two
  disagree. Hornsey calls it a dead end and reads Rubin & Hewstone (1998)
  as supporting only "bias raises self-esteem". This chapter cites the
  same meta-analysis as "extensive empirical support". The difference is
  partly which hypothesis is meant. The chapter states only the
  bias-raises-esteem direction ("positive differentiation enhances
  esteem"), which is the one Hornsey says the meta-analysis favours. So
  they may agree on substance and differ in tone.
- **Turner et al. 1994 (NOTE-tmpf9mzh).** The chapter's account of fit
  follows it closely, but its motive (need to belong) is one the 1994
  paper does not give.
- **Biel (LIT-1133) and Pettigrew (LIT-1408)**, both unread: the common
  in-group identity model and recategorization are the frame of each.

## Bearing on the record

- **CASE-104.** The dual-identity and in-group projection findings bear on
  fans who stay loyal to a club while opposing its owner. A fan can hold
  "supporter of the club" and "opponent of the owner" as nested
  identities. If the owner is cast as speaking for the club, in-group
  projection predicts conflict over what "the club" means. That is an
  inference.
- **THEORY-tmpo85ke.** Adds nothing independent: its account of fit and
  depersonalization is secondary, citing the same sources.

## Limitations

- **Secondary and brief on SCT.** It cannot stand in for the book's
  argument. It reports the theory's conclusions, not its reasoning.
- **A preprint**, not compared with the published chapter.
- **Effect sizes and replication status are not given** for any finding.

## Open questions

- Does the self-esteem hypothesis survive in its bias-raises-esteem form
  in a preregistered test? The disagreement with Hornsey would be settled
  by reading Rubin & Hewstone (1998) and any later replication.
