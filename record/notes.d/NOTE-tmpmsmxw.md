# NOTE-tmpmsmxw: The Afterlife (Tanner Lectures I and II)

## Contribution

It names a belief nearly everyone holds and almost no one examines: that
people will go on living after one dies. It argues, by thought
experiments, that this "collective afterlife" carries much of the weight
of our valuing. It then draws three lessons: about the shape of valuing,
about the limits of egoism and individualism, and about our relation to
future generations.

## Key insight

Our own deaths do not drain our projects of point; humanity's imminent
end would. People pursue cancer research knowing the cure may come after
they die. They would lose the will to pursue it if no one were to come
after them. So the survival of others after we die is "a condition of
other things mattering to us" (Lecture I, part 3). Scheffler's gloss is
not that we are secretly altruistic. "The point has not been that we are
more highly motivated to advance the interests of future generations,
but rather that we are in certain ways more dependent on them" (Lecture
II, part 6).

## Assumptions

- **A "wait-and-see" we.** Claims about "our" attitudes are claims about
  Scheffler's and those of whoever shares them, not universal ones
  (Lecture I, part 1, borrowing from David Lewis).
- **"We" do not believe in a personal afterlife.** The attitudes
  described are, in the first instance, those of non-believers (Lecture I,
  part 1). Believers are taken up in Lecture II, part 6.
- **Valuing is a syndrome**: a belief that the thing is valuable, a
  susceptibility to emotions about it that one takes to be merited, and a
  disposition to treat considerations about it as reasons (Lecture I,
  part 1). Caring and mattering are treated as close enough to it.
- **Conjectures about counterfactual reactions.** Scheffler grants they
  are hard to verify. He stipulates the key one as "the afterlife
  conjecture" (Lecture I, part 9).

## Key results

- **The doomsday scenario** (Lecture I, part 2). You live a normal span;
  the earth is destroyed by an asteroid thirty days after your death.
  - Few would say "So what?". So something after our deaths matters to
    us, and so things other than our experiences matter: valuing is
    *nonexperientialist*. Scheffler cites Nozick's experience machine.
  - Few would weigh the end of all joy against the end of all suffering.
    Valuing is *nonconsequentialist*.
  - We want what we value sustained: valuing is *conservative*. Wills and
    bequests extend our agency past death to that end.
- **What would lose its point** (Lecture I, part 3). Projects whose
  success lies well in the future, or whose value lies in long-term
  benefits to many: research, activism, institution-building,
  infrastructure, the environment. Perhaps creative and scholarly work,
  and having and raising children. Least affected: personal comfort and
  pleasure. Hence "in some significant respects, the existence of the
  afterlife matters more to us than our own continued existence". This
  is not a mere causal condition, like a mineral in the diet: humanity's
  continuation "also matters to us in its own right", and its loss would
  be a reason, not just a cause, for other things to matter less.
- **Instrumental rationality is not the point** (Lecture I, part 3). Of
  course a doomed goal loses its appeal. What is notable is that we
  gladly pursue goals we expect to be reached only after we die.
- **Loved ones and a personalized future** (Lecture I, part 4). We want
  some of those we love to outlive us. Partly from concern for their
  lives "and not merely about the effects on us". Partly to be spared
  grief (loss minimization, "the efficient organization of personal
  disaster"). And partly because their survival personalizes our
  relation to the future: we retain a social identity in an ongoing
  world, and may be remembered. The future becomes "more like a party one
  had to leave early and less like a gathering of strangers".
  - Calling this irrational, Scheffler says, presupposes the
    experientialism he has rejected.
  - Children have a special place, but the desire is not limited to
    parents. Evolutionary explanations of it are "doubly irrelevant".
- **Group-based strategies** (Lecture I, part 5).
  - Traditions are practices "whose organizing purpose is to preserve
    what is valued beyond the life span of any single individual or
    generation". In them one acts as a custodian of values that will be
    one's successors'.
  - Survival of one's community or nation lets one imagine oneself "as
    retaining a social identity in the world of the future", with no
    belief that one survives. Groups outlast individuals.
  - The two strategies are one: "a unified attempt to defend and extend
    the coherence and integrity of our selves and our values over time".
- **Not only particular people** (Lecture I, part 7). The desire for a
  personalized future seems to need particular people or groups. But
  cancer research would lose its point without anyone one loves dying.
  In P. D. James's *Children of Men* no one dies early, yet the coming
  extinction is a catastrophe. So there is "another powerful element
  ... the impact that the imminent end of humanity as such would have on
  us".
- **The infertile world** (Lecture I, part 8). Scheffler finds James's
  picture plausible: apathy, anomie, despair, eroded institutions, lost
  conviction. Four kinds of activity are threatened: goal-directed
  projects, work that needs a future audience, activities that sustain
  values and traditions over time, and work for particular groups. James
  suggests a fifth: enjoyment of nature, art, knowledge and food. Our
  idea of "a human life as a whole" may assume a place in "a temporally
  extended chain of lives and generations".
- **The limits of egoism** (Lecture I, part 9). Everyone now alive will
  soon die, and no one treats that as a crisis. Yet the infertile world,
  equally inevitable, is a catastrophe. So "the coming into existence of
  people we do not know and love matters more to us than our own
  survival and the survival of the people we do know and love". He
  allows that part of this is concern for "particular groups with which
  we specially identify". Three objections are answered:
  - *Disappointed expectations*: a generation raised knowing it was the
    last would, he thinks, lack confidence from the start.
  - *"The end of society"*: that only redescribes concern about the
    nonexistence of future people.
  - *Egoism after all*: the young would have no one to care for them in
    old age. Scheffler grants this "some merit" but not the whole story:
    with caregiver robots guaranteed, the scenario would still be a
    catastrophe.
- **Whose survival** (Lecture I, n. 13). Human beings, persons, or people
  who share our values? Most of us hope future people share our values,
  but humanity's survival "also matters to us in a way that is not
  exhausted by this concern", even knowing its values will change.
  Nonhuman persons might console; they would not give "a personalized
  relation to the future".
- **Three dependency theses** (Lecture II, part 1). *Attitudinal*: what
  matters to us depends on our confidence in the afterlife. *Evaluative*:
  things are valuable only if there is in fact an afterlife.
  *Justificatory*: we are justified in valuing them only if there is. A
  drug that gave a false belief in the afterlife would make things seem,
  not be, worth doing. The conjecture directly supports only the first,
  and only indirectly our acceptance of the other two.
- **Exceptions** (Lecture II, part 1): relief from severe pain,
  friendship (probably), the afterlife itself ("the afterlife matters to
  us whether there will be an afterlife or not"), and games, whose
  stakes were already pointless.
- **The limits of individualism** (Lecture II, part 2). Much individual
  valuing, however solitary its content, has an implicit collective
  dimension. "Humanity itself as an ongoing historical project provides
  the implicit frame of reference". Indeed "we need humanity to have a
  future for the very idea that things matter to retain a secure place
  in our conceptual repertoire".
- **Valuing and time** (Lecture II, part 3). Valuing is diachronic: one
  acquires a stake in how what one values fares. It is *risky*, since we
  give hostages to fortune, and *proprietary*: "we lay claim to the
  future". To value is to resist time's normative authority.
- **Why the far future does not trouble us** (Lecture II, part 4).
  Equanimity needs humanity to last a "healthy and indefinitely long
  period", not forever. Scheffler offers no reason for this; he thinks we
  cannot integrate "billions of years" into judgements of importance.
- **The two afterlives** (Lecture II, part 5). The personal afterlife has
  mattered for survival, reunion with loved ones, justice and
  explanation, and meaning. The collective afterlife delivers none of the
  first four. Both reveal two desires: to keep one's place in a web of
  valued relationships, and to live in a just world. Non-believers in a
  personal afterlife live value-laden lives; loss of the collective
  afterlife would erode them. So "there is a very specific sense in which
  our own survival is less important to us than the survival of the
  human race". Scheffler distinguishes this from Johnston's *Surviving
  Death*, on which some of us "can hope to survive our own deaths", and
  from Parfit, on whom understanding personal identity might make others'
  survival matter more (n. 10, n. 11). His claim is that it already does.
- **Dependence, not motivation** (Lecture II, part 6).
  - Believers may need only one afterlife or the other. Scheffler grants
    this and adds a danger: belief in a personal afterlife may reconcile
    people too readily to the end of life on earth.
  - "Care more" can mean more motivated or more dependent. The arguments
    support the second. Our surprise shows less that "we have been
    blinded to our own altruism than ... that we have overestimated our
    independence and self-sufficiency".
  - So we have reasons to attend to future generations beyond justice
    and responsibility, "simply because they matter so much to us". Their
    causal dependence on us becomes an opportunity: the collective
    afterlife, unlike the personal one, is partly under our control
    (climate change, nuclear proliferation).
  - In some contexts we are also more motivated. Offered an earlier death
    with humanity surviving, or a longer life as the last human,
    Scheffler would not hesitate to take the first. But "it would be
    misleading to interpret these preferences as evidence of hitherto
    unsuspected reserves of altruism". His reason is that a world without
    other people would be a world without value; he would decline to live
    on alone even if everyone, himself included, would otherwise die. That
    shows the limits of individualism, not of egoism.
  - Kolodny's variant (n. 12): die sooner and humanity survives, or live
    to old age and humanity perishes when you do. Choosing the first
    "cannot be because of an unwillingness to live as the only human
    being on earth". Scheffler raises it and leaves it.

## Concepts

- **the afterlife (collective afterlife)**: the continued existence of
  other people after one's own death; opposed to the personal afterlife,
  one's own survival.
- **afterlife conjecture**: in an infertile world people would lose
  confidence in the value of many activities, cease to see reasons for
  them and become emotionally detached from them.
- **personalized relation to the future**: conceiving the future after
  one's death as a social world in which one retains a social identity,
  through loved ones, groups or traditions that survive.
- **attitudinal, evaluative and justificatory dependency**: of what
  matters to us on our confidence in the afterlife; of what is valuable on
  its existence; of our being justified in valuing on its existence.
- **limits of egoism / limits of individualism**: the first concerns the
  power of self-interested motives relative to others; the second, how
  far individual valuing is part of a collective enterprise.
- **conservatism of valuing**: valuing something involves wanting it
  preserved and seeing reasons to preserve it.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** The record filed Scheffler as
  the hardest case for the thesis in time. Read, he is a mixed case. These
  are inferences; Scheffler does not discuss the self's extent.
  - *Against the thesis.* He finds a concern for future people that is
    not for one's experiences, not only for loved ones or one's groups,
    and not explained by self-interest in the ordinary sense: the robot
    caregivers remove it. He invokes no extended self and expressly
    declines Johnston's survival through humanity.
  - *But he denies it is altruism.* What he finds is dependence. The
    future matters because, without it, our present valuing fails. Even
    his own choice to die sooner so that humanity survives he explains by
    what a world without people would be for him, a world without value.
    So the concern he documents is for the continuation of the frame in
    which the living value, not for the good of future persons. He never
    claims we care about their welfare.
  - *The thesis can claim him only by redefining the self served.* Here
    it is neither welfare nor, as in the fusion paper ([NOTE-tmp3g9n4](NOTE-tmp3g9n4.md)), a
    self-concept kept true, but one's standing as a valuer: the
    "coherence and integrity of our selves and our values over time".
    That is a third sense of "self-interest", and [CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md) applies
    to it with force.
  - *His own pair of terms fits the thesis better than "egoism".* He
    separates the limits of egoism (how strong self-interested motives
    are) from the limits of individualism (how far valuing is collective)
    and puts his main result under the second. The thesis says the self
    extends; Scheffler says the individual's valuing depends on a
    collective that need not be part of the self. The second is the
    weaker claim and is all the lectures support.
  - *Where he does fit the thesis.* The personalized relation to the
    future, through loved ones, nation and tradition, is concern for a
    world in which one "retains a social identity". That is the thesis's
    extended self in time, and Scheffler presents it as one strand, not
    the whole.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** The attitudinal
  dependency thesis is a test that can come apart from caring.
  Scheffler's exceptions show it: we care about relief from pain and about
  friends without that care depending on humanity's future. A criterion
  of the self's extent in time could be built on it: what one's present
  valuing depends on. That is an inference, and the lectures test it only
  by imagined reactions.
- **The legacy question ([QUESTION-tmpruhu4](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md)).** Of the question's three
  readings, Scheffler's is not "care for a future that does nothing for
  us" as the question puts it. He says the future does a great deal for
  us: it keeps our valuing secure. The question's summary should say so.
- **Obligations to the future ([QUESTION-tmpmx9ha](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.md)).** He grounds reasons,
  not obligations, in what the living value, and says that framing makes
  our power over future people an opportunity rather than a burden.

## Limitations

- **Conjecture, by design.** Every result rests on how Scheffler expects
  "we" would react to fictions. He says so, and stipulates the key one.
- **Non-believers only**, in the first instance.
- **Not the book.** The lectures as revised for the Tanner site; the
  2013 text, the third essay, the commentaries and the replies are not
  read here.
- **Copy.** The accessible HTML has no page numbers, unnumbered
  footnote texts, and run-together or split words from conversion
  ("contextdependent", "nonconsequential ist", "partic ularistic"). The
  author's note at the head breaks off: "where he joined the faculty in"
  with no year (the lecture page gives 2008).
- **Typos**:
  - "providing with them with thoughtfully preprogrammed caregiver
    robots" (Lecture I, part 9);
  - "we care less about own survival" for "our own" (Lecture II, part 6);
  - "its only a matter of time" for "it's" (Lecture I, part 4).

## Open questions

- Would people actually lose confidence in their projects if told no one
  would come after them? An experiment that varied whether a project's
  beneficiaries would exist, holding the agent's own life fixed, would
  test the attitudinal dependency thesis directly.
- Does the dependence follow identification with humanity or with one's
  groups? Measuring identification alongside the loss of confidence would
  show whether the thesis's extended self carries it.
- What do Wolf, Frankfurt, Shiffrin and Kolodny say? Shiffrin's title,
  "Preserving the Valued or Preserving Valuing?" (Crossref), names this
  NOTE's question.
