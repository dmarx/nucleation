# LIT-632: Comparing the Major Theories of Consciousness

Ned Block (2009), "Comparing the major theories of consciousness", in M. S.
Gazzaniga (ed.), *The Cognitive Neurosciences*, 4th edition, Cambridge, MA:
MIT Press, chapter 77, pp. 1111–1122.

## Key takeaways

- **Three theories.** *Higher-order thought (HOT)*: an experience is
  phenomenally conscious in virtue of a thought that one has it.
  *Global workspace*: consciousness is global broadcasting of a winning
  coalition to frontal consumers; it abstracts from neural details and is a
  form of functionalism. *Biological*: consciousness is a biological state
  of the brain, illustrated by a recurrent MT+ representation of motion
  plus a background such as the "thalamic switch".
- **The explanatory gap as the test.** For HOT, why a thought about an
  unconscious representation should make it conscious is a puzzle of HOT's
  own making, like a "prime order thought" theory's puzzle about primes,
  and not the real gap. The same holds for the workspace: why broadcasting
  is conscious would arise equally for an electronic workspace. Only the
  biological theory states the gap as it is.
- **Gap without dualism.** Following Nagel and Loar, subjectivity and
  objectivity are properties of concepts, so Mary acquires a new concept of
  a fact she knew. The gap is a failure to see how a subjective and an
  objective concept could pick out the same thing.
- **Against ambitious HOT.** Its conditions are stipulated to avoid
  counterexamples ("the advantages of theft over honest toil"); it faces the
  mismatch problem between a HOT and the sensory state it is about; and our
  case for experience in infants and autistic children exceeds our case for
  higher-order thought in them.
- **Machines.** The workspace and HOT views are friendly to machine
  consciousness. On the biological view it may matter that neural coding
  passes from electrical to chemical and back, so only machines with the
  right biology could be conscious.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, with six other papers by Block
on consciousness. No anthology topic holds a comparison of theories of
consciousness. The section on machines is an argument about which theories
allow machine consciousness, not an instruction for machine-learning
practice.

It is the "Block 2009" that Chalmers's meta-problem paper (read in [NOTE-354](../notes.d/NOTE-354.md))
names as the source of "biological theories", against which it argues that
non-conscious silicon duplicates would share our intuitions. It carries
forward the concept-dualist reply to the gap of Block (2002),
[LIT-639](LIT-639.md), in a shorter form, and leans on Block (2007), [LIT-646](LIT-646.md),
for the claim that the biological theory allows experience a subject does
not know about.

**Bearing on [THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md).** That theory holds that architectural indicators
presuppose computational functionalism. This chapter states why the
global workspace, the source of four of Butlin et al.'s indicators
([LIT-056](LIT-056.md)), is a functionalist theory, whose architecture "can just as easily be
realized in silicon-based computers as in protoplasm", and states the biological alternative on which those
indicators would not suffice. It is a clear statement of the rival premise
that theory says the evidence cannot yet decide.

**On integrated information.** The chapter calls the integrated information
theory functionalist and criticises it as unable to tell consciousness
from intelligence, since a seizure lowers both. It discusses the 1998
Tononi–Edelman version, not IIT 3.0 ([LIT-445](LIT-445.md), read in [NOTE-365](../notes.d/NOTE-365.md)), and offers
integration as an adjunct to a biological theory. No relation is
declared.
