## Key takeaways

*Registered from the publisher's description, the author's abstract and the
chapter abstracts, not a reading. What follows is what the book says about
itself.*

The author's abstract describes four parts:

- **Part One** discusses "some ways in which theories about morality and
  rationality can be self-defeating", giving aims while telling us "to act in
  ways that frustrate these aims".
- **Part Two** discusses what one person can rationally want or do at
  different times, against what different people can, and "the rationality
  of four attitudes to time: temporal neutrality, and the three kinds of bias
  towards the present, the near, and the future".
- **Part Three** discusses personal identity: "Most of us, I argue, have
  certain beliefs about this subject that are false, but hard to give up. If
  we accept these claims, they may change some of our emotions or attitudes,
  and we should revise some of our beliefs about both rationality and
  morality."
- **Part Four** discusses obligations to future generations. "The most
  difficult question here, which I fail to answer, is about the relative
  importance of the number of people who will exist, and the quality of life
  of these people."

Part III's six chapters, as their abstracts on Oxford Academic describe
them (pages of the 1986 printing):

- **"What We Believe Ourselves to Be"** (pp. 199–218): numerical and
  qualitative identity; the Physical Criterion ("the continued existence of
  enough of our brain") and the Psychological Criterion ("overlapping chains
  of psychological continuity and connectedness"); our belief that our
  identity "must be determinate".
- **"How We Are not What We Believe"** (pp. 219–244): the Psychological
  Criterion is not circular; Cartesian egos and how a non-reductionist view
  "might have been true"; "spectrum arguments against both the Physical and
  Psychological Criteria".
- **"Why Our Identity Is not What Matters"** (pp. 245–280): brain bisection,
  the unity of consciousness, and full division, "why neither of the
  resulting people would be us; why this would not matter, since our relation
  to each of these people contains what matters in the prudential sense".
- **"What Does Matter"** (pp. 281–306): whether the body matters; "why it
  does not matter whether psychological continuity has its normal cause";
  the Branch-Line Case, series-persons, partial survival and successive
  selves.
- **"Personal Identity and Rationality"** (pp. 307–320): an argument
  "against the Classical Self-interest Theory", with a discount rate "with
  respect to the degree of psychological connectedness" rather than time.
- **"Personal Identity and Morality"** (pp. 321–348): autonomy and
  paternalism, desert, commitments, and the separateness of persons, with "a
  reductionist view" giving "some support to the utilitarian rejection of
  distributive principles".

Appendices relevant to Part III, by title: "Nagel's Brain" (pp. 469–477),
"The Closest Continuer Schema" (pp. 478–479) and "Buddha's View" (pp.
503–504).
