# NOTE-tmp0e1ym: Kuppens, Van Mechelen, Smits & De Boeck — The appraisal basis of anger

## Contribution

It asks of appraisal theory a question the field had left implicit: is a
given appraisal necessary for an emotion, sufficient for it, or neither?
By instructing people to recall situations in which a component was
absent, as well as present, it could compute how far each component's
presence is implied by anger and implies it. For anger the answer was
neither. That moved the claim from "anger goes with these appraisals",
which it confirmed, to "they are contingently associated", which most
strong statements of appraisal theory did not allow (p. 266).

## Key insight

Agreement across studies on which appraisals go with which emotion does
not show that any appraisal is required. Sample situations where the
appraisal is absent and anger still turns up, and the "syndrome" picture,
in which an emotion's components are tightly bound to it, fails (pp.
266–267).

## Assumptions

- **Recall under instruction samples the space.** Asking for "three
  unpleasant situations in which ... someone else was not accountable"
  yields situations where the component was absent; the study 2
  manipulation check supports this (p. 263).
- **Self-report measures appraisal, action tendency and emotion.** All are
  rated on 0–3 scales, or for emotion also named freely (study 2).
- **Dichotomizing ratings** (0–1 against 2–3, with alternatives) gives
  presence and absence for the necessity and sufficiency indices
  (pp. 259–260).
- **Logical, not causal, relations.** "The issue of necessity and
  sufficiency is addressed in logical terms only, without aiming to make
  causal claims"; the authors take no position on whether appraisals are
  elicitors or content of emotions, citing Frijda (1993) and Solomon
  (p. 257).
- **Only unpleasant situations** were sampled, and only four negative
  emotion families (fear, anger, shame, sadness, after Diener et al. 1995).

## Key results

- **Positions tested (pp. 256–257).** Smith and Lazarus (1993) "consider
  appraisal to be a sufficient condition of emotion, as well as a
  necessary one"; Roseman and Smith (2001) claim "strong and invariant
  one-to-one relationships"; and defining emotions as "felt changes in
  action tendency or action readiness (Arnold, 1960; Frijda, 1986 ...)"
  implies "a relation of equivalence between them (necessity plus
  sufficiency)" (p. 256). Against these, Scherer (2001), Parkinson and
  Reisenzein allow contingent links.
- **Study 1 (pp. 257–261).** 110 Flemish high-school seniors (of 119;
  mean age 17.5), randomly assigned to one of eight instructions (four
  components, present or absent; Table 1), each describing three
  unpleasant situations from school or work, close relations and leisure,
  then rating 16 emotion words (305 situations). Anger was associated with
  frustration, other accountability and arrogant entitlement (Table 2:
  regression coefficients .39, .59 and .91); other accountability and
  arrogant entitlement were specific to anger. The antagonistic action
  tendency ("you wanted to do something about the situation") was
  associated with no emotion, which the authors put down to a weak
  wording (p. 261). Necessity and sufficiency indices were "considerably
  lower than 1" for every component and every emotion (p. 260) and
  "generally did not exceed a value of .40" (p. 261).
- **Study 2 (pp. 261–265).** 100 university students (mean age 20.5), ten
  instructions (goal obstacle, other accountability, unfairness, control,
  antagonistic action tendency, each present or absent; Table 3, wordings
  taken from earlier studies), 300 situations, an open emotion question
  before the fixed list, and ratings of each component. Manipulation
  checks: present instructions gave component ratings more than a scale
  point higher, p < .001 (p. 263). Anger was associated with other
  accountability (.15, p = .03) and unfairness (.29, p < .001), marginally
  with the action tendency "you wanted to express your opposition" (.12,
  p = .09), not with goal obstacle or control (Table 4). In free reports,
  anger was named in 55% of situations. Other accountability was specific
  to anger; unfairness also went with sadness.
- **No necessity, no sufficiency (p. 265).** All indices, by forced-choice
  or free report and by instruction or rated component, "clearly reflected
  lack of necessity and sufficiency of each individual component":
  "participants reported a nonnegligible number of situations in which a
  component was present, but in which they had not experienced anger, and
  vice versa."
- **At least one component (p. 267).** "There is, indeed, not a single
  reported instance in which anger occurs when all the components under
  study are absent." The authors suggest anger may need some, not all,
  central components, or that people differ in which appraisals they need.
- **Conclusion (p. 266).** Relations should be "specified in terms of
  contingent connections ... meaning that they usually co-occur, instead
  of in terms of necessity (or sufficiency)"; "a deterministic 'syndrome
  theory' of emotions ... is no longer tenable".

## Concepts

- **Specificity** — a component associated with one emotion and not the
  others studied.
- **Necessity / sufficiency (asymmetric phi)** — for necessity,
  (P(component | emotion) − P(component)) / (1 − P(component)); for
  sufficiency the same with the roles swapped. 1 means strict necessity or
  sufficiency, 0 no association (pp. 259–260).
- **Contingent association** — components and emotion "usually co-occur"
  without either implying the other (p. 266).
- **Arrogant entitlement** — "someone else arrogated something to
  him/herself which he/she had no right to", study 1's instance of
  unfairness (Table 1).

## Connections

It tests appraisal theory in general, and names Frijda among those whose
definition of emotion as change in action readiness implies necessity and
sufficiency (p. 256). It cites the prototype approach (Shaver et al. 1987)
and Russell and Fehr's semantic analyses as consistent with no necessary
features (p. 266). Parkinson's (1997) "at best ... a contingent connection"
is the position it supports. Barrett's constructionist account
([THEORY-057](../theory.d/THEORY-057.md), from [LIT-542](../literature.d/LIT-542.md)), which the record holds as the rival of
Frijda's, makes the same kind of prediction about category instances.

## Bearing on the record

- **THEORY-055: it weighs only on the claim that emotions differ by mode,
  in a kind of evidence its `promote_when` says cannot settle it.** The
  `promote_when` sets aside "correlations between people's appraisal
  reports and their emotion labels, since the rival reads appraisals as
  descriptions produced by the same categorization". This design is a
  refinement of that kind, not a way out of it. What it manipulates is
  which memories people recall (an instruction to recall situations with
  or without an appraisal), not the event or its pertinence to a concern;
  and appraisals, action tendency and emotion are all reported by the
  same person about the same recalled episode. The authors themselves
  treat necessity and sufficiency "in logical terms only, without aiming
  to make causal claims" (p. 257). So it is not the result the account's
  `promote_when` asks for ("holding the event fixed and changing only its
  pertinence to the person's concerns"), nor the rival's fatal one.
  - **The elicitation thesis is not reached.** Anger did occur where
    people denied goal obstacles. But every situation sampled was
    unpleasant, so concern pertinence may have held throughout (see
    Limitations), and the paper cannot show anger without it.
  - **The mode thesis is weighed against, weakly.** Wanting to express
    opposition, the nearest item to Frijda's mode of hostility, was only
    marginally associated with anger and was neither necessary nor
    sufficient for it (study 2). The paper names Frijda's definition of
    emotion as change in action readiness as implying equivalence
    (p. 256). The action-tendency wording failed in study 1, and the
    finding is one self-report item.
  - The one finding that leans the other way, that no anger was reported
    with every component absent (p. 267), is post hoc. Because the rival
    account also predicts instances with no common feature, this result
    does not tell the two apart.
- **[THEORY-057](../theory.d/THEORY-057.md).** Consistent with its denial of necessary features for
  category instances; the paper cites the prototype view in support. The
  same self-report limit applies.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Self-report of recalled episodes** for every variable, so shared method
  variance and reconstruction in memory may link or unlink them.
- **The manipulation is of recall, not of situations.** Instructions shift
  which situations are recalled; they do not fix an event and vary an
  appraisal.
- **Only unpleasant situations** and four negative emotion families.
  Unpleasantness itself was required of every situation, so a
  concern-relevance condition may have been met throughout.
- **Item wording mattered.** The action tendency failed in study 1 and was
  marginal in study 2; goal obstacle behaved differently from frustration
  (p. 265). Other wordings might yield other indices.
- **Group-level indices.** The authors raise individual differences in
  which appraisals are necessary (p. 267) and do not test them.
- **Figures not seen.** The plotted index values in Figures 2 and 5 were not
  read; the text's summary ("generally did not exceed a value of .40",
  study 1) is relied on.

## Open questions

- With events held fixed and appraisals manipulated (by vignette or in the
  laboratory), do the same components prove unnecessary for anger?
- Is "at least one component" a real disjunctive condition, or a sampling
  artifact of recall instructions that always named one?
- Do individuals have their own necessary appraisals for anger?
