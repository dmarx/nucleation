# NOTE-tmp0gu2z: Dekker and Nyce — Just culture: "Evidence", power and algorithms

## Contribution

An argument that "just culture", as hospitals implement it through
decision algorithms, cannot deliver the neutrality it promises: the
classification of an error as forgivable or culpable is made by someone,
from somewhere in a hierarchy, and is judged just mostly by those at the
top of it.

## Key results

- **The case** (p. 73): Kimberley Hiatt, a nurse whose patient died of a
  calcium chloride overdose, "was first suspended, and then fired", and
  later took her own life.
- **The algorithm** (p. 74): it "distinguishes between human error,
  at-risk behavior, and reckless action"; errors lead to reflection on
  "clinical processes and procedures, training or design", at-risk
  behaviour to coaching, and reckless behaviour to sanction "including
  possible dismissal" (after Marx 2001).
- **Its assumption** (p. 74): it "takes the reality of someone's behavior,
  as well as the intentions behind it, as unambiguously and objectively
  available to others—for them to appraise and judge."
- **Justice depends** (p. 75): "It depends on where one is located in the
  organization and medical competence hierarchy, and who gets to say."
- **The survey they relay** (p. 75; von Thaden et al., 2006): almost 2,000
  staff across 12 US facilities; "efforts at reporting and feedback
  receiving the most positive assessment, yet accountability the worst";
  "a widespread perception of negative repercussions for reporting and
  fears of blame"; "The most powerful group (doctors) was most likely to
  see responses as just."
- **Whose interest** (p. 75): "A conclusion of wrongdoing could owe more
  to a hospital's risk manager's fears (of liability, loss of reputation
  or political influence)". Just-culture consultancy "has been seen as as
  a way to restore management control over staff performance after the
  rise of emancipatory practices (and policies) that tended to blame the
  system, not the worker", citing Reason (1997), Dekker (2009) and Wachter
  (2008) together.
- **The risk** (p. 75): just culture "risks becoming one more rhetoric or
  metric by which dominant groups can judge others."

## Assumptions

Hospitals, after harm to patients, where a sanction on an individual is
on the table. The argument is interpretive, drawing on the sociology of
evidence-based medicine; the one empirical result is someone else's
survey, relayed without its numbers.

## Concepts

- **Just culture**: an organization's scheme for deciding when an error
  calls for learning and when for sanction.
- **Just-culture-by-algorithm**: that scheme run as a decision procedure on
  intention, volition and repetition.
- **Second victim**: the clinician harmed by having harmed a patient.

## Bearing on the record

Filed as THEORY-tmpfomub. Read as the neighbour of Dekker (2007),
LIT-tmpfucfe; it does not restate the book.

- **QUESTION-476**, its third answer ("blameless" in practice relocates
  blame). Dekker and Nyce argue that a just-culture scheme can do exactly
  that, sorting blame by rank. Bears on, as argument and a relayed survey.
- **CLAIM-tmp5g7xc** (the trace stops at the reviewing body's authority).
  The mechanism the claim needs is that whoever governs the review shapes
  what it finds; "who gets to say" is that mechanism, argued for hospitals.
  Corroborates the mechanism, not the prediction about postmortems.
- **CLAIM-707.** The Hiatt case and the survey are the crumple zone seen
  from inside a just-culture scheme: the nurse dismissed, the most powerful
  group the most satisfied. No relation is written: the paper does not
  show where control over the conditions lay.
- **Reason (1997)**, LIT-tmpcqntz, is cited in a string of three for the
  "restore management control" sentence; what Reason himself says is not
  shown.

## Limitations

- An essay. The survey is relayed without figures, and the authors ran no
  study.
- Hospitals only, and a sanction-sorting scheme. It is not about blameless
  postmortems, which sort nothing, though the relocation of blame it
  describes is QUESTION-476's third answer.
- One of the authors wrote the book it stands in for; it is not a
  summary of that book.
