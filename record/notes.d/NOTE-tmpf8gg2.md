# NOTE-tmpf8gg2: Cuéllar — The New Presidential Adjudication

## Contribution

It names "presidential adjudication" as a category cutting across
executive orders and agency action, identifies a newer, less constrained
form of it, defines "executive coercion" as its distinctive risk, and
places a doctrinal remedy between the status quo and onerous review.

## Key insight

Decisions about particular people and entities, which conventional
adjudication surrounds with procedure, are increasingly made by the
President with procedure bypassed; the target pays before any court
decides.

## Assumptions

- Some presidential adjudication is legitimate, especially in foreign
  affairs; the problem is its unconstrained form.
- Courts can calibrate review through existing doctrine (arbitrariness
  review, statutory limits, the foreign/domestic line, a defeasible
  presumption of regularity).
- Weber's tension between bureaucratic and charismatic authority frames
  the analysis.

## Key results

- **What it is** (abstract, p. 597): presidents increasingly "make
  fact-specific, high-stakes decisions about sanctions, investigations,
  contracts, legal interpretations, and enforcement, with limited or
  minimal judicial oversight".
- **The risk** (abstract, p. 597): "This development heightens the risk of
  the presidency devolving into a roving tribunal with vast discretionary
  power to make deals and impose arbitrary costs on individuals and
  institutions."
- **Executive coercion** (p. 626): "the intentional use of presidential
  power against a specific target with the goal of extracting concessions
  valuable to the President or the administration but unrelated to either
  public policy or the purpose behind the grant of power itself".
- **Agency adjudication drawn in** (p. 617): "Put differently, presidential
  adjudication now threatens to extend to the fact-specific applications
  of statutory authority that were historically meant to be taken up by
  impartial adjudicators." And: "These norms are quickly eroding."
- **Speed** (p. 627): "Presidential adjudication can lower the cost of
  monitoring and reviewing agency decisions and increase the President's
  capacity to act swiftly in the face of sudden crises and
  opportunities."
- **Cost before correction** (p. 647): "First, the White House can
  successfully target opponents even when courts shut down an initial line
  of attack." The tools allow attacks "that might be thwarted by a court
  eventually but levy a high cost on those targeted in the meantime".
- **Remedy, judicial** (p. 643): "A harder look at presidential
  adjudication would still call for a lighter touch than in the review of
  intricate, agency-level rulemaking determinations."; its limit in time
  (p. 645): "Moreover, such a harder look generally would only occur after
  the sort of final agency action that tends to be necessary for judicial
  review".
- **Remedy, civic** (p. 648): governors and civil society "can also create
  friction and slow down White House edicts"; "a court decision buys time
  for broader civic deliberations on specific presidential
  adjudications".
- **Conclusion** (p. 651): without change, "the country risks having the
  President become an insulated, virtually unreviewable, one-man judge".

## Concepts

Presidential adjudication; the new presidential adjudication; executive
coercion; government by dealmaking; roving tribunal; "harder look" review;
defeasible presumption of regularity; foreign-affairs exceptionalism;
Londoner/Bi-Metallic; Kadi justice; DOGE; civic friction.

## Verdict on the exchange's characterization

Faithful. A25's "particularized, consequential decisions ... with
constrained opportunities for independent scrutiny" matches the abstract.
Placing it beside the owner's U13 on the speed of executive action is the
record's: speed is incidental to Cuéllar, whose subject is arbitrariness
and the scarcity of review.

## Limitations

A Forum essay: argument illustrated from 2025–2026 events, not systematic
evidence. The "harder look" is a label for several doctrinal moves rather
than a test. US-specific. Part II and III.A were skimmed.
