# LIT-2670: Fiduciary Duties and Moral Blackmail

Simon Keller (2016; issue 2018). Fiduciary Duties and Moral Blackmail. *Journal of Applied Philosophy* 35(3): 481–495. DOI 10.1111/japp.12234. Read from the author's preprint (pages 1–30).

## Key takeaways

Moral blackmail: a wrongful change to someone's circumstances that makes her alternatives morally
unacceptable, creating a genuine obligation she is wronged in having. Fiduciary work predictably
generates special obligations that exceed and outlast the role, which leaves carers open to it, from
beneficiaries and from employers. It makes striking harder for nurses and teachers than for software
developers.

## Reading of 2026-11-16

Read in full in the author's pre-print (30 pp.); [NOTE-2005](../notes.d/NOTE-2005.md) holds the reading and [THEORY-1917](../theory.d/THEORY-1917.md) its account.
Keller's opening case, a neighbour left with a dog that will starve unless she minds it, is the case
A13 of the exchange retells as Alice and Bob. His definition (p. 20) and his conclusion that "you can
have a genuine moral obligation because you have been treated wrongly" (p. 21) are what A13
attributes to him, and they corroborate [CLAIM-703](../claims.d/CLAIM-703.md). The exchange's limit on the created
obligation ("does not establish that the obligation is unlimited") is A's, not Keller's. A13 §3's
teachers are Keller's, with a government official as the blackmailer (p. 24). Keller adds three things
the exchange did not report: the manoeuvre works through what the victim believes (p. 21); it can work
when it is common knowledge (p. 23); and caring roles carry an unpaid "moral burden" that would
justify "greater pay or improved conditions" (p. 26), a burden that falls disproportionately on women.

## Reading of 2026-11-17

Read a second time on 2026-11-17 by the continuation's readings and reconciled with the reading above on 2026-11-18: [NOTE-2005](../notes.d/NOTE-2005.md) now carries both readings and [THEORY-1917](../theory.d/THEORY-1917.md) both accounts. What that reading recorded here:

**The exchange's characterization: faithful, with slips of detail.** A13's
"someone can wrongfully place another person under a real moral obligation
and exploit that obligation for their own ends" matches pp. 21 and 25. The
dog example is Keller's (pp. 1–2); the names "Alice" and "Bob" are A's.
The teachers example is Keller's (p. 24), with both of A's horns, leverage
and "extra ammunition" in "the publicity war"; but his actor is "a
government official in charge of teachers' employment", not
"Administrators", and the teachers commit no wrong: striking is their
"right", and the publicity is a smear. A's use of the example in
[CLAIM-726](../claims.d/CLAIM-726.md), as a true accusation of a real wrong, goes beyond the
paper.
