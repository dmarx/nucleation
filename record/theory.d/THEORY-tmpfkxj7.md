# THEORY-tmpfkxj7: Cesarini, Lindqvist, Notowidigdo and Östling find, using lottery prizes randomly assigned among Swedish players, that an unconditional wealth shock reduces the winner's gross labour earnings by about 1.1% of the prize a year, beginning in the year of the win and lasting more than ten years, mostly through fewer hours and varying little with age, sex, education or prize size; non-winning spouses reduce theirs by less than half as much, and the authors call the response modest

## Source

Cesarini, Lindqvist, Notowidigdo & Östling (2017), LIT-tmphx3z0; read in
NOTE-tmp2or0k in NBER Working Paper 21762 (November 2015). Page numbers
and numbers are the working paper's.

## What was actually shown

- **Random wealth.** Prizes in three Swedish lotteries are random within
  cells fixed by each lottery's rules; players aged 21–64 at the win are
  followed in tax and wage registers for 1991–2010 (pp. 5–11).
- **Size and timing** (Table 2, p. 13): per 100 SEK won, −1.152 at t = 1,
  −1.177 at t = 2, −8.033 cumulated over ten years; event-study estimate
  −1.068 (s.e. 0.149), N = 249,278. "In the year of the lottery event,
  labor earnings decline immediately and stabilize at a level roughly
  1,150 SEK lower for each 100,000 SEK won." Pre-trends show "no
  statistically significant difference".
- **Margins** (Table 4, pp. 15–16): a 1M SEK prize cuts hours by 3.11% of
  full time, 1.3 hours a week; participation falls 2.07 points on 77.3%,
  about half the response at first and a third six years on.
- **Little heterogeneity** (pp. 16–17): by prize size "the difference is
  not statistically significant", and the point estimates make the
  marginal effect smaller for larger prizes; similar by age in the first
  years, sex, education, lottery and form of payment.
- **Spouses** (Table 7, pp. 26–27): married winners −0.981, spouses
  −0.458, difference −0.522 (p = 0.059).
- **What could have come out otherwise.** A temporary holiday from work would have
  shown a response fading within a few years; it is roughly flat to year
  ten. A unitary household would have shown equal responses whichever
  spouse won.

## What this does not say

- That a windfall erodes capability: earnings and hours were measured,
  not skill, and no one changed occupation or employer (p. 16).
- What happens when support is withdrawn: the prizes never were.
- That winners keep their wealth. The paper says "lottery winners do not
  squander their wealth" (p. 11), but the text read gives no wealth
  trajectory or bankruptcy outcome; it rests on appendix tables not read.
- That there is a size threshold: none was found in the labour response,
  which bears against reading CLAIM-tmpw4ai2's threshold into individual
  labour supply, not against it elsewhere.
- That the lifetime marginal propensity to earn is −0.11: that is the
  working paper's calibrated model, revised in the published version.
- That the effect carries to poor households or to organizations: these
  are Swedish adults in a welfare state, and CLAIM-tmpc3x0c's and
  CLAIM-tmpqa0qu's settings are reached only by analogy.
