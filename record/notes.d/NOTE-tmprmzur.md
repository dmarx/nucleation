# NOTE-tmprmzur: Meckes, Cole, Gee and Lancaster, read for whether cutting a safety behaviour with exposure held fixed produces lasting change

## Contribution

The first trial to compare, in one study, exposure with a safety
behaviour faded, the same exposure with it kept throughout, and the same
exposure without it, as the "judicious use" account requires. It also
tests two moderators: how far the safety behaviour lowered the person's
threat expectancy, and distress intolerance. The reading is
THEORY-tmpai05z.

## Key results

- **Design (Methods, Design and Randomization; Safety Behavior
  Manipulation).** "a three-arm, parallel-group, randomized controlled
  trial". N = 56 adults with elevated spider fear, 91% meeting
  clinically significant fear on the ADIS-V. Psychoeducation, then "30-
  minutes of exposure therapy" with a tarantula in a room, 13 approach
  steps. "In the faded safety behavior condition, participants wore the
  boots and socks at the start of therapy, for the first 20 out of 30
  minutes. In the unfaded safety behavior condition, participants wore
  the boots and socks for all 30 minutes of therapy. In the no safety
  behavior condition, participants were barefoot for all 30 minutes".
  Behavioural approach tests at pre, post and "Approximately one month
  later (M = 31 days, SD = 13 days)", in the treatment context and in a
  generalization context, all done barefoot.
- **Outcomes (Results, Treatment Outcomes).** "Repeated measures ANOVAs
  yielded no significant time × SB condition interactions across
  phobia-related outcomes (ps > .05), which suggests that the impact of
  exposure on phobic responding did not differ based on SB condition."
  Exposure itself was strong: approach steps in the treatment-context test
  η² = .80.
- **Process (Results, Treatment Process).** "during trial one, both SB
  groups approached the spider more closely than the no SB group". At
  trial three the unfaded group reported lower peak fear, but "this
  difference during treatment was not evident at post-treatment or
  one-month follow-up."
- **Acceptability (Results, Treatment Acceptability).** No difference at
  pre-treatment; at post-treatment "the no SB group reported lower
  treatment acceptability than both the unfaded SB group ... and the
  faded SB group".
- **Threat expectancy (Moderation Analyses).** On average the boots did
  not lower it: "there were no between-group differences in the level of
  change in threat expectancy from baseline tBAT to exposure trial 1".
  Where they did for a person, the interaction ran against the
  hypothesis: "when SBs attenuate threat expectancy, we found that faded
  SBs had a detrimental long-term effect, whereas unfaded SBs did not",
  significant only for disgust ratings at follow-up, not for fear or
  approach.
- **Distress intolerance (Moderation Analyses).** In the unfaded arm,
  greater distress intolerance went with lower disgust; in the no-SB arm,
  with higher anticipated fear and disgust at post-treatment, "although
  these effects were not maintained at follow-up" (Discussion).
- **The authors' verdict (Conclusion).** "using (vs. not using) SBs during
  a single session of exposure generally did not hinder exposure therapy
  outcomes", and "fading SBs did not improve long-term therapy outcomes."
- **The literature, as the authors report it (Introduction).** Meulders
  et al. 2016 (LIT-tmpgjzit, not read): "a meta-analysis of 20
  experiments", with effects "moderately to highly heterogeneous"; "In
  both cases, the direction of the overall effect favored fewer safety
  behaviors, but neither effect was statistically significant." Blakey et
  al. 2019 (LIT-tmpw8nym, not read): safety behaviours "reduced threat
  expectancy but did not interfere with anxiety reduction". Powers et al.
  2004 (LIT-tmp83n75) and Sloan and Telch 2002 are cited on the other
  side.

## Assumptions

- A safety behaviour is an action that reduces anxiety without
  "meaningfully" protecting against a true threat (Telch & Lancaster
  2012), and an experimenter-supplied one (boots) stands in for a
  person's own.
- Threat expectancy is a six-item composite of thinking and feeling that
  one will be harmed physically, socially and mentally, 0–600; its
  change on first wearing the boots indexes how far the safety behaviour
  "blocks" expectancy.
- Intent-to-treat with the last observation carried forward for the
  12.5% lost to follow-up (and 5.4% unable to attend under COVID-19
  restrictions).

## Concepts

- **Judicious use** (Rachman et al. 2008): safety behaviours allowed early
  in exposure to make approach tolerable, then faded.
- **Faded / unfaded / no safety behaviour**: the three arms; "unfaded" is
  the safety behaviour kept for the whole session.
- **Expectancy violation**: the inhibitory-learning account (LIT-1500)
  on which a safety behaviour harms exposure by lowering the expectancy
  that the outcome could violate.

## Bearing on the record

- **QUESTION-045 (evidence on the experiment it asks for; against, with a
  limit).** The design holds exposure fixed and varies only the safety
  behaviour, and the question asks for "lasting change that the same
  exposure without the cut does not" produce. Here the arms did not
  differ at one month. The limit is the one the question turns on: the
  boots are a safety behaviour added for the study, not the maintaining
  link of the person's own regime, so the trial tests whether a new safety
  behaviour harms exposure, not whether cutting an old one frees the
  person. The authors name this first among their limitations
  ("experimenter-selected SBs may impact exposure differently than an
  established SB selected by the participant"). Salkovskis et al. 1999
  (LIT-tmplsgie) is the registered trial that cut patients' own safety
  behaviours, over a two-day outcome.
- **THEORY-058 (its promote_when, route 2; does not meet it).** The route
  asks for experiments that cut a maintaining link with exposure held
  constant "and produce lasting remission that the same exposure without
  the cut does not". This one finds no such difference, and for the
  reason above it is not a test of a maintaining link. It neither meets
  nor refutes the route.
- **LIT-1491 / THEORY-903 (contrast).** The record holds the meta-analysis
  of safety-behaviour manipulations outside exposure (reduction lowered
  symptoms, d = 0.43, about 0.31 corrected). This trial is the
  within-exposure half, where the effect is null; together they say that
  safety behaviours moved symptoms on their own in that literature, but
  that adding one to exposure did not blunt it here.
- **CLAIM-446 (background, no edge).** The claim's test holds the
  represented threat fixed and changes the defensive process's evidence
  access. The boots changed approach without changing threat expectancy
  on average, and outcomes did not differ; that is a manipulation of
  safety behaviour with expectancy unchanged, but not of who governs
  inquiry, so it bears only as background.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- **A single 30-minute session** with fading after 20 minutes; the
  authors note fading may need longer.
- **An experimenter-supplied, novel safety behaviour**, the same for all,
  so it cannot speak to established, idiosyncratic safety behaviours.
- **Small and narrow**: N = 56, powered for a medium effect (f = 0.20),
  87.5% female, 82.1% white, spider fear only; some moderation analyses
  underpowered, as the authors say.
- **Null results read as no effect.** The main finding is non-significant
  interactions, with no equivalence test, so it shows no detected
  difference rather than equivalence.
- **The moderation results are post hoc in direction**: the fading effect
  ran against the hypothesis and appeared on disgust only, across many
  outcomes, with no correction reported for multiplicity.
- **An unexplained imbalance**: income differed between arms at baseline
  (61.1% in the highest bracket in the unfaded arm against 5.3% and 10.5%).
