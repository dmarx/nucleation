# CASE-128: The voluntary delegation: a person gives a friend the password to a distracting application during a calm period and is refused it later under temptation, while another person lives under the same restriction imposed without consent

## The case

A18: "A person knows that impulsive late-night decisions often undermine
an important commitment. During a calm period, they arrange for a trusted
friend to hold the password to a distracting application. Later, they
feel a strong urge to use it. The friend refuses to disclose the password.
A second person is subject to the same arrangement, but an external
authority imposed it without their consent. Both experience the same
immediate restriction and may behave identically."

The harder variant: "suppose the first person arranged for the
restriction to be deliberately difficult to revoke during a predictable
temptation. Immediate revocability cannot simply be required in every
context, or precommitment becomes impossible."

It varies [CASE-013](CASE-013.md), the reluctant writer who blocks distracting websites,
by adding the second person and making the device someone else's hand.

## What it shows

A18: "This is an essential counterexample to a simplistic non-domination
theory. An agent does not cease governing itself whenever it delegates or
constrains a constituent process." The questions are "who authorized the
arrangement, under what conditions it can be reconsidered, and whether the
delegation remains answerable to its justifying purposes."

[CLAIM-344](../claims.d/CLAIM-344.md) already holds that resolve keeps a temporally extended policy
effective while keeping the capacity to revise it. This case shows the
capacity to revise can be deliberately placed outside the moment of
temptation and still be the person's. Against [CLAIM-362](../claims.d/CLAIM-362.md) and [CLAIM-364](../claims.d/CLAIM-364.md) it
marks a limit: whether a constituent is dominated cannot be read from
whether it is overridden, but from who authorized the override and
whether it stays answerable.

## What it does not show

Where legitimate precommitment ends. A18: "A good theory must distinguish
legitimate precommitment from a restriction that has become unaccountable
to the purposes for which it was adopted." The case poses that line and
does not draw it. Bénabou and Tirole's personal rules ([LIT-1389](../literature.d/LIT-1389.md)) and
Ainslie's bargaining interests ([THEORY-067](../theory.d/THEORY-067.md)) are the existing accounts it
would be tested against.
