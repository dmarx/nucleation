# THEORY-1611: Dovidio, Gaertner, Shnabel, Saguy and Johnson report that recategorizing two groups as one removed in-group bias in helping, that White students helped a Black student more when he expressed a common university identity, partly through empathic concern, and that they helped him no more when he expressed a dual identity than when he expressed only his subgroup identity

## The account

A shared identity extends help across a group line, in the form the
helper prefers. Recategorizing an out-group member inside a common
category raised help to the in-group level. A minority member who
called himself "a Black Colgate student" was treated like one who called
himself only Black: "The responses of White participants ... were
positive and prosocial when a Black person identified himself in a way
compatible with an assimilation ideology" (p. 204). Empathic concern
partly mediated the effect of expressed common identity on helping;
attitudes did not.

## Evidence

From [NOTE-1286](../notes.d/NOTE-1286.md): a laboratory study (posters for an out-group member
rose to the in-group level); Nier et al. (2001), White fans at a stadium
complying with a Black interviewer 59% of the time when he wore their
university's hat against 36% when he did not; two studies of dual
identity on one campus with one confederate; mediation with β for
condition falling from .46 to .35.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-300](../questions.d/QUESTION-300.md) asks whether other-directed motivation survives once
the self's extent is controlled. Here the experimenter moves the
category boundary, not what participants care about, and helping
follows it, with empathy downstream of the identity. The common
category that carries help is the helper's, not one both parties
share.
