# THEORY-434: Rawls's theory of justice is transcendental only in the formal sense that its principles state when a society is perfectly just; it already yields comparative judgements, prescribes no single set of institutions and sits inside a revisable reflective equilibrium, so Sen's case against 'transcendental institutionalism' misses Rawls, and what it shows is that the theory is incomplete, not that a new paradigm is needed

## Source

- Valentini, "A Paradigm Shift in Theorizing about Justice? A Critique of
  Sen", [LIT-290](../literature.d/LIT-290.md) (*Economics & Philosophy* 27(3), 297–315, 2011), read in
  full in [NOTE-262](../notes.d/NOTE-262.md) from the Oxford CSSJ working paper of November 2010.
  Page numbers are the working paper's.
- Zwolinski (2010), review of *The Idea of Justice*, [LIT-286](../literature.d/LIT-286.md), read in
  [NOTE-261](../notes.d/NOTE-261.md). It is a page-referenced map of the book and corroborates the
  reconstruction of Sen's Part One. It offers no criticism.

## What was actually shown

**Sen's case, as reconstructed (per Valentini, with Zwolinski agreeing on
content).** The book lists six complaints against "transcendental
institutionalism" (IoJ p. 90), which Valentini groups into three (p. 3,
fn. 9). (A) An account of perfect justice is neither necessary for
comparative judgements, since the "iniquities of hunger, illiteracy,
torture" are recognised without one (IoJ p. 96), nor sufficient, since a
metric of distance from the ideal is also needed (IoJ pp. 98–101).
Zwolinski reports the same argument at IoJ pp. 95–102, and the
realisation focus at pp. 20–22 and 67–69. (B) It is parochial. (C) It is
inflexible: it reduces plural principles to "one tidy box" (IoJ p. 46).

**The rebuttal.**

- **"Transcendental" (§II, pp. 5–6).** Taken as "beyond human reach", the
  label is false of Rawls, who builds in moderate scarcity, limited altruism
  and "realistic utopia". Taken as categorical ("X is perfectly just
  iff…"), it is true but a poor label.
- **"Institutionalist" (§II, pp. 6–7).** Rawls's principles apply to the
  basic structure, but the institutions that realise them vary with the
  society (affirmative action where there is prejudice). Per Pogge, Rawls
  assesses a basic structure by the distribution it would tend to produce
  in the actual social system.
- **Sufficiency (§III, pp. 9–10).** "Taken literally, this claim is false."
  Lexical priority ranks violations of liberty above those of fair equality
  of opportunity above those of the difference principle, and the difference
  principle is comparative, as Sen himself concedes (IoJ p. 97). The
  reading rates this strong as a textual point. The true nearby claim is
  that the theory is incomplete.
- **Necessity (§III, pp. 8–9).** True but inconsequential. The obvious cases
  are the inputs of theory; theory is needed where intuitions diverge
  (Rawls, *PL* pp. 45–46).
- **Scope (§IV, pp. 10–12).** Rawls's and Nagel's denial of global
  distributive justice rests on the relational appropriateness of justice,
  not on transcendental institutionalism.
- **Inflexibility (§V, pp. 12–14).** Rawls's method is reflective
  equilibrium, in which the fixed points and the original position's
  conditions are revisable (*TJ* pp. 17–18, 42–43, 506–509), and later Rawls
  allows "many liberalisms" (*PL* pp. 223, 227). Rated strong as a textual
  point. The process Sen describes "turns out to be nothing other than
  reflective equilibrium" (p. 13).

What could have come out otherwise: Rawls's principles could have been
silent on non-ideal comparisons, or his method fixed. The texts cited show
neither.

## What this does not say

- **That Sen's comparative approach is mistaken.** Valentini grants that his
  focus on advancing justice in the real world and on comparative judgement
  is "timely and laudable" (p. 14). The charge may fit Nozick better
  (fn. 47).
- **That Rawls's principles are right.** The defence is of his method only
  (p. 14).
- **That a complete account of perfect justice is needed.** §III.A argues
  that knowing perfect justice matters because justice "may" have a cut-off
  point. It does not establish that it has one.
- **That the reconstruction is first-hand.** Every claim about *The Idea of
  Justice* here is per Valentini or per Zwolinski. The record has not read
  the book.
- **That Sen's open public reasoning collapses into Rawls's.** The dilemma of
  §IV (either near-empty or presupposing liberal moral equality) is not
  tested against Sen's impartial spectator and positional objectivity, which
  Zwolinski reports as central to Part Two (IoJ p. 157).
- **That the capability approach is touched.** Valentini sets aside Sen's
  "path-breaking contributions", "whose value is not in dispute" (p. 2).
