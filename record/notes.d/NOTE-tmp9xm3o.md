# NOTE-tmp9xm3o: On Linguistic Aspects of Translation

## Contribution

A short essay that gives translation theory its most cited taxonomy and
puts translation at the centre of linguistics. It defines meaning
semiotically, as translatability into further signs; it distinguishes three
kinds of translation; and it relocates the untranslatability debate from
what languages can express to what their grammars force speakers to
express. That last move is the essay's substantive thesis.

## Key insight

Languages do not differ in what can be said in them but in what must be
said. A grammar poses yes-or-no questions (completed or not? male or
female? one, two or more?) that every sentence has to answer. Translation
between languages whose grammars pose different questions is therefore not
loss alone: it also forces the translator to supply answers the source did
not give, and to leave unanswered questions the source did answer.

## Assumptions

- **A semiotic theory of meaning.** Meaning is not the referent but the
  sign's translatability into other signs (Peirce's interpretant): "no
  signatum without signum". The essay argues this against Russell with
  ordinary-language examples, not by a theory of reference.
- **A cognitive/poetic split.** In its cognitive function language is
  "minimally dependent on the grammatical pattern"; in jest, dreams, magic,
  everyday verbal mythology and poetry, grammar carries "high semantic
  import". The essay's conclusions about translatability differ on the two
  sides of this line, and where the line falls is not specified.
- **Metalanguage.** Speakers can talk about their language, so vocabulary
  can be revised and redefined; this is what makes deficiencies
  repairable.

## Key results

The essay argues; it does not prove or test. Its theses:

- **T1 (three kinds).** Intralingual translation (rewording),
  interlingual translation (translation proper), intersemiotic
  translation (transmutation): interpretation of verbal signs by signs of
  the same language, another language, or a nonverbal sign system.
- **T2 (no full equivalence of code-units).** Within a language synonymy is
  not complete equivalence ("every celibate is a bachelor, but not every
  bachelor is a celibate"); a code-unit is fully interpreted only by a
  message about it. Between languages there is "ordinarily no full
  equivalence between code-units", but "messages may serve as adequate
  interpretations of alien code-units or messages". Translation
  "substitutes messages in one language not for separate code-units but
  for entire messages"; it is reported speech, "two equivalent messages
  in two different codes".
- **T3 (expressibility).** "All cognitive experience and its
  classification is conveyable in any existing language", by loanwords,
  loan-translations, neologisms, semantic shifts and circumlocutions
  (Chukchee "rotating nail" for screw, "writing soap" for chalk); "any
  assumption of ineffable or untranslatable cognitive data would be a
  contradiction in terms". A missing grammatical category is translated
  lexically (Old Russian dual: "two brothers"); "and/or" can be rendered in
  languages that lack it.
- **T4 (obligatory categories).** After Boas, the grammatical pattern
  "determines those aspects of each experience that must be expressed".
  "Languages differ essentially in what they must convey and not in what
  they may convey." Translating into a language with a category the source
  lacks forces a choice (dual/plural for "She has brothers") or an explicit
  disjunction ("either two or more than two"). For "I hired a worker" a
  Russian must choose perfective or imperfective (*nanjal* / *nanimal*) and
  a masculine or feminine noun (*rabotnika* / *rabotnicu*), and the
  Russian still does not say whether I "hired" or "have hired", or "a" or
  "the" worker.
- **T5 (chains).** Because the two grammars pose "quite different sets of
  two-choice situations", "a chain of translations of one and the same
  isolated sentence from English into Russian and vice versa could
  entirely deprive such a message of its initial content" (Karcevski's
  image: a circular series of unfavourable currency transactions). "But
  evidently the richer the context of a message, the smaller the loss of
  information."
- **T6 (grammar in verbal mythology).** Grammatical gender shapes
  personification: a 1915 Moscow Psychological Institute test found
  Russians personify Monday, Tuesday and Thursday (masculine nouns) as
  male and Wednesday, Friday and Saturday (feminine) as female; the fallen
  knife and fork superstition; Repin and German *die Sünde*; Pasternak's
  *My Sister Life* defeating a Czech translator, since Czech *život* is
  masculine. Constantine the Philosopher's preface to the Old Church
  Slavonic Gospels already names the problem and resolves it in favour of
  "cognitive values" over words.
- **T7 (poetry).** In poetry "verbal equations become a constructive
  principle of the text"; every constituent of the code carries
  "autonomous signification" and paronomasia reigns. "Poetry by definition
  is untranslatable. Only creative transposition is possible":
  intralingual, interlingual or intersemiotic (into music, dance, cinema,
  painting). *Traduttore, traditore* rendered as "the translator is a
  betrayer" loses its paronomasia, and a cognitive attitude would ask:
  "translator of what messages? betrayer of what values?"

## Concepts

- **intralingual translation / rewording**: interpretation of verbal signs
  by other signs of the same language.
- **interlingual translation / translation proper**: interpretation of
  verbal signs by some other language.
- **intersemiotic translation / transmutation**: interpretation of verbal
  signs by signs of nonverbal sign systems.
- **code-unit**: a word or idiomatic phrase-word, "a code-unit of the
  highest level"; contrasted with a message.
- **equivalence in difference**: the relation between two messages in two
  codes that translation aims at; called "the cardinal problem of language".
- **creative transposition**: what remains possible where translation is
  not: recasting a poem into another poetic shape, language or medium.

## Connections

The essay sits between Peirce's semiotics (meaning as interpretant, via
Dewey's account) and Boas's view of grammatical categories as obligatory,
and it opposes Whorf's "Mr. Everyman" inference from unlike grammars to
unlike facts. It anticipates Jakobson's own "Linguistics and Poetics"
(1960) on the poetic function, here stated briefly. In the record, Nida's
(LIT-tmp1ysoz) formal and dynamic equivalence and House's (LIT-tmp1e7m4)
overt and covert translation are later answers to the question this essay
calls "equivalence in difference"; neither was read.

## Bearing on the record

- **Produces THEORY-tmpo1mei**, the obligatory-categories thesis (T4 with
  T5), stated with its evidence and what it does not establish. The record
  held no claim of this kind.
- **The manuscript's argument (`what-survives-translation`).** Its claim
  codes are left to the coordinator; the bearings, stated here so a reader
  can check them:
  - On the claim that a translation can broaden readings along one
    dimension while narrowing them along another: T4 is an early, concrete
    statement of it at the level of grammar. The Russian rendering of "I
    hired a worker" fixes aspect and sex the English left open, and leaves
    open tense and definiteness the English fixed.
  - On the central thesis ("not the wording or the proposition"): C1 is a
    classical ancestor of "not the wording", but Jakobson's unit is the
    message's cognitive content, not its pragmatic force. He does not
    address footing or illocution, so the essay gives nothing to the claim
    that propositional sameness is insufficient.
  - On reconstruction chains: T5 is a 1959 statement that back-and-forth
    translation can lose content, with context as the damping factor. It is
    an assertion with an analogy, not evidence.
  - On cross-modal renderings: T1 names intersemiotic translation as one of
    three kinds of translation, which is prior art for treating cross-modal
    rendering under one account. The essay says nothing about what such
    renderings preserve.
  - On fidelity as one goal among adaptation, critique and parody: T7 is a
    narrow precedent. For poetry Jakobson replaces fidelity with "creative
    transposition", and his closing question ("betrayer of what values?")
    makes fidelity relative to a chosen value. He does not discuss
    adaptation, critique or parody.
  - On significance fixed by contrasts within a system: the essay's
    semiotics is Peircean (meaning as translation into further signs), not
    Saussurean differential value. It is compatible with that claim but
    does not support it.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- An essay of eight pages argued from examples; nothing is tested.
- T3's universal expressibility is asserted, and the essay's own
  exceptions (poetry, jest, magic, verbal mythology) are large and not
  bounded.
- The chain claim (T5) is a "could", with no example traced through
  several steps.
- The 1915 personification test is cited without data or a source.
- It treats cognitive content as the thing to be preserved and does not
  consider pragmatic force, register or the social act of the utterance.

## Open questions

- Whether round-trip chains between languages with mismatched obligatory
  categories do lose the source's distinctions at the rate T5 implies, and
  whether added context damps the loss: a controlled study of translation
  chains would close it.
- Where the cognitive/poetic line falls, and whether ordinary
  conversational language (stance, politeness) sits on the cognitive side
  or with "verbal mythology".
