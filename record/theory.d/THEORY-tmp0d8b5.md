# THEORY-tmp0d8b5: Hermeneutical injustice is having a significant area of one's social experience obscured from collective understanding owing to hermeneutical marginalisation, which may be systematic or incidental, so it does not require that the subject belong to a powerless social group

## Source

- Fricker (2006), "Powerlessness and Social Interpretation", *Episteme*
  3(1–2), [LIT-tmp71qsd](../literature.d/LIT-tmp71qsd.md), read in full in [NOTE-tmpgy7n9](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpgy7n9.md), from the
  author-posted manuscript. Its first note says it "is drawn from the
  final chapter" of the book.
- Fricker (2017), "Evolving Concepts of Epistemic Injustice", [LIT-tmphrja4](../literature.d/LIT-tmphrja4.md),
  read in full in [NOTE-tmp3a0fd](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp3a0fd.md).

The book ([LIT-1004](../literature.d/LIT-1004.md)) was not read. Page references are to the
manuscripts.

## What was actually shown

**Injustice, not bad luck (2006, pp. 3–6).** A collective gap that
disadvantages someone is not by that fact unjust. Someone with a
misunderstood, undiagnosed condition suffers "circumstantial epistemic
bad luck". What makes Carmita Wood's case unjust is that "unequal
relations of power prevented women from participating on equal terms
with men in those practices by which collective social meanings are
generated".

**Marginalisation (p. 6).** Members of a group are "hermeneutically
marginalized" when there is "unequal hermeneutical participation with
respect to some significant area(s) of social experience". It may be
"only fleetingly, and/or only in respect of a highly localized patch". It
is "always socially coerced" and "always a form of powerlessness, whether
structural or one-off".

**The definitions (pp. 7–12).** The systematic case, defined first, is
"owing to persistent and wide-ranging hermeneutical marginalization", or
"owing to a structural prejudice in the collective hermeneutical
resource". The incidental case leads to a generic definition: "the
injustice of having some significant area of one's social experience
obscured from collective understanding owing to hermeneutical
marginalization". This "simply omits what is special to the systematic
case".

**The incidental case (pp. 9–11).** Joe is stalked. The police's
"interrogative flow chart" leaves his grievance to be "poured into the
available bureaucratic mould", and he cannot make it intelligible. His
"social identity is that of the proverbial white, educated, straight
man", and the injustice "has nothing to do with any general social
powerlessness". He suffers it "not because of but rather in spite of the
hermeneutical ease he normally enjoys". Incidental injustices "stem not
from any structural inequality of power but rather from a more one-off
moment of powerlessness".

**Structural and culprit-free.** "Whether the injustice is of the
systematic or the incidental kind, it involves no perpetrator, no
culprit" (2006, p. 13). It persists "regardless of individual efforts",
because its cause is structural (2017, pp. 3–4).

What could have come out otherwise: Fricker could have kept the
systematic definition as the definition, which is what her précis
states. She widens it on the strength of one case, and keeps the
systematic case "central".

## What this does not say

- **Not that every conceptual gap is an injustice.** It needs
  marginalisation behind it. Novelty alone, as with the undiagnosed
  condition, is bad luck.
- **Not that any idea that fails to fit is covered.** The obscured thing
  must be "some significant area of one's social experience", and
  "significant" is not defined.
- **Not that choosing to opt out is marginalisation.** It must be
  "socially coerced". The hermit "could have opted in".
- **Not that incidental cases are as central.** They are "every bit as
  harmful" but not central "from the point of view of an interest in how
  epistemic injustice is woven into the fabric of social injustice".
- **Not a claim about the published book.** The paper is drawn from a
  chapter then "forthcoming", and the published chapter was not read.
