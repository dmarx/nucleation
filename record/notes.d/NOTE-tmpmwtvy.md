# NOTE-tmpmwtvy: Pettigrew — Intergroup contact theory

## Contribution

It turns Allport's (1954) hypothesis, a list of the conditions under
which contact reduces prejudice, into a theory with processes, stages
and levels. It names the four problems with the hypothesis (causal
direction, an ever-growing list of conditions, no process, no account of
generalization), answers each, and proposes the model the later
meta-analyses test ([LIT-1413](../literature.d/LIT-1413.md), [LIT-1405](../literature.d/LIT-1405.md)). It adds cross-group friendship
as the potent case and "friendship potential" as a fifth condition.

## Key insight

Contact changes more than beliefs about the outgroup. It changes
behavior first, and the attitude follows to resolve the dissonance; it
reduces anxiety and builds affective ties; and it shows that "ingroup
norms and customs turn out not to be the only ways to manage the social
world", which makes one's own group look provincial. Friendship works
because it engages all four processes, so the theory is about long-term
relationships, not first acquaintance.

## Assumptions

- **A review**, not a study: the evidence is the literature, a
  re-analysis of the 1988 Eurobarometer surveys (over 3,800 majority
  respondents in seven samples from France, Britain, the Netherlands and
  West Germany), and Cook's (1984) laboratory experiment.
- **Causal claims from cross-sectional data** rest on a nonrecursive
  structural model with mixed-neighbourhood residence as the instrument:
  living in a mixed neighbourhood is assumed to affect affective
  prejudice only through friendship.
- **Meso level.** Contact is a situational phenomenon placed within
  individual (prior attitudes, anxiety) and societal (norms, conflict)
  contexts.

## Key results

- **Selection bias (pp. 69, 72).** Prejudiced people avoid contact,
  but in the European data the path from outgroup friendship to lower
  affective prejudice (−.210) is significantly stronger than that from
  prejudice to less friendship (−.137); mixed neighbourhoods raise the
  chance of an outgroup friend (+.356).
- **Conditions (pp. 69–70, 76).** The proposed extra conditions
  (common language, voluntary contact, a prosperous economy, initial
  views not too negative, stereotype disconfirmation) are mostly
  facilitating. The reformulated model treats Allport's four and
  friendship potential as essential, the rest facilitating.
- **Four processes (pp. 70–73).** Learning about the outgroup, limited
  by the cognitive conditions for stereotype change (Rothbart and John);
  **changing behavior**, "a benign form of behavior modification.
  Behavior change is often the precursor of attitude change", resolved
  by dissonance and reinforced by repetition; **generating affective
  ties**, anxiety reduction and empathy, with friendship strongest; and
  **ingroup reappraisal**: the new perspective "can reshape your view of
  your ingroup and lead to a less provincial view of outgroups in general
  ('deprovincialization')". Its evidence: outgroup friendship relates to
  less national pride with education, age and conservatism controlled
  (Pettigrew 1997c, an unpublished manuscript), and Wilder and Thompson's
  (1980) finding that less ingroup contact lowered bias.
- **Generalization (pp. 73–75).** Three kinds: across situations, from
  the individual to the outgroup, to uninvolved outgroups. Brewer and
  Miller's decategorization and Hewstone and Brown's salient
  categorization conflict, since those likeliest to have contact are
  atypical of their groups and contact generalizes best from typical
  members; they are reconciled as a sequence, ending, if ever, in the
  recategorization of Gaertner and colleagues' common ingroup identity
  model. Wright and colleagues' extended contact effect is noted.
- **Uninvolved outgroups (p. 75).** In the European data, friends of
  another group relate to more favourable views of eight outgroups, many
  not in the country, with seven controls; again the friendship-to-
  prejudice path is the stronger.
- **Overall (p. 79).** Friendship–prejudice correlations across five
  measures and seven samples average −.14 to −.32 without controls and
  −.10 to −.25 with seven controls, consistent with the preliminary
  meta-analysis then under way.

## Concepts

- **Deprovincialization**: the less provincial view of outgroups in
  general that follows when contact shows one's own group's norms and
  customs are not the only ways to manage the social world. The word
  occurs once, in parentheses, as the name of the outcome of ingroup
  reappraisal.
- **Ingroup reappraisal**: the process by which optimal contact gives
  insight about the ingroup as well as the outgroup.
- **Friendship potential**: the situation's opportunity for close,
  repeated interaction across varied settings, allowing self-disclosure;
  Cook's "acquaintance potential".
- **Facilitating versus essential conditions**: conditions that enhance
  contact's effects against those it requires.

## Connections

The 2006 meta-analysis ([LIT-1413](../literature.d/LIT-1413.md)) tests this review's problems and
partly overturns its model: it finds Allport's four conditions
facilitating too, where this review's reformulation kept them, with
friendship potential, as essential. The 2008 meta-analysis ([LIT-1405](../literature.d/LIT-1405.md))
tests three of the four processes' components (knowledge, anxiety,
empathy) and leaves ingroup reappraisal untested. Gaertner and
colleagues' common ingroup identity model is registered ([LIT-1709](../literature.d/LIT-1709.md)), as is
Paluck, Green and Green's later re-evaluation of the experimental
evidence ([LIT-1420](../literature.d/LIT-1420.md)).

## Bearing on the record

- **[LIT-1408](../literature.d/LIT-1408.md)'s flag is resolved.** The review uses "deprovincialization",
  once (p. 72), for the outcome of ingroup reappraisal. The exchange's
  gloss, seeing one's group's ways as one way among others, matches the
  review's wording. But the review's deprovincialization is a less
  provincial view "of outgroups in general" reached through a revised
  view of the ingroup, and its evidence is thin: one unpublished analysis
  of national pride and one laboratory study.
- **[CLAIM-275](../claims.d/CLAIM-275.md).** The review states the Franklin shape outright:
  "Behavior change is often the precursor of attitude change", with
  dissonance as the mechanism. It is argued, not tested.
- **[CLAIM-417](../claims.d/CLAIM-417.md)** (a perspective revised by being made one of several).
  Ingroup reappraisal is the process nearest the claim. The review
  offers it as a fourth mechanism with weak evidence; the later
  meta-analysis of mediators does not test it.

## Limitations

- **Its causal evidence is mostly cross-sectional**; the path
  comparisons depend on the exclusion assumption for neighbourhood.
- **Processes are proposed, not tested**; the author says they "may well
  overlap and interact in complex ways".
- **The longitudinal model is a heuristic**, "obviously oversimplified"
  in its three stages, and the review calls for the longitudinal research
  that would test it.
- **Errors and slips.** "Having an ingroup friend related to greater
  acceptance of minorities of many types" (p. 75) must mean an outgroup
  friend, as the surrounding text does. No other error was found.

## Open questions

- Does contact produce ingroup reappraisal, and does that mediate
  reduced prejudice? A study measuring views of the ingroup before and
  after contact would test it.
- Does the decategorization–salience–recategorization sequence occur
  in that order in real contact?
