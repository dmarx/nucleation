# NOTE-tmpdp3bl: Better to be a pig dissatisfied than a plant satisfied

## Contribution

Responding to Segundo-Ortin and Calvo's case that plants should be taken seriously as candidates for sentience, the authors note that critics tend to assume that because plants matter less than animals they must not matter at all. They propose a gradualist alternative: sentience and moral status are both matters of degree, so plants may be quasi-sentient and carry partial moral status. That lets one hold both that plants and animals have moral status and that animals have prima facie more of it.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- Introduction (p. 2): the aim is modest — to test whether "quasi-sentience" and "partial moral status" for plants make sense; the evolutionary premise is that complex traits come in degrees (Veit 2023a).
- Clarifying Concepts (pp. 3–4): the authors distinguish S-broad (any phenomenal or valenced experience) from S-narrow (valenced positive and negative feelings) and adopt S-broad, which they take to match Segundo-Ortin and Calvo; a footnote concedes these concepts have fuzzy boundaries.
- "Between Sentience and Moral Status" (p. 8): premise A1 is gradualism about sentience (Dennett's "hemi-semi-demi-pseudo-proto-quasi-minds"); A2 is that plants may be quasi-sentient.
- "Moral Status" (pp. 10–11): quasi-sentience is taken to be compatible with partial moral status, which the authors admit is only a coarse-grained account; Table 1 maps no/quasi/sophisticated sentience to no/partial/full status, with obligations to plants that animal interests "outweigh in most cases".
- Conclusion (p. 13): nothing is settled; gradualism is "likely the only game in town" against line-drawing debates, and it parallels the insect-sentience literature.
