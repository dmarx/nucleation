# NOTE-tmp87q7f: International Court of Justice — Chagos advisory opinion

## Contribution

It is the first ruling by the Court that a decolonization was unlawfully
completed because part of the colony was cut away before independence. It
gives the right to self-determination customary status by the 1960s,
dating its crystallization to resolution 1514 (XV) of 1960, and makes the
territorial integrity of a non-self-governing territory a corollary of it.
It draws two consequences: the United Kingdom must end its administration,
and every Member State must co-operate with the United Nations to complete
the decolonization. It does not decide sovereignty as a bilateral dispute,
and it does not decide whether the Chagossians can return.

## Key insight

The opinion is about a territory and a people defined by that territory
as a whole, the people of the colony of Mauritius, not about the
Chagossians as a people. Its wrong is the dismemberment of a
non-self-governing territory without the consent of its people, and its
remedy is the completion of decolonization. The expulsion of the islanders
is in the facts (paras. 113–131) and in the second question, but the
Court answers that part in one sentence (para. 181): resettlement "is an
issue relating to the protection of the human rights of those concerned,
which should be addressed by the General Assembly". The interstate wrong
and the wrong to the displaced are separated by the opinion itself.

## Assumptions

- **The relevant period is 1965 to 1968** (paras. 140–143). The Court may
  look at later instruments where they confirm or interpret rules already
  in force then.
- **Custom from General Assembly practice** (paras. 149–155). Resolution
  1514 (XV), "formally a recommendation", is held declaratory of a
  customary norm "in view of its content and the conditions of its
  adoption": 89 votes, 9 abstentions, none contesting the right.
- **Consent of a colony under its administering Power** (para. 172). "It
  is not possible to talk of an international agreement, when one of the
  parties to it, Mauritius, which is said to have ceded the territory to
  the United Kingdom, was under the authority of the latter." Consent to
  detaching part of a non-self-governing territory gets "heightened
  scrutiny".
- **Jurisdiction and discretion** (paras. 54–91). The request concerns
  decolonization, in which the General Assembly has a standing role, so an
  opinion does not circumvent the rule that a State need not submit a
  bilateral dispute to the Court without its consent.

## Key results

- **(a) Decolonization not lawfully completed** (para. 174; operative
  point 3, 13–1, Judge Donoghue against). The detachment "was not based on
  the free and genuine expression of the will of the people concerned"
  (para. 172).
- **(b) A continuing wrongful act** (paras. 177–178; point 4, 13–1). The
  United Kingdom "is under an obligation to bring an end to its
  administration of the Chagos Archipelago as rapidly as possible". The
  modalities are for the General Assembly (para. 179).
- **Duty of all States** (para. 180; point 5, 13–1). Self-determination
  is an obligation *erga omnes*, so all Member States must co-operate with
  the United Nations to complete the decolonization.
- **Resettlement** (para. 181): a human-rights issue for the General
  Assembly "during the completion of the decolonization of Mauritius". No
  operative point addresses it.
- **Jurisdiction** (point 1) was unanimous; the decision to answer at all
  (point 2) was 12–2, Judges Tomka and Donoghue against.
- **Findings of fact the Court relies on.** The UK Foreign Office's plan to
  present detachment to Mauritius as "a fait accompli" (para. 95); the
  Prime Minister's brief "to frighten him with hope" (para. 105); between
  1967 and 1973 "the entire population of the Chagos Archipelago was
  either prevented from returning or forcibly removed and prevented from
  returning" (para. 43); the 1982 compensation paid to 1,344 islanders on
  an English-only renunciation form, which 12 refused to sign (para. 120);
  the UK's statement at the hearings that the removal was "shameful and
  wrong" (para. 116); and the Foreign Office's 1965 advice that "the
  islands chosen have virtually no permanent inhabitants" (para. 111).

## Concepts

- **Non-self-governing territory**: a territory under Chapter XI of the
  Charter, listed by the General Assembly from 1946; Mauritius with its
  dependencies, the Chagos among them (para. 170).
- **People concerned**: the people of the whole non-self-governing
  territory (para. 160), not a population of one part of it.
- **Lancaster House agreement**: the record of the meeting of 23 September
  1965 in which Mauritian ministers accepted detachment "in principle" on
  undertakings, among them return of the islands when no longer needed for
  defence (para. 108).

## Connections

Human Rights Watch's report of 2023 (LIT-1593, NOTE-tmpask7i) relies on
this opinion for most of its history and takes the human-rights question
the Court left open as its own subject. The opinion cites the Chagos
Marine Protected Area arbitration of 2015, which found the United
Kingdom's undertaking to return the islands legally binding (para. 50),
and the English Bancoult cases (paras. 121–130). It applies the Court's
Western Sahara (1975) and Namibia (1971) opinions. The May 2025 treaty
that CASE-169 records postdates it.

## Bearing on the record

- **CASE-169 and CLAIM-348.** The case's sentence that the Court "treated
  resettlement as a human-rights question within its completion" is
  accurate, and the opinion is a direct instance of the case's point that
  a settlement adequate to one target need not settle another. The Court
  answers the question about the territory and declines the question about
  the displaced: return gets one sentence, addressed to another body, and
  no operative point. A settlement between states that satisfied every
  operative point of this opinion would leave CLAIM-361's return claim
  untouched.
- **The "no permanent population" particular in CASE-169.** The opinion
  records the line only in passing (para. 111, "virtually no permanent
  inhabitants", the Foreign Office's advice of November 1965). The
  documented history is Human Rights Watch's (NOTE-tmpask7i).
- **CLAIM-418.** The "people" in this opinion is the people of the colony
  of Mauritius. The Chagossians appear as "Mauritian nationals, including
  those of Chagossian origin" (para. 181). Whose self-determination is at
  stake is itself fixed by the territorial frame, which bears on the
  claim's separation of a perspective from a recognized collective agent.

## Limitations

- **Advisory.** The opinion binds no State; its force is the General
  Assembly's and the Court's authority.
- **Scope.** It does not decide sovereignty as a dispute, does not
  decide the Chagossians' rights, and does not say who among Chagossians
  must be consulted in completing the decolonization.
- **This reading** is of the opinion alone. Judge Donoghue's dissent and
  the separate opinions, among them Judge Cançado Trindade's on
  reparations, which Human Rights Watch cites, were not read.
- **Errors and slips.** None found. A/73/773 has one typesetting slip
  ("del ivered", para. 2 of the Secretary-General's note).

## Open questions

- Does completing the decolonization of Mauritius, on the opinion's terms,
  carry any obligation toward Chagossians who are not Mauritian nationals,
  in the Seychelles or Britain? The opinion's wording reaches only
  "Mauritian nationals".
- The dissent and separate opinions, which this copy lacks, are where the
  objections to answering at all are argued; a reading of them would close
  what this note leaves as the Court's majority view.
