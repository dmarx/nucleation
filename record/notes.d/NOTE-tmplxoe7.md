# NOTE-tmplxoe7: Pettigrew and Tropp — A meta-analytic test of intergroup contact theory

## Contribution

The first meta-analysis of the whole contact literature of the 20th
century under explicit inclusion rules: contact must be actual
face-to-face interaction between members of discrete groups, observed,
reported or unavoidable, and prejudice must be measured on individuals.
It establishes that contact and prejudice are negatively related across
targets, settings, ages and countries, that the relation is not larger
where participants chose their contact, and that it is larger, not
smaller, in the more rigorous studies. It turns Allport's conditions
from necessary into facilitating.

## Key insight

Contact reduces prejudice under most conditions, not only Allport's.
The authors' explanation is that familiarity breeds liking (mere
exposure) and that contact lowers intergroup anxiety and uncertainty, so
the open theoretical question is not what makes contact work but what
stops it working.

## Assumptions

- **Random-effects model** on Fisher's z, back-transformed to r;
  negative r means more contact, less prejudice. Effects reported only
  as "nonsignificant" set to 0 (17 samples, 2.4%), then dropped from
  moderator analyses. Very large studies capped (5,000 per study, 3,000
  per sample, 2,000 per test). The analysed file is 696 samples.
- **Allport's conditions** cannot be rated for most studies, which do
  not describe the contact. The global test is whether the contact was
  a structured program designed to meet them; the four conditions are
  rated singly only within the 134 structured samples.
- **The causal question** is handled by rating participants' choice to
  engage in the contact, by the study type, and by the reciprocal-path
  literature cited, not by any new causal design.
- **Most of the evidence is cross-sectional**: surveys and field
  studies are 71% of studies, quasi-experiments 24%, true experiments 5%.
  The United States gives 71% of studies.

## Key results

- **Overall (Table 1).** Random effects: r from −.205 to −.214 across
  studies, samples and tests; −.215 for the 696 corrected samples. 94%
  of samples are negative. Heterogeneity is large, Q(695) = 4,990, and
  remains after the fifth most extreme samples are removed.
- **Choice (Table 2).** No choice −.280 (k = 116), some choice −.190
  (279), full choice −.218 (301). Selection by the prejudiced into
  avoiding contact would predict the reverse.
- **Publication bias.** Fail-safe N above 1,200 samples; sample size
  uncorrelated with effect size (r = .04); unpublished samples −.237 and
  published −.211 (QB = 2.17, p = .14); trim-and-fill estimates 72
  missing samples and an adjusted mean of z = −.245. **Vevea and Hedges's
  model finds the file missing many small studies with small effects and,
  adjusted, no significant overall relationship (Z = −.02)**; for the 118
  between-groups samples with strong controls the adjusted effect is
  still significant (z = −.109, one-tailed p < .02).
- **Generalization (Table 2, tests).** Within the situation −.231
  (152 tests); whole outgroup −.213 (1,164); across situations −.244
  (17 tests from 9 samples); to outgroups not in the contact −.190 (18).
- **Rigor (Tables 3, 4).** Experiments −.336 (k = 36), quasi-experiments
  −.237 (168), surveys −.204 (492). Controls without prior contact −.244,
  with extensive prior contact −.138. Observed contact −.246, assumed
  −.132. Reliable contact measures or manipulations −.296 against −.189.
  The 77 samples best on all three method ratings give −.323 against
  −.202.
- **Allport's conditions (Tables 5–7).** Structured programs −.287
  (k = 134) against −.204 (562); in a weighted regression with the method
  ratings the program term stays significant (β = −.099, p = .027;
  model R² = .10). Within programs, common goals, cooperation and equal
  status do not separately predict effects, are intercorrelated (.51 to
  .63), and 72% of programs have three of the four. Programs with only
  authority sanction (k = 31) give −.286, those with two or more
  conditions −.290.
- **Racial and ethnic targets (Tables 8–10).** The two subsets have the
  same mean (−.218 and −.220). But the rigor gradient by study type is
  carried by the other targets: experiments give −.221 (k = 9) for racial
  and ethnic samples and −.377 (k = 27) for the rest. Structured programs
  raise the effect significantly for the other targets (QB = 19.67) and
  not for racial and ethnic ones (QB = 2.62, p = .11); in regression the
  program term is marginal in both subsets (p = .069, .094).
- **Moderators (Tables 11, 12).** Largest for heterosexuals' contact with
  gay men and lesbians (−.271); smaller for the mentally ill and the
  elderly (−.183); tourism smallest (−.113, 13 samples); recreational and
  laboratory settings largest (−.287). Samples after 1979 −.236 against
  −.184 before 1980, mostly but not wholly explained by rigor. No
  difference between U.S. and other samples.

## Concepts

- **Intergroup contact**: "actual face-to-face interaction between
  members of clearly defined groups"; proximity and group proportions do
  not count, nor does information about an outgroup.
- **Structured program**: a contact situation the researchers designed
  to approximate Allport's optimal conditions; the paper's global test of
  them.
- **Facilitating, not essential**: Pettigrew's 1998 distinction (LIT-1408),
  here given meta-analytic form.

## Connections

It tests the reformulation in Pettigrew's 1998 review (LIT-1408): the
causal sequence, the facilitating-not-essential distinction and the three
kinds of generalization are that review's problems, and friendship as a
contact measure (−.246 over 154 tests) its "friendship potential". Its
file of 515 studies is the base the authors' 2008 meta-analysis of
mediators extends (LIT-1405). Paluck, Green and Green (LIT-1420) re-run
the question on the 27 randomized studies with delayed outcomes; their
finding that ethnic and racial targets show weaker effects matches this
paper's own Table 8, where the experimental advantage disappears for
racial and ethnic samples.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-275** (an intervention can come before the interpretation it
  makes available). The meta-analysis is the strongest support the
  record holds for contact changing attitudes, but most of its samples
  are cross-sectional surveys of reported contact, which cannot order the
  two. The ordered evidence is the 36 experimental samples and the
  no-choice samples; both give larger effects. The authors' own
  mechanism, mere exposure and anxiety reduction, is an encounter acting
  before any revised view, which fits the claim.
- **CLAIM-297** and **CLAIM-330**: nothing here bears on performance-
  weighted authority or on the linearized model; the coupling the
  exchange drew is an analogy the paper neither makes nor tests.
- **LIT-1413's own "why this is here"** said the exchange's three
  figures agree with the abstract. They agree with the paper. What the
  exchange omitted, and the abstract softens, is the Vevea–Hedges result
  and the absence of a rigor gradient for racial and ethnic targets.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Correlational at base.** Only 5% of studies are experiments; the
  causal reading rests on the choice ratings, the rigor gradient and
  other work's reciprocal-path estimates.
- **Publication bias is not ruled out.** The authors call the
  Vevea–Hedges result a reason for "caution", and the abstract's "appears
  not to result from … publication biases" is stronger than their own
  text.
- **Allport's conditions are tested crudely**: one global indicator, and
  single ratings only for the 134 samples where they are nearly always
  together.
- **Generalization beyond the outgroup** rests on 17 and 18 tests.
- **Errors and slips.** The trim-and-fill adjustment is reported as
  raising the effect to z = −.245 from a mean near −.21; filling in
  missing studies on the small-effect side should lower it, and the paper
  does not explain the direction. "Z" is used both for the test statistic
  and for Fisher's z effect size in the same paragraphs. The
  authority-sanction comparison gives the remaining samples as k = 564,
  where Table 5 has 562 (134 + 562 = 696).

## Open questions

- What do the randomized, delayed-outcome studies give for adults'
  racial and ethnic prejudice? Paluck, Green and Green (LIT-1420) found
  none had been run.
- Which negative factors stop contact from reducing prejudice? The
  authors name the question and do not answer it.
