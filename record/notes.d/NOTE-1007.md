# NOTE-1007: Reuter, Sienhold & Sytsma — Putting pain in its proper place

## Contribution

The paper turns a philosophers' intuition into something measured: that
"the pain is in my fingertip; the fingertip is in my mouth; therefore the
pain is in my mouth" fails. It shows that lay participants share the
intuition. They share it as much for tissue damage and inflammation,
where neither of the two standard explanations applies. It offers a third
explanation, a misleading implicature in the conclusion, and gives two
pieces of evidence for it: a correlation across items, and a wording
manipulation.

## Key insight

The failure of the pain-in-mouth argument is not special to pain. Any
"X is in my mouth" that suggests something is wrong with the mouth is
rejected when X is really in a finger that is in the mouth. So the
argument cannot show that "pain" is intensional or that its "in" is not
spatial. Read at face value, "there is a pain in my fingertip" locates
the pain in the fingertip.

## Assumptions

- **What "fails" means.** The authors take the target to be "the
  ordinary understanding of the premises and conclusion" that drives the
  intuition (p. 4), not validity in a technical sense. So agreement with
  the conclusion on a 7-point scale is the measure.
- **Analogy by matched behaviour.** If pain, tissue damage and
  inflammation are rejected "to a roughly equal degree" (p. 6), one
  explanation should cover all three. This is a parsimony argument
  ("Occam's razor", p. 8), not a demonstration that the same process
  operates in each case.
- **Misleadingness is measurable by a direct question.** A separate
  group rated how misleading "[item] in their mouth" would be if one later
  learned the item was in or on a thumb that was in the mouth (p. 7).
- **No bodily-versus-mental contrast term.** There is no item that
  everyone treats as mental and that still takes a bodily "in". The test
  is pain against bodily states and a paper-clip, not pain against
  mental states.

## Key results

- **Study 1 (pp. 5–6, Fig. 1).** 174 participants on Amazon Mechanical
  Turk (54.0% women, mean age 36.6, fn 5) each judged one argument. Mean
  agreement with the conclusion: pain 2.43, tissue damage 2.53,
  inflammation 2.86, paper-clip ("on my fingertip") 6.10. The first three
  are below the midpoint (pain t(43) = −5.44, p < 0.001); the paper-clip
  is above it (t(40) = 10.038). Pain did not differ from tissue damage
  (p = .812) or inflammation (p = 0.308); Wilcoxon tests agreed (fn 6). A
  reviewer's item, serum in a fingertip, drew 5.93 from 37 participants
  (fn 7). The authors grant that the results "do not necessarily show
  that the pain-in-mouth argument does not suffer from the problems that
  Tye and Noordhof identify" (p. 6).
- **Study 2 (pp. 6–8, Fig. 2).** Fourteen items in or on a finger. Part
  one: 70 participants rated misleadingness of all fourteen, within
  subjects. Part two: 560 new participants, 40 per item, rated "There is
  [item] in Susan's mouth", recruited through the Philosophical
  Personality website. Part two replicated Study 1: pain 2.25, tissue
  damage 2.55, inflammation 3.40, paper-clip 5.25 (fn 10). Regressing
  mean acceptance on mean misleadingness: all items F(1,12) = 104.83, R²
  = 0.897; "in" items only F(1,5) = 11.79, p = 0.019, R² = 0.702 (fn 11).
  On the rendered Figure 2, pain is the lowest point on both axes, with
  tissue damage, cut, inflammation, bone, sore, splinter and infection
  near it, and paper-clip, perfume, nail, gasoline and blood on at the
  top. The authors warn that "one should be wary of moving from
  correlation to causation" (p. 8).
- **Study 3 (pp. 8–9).** 244 MTurk participants, randomised to the
  original conclusion or one prefixed "Although it might sound misleading
  to say that there is a pain in Susan's mouth, technically speaking
  there is a pain located in her mouth", for the four lowest-rated items.
  Revised against original: pain 3.79 against 2.10, inflammation 4.14
  against 2.42, cut 4.97 against 2.66, tissue damage 4.66 against 3.21.
  The main effect of wording was F(1, 236) = 48.71, η² = .17, and there
  was no wording × item interaction (F = 0.54, p = 0.659). Under the
  revised wording, only cut was significantly above the midpoint; pain
  and inflammation were not (fn 13).
- **The conclusion (p. 9).** "Not only can the pain-in-mouth argument no
  longer be used to support a representationalist view of pain, the
  results suggest that pain is a state of the body, not of the mind. The
  semantics of reports like 'there is pain in my fingertip,' taken at
  face value, tells us that pains are located in body parts."

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Lay people reject the conclusion of the pain-in-mouth argument | moderate | Study 1 and the Study 2 replication, two samples, about 40 per cell |
| C2 | They reject it about as much when "pain" is replaced by tissue damage or inflammation, and accept it for a paper-clip or serum | moderate | Study 1, no significant pain–bodily-term differences; pairwise nulls without equivalence tests |
| C3 | Acceptance across items tracks rated misleadingness of the conclusion | moderate as a correlation | Study 2, 14 item means, R² 0.897; between-group, item-level, not causal |
| C4 | Cancelling the implicature raises acceptance | moderate | Study 3, η² .17, no interaction; pain rises only to the midpoint |
| C5 | Implicature, not an intensional context or a non-spatial "in", explains the argument's failure | moderate as best explanation | C2–C4 plus parsimony; the authors grant that the other factors may also operate |
| C6 | The pain-in-mouth argument no longer supports representationalism about pain | moderate, conditional on C5 | argument from C5 |
| C7 | Pain is a state of the body, not of the mind | weak | the authors' closing inference from C5; no item contrasts pain with a mental-state term |

## Concepts

- **Pain-in-mouth argument** — Block's (1983) example of an apparently
  invalid inference with "in", used by Tye and Noordhof.
- **Intensional context** — Tye's diagnosis: "pain", like "want",
  blocks substitution, as it would if pains were representational
  states.
- **State-attributing "in"** — Noordhof's diagnosis, as in "a hole in my
  shoe".
- **Conversational implicature** — what the conclusion suggests beyond
  what it says (here, that something is wrong with the mouth), by Grice's
  maxims of relevance and manner.
- **Bodily conception of pain** — the authors' programme's view that the
  folk locate pains in body parts, not in the mind.

## Connections

The paper continues Sytsma and Reuter's experimental work on the folk
concept of pain (Sytsma 2010; Reuter 2011; Reuter, Phillips & Sytsma
2014; Sytsma & Reuter 2017), cited and not read here. Footnote 3 places
it in a "growing series of results" that "many of the commonsense facts
that philosophers have claimed about phenomenal consciousness are not in
fact commonsense", citing Sytsma & Machery 2010. Its targets are Tye's
representationalism about pain and Noordhof's reply, both in *Analysis*.

  not in doubt. The only connection is a point of language that I am

## Limitations

- **Small cells, online samples.** About 40 per cell in Studies 1 and 2.
  The nulls between pain and the bodily terms carry the analogy, and
  they have no equivalence test. One sample came from a philosophy
  personality-test website.
- **Item-level correlation.** C3 regresses fourteen means rated by two
  different groups. It cannot show that any one participant rejects the
  conclusion because it is misleading, and the authors say so.
- **The misleadingness question builds in the answer.** It asks how
  misleading the claim would be "if you were later told" the item was on
  a thumb in the mouth. Rating that misleading is close to rejecting the
  inference, so the two measures may share a construal and not only a
  cause.
- **The Study 3 wording is leading.** "Technically speaking there is a
  pain located in her mouth" asserts the conclusion it then asks about.
  Raised agreement fits cancelled implicature, and also fits deference to
  the stated answer. Pain reached only 3.79, not above the midpoint.
- **No mental comparison term.** Without an item that people treat as
  mental and that still takes "in", the studies cannot show that "pain"
  differs from a mental-state term in this schema. That is the contrast
  C7 needs.
- **Version.** Read from the accepted manuscript; the typeset text was
  not compared.

## Open questions

- Would a sensation term that people call mental ("an itch", "a tingle",
  "a feeling of pressure") pattern with pain or with the paper-clip?
- Does the result hold for people in pain, or for chronic and diffuse
  pain, which Borg, Harrison, Stazicker & Salomons (2019) say the bodily
  view's studies leave out?
- Does the implicature account survive a measure of misleadingness that
  does not mention the thumb-in-mouth outcome?
