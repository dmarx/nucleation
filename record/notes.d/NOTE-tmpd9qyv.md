# NOTE-tmpd9qyv: Hardesty et al. — Toward a Standard Approach to Operationalizing Coercive Control and Classifying Violence Types

## Contribution

A proposed standard for classifying Johnson's types: coercive control
scored by the frequency of tactics on the PMWI Dominance–Isolation
subscale, with high control at 19 or more. And a test, in one sample, that
the types differ because of the control context and not because of the
amount of violence.

## Key results

- **Definitions** (Introduction): "Coercive controlling violence (or
  intimate terrorism) is violence that occurs in the context of coercive
  control, with the use of both violent and nonviolent tactics aimed at
  maintaining dominance over one’s partner." "Situational couple violence
  results from arguments or situations that escalate to physical acts but
  is not part of a larger motive to control one’s partner."
- **Johnson 2008 quoted** (Background): "Control is a continuum. Everyone
  ‘controls’ their partner to some extent" (p. 87).
- **Against counting** (Background): "A risk of the count approach is
  (mis)classifying highly controlling abusers as “low control” when they
  rely on very few control tactics but enforce them relentlessly."
- **Stability** (Results): under frequency "Only eight (4.2%) of the 190
  cases changed cluster classifications" between hierarchical and k-means
  solutions; under count, "Forty-one (21.5%)".
- **Cutoff** (Results): "A cutoff score of 19 or greater for high control
  was selected".
- **Four groups** (Results): no violence/low control (n = 73), situational
  couple violence (n = 57), nonviolent coercive control (n = 12), coercive
  controlling violence (n = 48).
- **Control, not violence** (Results): after controlling for the frequency
  and severity of violence, "The results were identical to those reported
  in Table 3, suggesting that the group differences reflect the control
  context rather than differences in violence."
- **Conclusion** (Discussion): "Our results provide substantial support
  for operationalizing coercive control by the frequency, rather than
  number, of tactics used by a partner."

## Assumptions

- The seven PMWI-DI items measure coercive control.
- Any CTS2 physical or sexual item counts as violence.
- Mothers' reports alone describe the relationship.

## Concepts

- **Coercive controlling violence / intimate terrorism**; **situational
  couple violence**; **nonviolent coercive control** (high control, no
  violence).
- **Count vs frequency approach** to scoring control tactics.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-709](../claims.d/CLAIM-709.md)**: corroborates, empirically. The group differences in
  fear, threat and harassment track the control context with the violence
  held constant, and a count of control tactics misclassifies controllers
  who "enforce them relentlessly". Both fit the claim that a tally of acts
  does not settle who dominates.
- **[CASE-279](../cases.d/CASE-279.md)**: the nonviolent coercive control group shows high
  control with no violence at all, a plainer divergence of the two
  questions than Carol and Dan, and a documented one.

## Connections

Stands in for Johnson (2008), [LIT-2715](../literature.d/LIT-2715.md), with Johnson as co-author. It
restates the book's two main types and that they are told apart by control
context, and quotes the book once (p. 87). It does not restate violent
resistance, mutual violent control, or the book's evidence.

## Limitations

One US county; divorcing mothers only; a 24% response rate; women's
reports only; a small nonviolent-control group (n = 12). One figure looks
garbled in the manuscript: the high-control hierarchical cluster has "a
mean of 17.95 (SD = 24.95, range: 17–35)".
