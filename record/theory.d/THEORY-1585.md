# THEORY-1585: Kruse and Schmitt, following McAdams, hold that generativity springs from cultural demand and an inner desire that includes a need for symbolic immortality, that its motives can be selfish or altruistic so that it is not altruism, and report, without statistics, that it predicted life satisfaction in older adults and rose in intergenerational projects

## The account

On the McAdams model the authors adopt, generativity has two
motivational sources: cultural demand, and an "inner desire" with two
parts, a "need to be needed" and a need for "symbolic immortality, that
is, to invest resources and potentials into things that outlive the
self" (§2). These lead to concern for the next generation and commitment
to it. The authors conclude that "engagement for younger generations can
reflect selfish as well as altruistic motives" (§2), and so keep
generativity apart from altruism, taken as behaviour that benefits a
recipient at a cost to the actor.

## Evidence

- In each of seven regions, generativity was "a highly significant
  predictor" of optimism and life satisfaction in people aged 59 to 108,
  more than health, finances or family status (§2); no statistics are
  given.
- In 14 evaluated intergenerational projects, Loyola Generativity Scale
  scores rose "substantially" in old and young participants (§3); no
  control group is mentioned.
- Many people with dementia showed generative concern (§2), described
  qualitatively.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-301](../questions.d/QUESTION-301.md)'s note of 2026-10-29: "Generativity includes a need for
symbolic immortality (Kruse and Schmitt, [NOTE-1261](../notes.d/NOTE-1261.md))." On the model, one
source of care for the next generation has the shape the question asks
about, a self extended in time through what outlives it; but cultural
demand is a second source, concern is a separate stage, and no study
reported measures the immortality motive apart from concern.
