# NOTE-136: Safron et al. — World models and life–mind continuity

## Contribution

This editorial introduces a theme issue on how natural and artificial intelligences model the world, and what that reveals about cognition and the continuity of life and mind. The editors take no single definition. They survey causal, self-referential, goal-directed, collective and narrative forms of world modelling. A recurring question is whether AI trained on vast data captures the context-sensitive, temporally embedded, value-laden aspects of biological world modelling, or mainly exploits statistical surface regularities. Contributions also address embodiment, complexity, learning architectures and the social settings of world models. The aim is to clarify the conceptual landscape and to frame questions about grounded understanding, robust agency and human-like general intelligence.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1: the issue opens with a long passage from Hofstadter: when words "act like" things in the world they mean them, and a strange-loop self-model is all an "I" needs. The editors pose the issue's questions: which world models go with which systems, with which conscious phenomena, with LLMs, and with all life including cells.
- §1: they warn that "world model", like "consciousness", risks becoming a "suitcase word". They also float the speculative idea that the two concepts may be ambiguous in parallel because world modelling helps explain subjective experience.
- §2 structure: (a) approaches to emergent intelligence (Hofstadter on an "I" in AI; Krakauer et al. on LLMs and emergence; Ku et al. on levels of analysis for LLMs), (b) principles of world-modelling systems (empowerment and causal model construction, self-modelling, model-based vs model-free RL complexity, the good-regulator theorem, physics for AI), and (c) collective intelligence (cognitive glues, Rogers' paradox with human–AI interaction, Battleday and Gershman on AI for science, "what does it mean to have an experience?").
- §2(a)(i) summary: Hofstadter holds that passing a consistent-understanding Turing test suffices for attributing thinking, and that there is no sharp boundary between machine and human meaning. His examples include LLM failures on object-stacking tasks.
- §3: LLMs model transitions between words, not the causal processes words refer to. The power of language may lead us to overestimate them. A graded criterion: information about the world (LLMs pass), representation of causal structure (uncertain), causal prediction in service of agency (larger divergence from biological systems). The editors close with a call for pluralism.

## Open questions

- The three-tier criterion for "world model" in §3 is a usable distinction for any note on whether LLMs have world models, and it could anchor a nucleation cluster on grounding and understanding (compare [LIT-131](../literature.d/LIT-131.md) Freeborn and [LIT-207](../literature.d/LIT-207.md) Roberts).
- It is an editorial: it surveys and frames rather than argues. The individual contributions (Hofstadter; Krakauer et al.; Ku et al.) are where the claims live and may deserve their own notes.
- The editorial is by the issue's guest editors and co-organisers; the author list (14) is broad and several listed authors contributed "conceptualization" only, per the contributions statement.
