# THEORY-477: None of the ten morphosyntactic criteria linguists use to identify words is necessary and sufficient for the orthographic word across languages, and the test batteries that combine them pick criteria opportunistically, so no cross-linguistically valid definition of the word is yet available

## Source

Haspelmath (2011), [LIT-063](../literature.d/LIT-063.md) (*Folia Linguistica* 45(1), 31–80), read in
full in [NOTE-073](../notes.d/NOTE-073.md) from the author's deposit of the publisher's PDF.

## What was actually shown

**The target.** A criterion succeeds only if it picks out "words as
identified by written spaces in the normal spelling used by speakers
and/or by linguists" (p. 38). Four other bases are set aside first (§2):
speaker intuitions, semantic non-compositionality, orthography itself and
the phonological word.

**Criterion by criterion (§3).** Each of the ten criteria in (2), p. 38,
meets at least one counterexample in each direction it would need to hold.
Dalabon allows pauses inside a word, and clitics cannot be preceded by
one. Bloomfield's "minimum free form" makes *firewater* a phrase and
*take the money* a word. Mobility depends on telling "same construction"
from "different construction", for which there is no criterion (fn. 18).
Incorporation interrupts words (Pawnee). Edge inflection and English
possessive *'s* are promiscuous without being words. Affixes are ellipted
and coordinated (*pro- and anti-war*, Turkish suspended affixation).
Anaphoric islandhood tracks non-referentiality, not wordhood, and Georgian
allows inbound anaphora. Extraction targets referential phrases, so
nonextractability says nothing about words (p. 52). Morphophonological
idiosyncrasy occurs in clitic combinations. All four kinds of deviation
from biuniqueness occur in syntax, (31)–(37).

**The batteries (§4, Table 1, p. 60).** Nine studies that combine criteria
use different subsets. No criterion is used by all nine; non-selectivity
and non-coordinatability are used by six, biuniqueness by one. The method
"is not rigorous, because the criteria can be selected opportunistically
by the author" (p. 59).

**The consequence, as the paper hedges it.** (41), p. 65: "Linguists have
no good basis for identifying words across languages, and hence no good
basis for a general distinction between syntax and morphology".
Morphosyntax is "for theoretical purposes ... currently best viewed as a
unitary domain" (p. 72), and the paper leaves open that a cross-linguistic
word could still be found (p. 33; (42a), p. 66).

**What could have come out otherwise.** Any one criterion could have
survived the counterexample search, or the batteries could have converged
on a shared set. Neither happened.

## What this does not say

- **That the word cannot be defined in a given language.** §5 and §10 say
  "word in language X" can often be defined consistently, even so as to
  predict the spelling (p. 71). The finding is about comparison across
  languages.
- **That some non-orthographic intermediate unit is not universal.** The
  target is orthographic practice by design (p. 38). A critic can accept
  every counterexample and still posit such a unit. Haspelmath's reply is
  that it would not deserve the name "word" and that there are too many
  candidates to privilege one (§5).
- **That every combination of criteria fails.** The claim about
  combinations (C2 in [NOTE-073](../notes.d/NOTE-073.md)) is argued from disagreement among nine
  studies, not from a search over weightings. A weighted or fuzzy word
  would rescue the distinction if units clustered on a boundness scale,
  and that is untested (§6, Fig. 1; fn. 24 says why a test is hard).
- **That morphology and syntax are shown to be one domain.** That step
  needs the further premise that both are defined through the word, which
  the paper asserts (p. 32, fn. 2) and does not survey. It is the
  Proposed half, and belongs with [THEORY-643](THEORY-643.md)'s independent route.
- **That sub-word units, or any tokenization, are privileged.** Morphs
  fail biuniqueness in syntax as in morphology (§3.10). The paper says
  nothing about tokenization, and its practical advice, computational
  linguists included, is to keep the spaced word as a convention without
  theoretical status (§9). [NOTE-073](../notes.d/NOTE-073.md) corrects the seed that said otherwise.
- **That the counterexamples are uncontested.** Several rest on cited
  analyses that are disputed (Lezgian *-ni*, Japanese (19), Romance object
  markers as affixes). One per direction is enough to defeat "necessary
  and sufficient", but the paper does not say how often each criterion
  fails.

## Connections

- Bruening (2018; [LIT-071](../literature.d/LIT-071.md), [NOTE-065](../notes.d/NOTE-065.md)) cites this paper in fn. 2 and then
  grants, for the argument, that words can be identified. His case against
  a separate word component ([THEORY-643](THEORY-643.md)) therefore does not rest on
  this one, and the two reach the same negative verdict on lexical
  integrity by independent routes. Haspelmath's §§3.6–3.8 and Bruening's
  §§3.1, 5.1 and 5.3 use the same diagnostics.
