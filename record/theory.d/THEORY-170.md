# THEORY-170: Languages differ in what their grammar obliges a speaker to convey rather than in what they can convey, so interlingual translation must add some information and leave some unexpressed, and repeated translation can erode a message

## Source

Roman Jakobson (1959), "On Linguistic Aspects of Translation",
[LIT-786](../literature.d/LIT-786.md), pp. 235–236, as read in [NOTE-614](../notes.d/NOTE-614.md). Jakobson credits the
core observation to Boas ("Language", 1938) and the chain image to
Karcevski.

## What was actually shown

Nothing was tested; the support is argument from worked examples.

- **Expressibility.** "All cognitive experience and its classification is
  conveyable in any existing language": deficiencies are made good by
  loanwords, loan-translations, neologisms, semantic shifts and
  circumlocution, and a grammatical category the target lacks is rendered
  lexically (a dual as "two brothers").
- **Obligation.** "Languages differ essentially in what they must convey
  and not in what they may convey." Each verb "imperatively raises a set
  of specific yes-or-no questions". Rendering "She has brothers" in a
  language with a dual forces a choice between two and more than two, or
  an explicit disjunction. Rendering "I hired a worker" in Russian forces
  a choice of perfective or imperfective aspect and of a masculine or
  feminine noun, while the Russian does not carry the English
  distinctions of "hired" against "have hired" or "a" against "the".
- **Chains.** Because the two grammars pose different sets of two-choice
  situations, "a chain of translations of one and the same isolated
  sentence from English into Russian and vice versa could entirely deprive
  such a message of its initial content". "The richer the context of a
  message, the smaller the loss of information."

The thesis could come out wrong in two ways: if translators reliably leave
obligatory target categories unmarked or recoverable from the source, or if
round-trip chains of isolated sentences preserve the source's distinctions
as well as single steps do. The essay checks neither.

## What this does not say

- **Not linguistic relativity.** Jakobson rejects Whorf's inference from
  unlike grammars to unlike facts: cognitive content is translatable, and
  only what is obligatory differs. The thesis does not say speakers of
  different languages think differently, and the essay's only evidence
  about thought (a 1915 test of personified weekdays) is cited without
  data and concerns "verbal mythology", not cognition.
- **Not that translation is lossy overall.** The forced additions and
  omissions concern grammatically obligatory information; Jakobson holds
  that the message's cognitive content can always be rendered, if need be
  lexically and at length. The loss is a default of fluent, grammatical
  rendering, not an impossibility.
- **Not about pragmatic force.** The examples are all of referential
  content (number, aspect, sex, tense, definiteness). The essay says
  nothing about stance, footing, register or illocution, so it gives no
  support to claims that those are or are not preserved.
- **Not a measured rate.** The chain claim is a "could", stated through an
  analogy (currency exchanges at a loss), and the damping by context is
  asserted. Nothing fixes how fast a chain loses content, or when.
- **Not for poetry.** In poetry, jest, magic and "everyday verbal
  mythology" Jakobson holds that grammatical categories carry meaning
  themselves, and for poetry he says translation gives way to "creative
  transposition". The thesis is stated for the cognitive function of
  language.

Later accounts of equivalence held in the record, Nida's ([LIT-791](../literature.d/LIT-791.md)) and
House's ([LIT-789](../literature.d/LIT-789.md)), are unread here and are not evidence for or against
this.
