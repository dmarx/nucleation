# THEORY-1470: Collins and Shester find that US cities whose states enabled federal urban renewal earlier received more renewal grants and, per $100 of grants per 1950 resident, had about 2.4% higher median family income, 6.9% higher median property value and 9–11% more population and housing units in 1980 than similar cities, gains not explained by a changed racial or educational composition and not faded by 2000, decades after new funding ended, while the projects cleared or planned to clear over 400,000 housing units and relocated over 300,000 families, about 54% nonwhite, whose costs the city-level design does not measure

## Source

Collins & Shester (2013), [LIT-2161](../literature.d/LIT-2161.md); read from NBER Working Paper 17458 (November 2012 revision) in [NOTE-1683](../notes.d/NOTE-1683.md).

## What was actually shown

- **The instrument** (pp. 14–16): an extra year of eligibility under state
  enabling legislation brought $9.71 (s.e. 2.67) of grants per capita,
  first-stage F 13.8. In about 1,500 rural counties (under 25% urban in
  1950), the legislation predicts nothing. What could have come out
  otherwise: a nonzero rural relationship would have pointed to a
  confounder of state timing.
- **1980** (pp. 17, 25; Table 3): "a $100 per capita difference in grant
  funding is associated with a 2.4 percent difference in 1980 median income
  and a 6.9 percent difference in 1980 median property value", with 9–11%
  more population and housing units. Robust to a second instrument from
  state court reversals (F 32.2) and a long list of controls (pp. 18–22).
- **Composition** (pp. 24–25; Tables 5–6): no fall in the black population
  share and no rise in schooling; with 1980 composition controlled the
  coefficients are slightly larger.
- **Timing** (pp. 23, 26): income effects by 1960, property value by 1970;
  in 2000 "the estimated urban renewal effects do not dissipate with
  time", with larger but imprecise point estimates.
- **Displacement** (p. 5): the program's own figures to mid-1966, which
  the paper reports and does not estimate.

## What this does not say

- That the program was good for the people it displaced, or for the
  neighbourhoods cleared: the design is city aggregates, and the authors
  say the displaced's costs "would not be capitalized in local property
  values" (p. 27). LaVoice (2024), [NOTE-1680](../notes.d/NOTE-1680.md), finds the cleared
  neighbourhoods' poorest residents worse off.
- That the gains are net: weak county effects leave open relocation from
  surrounding areas into central cities (pp. 22–23).
- That the effects grew after 2000 or after 1974: the 2000 estimates are
  larger but imprecise, and "we hesitate to make strong inferences".
- That agglomeration, a threshold or multiple equilibria explains the
  persistence: these are offered (p. 7, fn. 40), not tested.
- That the estimates are social returns or fiscal multipliers, which the
  authors disclaim (p. 17).
- Anything about the Northeast and Midwest with precision, where the
  instrument varies little.
