# NOTE-tmpglh7k: Tiktok's enshittification

## Contribution

A name and a three-stage schema for a pattern in platform businesses, and
a policy conclusion drawn from it: since platforms will decay, protect
users' ability to leave rather than the platforms. It is an essay, not a
study.

## Key insight

A platform that can cheaply change who gets the value passing through it
will spend surplus on whichever side it needs to attract and then take it
back once that side is locked in. How far it can go is set by how costly
leaving is, so the decisive variable is the cost of exit, not the
platform's intentions.

## Assumptions

- Platforms are two-sided markets that hold each side "hostage to the
  other".
- Reallocating value is cheap: "It's just too easy to turn the
  enshittification dial up to eleven."
- Firms' internal actors prefer quick returns: product managers,
  executives and shareholders "are in a race to see who can eat their
  seed-corn first".
- Users leave once abuse passes a breaking point, and the point rises with
  switching costs.

## Key results

- **The lifecycle**, stated in the opening paragraph and restated as
  "surpluses are first directed to users; then, once they're locked in,
  surpluses go to suppliers; then once they're locked in, the surplus is
  handed to shareholders".
- **Amazon**: subsidised prices and search, DRM and Prime; then generous
  terms for sellers; then fees and paid placement (the author's figures,
  unchecked: 45% and more in fees, the first screens of a search half
  ads).
- **Facebook**: friends' posts; then publishers' traffic; then throttling
  publishers unless they pay to boost; then more ads and higher prices.
- **TikTok**: the Forbes report that staff "heat" chosen creators'
  videos, read as surplus handed to creators to commit them, to be
  withdrawn later. Doctorow notes the limit: "there's only so much
  enshittification they can visit upon their users' feeds".
- **Interoperability**: blocking clients and APIs makes leaving harder and
  so raises the abuse users will bear.
- **Equilibrium**: incentives push results "to the precise threshold at
  which users are nearly pissed off enough to leave, but not quite", an
  equilibrium that is "fragile" to competitors, scandals and "a worker
  uprising".

## Concepts

- **enshittification** — the three-stage lifecycle; "I call this
  enshittification".
- **two-sided market** — a platform between buyers and sellers, "holding
  each hostage to the other".
- **end-to-end principle** — "willing speakers' messages would be
  delivered to willing listeners' end-points as quickly and reliably as
  they could be".
- **freedom of exit** — "the right to leave a sinking platform while
  continuing to stay connected to the communities that you left behind",
  keeping media and data.

## Connections

year and a half later and organises the checks on it as four forces:
competition, regulation, interoperability and tech-worker power. This essay
already has three of them in some form, competitors, interoperable clients
and "a worker uprising", but not regulation as a check; here lawmakers
appear only as the source of the remedy. The 2025 book ([LIT-tmpzoc28](../literature.d/LIT-tmpzoc28.md)) is
unread. The essay draws on the Netheads' end-to-end tradition and on Page
and Brin's 1998 remark that advertising-funded search will be biased
toward advertisers.

## Bearing on the record

Source of [THEORY-tmptfh4j](../theory.d/THEORY-tmptfh4j.md). For the owner's essay:

- **§6.4** (alien interests). This is the essay's cited source. Doctorow's
  platform comes to pursue an interest, its shareholders' returns, that is
  served by working against the users and businesses it is made of and
  for: "every time Tiktok shows you a video you asked to see, it loses a
  chance to show you a video it wants you to see".
- **§6.3** (extraction from above). The record reads the third stage as an
  instance of extraction from above: the platform, the larger unit, takes
  value from the users and businesses inside it. Doctorow does not use
  levels.
- **§5.4** (protective lock-in). Doctorow's lock-in is not protective; it
  is built so that abuse can follow. It shares the essay's point that a
  regime can create the conditions that keep people in it.

## Limitations

The evidence is anecdote, linked journalism and the author's own
experience, with no measure and no counter-cases: no platform that stayed
good to its users is considered. "Seemingly inevitable" is hedged in the
same essay by the need for "measured amounts". The scope is platform
capitalism, not organizations in general.

## Open questions

Whether the lifecycle holds when switching costs are measured, and whether
platforms with low lock-in decay less; whether the stages are a sequence
or a description of a platform's choices at any one time.
