# THEORY-tmpt5gz6: In vignette studies of US online participants, judgements that an outcome happened because the agent wanted it move with judgements that it was done intentionally when luck is manipulated, and predict them better than know-how or probability-raising do; but they account for only about half of the luck effect, and in one morally neutral case the two come apart

## Source

Quillien (2026), "Luck and Intentional Action: A Causal Account", [LIT-132](../literature.d/LIT-132.md)
(*Open Mind* 10, 857–883), read in full in [NOTE-092](../notes.d/NOTE-092.md). The supplementary
information was not read.

## What was actually shown

Participants rated four statements per scenario on 1–7 scales: that the
agent did X intentionally, that X happened because the agent wanted X, that
the agent knew how to do X, and that the action raised the probability of X.

- **Study 1** (Knobe's marksman; skill × morality, within-subjects; N = 198;
  pre-registered). Low skill lowered both intentionality (6.65 vs 5.19,
  F(1, 591) = 240) and causation (6.56 vs 5.35, F = 194). Standardized β:
  causation .46, know-how .33, probability-raising .14.
- **Study 2** (Kraemer means/ends × morality, between-subjects; N = 673; not
  itself pre-registered, following pre-registered pilots). β: causation .56,
  know-how .20, probability-raising .16. In the morally bad vignette
  causation tracked the effect (5.39 vs 2.68, t(315) = 11.6). **In the
  neutral vignette intentionality differed (2.70 vs 2.20, p = .009) and
  causation did not (2.22 vs 2.20, p = .91).**
- **Study 3** (gumball machine; knowledge × process, within-subjects; N =
  142 after 56 of 198 were excluded; pre-registered). The process (normal or
  a mix-up after the choice) mattered more when the agent knew how the
  machine worked: Knowledge × Process F(1, 423) = 32 for intentionality and
  36 for causation. Know-how and probability-raising showed no such
  interaction (p = .12, .79), as their accounts are "agnostic" about it. β:
  causation .53, know-how .27, probability-raising .10. A causation-only
  model fit better than a know-how × probability-raising model (conditional
  R² .57 vs .44, difference CI [.07, .19]).
- **Across the 12 condition means**, intentionality correlates with causation
  r(10) = .98, with know-how .82 and with probability-raising .88.
- **Mediation** (exploratory, not pre-registered): causation carries 64%,
  45% and 49% of the luck effects.

What could have come out otherwise: causal judgements could have stayed flat
under the luck manipulations, or the Study 3 interaction could have been
absent or shown up in know-how instead. Neither happened. The neutral
Kraemer case is where the prediction failed.

## What this does not say

- **That luck lowers intentionality by way of causal judgement.** The
  mediation is exploratory and covers about half the effect, and the author
  calls the evidence "mostly correlational". Know-how and probability-raising
  stay weakly or moderately associated with intentionality when causation is
  controlled (General Discussion).
- **That the causal account explains the Kraemer effect.** It does in the
  morally bad vignette only.
- **That a computational model was fitted.** The account is verbal:
  necessity and robustness are taken informally from counterfactual theories
  of causal judgement, and robustness is inferred from the manipulations,
  never measured ([NOTE-092](../notes.d/NOTE-092.md), corrections).
- **That the causation item measures causal strength.** "Because [she]
  wanted" is close in wording to the intentionality item, which invites
  consistency effects the author himself raises.
- **That the pattern is general.** One scenario family per study; the
  morality contrast is confounded with difficulty; US Prolific workers only.
  The r = .98 rests on 12 condition means.
- **Anything about what agents are.** This is the folk concept of
  intentional action. The paper's analogy with knowledge in Gettier cases,
  that the concept is "externalist", is argued by analogy only. It does not
  study attributions to machines.

## Connections

- Goldstein & Lederman ([THEORY-tmpqr2ow](THEORY-tmpqr2ow.md)) concern whether and which desires
  to attribute to a language-model instance. This account concerns how,
  once a desire is attributed, people decide an outcome was done
  intentionally: the desire–outcome link must be robust.
- Pernu ([LIT-144](../literature.d/LIT-144.md)) shares the necessity and sufficiency vocabulary of
  counterfactual causation, but as metaphysics, not folk psychology.
