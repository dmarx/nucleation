# LIT-415: Extended Cognition and Extended Consciousness

David J. Chalmers (2019), in Matteo Colombo, Elizabeth Irvine and Mog
Stapleton (eds.), *Andy Clark and His Critics*, Oxford University Press,
ch. 1, pp. 9 ff. — DOI-10.1093/oso/9780190662813.003.0002

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, as part of the extended-mind
programme of David Chalmers. [NOTE-356](../notes.d/NOTE-356.md) is the close reading of the
same day, and it placed the work: **Active**.

**It carries [LIT-097](LIT-097.md) further, and corrects it.** The chapter opens by
saying it will be "criticizing and revising the statement of the thesis in
our original article". It reconstructs the 1998 thesis (constitution by
external entities "when those entities play the right role in driving
cognitive processes"), concedes Farkas's objection that this is
near-obviously true of an implanted circuit and is accepted by Adams,
Aizawa and Rupert, and replaces it with a perception-and-action version.
It also withdraws [LIT-097](LIT-097.md)'s reply that Otto's access to his notebook "is
not perceptual at all": that reply was "something of a stretch", since
Otto plainly sees the notebook. [NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md) recorded the same reply as the
paper's weakest step; this is its co-author agreeing.

**It carries the foreword further.** Chalmers's foreword to Clark's
book ([LIT-421](LIT-421.md)) first set out the perception-action boundary as "by
far the strongest objection" and first floated a bandwidth explanation of
why consciousness does not extend. This chapter keeps the objection,
makes it the definition of what is controversial, and drops the bandwidth
explanation in favour of directness, conceding Karina Vold's point that
vision is a very high-bandwidth channel.

**What it reaches and what it does not.** On this statement the thesis
covers standing beliefs and processes like mental rotation, linked by
sensorimotor interaction. Wired-in circuits, brain-computer interfaces and
intravenous stimulation fall outside it: the chapter places them with the
subpersonal "extended circuit" cases, which everyone already accepts. That
bears on Register's account of BCIs as body parts ([LIT-138](LIT-138.md)): on this
chapter's terms a BCI is the uncontroversial kind of extension, not the
extended mind. Consciousness is not reached by the perception-and-action
route: the conclusion is that "even if there is extended cognition, there
is no extended consciousness". The chapter does allow that an extended
circuit could carry consciousness, since directness is no obstacle there.
So the denial is about notebooks, phones and screens, not about implants.

**On the hard problem.** The chapter stays neutral between physicalism and
dualism by reading "partly constituted" as partial grounding, or, for a
property dualist, as nomological dependence of consciousness on extended
physical states. Its consciousness criterion, direct availability for
global control, is the one Chalmers's *The Conscious Mind* and his work on
the neural correlate proposed as the physical correlate of consciousness;
those are being filed in this record in the same batch. So the chapter
uses a correlate, not an explanation of experience, and leaves the hard
problem where it was.

**On [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md).** That account says the upward move from minds to a
conscious group is refused either by anti-nesting or by an architectural
criterion, and that neither is yet principled. The directness criterion is
a candidate architectural criterion, stated by a different route, and it
has a feature that makes it bite on groups. Direct access, the chapter says,
"requires access that is not mediated by other mental states". In a nation,
every link between parts runs through the citizens' own perceptions and
actions, which are mental states of the parts. A society of mindless
agents in one skull has no such mediation. So, as I read it, the criterion
would separate Minsky's case from Schwitzgebel's, and it does so by
something close to an anti-nesting principle in functional dress. Two
cautions. Chalmers never applies it to groups. And its motivation has the
weakness [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) finds in the others: he offers it to explain an
independently held verdict ("there seem to be no plausible cases"), and
says it is not a knockdown. It is also not the objection Schwitzgebel
answers in [LIT-159](LIT-159.md) §6 ([NOTE-131](../notes.d/NOTE-131.md)): that was a relational-capacity
principle Chalmers put in correspondence. Applying the directness
criterion to both models is the kind of computation the theory's
`promote_when` asks for, and nobody has done it.

**On AI consciousness.** Direct availability for global control is close
kin to the global-workspace indicators in [LIT-056](LIT-056.md) and so to the
architectural half of [THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md)'s dispute. For tools, the chapter's
verdict is that a user's phone or assistant, reached by perception, can at
most extend belief and cognition, never experience.

The extended-mind debate this chapter enters is the one held here in
Adams and Aizawa ([LIT-438](LIT-438.md)), Rupert ([LIT-422](LIT-422.md)) and Clark's reply
([LIT-403](LIT-403.md)); its §2 answers each of their objections in a sentence. It
carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.
