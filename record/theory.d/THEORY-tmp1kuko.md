# THEORY-tmp1kuko: In a 2024 survey of a quota-matched online sample of 2,092 US adults and a convenience sample of 103 antitrust lawyers, the public weighed layoffs, worse product quality and more lobbying well above price reductions in judging whether a merger should be challenged, the consumer welfare standard's own objective, while support for replacing that standard was 42.6 points lower among the lawyers than among the public, against a partisan gap of 5.9 points

## Source

Short, Hill and Brown (2025), *Perspectives on Politics* 24(2): 680–700,
[LIT-tmpm7glb](../literature.d/LIT-tmpm7glb.md), open access, read in [NOTE-tmpxvj22](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpxvj22.md). Hypotheses H4, H6 and
H8 of the pre-analysis plan (osf.io/nyxha).

## What was actually shown

**The conjoint (H8).** Each public respondent judged four hypothetical
mergers whose attributes varied at random: prices, quality, employment,
lobbying, bailout risk, firm size and industry. The outcome was whether
the government should challenge the merger. Average marginal component
effects, in percentage points of support for a challenge: price cuts of
5%, 10% and 20% lowered it by 2.6 to 7.7, significant only at 10% and 20%;
a 20% workforce cut raised it by 26.3; worse quality by 26.6; more lobbying
by 8.1; more bailout risk by 4.7. Price changes and layoffs were varied on
the same scale, from a 5% increase to a 20% decrease, "to avoid biasing the
results". Partisan subgroups did not differ significantly in any
attribute's weight.

**The cleavage (H4, H6).** Asked whether the government "should mainly
consider the impact on consumers" or "give equal weight to the impact on
consumers, workers, small businesses, and local communities", 80.6% of
Democrats, 81.4% of independents and 74.8% of Republicans chose the second.
Among the lawyers 36.7% did. The partisan gap was 5.9 points and the
public–lawyer gap 42.6 (both p < 0.05). The paper reports the result robust
to demographic controls in an appendix not read here.

What could have come out otherwise: price cuts could have weighed as much
as layoffs, and lawyers could have differed from the public no more than
Republicans differ from Democrats. Neither happened. The randomized
attributes make the conjoint weights a within-design comparison, so they
do not depend on the sample matching the population's composition.

## What this does not say

- **That the samples are representative.** The public sample is a
  nonprobability panel weighted to national margins. The lawyers are a
  convenience sample, recruited by Facebook advertising and email to
  practice groups, and 51.4% Democrats.
- **That the public's weights are its interest.** The paper itself does not
  equate the two (note 9).
- **Why lawyers differ.** They are about 18 points less likely to believe
  large corporations have too much power. Whether training or selection
  produces that is not identified.
- **That the consumer welfare standard is ideological capture.** The
  measurement shows that values outside the standard weigh heavily with
  the public and that practitioners diverge. The verdict of capture also
  needs the paper's historical case and is held, Proposed, as
  [THEORY-tmpw39mh](THEORY-tmpw39mh.md).
