# THEORY-491: Of the five standard arguments that randomisation gives a clinical trial special evidential weight, only the selection-bias argument is sound, and it supports only taking allocation out of the experimenters' hands; given balance on plausible confounders, a placebo or active control and blinding, randomisation is argued to add nothing, since balance on unknown confounders is not even probable in the one division a trial makes

## Source

Worrall (2007), [LIT-175](../literature.d/LIT-175.md) (*Philosophy Compass* 2(6), 981–1022), read in full
in [NOTE-126](../notes.d/NOTE-126.md). Page references are the journal's.

## What was actually shown

**The target (§4 frame).** Evidence hierarchies let high-quality RCTs
"trump any number of observational studies" (Barton 2000), however well
matched and however large. The question is whether general principles of
evidence underwrite that. The shared core Worrall assumes is that a theory
gets little support from data its plausible rivals also entail (fn. 6).

**Five arguments, four rejected.**
- (a) *Fisher's significance-test logic.* In the tea-tasting example a
  perfect score has p = 1/70 under randomised order, but after randomisation
  there remain indefinitely many ways the null can be false (a confederate,
  visual cues, a belief that cups alternate, which gives p = 1/2). It fails
  "even in its own terms".
- (b) *Unknown confounders.* "Probable balance" is defined only over
  indefinite repetitions of the division, "But medical researchers … do it
  once!" (p. 1007). Over an open-ended list of unknown factors it is less
  defined still. The Leibovici retroactive-prayer trial (n = 3393; length
  of stay p = 0.01, fever duration p = 0.04) was impeccably randomised and
  double-blind. Randomisation is neither necessary (appendectomy and other
  untrialled standard treatments) nor sufficient for real evidence.
- (d) *Observational studies "exaggerate".* Inferring exaggeration
  presupposes that RCTs give the true effect. Newer comparisons (Benson &
  Hartz; Concato et al.; Kunz & Oxman; LeLorier et al.) do not show
  systematic overestimation.
- (e) *Probabilistic causality* (Cartwright, Papineau, Pearl): the
  common-cause argument is (b) in another guise. This is asserted here and
  argued elsewhere.

**One accepted.** (c) *Selection bias*: if clinicians choose arms, their
prognostic views can confound the trial. This is "a cast-iron argument for
randomization", endorsed by Bayesians too (p. 1009). But it makes
randomisation "very much a means to an end", and haphazard allocation
(alternation, birthday order) would serve (p. 1010).

**Conclusion.** What matters is balance on plausible confounders, a
placebo or active control, and blinding. Whether balance comes from
randomising and checking or from matching "seems epistemically
unimportant". Carefully matched historically controlled trials can give
strong evidence of large effects; RCTs are "useful" for small expected
effects (p. 1018).

**What could have come out otherwise.** The meta-research on observational
estimates could have shown consistent overestimation by matched designs.
It is mixed. The rest is argument, and the reading grades it moderate (C3,
C4, C9) except the selection-bias concession (strong).

## What this does not say

- **That randomisation is worthless.** It is sound protection against
  selection bias, and the paper keeps RCTs for small effects.
- **That randomised designs are no more reliable in practice.** The
  argument against (b) shows that frequentist balance is no single-case
  guarantee. It does not compare track records, and the cited comparisons
  disagree with each other.
- **That background knowledge suffices to name the plausible confounders.**
  That is what defenders of randomisation doubt for small effects, and the
  reply, that such effects may not be worth having, is one undeveloped
  sentence (C11).
- **That haphazard allocation is safe.** Alternation and birth order can be
  predictable or correlate with prognosis. The paper does not discuss it.
- **Anything about allocation concealment** as a separate virtue. It is
  folded into selection bias.
- **That repeated randomisation is idle.** The single-case argument does not
  touch designs that do repeat the division, where the long-run rationale
  applies ([NOTE-126](../notes.d/NOTE-126.md)).

## Connections

- Cartwright ([THEORY-451](THEORY-451.md)) argues, from a different definition of
  rigour, that even a well-run RCT secures at most a study-population
  average and is not by its method evidence about another population. The
  two accounts deflate the hierarchy from different sides and do not
  conflict. Worrall lists Cartwright's 1989 book among randomisation's
  defenders.
- Reiss, [LIT-118](../literature.d/LIT-118.md), states the eliminative core Worrall calls "scientific
  commonsense": warrant comes from eliminating relevant alternatives.
- Leonelli ([THEORY-637](THEORY-637.md)) criticises the RCT as a universal exemplar of
  reproducible research, from a practice-oriented direction.
- [THEORY-138](THEORY-138.md) is the interventionist account of causation. The probabilistic
  causality defences in (e) are close relatives, and Worrall answers them
  only in summary.
