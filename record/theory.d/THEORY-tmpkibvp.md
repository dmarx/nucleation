# THEORY-tmpkibvp: Eyerman argues, in his book's first chapter, that cultural trauma is a loss of identity and meaning in a group rather than an individual wound, and that slavery became the cultural trauma of African Americans in retrospect, for a generation after emancipation, by shared fate rather than by experience or descent

## The account

Eyerman separates two kinds of trauma. Psychological trauma is a wound in
an individual. Cultural trauma is "a dramatic loss of identity and
meaning, a tear in the social fabric, affecting a group of people that has
achieved some degree of cohesion" (p. 2), and it "need not necessarily be
felt by everyone in a community or experienced directly by any or all"
(p. 2). Its meaning must be established and accepted, through mediation
and representation, by carrier groups: intellectuals as a role, and social
movements.

His case is slavery, taken "not as institution or even experience, but as
collective memory" (p. 1). It "was traumatic in retrospect" (p. 1), for a
generation "for whom slavery was a thing of the past" (p. 2). It was set
off when Reconstruction failed and white North and South reconciled
around a memory of slavery as benign; the black memory formed as a
counter-memory to that one. Membership was not set by descent: "Whether or
not they directly experienced slavery or even had ancestors who did,
blacks in the United States were identified with and came to identify
themselves through the memory and representation of slavery" (p. 14).
Slavery is traumatic "for those who share a common fate, not necessarily a
common experience" (pp. 14–15). Each social generation reworks the memory,
and it became a national trauma only with the civil rights movement
(p. 18).

## Evidence

The chapter itself ([NOTE-1246](../notes.d/NOTE-1246.md)), read from the publisher's sample. It
reports no data. Its claims are definitions and a plan for the book; the
historical argument is in chapters 2–6, unread. The account of the white
reconciliation memory rests on historians it quotes. The chapter gives no
criterion for when a memory has been "accepted and publicly given
credence", and it sets individual experience aside by design, so it says
nothing about what members feel.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-297](../questions.d/QUESTION-297.md), the identity route. On this account what passes is a change
in what a group takes itself to be, not distress. It passes through a
category, so that anyone placed in the category takes the past on,
whether or not descended from those who suffered. And the trigger was a
present rejection, a generation after the event. The question's note of
2026-10-29 on the other routes reads it so.
