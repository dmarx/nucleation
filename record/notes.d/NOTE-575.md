# NOTE-575: Précis of Responsibility and Control: A Theory of Moral Responsibility

## Contribution

A compressed statement, by the authors, of the mature version of Fischer's
reasons-responsiveness account. Compared with Fischer's 1987 paper ([LIT-687](../literature.d/LIT-687.md),
read in [NOTE-579](NOTE-579.md)), it adds two things: moderate in place of weak
responsiveness, and an ownership condition met by taking responsibility.

## Key insight

What makes an action free enough for responsibility is not the agent's
ability to do otherwise. It is the kind of process the action actually came
from: one that tracks reasons in an intelligible pattern, and that the agent
has, by an ordinary history, come to treat as hers to answer for.

## Assumptions

- Responsibility in the Strawsonian sense: being "an appropriate candidate
  for the reactive attitudes" (book, ch. 1, p. 6).
- An epistemic condition exists alongside the control condition, and is set
  aside (book, p. 13).
- Mechanisms can be individuated into kinds; the précis does not say how.

## Key results

Argument, summarised rather than made:

- Moral responsibility "simply requires guidance control"; footnote 1 calls
  guidance control "the freedom-relevant condition necessary and sufficient
  for moral responsibility" (p. 441).
- Ownership by taking responsibility, through moral education, as a
  standing policy carried by sameness of kind of mechanism (pp. 441–443).
- Moderate reasons-responsiveness: regular receptivity, including to moral
  reasons, in a pattern a third party can understand, and at least weak
  reactivity (p. 444).
- Responsiveness is a "general capacity", not an alternative possibility
  (n. 3, p. 444).
- Semicompatibilism (p. 445).

## Concepts

- **regulative control** — dual power over alternatives.
- **guidance control** — acting from one's own moderately reasons-responsive
  mechanism.
- **taking responsibility** — coming, by an appropriate route, to see oneself
  as an agent whose choices make a difference and as a fair target of the
  reactive attitudes for what a kind of mechanism produces.
- **moderate reasons-responsiveness** — regular receptivity, at least weak
  reactivity.

## Connections

It summarises the book ([LIT-684](../literature.d/LIT-684.md)) and descends from Fischer's 1987 paper
([LIT-687](../literature.d/LIT-687.md)), which had weak responsiveness and no ownership condition. Its
subjectivist element is acknowledged: "being free and morally responsible
requires one to see oneself as free and morally responsible" (n. 2, p. 442).

## Bearing on the record

[THEORY-121](../theory.d/THEORY-121.md) cited this précis in its promotion condition as the reading
that would confirm the account. It does confirm the account as the
encyclopedia reports gave it, and the theory now takes it as a source.

For groups: the book sets them aside explicitly. "We shall be focusing on
the moral responsibility of individuals. Perhaps the theory will also be
relevant to evaluating the moral responsibility of groups, but it is
intended in the first instance to apply to individual responsibility"
(ch. 1, n. 13, p. 10). It lists "corporate responsibility" among uses of
"responsibility" other than the moral one (ch. 1, n. 1, p. 2). The owner's
essay (§7.2) would be extending it, not applying it.

## Limitations

A summary: none of the arguments is given here. Its subjectivism means an
agent who never comes to see herself as responsible fails the ownership
condition. How mechanisms are individuated is not addressed.

## Open questions

Whether ownership by taking responsibility can be had by an organization,
whose "beliefs about itself" would have to be corporate attitudes in List
and Pettit's sense ([THEORY-127](../theory.d/THEORY-127.md)).
