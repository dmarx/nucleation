# NOTE-tmpmqae9: Moral Luck

## Contribution

A survey of the problem of moral luck and the responses to it. For this
record it stands in for Williams's 1976 paper, which could not be fetched.
It is a second statement, beside Nagel's own (LIT-tmp2dx6l), of the four
kinds of luck.

## Key results

- **The pair of papers (preamble).** "as Williams (1976) and Thomas Nagel
  (1979) showed in their now classic pair of articles", everyday judgment
  commits us to moral luck. Williams later: "when I first introduced the
  expression moral luck, I expected to suggest an oxymoron" (1993, 251).
- **The Control Principle (§1).** "We are morally assessable only to the
  extent that what we are assessed for depends on factors under our
  control." Corollary: "Two people ought not to be morally assessed
  differently if the only other differences between them are due to
  factors beyond their control."
- **Four kinds (§1).** "Nagel identifies four kinds of luck in all:
  resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, and causal." Circumstantial luck
  reaches intentions themselves: "circumstantial luck affects even our
  intentions, so it seems that we cannot be assessed in virtue of our
  intentions".
- **Williams's agent-regret (§3.1).** "Williams elucidates a notion of
  “agent-regret,”": a first-person regret, unlike bystander regret, whose
  expression may include "the willingness to compensate a person who was
  harmed by one’s actions". On the lorry driver who, through no fault of his
  own, runs over a child: "we feel sorry for the driver, but that sentiment
  co-exists with, indeed presupposes, that there is something special about
  his relation to this happening, something which cannot merely be
  eliminated by the consideration that it was not his fault" (1976
  [1993b, 43]). The entry files this under a strategy for explaining moral
  luck away: "it is understandable or even appropriate to feel differently
  about the driver who kills a child than about the one who does not",
  while assessing both alike.
- **Wolf's nameless virtue (§3.1).** "taking responsibility for one’s
  actions and their consequences" (2001, 13), which "involves a willingness
  to give more…that justice requires" (14).
- **Reading Williams (§3.4).** "Extracting Williams’ position on “Moral
  Luck” is a notoriously difficult task." "Many commentators have read
  Williams as advocating the position that moral luck exists and is deeply
  threatening to morality." But "in the Postscript, Williams makes a
  distinction between morality and ethics that allows him to deny the
  existence of moral luck". Morality's point was to "provide a shelter
  against luck, one realm of value (indeed, of supreme value) that is
  defended against contingency" (1993, 251). Since Gauguin-type cases show
  overall value depends on luck, "Instead, Williams suggests, we should
  care about ethics".
- **Accepting moral luck (§4.1).** Browne would keep anger without
  hostility. Watson's "there but for the grace of God go I" is reported as
  a reason for less blaming attitude.

## Assumptions

- Moral luck is defined against control. The entry sets aside broader
  analyses of luck.
- The focus is on responsibility, blame and praise, not deontic or
  axiological judgment.

## Concepts

- **Control Principle** and its corollary.
- **resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal luck**.
- **agent-regret** vs **bystander regret** (Williams).
- **taking responsibility** as a virtue (Wolf).
- **morality vs ethics** (Williams's Postscript).
- **epistemic argument** (Richards, Latus).

## Bearing on the record

- A7 §2 ("moral residue") and A7 §7 (moral luck), the exchange's two
  citations of Williams. See the LIT update for the verdict: agent-regret
  supports A's "regret need not imply culpability" and the idea that one
  can owe repair without deserving blame. The "painful choices ...
  regardless of which option they select" are Williams's moral-dilemma
  remainder, from "Ethical Consistency" (1965), which Nagel cites; they are
  not this paper's topic.
- CLAIM-tmpyjzi8 and CLAIM-tmpnfjln: Wolf's virtue, giving "more…that
  justice requires" by taking responsibility for outcomes beyond one's
  fault, describes the disposition those claims say an organization can
  exploit. Note only.
- CLAIM-tmpnw7ez: none directly.

## Limitations

one quoted passage and the entry's reading of it and of the 1993
Postscript. The Gauguin case is mentioned, not set out.
