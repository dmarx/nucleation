# NOTE-tmprub8s: Berridge and Kringelbach — Pleasure systems in the brain

## Contribution

A review of fifteen years of causal mapping, mostly from Berridge's
laboratory. Its own move is to separate the components of reward by their
mechanisms and to prune two textbook pleasure generators, mesolimbic
dopamine and the self-stimulation electrode, by reassigning them to
'wanting'.

## Key insight

Pursuit and pleasure are separable functions, and pursuit can be driven to
its maximum while pleasure stays flat or falls. On the review's account,
"dissociated ‘wanting’ is merely a counterfeit pleasure or sham reward,
which lacks a true ‘liking’ component" (§ Incentive salience as sham
reward).

## Assumptions

- **Objective and subjective levels.** 'Liking' (in quotes) is "objective
  hedonic reactions", as distinct from "the subjective experience of
  liking" (§ A neuroscience of pleasure). The review assumes the objective
  reaction is the core process and that conscious pleasure is built on top
  of it: "Objective affective reactions likely appeared first during
  evolution, with subjective affective reactions following in some
  species, via the evolution of more elaborate and hierarchical brain
  mesocorticolimbic circuitry to translate core ‘liking’ reactions into
  conscious feelings of pleasure" (§ Evolutionary origins).
- **Taste reactivity is a valid measure of hedonic impact** because it is
  homologous across species and has "a uniquely specific relation to the
  hedonic evaluation of taste ‘liking’" (§ Objective hedonic reactions).
- **Gain and loss of function are different evidence.** A site that can
  amplify liking need not be needed for normal liking (§ Mapping brain
  pleasure generators?).

## Key results

As the review reports them, from experiments not read here:

- **The three components** are "liking (core reactions to hedonic impact),
  wanting (motivation process of incentive salience), and learning
  (Pavlovian or instrumental associations and cognitive representations)".
  In the reward cycle, "wanting processes tend to dominate the initial
  appetitive phase, while liking processes dominate the subsequent
  consummatory phase that may lead to satiety" (§ A neuroscience of
  pleasure).
- **Taste reactivity across species.** "Homologous ‘liking’ orofacial
  expressions can be elicited also in apes and monkeys, and even in rats
  and mice". The expressions are "appropriately modulated physiologically
  by relevant appetite versus satiety states (Cabanac and Lafrance,
  1990". That is alliesthesia, cited to Cabanac (§ Objective hedonic
  reactions).
- **Liking dissociates from intake.** "Yet, taste reactivity ‘liking’
  expressions are not diminished by such pharmacological dopamine
  blockade (Peciña et al., 1997), nor even by complete destruction of
  mesolimbic dopamine projections." So "dopamine is not actually needed
  for the hedonic impact of food pleasure, but rather only for their
  incentive motivation value" (§ Objective hedonic reactions).
- **Hotspots.** "The size of hedonic hotspots mapped so far is each about 1
  cubic millimeter in volume in rats". In the accumbens hotspot, "mu
  opioid stimulation via agonist microinjections can at least double the
  hedonic impact of sucrose" (§ Causal hedonic hotspots). At the same
  site, "neither dopamine stimulation or glutamate AMPA blockade alter
  hedonic ‘liking’ for sucrose at all, even though both elevate ‘wanting’
  to eat as effectively as opioid stimulation" (§ Interaction between
  hotspot site and neurochemical stimulation). Loss of function is
  concentrated in one place: "the posterior VP hotspot appears more
  crucial than any other known brain site for loss of hedonic function
  after damage, at least for taste pleasure" (§ Ventral pallidum hotspot).
- **Electrodes.** Berridge and Valenstein (1991): "LH stimulation failed to
  enhance ‘liking’ reactions to sweetness, even though it made the rats
  ‘want’ to eat at least four times more than normal amounts". Heath's
  patient B-19 was never quoted as saying the stimulation felt pleasant,
  and Schlaepfer et al.'s accumbens patients reported new desires without
  pleasure (§ ‘Pleasure electrodes’).
- **Dopamine agonists in Parkinson's patients.** They drive compulsive
  gambling, shopping and similar pursuits, "Yet these cases typically do
  not report intense pleasure" (§ Dopamine elevations produce higher
  ‘wanting’ without higher ‘liking’?).
- **Incentive sensitization.** "Incentive-sensitization would produce
  addiction, by selectively magnifying cue-triggered ‘wanting’ to take the
  drug again, and so powerfully cause motivation even if the drug became
  less pleasant" (§ Resolving the cocaine puzzle?).
- **Unconscious liking.** "objective hedonic ‘liking’ reactions can
  sometimes occur alone and unconsciously in ordinary people without any
  subjective pleasure feeling at all", and "Unconscious ‘liking’ reactions
  still effectively change goal-directed human behavior, though those
  changes may remain undetected or be misinterpreted even by the person
  who has them". Hence "Subjective ratings are not always more accurate
  about hedonic impact than objective hedonic reactions, and the latter can
  be measured independently of the former" (§ Subjective versus objective
  levels).
- **Wanting can be appraised as pleasure.** "To the external observer, and
  perhaps even sometimes to the experiencing person, ‘wanting’ may be
  appraised as a positive reward involving eager anticipation." So
  "subjective reward experience may be partly synthesized from motivation
  and cognitive appraisal components" (§§ Incentive salience as sham
  reward; Resolving the cocaine puzzle?).
- **The valence of wanting is plastic.** "the hedonic valence of
  dissociated ‘wanting’ can easily flip from positive incentive into a
  negative valence of anxiety, frustration or fear. Reversing the hedonic
  valence of the experience would not necessarily disrupt its motivating
  power." Tantalus is the example, and "Motivational salience is never
  neutral, but its valence is not fixed" (§ Incentive salience as sham
  reward).
- **The keyboard.** Inhibition of accumbens shell releases appetite at the
  front and fear at the back. "Retuning can completely reverse the valence
  generated at a site from desire to dread, or back from dread to desire"
  (stressful lights and noise against a home-like room), and "Thus
  corticolimbic regulation adjusts both the intensity and valence of
  motivations produced by NAc circuitry" (§ Retuning the affective
  keyboard).
- **A common currency, in imaging.** Diverse pleasures engage "a ‘common
  currency’ reward network of interacting brain regions" (§ Mapping
  pleasure in the brain). The review takes this from correlational
  imaging, and contrasts it with the much smaller set of causal hotspots.

## Concepts

- **'liking'** — the objective hedonic reaction to a reward, whether or not
  it is felt.
- **'wanting' (incentive salience)** — motivational value attributed to a
  cue or representation, which makes it attractive and attention-grabbing.
- **hedonic hotspot / coldspot** — a site where the same neurochemical
  stimulation amplifies (or suppresses) 'liking'.
- **incentive sensitization** — dopaminergic hyper-reactivity that
  magnifies cue-triggered 'wanting', independently of 'liking'.
- **affective keyboard** — the front-to-back gradient of accumbens shell
  generators for desire and dread, retunable by context.

## Connections

The record holds Dayan and Berridge (LIT-584, NOTE-463), which defends
model-based Pavlovian 'wanting' and names conditioned alliesthesia. Ainslie's
précis (LIT-586, NOTE-443) reads Berridge and Robinson's wanted-not-liked
behaviour as its itch-like cycle. Hesp et al. (LIT-tmpvgq87) call the
wanting/liking dissociation the strongest challenge to their own account of
valence. Kahneman, Wakker and Sarin (LIT-tmpd3ww1) draw the parallel
distinction between decision and experienced utility in economics.

## Bearing on the record

**Wanting without liking cuts against *valence is dominance* as written.**
TERM-tmp8gbrt makes hedonic valence a regime of dominance, whose
intensity "is the strength of the dominance". Sensitized 'wanting' is
dominance by every mark Frijda gives control precedence (THEORY-055). The
addict's drug-ranking wins conflicts against every other concern. It
persists, and it resumes after abstinence when cues return. Yet, on the
review's evidence, its 'liking' does not grow with it and may fall.
CLAIM-tmpz8lli names this defeater in advance: "intensity that does not
track control precedence", judged by marks independent of the account.
Taste reactivity is such a mark. So the intensity clause fails for
positive valence, which is where the claim had already hedged it, and the
evidence arrives from a different direction than Carver 2003 (LIT-tmpb4r95).
Four ways to rescue the claim were tried, and none is clean:

- *The kind clause.* The term restricts valence to an ordering of the
  system's own states "by their bearing on its maintenance", and incentive
  salience attaches to cues and world representations. But a hungry rat's
  'wanting' of food is about maintenance, and dopamine manipulations raise
  it without raising 'liking'. Invoked here, the clause looks like a
  patch.
- *Sign, not intensity.* The review's own point is that dissociated
  'wanting' is "never neutral" and can be Tantalus's torment. On the
  account, Tantalus is strong dominance with the system behind its course,
  so intense negative valence, which is right. That reading fits frustrated
  wanting. It does not fit the addict who *gets* the drug and enjoys it
  less: dominance is strong, progress is ahead, and the account predicts
  intense positive valence where 'liking' shows little.
- *'Liking' is not valence.* One could say the account is about
  motivational valence and 'liking' is a different quantity, hedonic
  impact. The review itself treats the valence of motivational salience as
  distinct from hedonic impact. But then the term's word "hedonic" is the
  wrong word. Its object would be what the review calls the valence of
  motivation, and the claim would no longer be about pleasure.
- *Two regimes.* Within the record's own terms, dominance by an incentive
  ordering and dominance by a maintenance ordering could be different
  regimes, only the second hedonic. That needs a way to tell the two
  orderings apart that does not use 'liking', and the record has none.

The candid reading is this. Dominance, as defined, measures what Berridge
calls 'wanting' better than what he calls 'liking'. The tranche supports
separating the two in the record's vocabulary, not identifying either with
"hedonic valence" by fiat.

**It vindicates layer (2) and supplies a measure for it.** The record's
plan measures dominance from conflicts won, separately from a hedonic
marker that does not depend on choice. This review is the best argument
that the two must be measured separately, since they come apart under
known manipulations. Taste reactivity is a non-report, non-choice marker
that works across species, because "the latter can be measured
independently of the former". The marker is limited to taste and to
consummatory 'liking'. It has no counterpart for an organization, so the
record's organizational line (CLAIM-tmpz8lli's organizations) has no
layer-(2) measure from this work.

**It partly anticipates the felt-as-self-model candidate, and also
complicates it.** "Unconscious ‘liking’" separates layer (2) from felt
pleasure, as the candidate needs. The review's evolutionary story, in which
"hierarchical" circuitry translates core 'liking' into conscious feeling,
has the same shape as Brouillet and Friston's felt fluency as "a
hierarchically deeper representation" (THEORY-662). But the deeper level
it posits takes 'liking', not dominance, as its object. And the claim
that 'wanting' "may be appraised as a positive reward" even by the
experiencing person implies something awkward. A self-model of one's own
dominance could feel like pleasure while 'liking' is absent. If felt
valence is the system's model of its dominance, the addict's felt craving
could be mistaken for pleasure, and the review says this can happen. That
makes the candidate a theory of felt *motivation*, open to exactly that
mistake. It is not obviously a theory of felt pleasure.

**On common currency.** The review's "common currency" is an imaging
finding of overlapping activation. It is not Cabanac's behavioural
trade-off (LIT-tmpt9xwi), and it is not the record's ordering
(CLAIM-tmp85p0g). It should not be cited as support for either.

## Limitations

A review by the principal advocate of the view it reviews. Much of the
human evidence is case reports read against the reporters' own
interpretations, as with B-19. Taste reactivity is restricted to taste.
Its validity as a measure of hedonic impact rests on homology and on
convergence with other measures, and those arguments are not re-made
here. "Unconscious ‘liking’" in humans is cited, not shown.

## Open questions

- Is there a non-taste, non-report marker of 'liking' that could serve as
  layer (2) outside the consummatory case?
- Does 'liking' ever dominate, winning conflicts and persisting, without
  'wanting'? The review gives no case of liking-without-wanting, and
  opioid hotspot stimulation raises both.
