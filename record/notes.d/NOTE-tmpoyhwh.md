# NOTE-tmpoyhwh: Hankins, Hoekstra and Skiba — Large lottery wins postpone rather than prevent bankruptcy among Florida winners

## Contribution

A causal estimate of whether a large, unconditional lump sum keeps people
near the bankruptcy margin out of bankruptcy. Before it, the authors say,
the evidence on whether large cash transfers improve the longer-term
outcomes of struggling people "consists of informal surveys of lump-sum
settlement recipients" (p. 1). The outcome is bankruptcy, "arguably the
most extreme signal of financial distress" (p. 3). The finding is about
timing: strong protection in the first two years, undone by year five.

## Key insight

Money can change when a terminal event happens without changing whether
it happens over the horizon. "The results show that giving $50,000 to
$150,000 to people only postpones bankruptcy." (p. 2) A large win bought
time, and what the winners had when they later filed shows the money was
not kept.

## Assumptions

- **Identification (pp. 2–4).** Conditional on a first win of at least
  $600, the amount won is uncorrelated with the propensity to file. Prize
  size does not predict filing 2, 3–5 or 5 years before winning once year
  fixed effects and a control for a change in the game's structure are
  included (Table 3, col. 4: +0.0041, −0.0002, +0.0039 for large
  winners, none significant). Without those controls, large and medium
  winners filed less often before winning (Table 3, col. 1).
- **Sample (pp. 2–3, Table 1).** Every Fantasy 5 winner of more than
  $600, 29 April 1993 to 27 November 2002; names not unique in the 2008
  county phone book dropped; first win only; prizes under $150,000.
  34,987 winners, of whom 1,212 won $50,000–150,000. Prizes are pre-tax,
  with 25% withheld above $5,000.
- **Outcome.** All Chapter 7 and 13 personal bankruptcy filings in
  Florida, 1985 to November 2007, from PACER, matched on first name, last
  name and county. Matching errors are assumed not to vary with prize
  size (p. 3).
- **Estimand.** Treatment on the treated, among lottery players, who the
  authors argue overlap with the people a bailout targets (pp. 4–5).
- **Timing.** The year 3–5 rise is read as postponed filings, not new
  distress; Table 6 Panel C is offered in support.

## Key results

- **Base rates (pp. 2–3, Table 2).** 1,934 winners linked to a filing
  within five years, 5.53% of the sample; a one-year rate of "about 1
  percent", against 0.50% for Florida adults.
- **Postpone, not prevent (pp. 6–7, Table 4, col. 4).** "the bankruptcy
  rates of moderate and large winners fall 0.87 and 1.63 percentage
  points in the first two years", relative declines of 27 and 50 percent;
  "These declines are offset, however, by increases of 0.5 and 1.21
  percentage points three to five years after winning". The rise is
  significant only for large winners (0.0121, se 0.0062). Over five
  years: −0.0042 (se 0.0078) for large winners, who are "no more or less
  likely" to file than small winners.
- **Size relative to debt (p. 7).** This holds "despite the fact that the
  median large winner won a cash prize ($65,000) that was sufficient to
  pay off all of the unsecured debt owed by the most financially
  distressed lottery players ($52,000)".
- **Across prize sizes (p. 7; Table 2).** The authors say the pattern
  holds "across the full distribution" of prizes (the figures that show it
  were not read). In the raw rates of Table 2, two-year filing falls from
  about 3–3.7% below $5,000 to about 1.3–1.6% at $10,000–50,000 and
  mostly under 1% above $50,000; five-year rates stay at about 4–7.6% in
  every band.
- **Robustness (pp. 7–8, Table 5).** The year 3–5 estimate for large
  winners holds with a control group of winners under $2,500 (0.0171),
  with Florida Lotto winners added and winners under $1,000 as the control
  group (N = 164,268; 0.0105) and with controls for exposure to the 2005
  bankruptcy reform, BAPCPA (0.0122); dropping
  winners who played one number several times on a card (N = 29,271)
  gives 0.0073, not significant. In a probit, large winners are 1.3 points
  less likely to file within two years (p = 0.000) and 1.16 points more
  likely in years 3–5 (p = 0.117) (fn. 7).
- **Balance sheets at filing (pp. 9–10, Table 6; cases filed 2004 or
  later).** Within two years (Panel B; 14 large against 50 small filers),
  large-winner filers had about $20,000 less in net assets and $27,000 more
  in unsecured debt: "the large winners who did not postpone bankruptcy
  were those who had the most debt." In years 3–5 (Panel C; 33 against
  53), they had less total debt (−74,474) and higher net assets. Over five
  years (Panel D; 47 against 103), net assets were −10,033 against
  −21,210, not significantly different, and unsecured debt $57,361
  against $63,447: "whatever the recipients did with their cash, they did
  not use it to either pay down debt or increase their assets."
- **No homestead gaming (p. 10).** "little evidence that lottery winners
  took advantage of the unlimited homestead exemption", since "large
  winners do not pay down their mortgage or buy new real estate".
- **Attrition (pp. 10–11).** In the 2008 phone books, large winners were
  a non-significant 3.0 points (years 0–2) and 3.1 points (years 3–5) more
  likely to be listed: no sign of selective exit from the county.
- **Conclusion (pp. 11–12).** The authors call this "compelling evidence
  that receiving large cash transfers only enables individuals to
  postpone rather than prevent bankruptcy", and say the results "may
  warrant some skepticism about the long-term efficacy of such a
  bailout."

## Concepts

- **Postponement against prevention**: a transfer that changes when a
  terminal event happens but not whether it happens over the horizon.
- **The negative-shock model of bankruptcy**: that filing follows adverse
  shocks, so cash covering the shock should prevent it. The published
  abstract calls the results inconsistent with it; the draft does not use
  the phrase.
- **Candidate mechanisms (p. 1), listed and not tested**: strategic
  consumption in expectation of a filing, high discount rates, mental
  accounting, "house money" risk-taking, a taste for luxury goods that
  outlasts the money, and low financial literacy; and bounded rationality
  among lottery players (p. 10).

## Connections

The authors set the result beside Agarwal, Liu and Souleles (2007), in
which debt paid down with federal rebate checks returned to its earlier
level, and note that the same appears true of far larger transfers
(p. 10); and beside the move in tort law from lump sums to structured
settlements (p. 1).

In this batch it is the bankruptcy-side counterpart to Cesarini,
Lindqvist, Notowidigdo and Östling (2017) and Golosov, Graber, Mogstad
and Novgorodsky (2024), which follow lottery wealth into labour supply and
earnings, outcomes this paper does not measure. Its "high discount rates"
mechanism, untested here, is the behaviour that Shah, Mullainathan and
Shafir (2012) and THEORY-tmp19alc (Haushofer and Fehr) treat as an effect
of scarcity rather than a trait. Against THEORY-tmp7qas0 (Banerjee et
al.) it says nothing on work: its support is large, one-off and then
gone, where theirs is small and continuing.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpit7m9 (a savings buffer is a stock of time).** Supports the claim that a
  buffer buys time: a windfall larger than the debt is a deep buffer, and
  it bought about two years. Qualifies "covering needs through the delay
  buys the time": time was bought and the five-year outcome was not
  changed, because the buffer was not kept. What the winners did with the
  time is not observed.
- **CLAIM-595 (its section on eventual success against success by a
  deadline).** Supports it by analogy: survival to two years and survival
  to five come apart, and the same transfer passes the short deadline and
  fails the long one. CLAIM-595's own argument is about committors in an
  assembly and is untouched.
- **CLAIM-tmpc3x0c (the synthesis).** Supports the last clause, that
  "persistence after withdrawal is needed to tell which, though slow
  reversion can mimic it": the two-year protection reverts by year five.
  Qualifies the size condition: a lump sum of about 1.25 times the
  unsecured debt did not carry winners past anything that kept itself
  going, since it was not turned into assets or debt reduction.
- **CLAIM-tmpw4ai2 (thresholds).** Qualifies: if a threshold exists, a
  windfall of this size was not enough to cross it, or what was received
  was not stored as a stock. No prize size in the draft lowers the
  five-year rate; cells above $70,000 are tiny.
- **THEORY-tmp5v6r5 (Balboni et al.).** A contrast: there a one-off
  productive asset crossed a threshold and was followed by accumulation;
  here one-off liquid cash larger than the debt was not kept. That what is
  transferred, and not only its size, decides whether a threshold is
  crossed is the assistant's inference, not the authors'.
- **TERM-105.** Supports its use: the windfall raises a replenishable
  reserve once, and the result concerns the running balance, drawn back
  down, not the total received.
- **CLAIM-tmpqa0qu, CLAIM-tmpoh07b, CLAIM-597.** None: the supply is
  one-off, no capability or capacity is measured, and no upkeep is
  supplied.
- **THEORY-tmp19alc (Haushofer and Fehr).** Weakly bears: high discount
  rates are a candidate reason for spending the money, untested here.

## Limitations

- **The draft, not the article.** The text read is the 2009 working
  draft; the published coefficients and any added analysis are unverified.
- **Small cells.** 1,212 winners of $50,000–150,000, with 12 filings within
  two years and 64 within five (Table 2).
- **A marginal rise.** The year 3–5 effect for large winners is about
  1.95 standard errors and is not significant in the probit (p = 0.117)
  or when multiple plays per card are dropped.
- **An imprecise null.** The five-year estimate, −0.42 points with se
  0.78, gives a 95% interval of about −1.9 to +1.1 points on a five-year
  rate near 5.5%. It is no detectable effect, not a proven zero.
- **A small balance-sheet subsample.** 47 against 103 filers, only cases
  filed from 2004 on, for a prize band ($25,000–150,000) wider than the
  main one; unsecured debt is reported only where filings separate it
  (fn. 8). In Panel D the large winners' total debt is significantly lower
  (−38,171) and only net assets and unsecured debt are similar, so the authors' reading that the
  money did not go to paying down debt is stronger than the table.
- **One outcome.** Only bankruptcy: distress short of filing, and the
  roughly 95% who never file, are not observed, and the paper does not say
  where the money went.
- **External validity.** Florida lottery players, with an unlimited
  homestead exemption, 1993–2007; the authors grant it may not carry to
  "higher income educated people not near the bankruptcy margin" (p. 5).
- **Mechanisms are listed, not tested.**
- **Figures not read.** The claim that the pattern holds across the full
  prize distribution rests on Figures 2–4, which are images; only the raw
  rates of Table 2 were checked.

## Open questions

- Does the published version report the same two-year decline, year 3–5
  rise and five-year null?
- Where did the money go? Consumption, transfers to kin, gambling or
  further debt would distinguish the listed mechanisms; spending records
  or survey data on winners would tell.
- Would the same sum given as a productive asset, or in instalments, be
  kept? A design varying the form of a windfall, not only its size, would
  answer what the contrast with Balboni et al. suggests.
