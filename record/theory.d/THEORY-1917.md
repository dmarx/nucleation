# THEORY-1917: Keller argues that a person can wrongfully place another under a genuine moral obligation, by changing her circumstances so that every alternative to what he wants is morally unacceptable, and that fiduciary roles expose those who hold them to this moral blackmail because they predictably generate special obligations that exceed and outlast the fiduciary duty

## Source

Keller (2016), [LIT-2670](../literature.d/LIT-2670.md), read in the author's pre-print; the reading is [NOTE-2005](../notes.d/NOTE-2005.md).

## What was actually shown

An argument by cases, not evidence. Moral blackmail is "a wrongful act that makes a person do something by making her alternatives morally unacceptable" (p. 20). It is possible because "if I have the power to change your circumstances, then I may have the power to change the moral demands you face" (p. 20), so "you can have a genuine moral obligation because you have been treated wrongly" (p. 21). It works "by way of the victim’s moral sensibility" (p. 22) and can succeed when the manipulation is common knowledge (p. 23). Fiduciary relationships tend "naturally and predictably" to become special moral relationships whose obligations exceed and outlast the fiduciary duty (p. 17), which exposes doctors, nurses, teachers and lawyers to moral blackmail by beneficiaries and by third parties such as employers (pp. 22-25).

Corroborates [CLAIM-703](../claims.d/CLAIM-703.md) (genuine obligation, wrongfully created, binding though seen) and, in part, [CLAIM-713](../claims.d/CLAIM-713.md) (an employer capturing unpaid work through employees' obligations to clients, p. 24). Bears on [QUESTION-472](../questions.d/QUESTION-472.md) (a right to strike made morally costly by timing, p. 24).

## What this does not say

- That a created obligation is no larger than the circumstances support: that limit in [CLAIM-703](../claims.d/CLAIM-703.md) is the exchange's.
- That the mechanism requires sound moral reasoning: success turns on what the victim believes (p. 21).
- What makes the blackmailer's act wrongful, or how often moral blackmail occurs.
- That institutions come to depend on it: Keller's agents are individual strategists.

## From the second reading (2026-11-17)

The second reading adds a relation: it corroborates the relational half
of [CLAIM-708](../claims.d/CLAIM-708.md), which does not rest on it. Moral blackmail mistreats its
victim, "I mistreat you by placing you in a difficult moral predicament"
(p. 20), though Keller's victim is pressed to do something right, not to
commit a wrong. It adds the consequence Keller draws (pp. 26–27: the
exposed can reasonably expect compensation, and the exposure falls on
caring roles, often poorly paid and more often held by women) and two
limits:

- **That the targeted party commits a wrong.** In every case the victim is
  pressed to do what is right. The teachers' strike is their "right", and
  the publicity the official expects is a smear. So it carries nothing for
  [CLAIM-726](../claims.d/CLAIM-726.md)'s true accusation of a real wrong ([ARG-115](../arguments.d/ARG-115.md)).
- **Unintended reliance.** All of Keller's cases are deliberate, where
  [CLAIM-713](../claims.d/CLAIM-713.md) allows reliance "without intending to exploit anyone".
