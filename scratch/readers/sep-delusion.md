### Summary
A survey organised around the **continuity thesis**: how far clinical delusions differ from ordinary (irrational) beliefs, and what if anything makes them pathological. §1 sets out the philosophical stakes (mind, psychiatry, ethics) and six non-exclusive answers to what makes delusions pathological (they masquerade as beliefs; signal a non-shared reality; defy folk psychology; harm wellbeing; have forensic import; have dysfunctional etiology), each with its difficulty. §2 criticises the DSM definition (Coltheart's five questions), notes the multidimensional and harm-based definitions and delusion attribution as "epistemic gatekeeping", and lays out the types. §3 surveys formation: psychodynamic/motivational, neuropsychological and hybrid accounts; one-factor (Maher), two-factor (Davies, Coltheart, McKay) and prediction-error (Fletcher & Frith, Corlett) theories; bottom-up versus top-down; explanationism versus endorsement. §4 asks whether delusions are irrational, whether they are beliefs, and whether they overlap with self-deception and conspiracy belief, answering each in favour of degree-not-kind continuity while saying the clinical distinction is still useful.

### Contribution
Not new research: an expert synthesis whose own voice (Bortolotti) defends doxasticism and continuity, and whose 2026 revision adds the social, affective and experiential turn (Feyaerts et al. 2021; Bell, Raihani & Wilkinson 2021; Miyazono & Salice 2021; Ritunnano et al. 2026; Williams, Miyazono & Wilkinson 2026, *The Social Roots of Delusions*).

### Key results (section, short quote)
1. Defining mark is resistance, not falsity (preamble): "they resist counterevidence and are not easily given up in the face of challenges"; delusions "may happen to be true".
2. Conviction fluctuates (preamble): "reported sincerely and with conviction, although ... the conviction in the delusional belief can fluctuate."
3. Some delusions benefit (preamble, §1.2): "some delusions can be meaningful and empowering, at least temporarily"; "adaptive in some sense, psychologically, biologically, and even epistemically" (citing McKay & Dennett 2009, Fineberg & Corlett 2016, Bortolotti 2016, Ritunnano, Humpston & Broome 2022). Insight paradox: "living with the delusion may be preferable to living without the delusion".
4. Attribution as gatekeeping (§2.1): "'delusion' is a value-laden term that is used to express a negative judgement about the speaker's contribution."
5. Cultural exemption (§2.1, DSM): "The belief is not one ordinarily accepted by other members of the person's culture or subculture"; Coltheart: "Couldn't a belief held by all members of one's community still be delusional?"
6. One-factor (§3.2): for Maher "the delusion is a reasonable hypothesis given the strangeness of the experience"; Sullivan-Bissett 2018, 2020: "no need to postulate any reasoning deficit".
7. Two-factor (§3.2): the experience "is not sufficient"; factor 2 is a hypothesis-evaluation deficit (Coltheart 2007) or "the tendency to 'jump to conclusion'" (Garety & Freeman 1999); in Coltheart et al. 2010 the problem is "failing to give up the delusional belief"; in McKay 2012 a "bias towards explanatory adequacy", adopting a hypothesis that "'fits' the data" despite a low prior.
8. Prediction error (§3.2): a signal is disrupted "when events invested of special significance (in psychosis this may be due to dopamine dysregulation) cause us to update our current beliefs"; delusion "principally an 'error of learning'".
9. **Aberrant salience**, without the phrase (§4.1, quoting McKay 2012, p. 18): "If there is an excess of prediction error signal, inappropriately heightened salience is attached to the data, and belief revision is excessively accommodatory". Kapur 2003 ("Psychosis as a state of aberrant salience") is in the bibliography but cited nowhere in the text.
10. Bottom-up/top-down (§3.3): Campbell 2001, "delusion is a matter of top-down disturbance in some fundamental beliefs"; Hohwy holds that the distinction dissolves under prediction error. Explanationism (content of experience vaguer than delusion) versus endorsement ("a way of taking the experience at face value").
11. Senses of irrationality (§4.1): evidential ("The 'incorrigibility' of delusions speaks in favor of their being held irrationally"); inconsistency with other beliefs; mismatch with action ("cooperative and even flirtatious with the alleged impostor"); reasoning biases, including "an accentuated need for closure which comprises a desire for clarity and structure" (Kruglanski 1989). Countered by Flores 2021: capacity to respond to evidence retained, "motivational factors" resist.
12. Maintenance (§4.1, Coltheart et al. 2010): "the information undermining the delusional hypothesis does not present itself as disconfirming evidence"; "an obstinate scientist". Davies & Egan 2013: a "prepotent doxastic response", "likely to be compartmentalised".
13. Verdict on irrationality (§4.1): "irrational to a higher degree than normal beliefs ... they do not seem to be irrational in a qualitatively different way".
14. Doxasticism (§4.2): rivals are imagination (Currie & Ravenscroft 2002), "empty speech acts" (Berrios 1991), attitudes to representations, acceptances (Frankish), in-between states (Schwitzgebel 2012), Gallagher's "delusional realities". The three negative arguments (integration, evidence-responsiveness, action guidance) "rely on an idealization of belief states, and impose constraints on delusions that not even typical beliefs would meet."
15. Self-deception (§4.3): three overlap views: Mele-style "motivationally biased treatment of evidence" (fits jealousy and persecution, not misidentification); delusion as extreme self-deception with "a protective and adaptive function" (Hirstein; Ramachandran's discrepancy detector); delusion as proof of doxastic conflict (Levy 2009 on anosognosia).
16. Conspiracy belief (§4.3): a two-factor template carries over: "a first factor which is unusual experience in the case of delusion and epistemic mistrust in the case of beliefs in conspiracy theories", and a common second factor; conspiracy beliefs, unlike delusions, can be "cementing some relationships in well-defined social groups".

### Assumptions
Clinical delusions as the object; reasoning biases are the statistical norm, so idealised (Bayesian) norms are a limited test; the folk-psychological notion of belief is the measure of doxasticism; the reported cases (Capgras, Cotard, anosognosia) stand for the categories.

### Concepts
Monothematic/polythematic; circumscribed/elaborated; primary/secondary (Jaspers, "arise out of nowhere"); authored/un-authored (Bortolotti & Broome 2008); one-factor/two-factor; prediction error; explanationism/endorsement; bottom-up/top-down; continuity thesis; insight paradox; epistemic gatekeeping; epistemic injustice.

### What is absent
No treatment of **shared or induced delusion** (folie à deux; zero occurrences); none of the **delusional mood, Trema, apophany or Conrad**; nothing on the **phenomenology of felt certainty**. "Conviction" is handled only as a report-and-action feature. "Insight" occurs only in the clinical sense (insight into one's illness). None on chatbots, AI or mathematics. "Epistemic innocence" is not named in the text; it appears only in the title of Sullivan-Bissett 2018, "Monothematic Delusion: A Case of Innocence from Experience". Epistemic benefits are asserted with citations (Bortolotti 2016, "Epistemic Benefits of Elaborated and Systematized Delusions in Schizophrenia"), not set out.

### Why it is being read: the inside position
- **Q2 analogue (motivated belief).** Yes, the entry has accounts on which the comfort of a belief selects it: psychodynamic persecution "developed in order to protect us from low self-esteem" (§3.1); Reverse Othello "a defense against the suffering" (§3.1); Mele-style motivationally biased treatment of evidence (§4.3); Ramachandran's "normal defense mechanisms that have an adaptive function" (§4.3). The entry splits delusions by whether motivation plays a role: misidentification delusions have "no fundamental role for motivational biases"; "A different analysis might be appropriate for delusions of jealousy or persecution" (§4.3).
- **Unmotivated cases.** These are described as a miscalibrated signal: reduced autonomic response, "excess of prediction error signal", "inappropriately heightened salience". No optimizer and no learning against the evaluator.
- **Which claim it favours.** It favours none in terms. Its case split has the **form of CLAIM-tmpu6c7h** (gaming where something is sought, miscalibration where not), and its unmotivated cases **support CLAIM-tmpu6fff's description** of those cases. But what is sought in the motivated cases is the comfort of a content, not the felt quality of insight, so the entry **qualifies** CLAIM-tmpu6c7h: the claim's criterion would have to widen from seeking the feeling to seeking the comfort of a belief, or count these as a neighbouring case. Against CLAIM-tmpcv6i6's "always", the misidentification cases are a counterexample in the entry's own terms, on neuropsychological accounts.
- **Closest the entry comes to a sought feeling.** The "need for closure which comprises a desire for clarity and structure" (§4.1), a desire aimed at the state of closure, not at a content. One citation (Kruglanski 1989), no development. This is the passage CLAIM-tmpu6c7h could use.

### Limitations, including gaps in the entry's argument
- A survey: it reports rather than argues, except in §4.1's close and §4.2's anti-idealisation argument.
- "Degree, not kind" is asserted from the existence of nonclinical irrationality, with no measure of degree offered. The entry itself notes a criterion-loosening worry ("the very notion of belief is not theoretically useful if the criteria ... become too loose") and leaves it unanswered.
- Epistemic benefits and the social turn are cited, not expounded.
- The phenomenological tradition (Jaspers, Conrad, delusional mood) is almost absent, as is folie à deux, so the record's apophany and shared-delusion questions get nothing first-hand here.
- §6 options of §1.2 lack a verdict; the etiology option is "to be better explored".
- The table of contents is misnumbered against the body.

### Candidate THEORY
> Bortolotti's SEP entry on delusion (2009, rev. 2026) holds that clinical delusions are irrational to a higher degree than ordinary beliefs, being less consistent with other beliefs and behaviour and more resistant to counterevidence, but not in a qualitatively different way; and that arguments denying them belief status rely on an idealized notion of belief, imposing on delusions constraints that typical beliefs fail too.

`promote_when`: A second reading of the entry, or of Bortolotti 2009 (*Delusions and Other Irrational Beliefs*), confirms that she states, in her own voice and not as a report of others, both that delusions differ from irrational nondelusional beliefs in degree and not kind, and that the anti-doxastic arguments impose on delusions constraints typical beliefs do not meet.
