# THEORY-tmpdnmds: Every well-developed theory of how mind fits into the cosmos is bizarre, contrary to what most people confidently believe, and none is epistemically compelling, because common sense about the metaphysics of mind is incoherent; so no coarse position, materialism included, deserves credence much above 50%

## Source

Eric Schwitzgebel (2024), *The Weirdness of the World*, [LIT-216](../literature.d/LIT-216.md), Princeton
University Press; read in full in [NOTE-188](../notes.d/NOTE-188.md) from the author's final manuscript
of 22 June 2022. Page numbers are the manuscript's. The published book
reorders the chapters, so its chapter 2 is the manuscript's chapter 3.

## What was actually shown

**Definitions (ch. 3 §2).** A position is *bizarre* if "most people without
specialized training on the issue confidently, but perhaps implicitly, believe
it to be false" (p. 65). It is *wild* if it is bizarre and "we are not
epistemically compelled to believe it". A topic is a *wilderness* if something
wild must be among its core truths (p. 66).

**Universal Bizarreness.** Every well-developed metaphysics in history defies
common sense, and the best explanation is that common sense about metaphysics is
incoherent (pp. 61–64). It accepts that one of materialism, dualism, idealism or
a compromise/rejection view must be true, and rejects each once its implications
are drawn out (p. 86). The cases:

- *Materialism* faces a trilemma over pain (p. 76). Neural chauvinism means no
  Martian pain. Simple functionalism means beer-can pain and no mad pain.
  Normality-based hybrids lose locality: "anaesthesia by genocide", or
  faster-than-light self-knowledge (pp. 74–76).
- *Dualism* faces a dilemma over causal powers and a quadrilemma over the scope
  of mentality, each horn bizarre (pp. 78–81). Reid and Descartes are examined
  as counterexamples and fail (pp. 81–83).
- *Idealism* is bizarre on its face, and *compromise/rejection* views (Kant,
  Russell, Chalmers, "no fact of the matter") are bizarre too.
- The other chapters supply cases: if materialism is true the United States is
  probably conscious (ch. 2), transcendental idealism about space is a live
  possibility (ch. 5), and under stated cosmological premises almost every act
  causes almost every future event (ch. 7).

**Universal Dubiety (§§8–10).** Three arguments: experts disagree, there is no
good method ("there is no conscious-ometer", p. 92), and cosmology is itself
uncertain. So no coarse position "deserves credence much over 50%", and no
moderately specific variant comes close to 50% (p. 86).

**What could have come out otherwise.** Someone could produce a developed
metaphysics of mind that common sense accepts. The book issues that challenge
("find me one", p. 63) and reports none.

## What this does not say

- **That every possible theory is bizarre.** The claim is an induction over
  theories actually developed. The most quoted sentence, "every possible theory
  of the relation of mind and cosmos defies common sense", outruns it ([NOTE-188](../notes.d/NOTE-188.md)).
- **That common sense is incoherent, as a finding.** That is offered as the best
  explanation of the bizarreness, with no independent test (C2, weak to
  moderate).
- **That the numbers are measurements.** The 50%, the 55% for materialism and
  the 0.1–1% for radical skepticism are the author's judgments, used "for
  convenience only" (p. 116).
- **That bizarreness counts for a theory.** It is denied the power to rule a
  theory out, not given the power to rule one in.
- **That any particular bizarre option is true.** The book spreads credence
  across them. Its conclusions about the United States, the garden snail and
  transcendental idealism are conditional or are how-possibly claims.
- **That the published text says the same.** The book read is the manuscript.
  The published version reorders chapters and updates the AI chapter.
