# NOTE-tmppfiub: Camerer et al. — Replicability of Nature and Science experiments, read for Shah et al.

## Contribution

A pre-registered replication of 21 experimental social-science studies
from Nature and Science, 2010–2015, at about five times the original
sample sizes, with prediction markets on which would replicate. "We find
a significant effect in the same direction as the original study for 13
(62%) studies, and the effect size of the replications is on average
about 50% of the original effect size" (abstract).

## Key insight

For a single paper the project's verdict is narrow by design. It
replicates "the first study reporting a significant treatment effect"
(p. 3), so for Shah et al. it tested the study the original authors call
least central, and it says of itself that it "does not provide
definitive insight on any of the original papers" (pp. 13–14).

## Assumptions

- **Selection by rule** (p. 3): the first significant study, then its
  most important result as judged by the project, not the original
  authors.
- **Two-stage power** (p. 4): stage 1 has "90% power to detect 75% of the
  original effect size"; if it fails, stage 2 brings the pooled sample to
  90% power for 50%.
- **Replicated** means a significant effect, two-sided p < 0.05, in the
  original direction.
- **Materials.** For Shah et al. "We use the same website code as used in
  the original article" (replication report, p. 2); the show-up fee rose
  from $0.35 to $2.00, and the original data were collected on AMT in
  2011.

## Key results

- **The hypothesis tested** (replication report, p. 1): "Low-wealth
  subjects, that are given fewer chances to win in repeated 'Wheel of
  Fortune' type word puzzle games, perform worse in a subsequent attention
  task (Dots-Mixed task) than do high-wealth individuals". Original
  "F (1, 54) = 4.16 and p = 0.046", n = 56, r = 0.267.
- **The original authors' objection was noted** (replication report,
  p. 1): "the original authors argued that Experiment 1 is less central to
  the paper than Experiments 2–5 which test the prediction that scarcity
  will lead to over-borrowing".
- **Stage 1** (n = 278): 46.94 poor against 44.51 rich, "F (1, 276) =
  2.0863 and p = 0.1498"; "the insignificant point estimate was in the
  opposite direction of the original study" (replication report).
- **Pooled** (n = 619): 45.07 against 44.63, "F (1, 617) = 0.1389 and p
  = 0.7095" (replication report). Supplement Tables S3–S4: replication r
  = −0.087 (stage 1) and −0.015 (pooled); relative effect size −0.326 and
  −0.056; not replicated. Table S5: the stage-1 market's belief of
  replication 0.38.
- **The project.** For the 8 studies that failed, "the mean relative
  effect size is 0.3%" (pp. 5–6); Shah et al.'s Study 1 is one of them.
- **The rest of the paper, at second hand** (p. 15): "Inspired by our
  replication, the original authors of Shah et al." replicated "all of
  their five studies", and "did replicate what they consider to be their
  most important finding, that scarcity itself leads to over-borrowing.
  They also failed to replicate study 1 in their paper consistent with our
  findings."
- **The original authors' comment.** Study 1 "had been conducted earlier
  and added during the revision process" and "tests a hypothesis about
  depletion, not the central hypothesis in our paper". "Having now twice
  failed to replicate Study 1, we are convinced that the original finding
  was a false-positive." In retrospect, "it would have been wiser to
  present our opening data in the form of intriguing observations, rather
  than a full-fledged Study 1."

## Concepts

- **Relative effect size**: the replication's r over the original's;
  near zero or negative for Shah et al.'s Study 1.
- **Dots-Mixed task**: congruent and incongruent key presses to symbols,
  done "Under strict time pressure" (replication report, p. 1), used as
  the measure of fatigue. The clock is in the measure, not in the
  scarcity manipulation, which is a count of guesses.
- The word "tunneling" does not appear in any of the four texts.

## Connections

The comment and the project both point to the authors' replications at
osf.io/vzm23, filed as LIT-tmpb0bn3. The project compares itself with the
Reproducibility Project: Psychology, whose average relative effect sizes
it calls "very similar" (p. 13).

## Bearing on the record

- **Shah et al. (2012), LIT-tmp6ahq0.** The one independent test of the
  paper, of its least central study; that study fails. The paper's
  borrowing and focus results, the parts this record cares about, have no
  independent replication; they stand on the authors' own
  (THEORY-tmplj3t9).
- **The owner's reading, deprivation as added time pressure.** The failed
  study manipulated guesses, not time, and tested fatigue afterwards. It
  removes one route, scarcity wearing down cognition for later tasks, and
  leaves the present-focus and borrowing route as the authors' own
  replications left it.
- **THEORY-tmp19alc (Haushofer & Fehr).** Its mechanism passage reports
  the cognitive-control result of Mani et al. (2013), which this project
  did not test; but any use of Shah et al.'s Study 1 as evidence that
  scarcity taxes later cognition should now be qualified.
- **THEORY-572 and LIT-161 (Feest).** A direct replication at high power
  says the Study 1 effect is not there in this design; the original
  authors' own account of why (the rich did more work in the game) is the
  kind of systematic-error question Feest says replication cannot settle.
- **CLAIM-tmpit7m9 and TERM-105.** None directly.
- Filed: THEORY-tmp6qwmt. No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **One study per paper.** "for papers reporting a series of studies we
  only replicate one of those studies" (p. 13).
- **The preprint, not the typeset article.** Page numbers and wording may
  differ.
- **Read selectively.** Only what bears on Shah et al. and the
  project-level numbers needed to weigh it.
- **One pool.** AMT, as the original study used.
