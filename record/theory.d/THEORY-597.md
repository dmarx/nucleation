# THEORY-597: Meditation-related experiences that Western Buddhist practitioners report as challenging span 59 kinds in seven domains, from fear and sleep change to altered self-other boundaries, and they are reported in daily practice, at under an hour a day and in the first days of practice, not only on intensive retreat; the frequencies come from a sample chosen for difficulty, and the causal link is the practitioners' own attribution

## Source

Lindahl, Fisher, Cooper, Rosen & Britton (2017), *PLOS ONE* 12(5),
e0176239, [LIT-066](../literature.d/LIT-066.md), the Varieties of Contemplative Experience study, read
in full in [NOTE-036](../notes.d/NOTE-036.md). The supporting files S1–S5 (interview protocols,
codebooks) were not read, so category definitions are as the article
gives them.

## What was actually shown

**The sample (Table 1).** Recruitment was purposive "deviant case"
sampling: a challenging, difficult or impairing meditation-related
experience was the condition of entry (p. 7). Of 73 interviewed, 13 were
excluded, leaving 60 practitioners from Theravāda, Zen and Tibetan
traditions, plus 32 teachers and clinicians, 11 of whom were also
practitioners. The sample is 94% White and 85% US, and 60% were
meditation teachers.

**The taxonomy (Table 4).** Thematic coding gave 59 phenomenology
categories in seven domains: cognitive, perceptual, affective, somatic,
conative, sense of self and social. A practitioner reported a mean of 19.6
categories (SD 7.2) across 6.1 domains. The commonest were
fear/anxiety/panic/paranoia (82%), changes in motivation or goal (78%),
positive affect (75%), somatic energy (63%), sleep changes (62%),
depression/dysphoria/grief (57%) and changes in self–other boundaries
(53%). Experts reported 56 of the 59. A double-coded 15% subset gave
weighted κ = .70.

**Where onset occurred (Table 3).** 72% had onset on or right after a
retreat, and 28% in daily practice. 25% were practising under an hour a
day at onset. 12% reported challenges within their first ten days of
practice, and 18% within their first 50 hours.

**Impact (p. 21).** Median duration was 1–3 years. 73% reported
moderate-to-severe impairment in at least one domain, 17% suicidality and
17% inpatient hospitalisation.

**What could have come out otherwise.** Every onset could have fallen on
long retreat, or after years of practice. It did not: a minority of cases
in each of the three conditions (daily practice, low dose, first days)
is enough to falsify "only on retreat" and "only after long practice".
The taxonomy could also have failed to saturate, or the experts' reports
could have diverged from the practitioners'. Saturation was judged at 30
and 60 interviews, and experts reported nearly every category.

## What this does not say

- **Not a prevalence or incidence.** The 82%, 73% and 17% are proportions
  within people recruited for having had difficulty. The authors call the
  100% rate of challenge "an artifact of sampling" (p. 27). Nothing here
  says how often an unselected meditator, or a participant in a
  mindfulness-based intervention, has any of these experiences, and the
  Limitations say the results "cannot be generalized" to such
  interventions.
- **Not that meditation caused them.** Each practitioner scored six
  self-reported causality criteria, and a score of two or more was an
  inclusion condition, so "100% met causality criteria" is guaranteed by
  the design ([NOTE-036](../notes.d/NOTE-036.md), Corrections). There is no control group and no
  base rate. The cut-off is also stated inconsistently (">2" on p. 13,
  "two or greater" on p. 10).
- **Not a list of risk factors.** The 26 influencing-factor categories
  (teacher relationship, worldview, practice intensity) are participants'
  views. The authors disclaim them as "a theory or hypothesis about risk
  factors" of their own (p. 22).
- **Not that the experiences are adverse.** The taxonomy is valence-neutral
  by design, and the paper reports that phenomenologically similar
  experiences were appraised from very positive to very negative. That
  appraisal claim is qualitative; no valence data are given.
- **Not that the predisposed are unaffected.** The exclusion criterion was
  a history of unusual experiences closely resembling the reported ones,
  not psychiatric history. 32% of the retained sample had a psychiatric
  history and 43% a trauma history (Table 3). The rebuttal is of "only
  people with pre-existing conditions", not of any difference in risk.
