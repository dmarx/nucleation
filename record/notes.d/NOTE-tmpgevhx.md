# NOTE-tmpgevhx: Impulsive action: emotional impulses and their control

## Contribution

The paper gives a theory of impulsive action and builds it from Frijda's
theory of emotion. An impulsive action is "a non-deliberate action that serves
the purpose of rendering one's relation to the object, event, or state of the
world more pleasant or less unpleasant". The purpose comes from appraisal,
which elicits action readiness. The paper then argues that impulse control and
emotion regulation are largely the outcome of several states of action
readiness interacting, not of a reflective system overriding an impulsive one.

## Key insight

A motive does not need a goal representation. An appraised object in front of
the agent, together with a state of readiness to change one's relation to it,
is enough to select and steer an action. The same mechanism regulates: seeing
what one's impulse would do sets off another readiness, which checks the
first. "Reflective and deliberate processes seek to help solving impulsive
dilemmas that impulsive processes alone are unable to solve." They do not
govern the impulses.

## Assumptions

- **Emotion is action readiness.** "Action readiness and changes in action
  readiness form the key to what we call 'emotions'" (Frijda 1986, 2007), so
  "all impulsive actions qualify as emotional actions", though not all
  emotional actions are impulsive ("Impulsive actions").
- **Appraisal elicits action readiness**, "in a fraction of a second", and
  largely "automatically and non-consciously". The central appraisal is
  concern pertinence: whether an event promotes or obstructs the person's
  goals, needs, values or concerns ("Action readiness elicitation").
- **Engagement** is a further necessary condition: the capacity for interest,
  identified with dopaminergic and related neuromodulatory systems
  ("Engagement").
- **States of action readiness** are "subthreshold preparatory
  potentiations" of relational actions and coping skills, and can be felt
  without overt action ("States of action readiness").

## Key results

No data. The results are the theory's propositions, summarized in the
conclusion as five points:

- **Elicitation.** Impulsive actions are instigated by non-reflective
  appraisal of an event's relevance to concerns, which elicits a motive to
  establish or modify a self–object relationship.
- **Selection.** Action is selected neither by deliberate goals nor by
  habit, "but from non-reflective action priming by affordances met and
  recognized along the way". Priming elicits a "pragmatic anticipation" of
  the action's effects, including its exteroceptive and interoceptive
  sensory consequences, which makes the action purposive.
- **No exclusive regulation processes.** "There is no clear distinction
  between processes of emotion and processes of emotion regulation. In
  large measure, emotion regulation also proceeds impulsively".
- **Against dual-process models.** Regulation as the interaction of multiple
  states of readiness is "an alternative" to a shift from an "impulsive" to a
  "reflective" system (Strack & Deutsch 2004). Not "the rider controlling the
  horse": "our perspective accords with Hume (1739), in viewing reason as the
  slave of the passions."
- **Control by recruiting concerns.** Impulse control "can be expected to be
  served by enhancing relevant multiple emotions by focusing on non-obvious
  but relevant concerns, such as moral concerns and self-esteem."

Supporting points in the body:

- **Modes of action readiness and emotion names.** Hostility corresponds to
  anger, avoidance to fear, submission to awe, and disinclination to act to
  apathy (Frijda & Parrott 2011) ("States of action readiness").
- **Control precedence**: persistence until an end state, resumption
  after interruption, selective attention, and priority over other
  actions. Rated control precedence correlates with rated emotion intensity
  (Sonnemans & Frijda 1994) ("Impulsive actions").
- **Multiple emotions are common.** 31% of diary-reported emotions were
  multiple (Oatley & Duncan 1992). Observers rate expressions for action
  tendencies about as consistently as for emotion labels (Tcherkassof et al.
  2007) ("Perceived events may induce multiple appraisals").
- **Regulation is grounded in concerns.** "Emotions are moderated or
  controlled to the extent that one emotionally cares about the affective
  consequences of their being uncontrolled" ("Emotion regulation and impulse
  control").
- **Neural sketch.** Pragmatic anticipation is formed in posterior parietal
  cortex and mapped to premotor and motor regions, with the cerebellum doing
  state estimation. Interruption and override engage inferior frontal cortex,
  pre-SMA and the basal ganglia ("Neural bases of impulsive action").
- **Loss of action readiness.** Where an event is unbearable but no aim
  presents itself, the result is the "background of urges toward suicide", of
  "true anxiety", dissociation or apathy ("Engagement").

## Concepts

- **action readiness**: a state of being set for a kind of relational
  action; "not representations of desirable states, but actual preparations
  for action". The term is borrowed from Dewey (1895).
- **control precedence**: the urgency of an impulse and its claim on attention
  and action, including persistence, resumption and priority.
- **concern**: what an individual cares about, from survival to values.
  Concern pertinence is "the condition for an event to operate as an
  emotional event".
- **appraisal**: "the information that confers personal meaning to brute
  perceived events". It is drawn from the stimulus, its context, interaction
  with it, and memory, and covers what the event may do to one and what
  actions it affords.
- **impulsive action**: non-deliberate action aimed at making one's relation
  to an object more pleasant or less unpleasant.
- **pragmatic anticipation**: the anticipated sensory effects of an action,
  used to retrieve and tune it.

## Connections

- **Frijda, The Emotions ([LIT-tmpkd06c](../literature.d/LIT-tmpkd06c.md)), Deferred.** This paper extends the
  book. Action readiness and control precedence are both cited to it (with
  The Laws of Emotion, 2007), and the paper's "earlier work" is the book's
  theory. Equivalence of actions within one mode of readiness is also cited
  to it ("When angry one can kick, shout insults, turn one's back").
- **Gross ([LIT-tmptr6ik](../literature.d/LIT-tmptr6ik.md)), read.** Not cited. Gross (1998, p. 286) records that
  "some theorists argue that emotion regulation is best seen as part and
  parcel of emotion (Frijda, 1986)" and keeps the distinction. This paper is
  that argument at full length. The two can be reconciled in part: Gross's
  cognitive change (choosing among meanings) is close to this paper's
  appraisal of consequences raising a competing readiness. The difference is
  whether the regulator is a distinct process.
- **Barrett ([LIT-tmp7xlrj](../literature.d/LIT-tmp7xlrj.md)), read.** Not cited. The two share a predictive
  framing, citing Friston (2012) and Clark (2013) for motives as expectations.
  They also share action-first sensory anticipation, which runs through
  interoceptive as well as exteroceptive consequences. They differ on
  appraisal. Here appraisal "is what generates the person's state of action
  readiness". For Barrett, meaning "results from" action, and appraisals are
  descriptions produced by categorization.
- **Damasio ([LIT-384](../literature.d/LIT-384.md), [LIT-388](../literature.d/LIT-388.md)).** Not cited. The paper's "affective appraisals of
  action options" are close to the somatic-marker idea that emotion prunes the option space. This
  paper puts that role on action readiness, not on re-enacted body states.

## Limitations

- **Theory without new tests.** Every empirical point is cited. The
  central regulatory claim (C5) is supported by everyday examples.
- **Scope is impulsive action.** Emotional experience, the laws of emotion,
  expression and physiology are treated only in passing. As a substitute for
  The Emotions, this paper covers the book's core constructs, not its range.
- **The mapping from action readiness to emotion names is sketched by
  example** (hostility to anger and so on) and not defended against the
  constructionist objection that instances of one emotion vary in action.

## Open questions

- Can the interaction account predict *which* state of readiness wins? The
  paper says only that it depends "upon their relative strengths".
- Can multiple-readiness regulation be distinguished experimentally from
  deliberate reappraisal? The paper predicts it can, since it is effortless
  and impulsive, but proposes no test.
