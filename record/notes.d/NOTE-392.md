# NOTE-392: North — Institutions

## Contribution

A short programmatic statement, for a general economics readership, of
the view North's 1990 book develops (the acknowledgement says the essay
is "based in part on" that forthcoming book). It defines institutions,
ties them to transaction costs, and uses three static forms of exchange
and one evolving one to ask why some institutional frameworks produce
growth and others do not. Its answer is the interplay of organisations
with the framework and path dependence.

## Key insight

Institutions are the constraints, not the players. Organisations (firms,
guilds, states) arise to exploit the opportunities a framework offers,
and their investment in knowledge alters the framework only when the
framework rewards productive rather than redistributive activity. That
is why frameworks can lock in, and why the same constitution exported to
Latin America did not reproduce the United States.

## Assumptions

- **Agents** are wealth-maximising, with asymmetric information about the
  attributes of what is exchanged.
- **Cooperation** is easy with repeated play, full information and few
  players, and hard otherwise. This is stated as the game-theoretic
  setting (p. 97), not modelled.
- **The staged story** (village to region to long-distance trade to
  modern economy) is a heuristic from the German historical school. North
  says explicitly that it need not match real historical sequence
  (p. 99, n. 1).
- **Path dependence** is taken from Arthur and David on technology and
  applied to institutions by analogy (n. 10).

## Key results

- **Definition (p. 97).** "Institutions are the humanly devised
  constraints that structure political, economic and social interaction.
  They consist of both informal constraints (sanctions, taboos, customs,
  traditions, and codes of conduct), and formal rules (constitutions,
  laws, property rights)."
- **Institutions and costs (p. 98).** Institutions and enforcement, with
  technology, determine the cost of transacting. "Effective institutions
  raise the benefits of cooperative solutions or the costs of defection."
- **Static exchange (pp. 102–104).** Tribal exchange rests on a dense
  social network in which deviance threatens survival (Colson; Posner).
  The Suq has high measurement costs, clientization and bargaining at
  every margin, and no legal underpinning that would make insuring
  organisations profitable. The caravan trade ran on personal protection
  (Geertz's *zettata*), with no political structure to extend property
  rights.
- **European evolution (pp. 105–108).** Innovations at three margins:
  - capital mobility: usury evasion, negotiable and discountable bills of
    exchange, accounting to monitor agents;
  - information costs: printed prices and merchant manuals;
  - risk: marine insurance and the commenda leading to the joint-stock
    company.

  Causation ran both ways between trade volume and enforcement. Merchant
  law began in guild codes enforced by ostracism and was gradually
  absorbed by the state, which had to be bound against confiscation (the
  Netherlands; England after 1688).
- **The transaction sector (p. 101, n. 7).** 25 percent of US GNP in 1870
  and 45 percent in 1970, cited from Wallis & North (1986).
- **Path dependence (pp. 109–111).** The institutional matrix has
  increasing returns through setup costs, learning, coordination and
  adaptive expectations. England's and Spain's colonies diverged and have
  not converged.

## Concepts

- **Institutions** — the constraints (rules of the game), formal and
  informal, as distinct from organisations, which are the players.
- **Transaction costs** — the resources spent to define and enforce
  exchange agreements, including measurement of what is exchanged.
- **Institutional matrix** — the interdependent web of institutions and
  the organisations they make possible.
- **Uncertainty versus risk** — uncertainty cannot be given a probability;
  risk can, and so can be insured (p. 106).

## Connections

- **Hindriks & Guala** ([LIT-481](../literature.d/LIT-481.md), read in [NOTE-395](NOTE-395.md)) take North's
  1990 version of this definition as the exemplar of the rules account.
  Their objection, that rules alone cannot say why some are followed,
  fits this essay: compliance here is a matter of enforcement cost, and
  the closing questions ("What is the relationship between formal and
  informal constraints?") are left as open.
- **Guala's book** ([LIT-472](../literature.d/LIT-472.md)) quotes the 1990 definition as the
  epigraph of chapter 1.
- **The SEP entry** ([LIT-487](../literature.d/LIT-487.md), §5.3) groups North with Williamson and
  Ostrom as "new institutional economics" and calls that programme
  largely game-theoretic. In this essay game theory is a framing device;
  the analysis is transaction-cost economics and history.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md): none.** The essay treats institutions as constraints on
  individual wealth-maximisers. It attributes no agency or mind to any
  collective, and organisations appear only as players.
- **Social choice** ([LIT-285](../literature.d/LIT-285.md), [LIT-282](../literature.d/LIT-282.md)). Sen's results are about
  aggregating preferences into a social ordering. North's are about the
  rules within which individuals choose. The essay does not touch
  aggregation.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice appears, so nothing here
  points to the Anthology of the SOTA.

## Limitations

- The definition is not argued for, and it does not say what makes a
  constraint an institution rather than, say, a physical obstacle, beyond
  being "humanly devised".
- No model is given; "game theoretic" means a verbal description of when
  cooperation is easy and hard.
- The historical cases are drawn from secondary literature, and the
  staged sequence is disclaimed as history (n. 1).
- Why informal constraints bind, which the essay calls central, is left
  as an open question in its last paragraph.

## Open questions

- What makes informal constraints so persistent, and how do they relate
  to formal rules? (North's own closing questions, p. 111.)
- Under what conditions does a path get reversed, as in Spain's modern
  revival?
