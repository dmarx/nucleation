# LIT-tmpoli85: The End of Ownership: Personal Property in the Digital Economy

Perzanowski, A., and Schultz, J. (2016). *The End of Ownership: Personal Property in the Digital Economy*. Information Society Series. Cambridge, MA: The MIT Press. Open-access edition (ISBN 9780262335959).

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, NOTE-tmpzh69c.*

- **Licensed, not sold.** Digital retailers say the buyer gets a licence,
  not a copy: "Kindle Content is licensed, not sold, to you." (p. 4). The
  licence then forbids resale, lending and transfer, and lets the seller
  revoke access (pp. 61–62, 84–85).
- **Exhaustion is tied to the copy.** The first sale doctrine lets the
  owner of a copy sell, lend or give it away against the copyright holder's
  wishes (pp. 25–27). Digital transfer makes a new copy, so it is not
  covered (*ReDigi*, pp. 40–41). Cloud and streaming leave no clear copy
  to own: "Without copies, under current law, there's simply nothing to
  own." (p. 48).
- **A licence is a permission, not a contract.** Courts since *ProCD*
  enforce terms nobody reads as contracts (pp. 65–71). The authors argue
  that a licence presupposes ownership and cannot create it: "A license
  does not—in fact, cannot—define property rights; it depends on
  predetermined property rights." (p. 72). Ownership should turn on the
  economic reality of the deal: duration, one-time payment, and how it was
  sold (pp. 76–77).
- **"Buy Now" misleads.** In the authors' survey of nearly 1,300 likely
  buyers, most thought a digital purchase was theirs to keep and use on
  any device, and more than 40 percent thought they could lend or give it
  away (pp. 90–95). A short notice cut the errors; "License Now" did not
  (pp. 99–101).
- **DRM and §1201 carry the licence into devices.** Section 1201 of the
  DMCA makes breaking DRM unlawful even for lawful uses (pp. 130–132), and
  the same locks now sit in printers, coffee makers, tractors, cars and
  pacemakers (chs. 7–8). Patent law offers a parallel route through
  post-sale restrictions (ch. 9).
- **Reforms.** Police "Buy Now" through the FTC; rein in form contracts;
  exempt owners from §1201; restore patent exhaustion; and a multifactor
  test for digital exhaustion that tracks "who has the right to use and
  transfer" a work rather than counting copies (ch. 10, pp. 179–188).

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-20 at the owner's request, with Lessig's *Remix*
(LIT-tmpreyek), as a second reading on what copyright's reliance on the
copy does to ordinary use. It does not cite Lessig. It reaches the same
copy-trigger diagnosis from the side of the buyer's property rather than
the remixer's creativity, and it sets remix aside explicitly (p. 183). It
quotes Doctorow on DRM lock-in (p. 134) and supplies the case law behind
the lock-in and interoperability steps of the enshittification argument
(THEORY-150, THEORY-470; LIT-731, LIT-033). How it bears on those and on
the translation line is set out in the reading.
