# NOTE-1489: Tsoukas & Chia — On organizational becoming

## Contribution

The paper asks "What must organization(s) be like if change is
constitutive of reality?" (p. 570). It answers with an "ontological
reversal": "Change must not be thought of as a property of organization.
Rather, organization must be understood as an emergent property of
change" (p. 570). It names the result "organizational becoming", and it
gives a mechanism, the radial structure of categories, for why ordinary
action changes organizations.

## Key insight

Organization is "a secondary accomplishment, in a double sense" (p. 570).
It is an input to action, institutionalized categories that make
behaviour predictable. It is also an output, a pattern that emerges as
those categories are applied in local circumstances. "Organization aims
at stemming change but, in the process of doing so, it is generated by
it" (p. 577).

## Assumptions

- **Process philosophy.** Bergson and James. Concepts fix what is in
  flux, so stage models describe change as "a series of immobilities"
  and miss change itself (pp. 570–571).
- **Prototype theory of categories.** Lakoff, Johnson and Rosch.
  Categories have a stable prototypical core and an unstable periphery
  (p. 573).
- **Human reflexivity.** Maturana and von Foerster. Humans interact with
  their own descriptions and so generate new ones (p. 575).
- **Actors as webs of beliefs.** After Rorty, "actors are conceived as
  webs of beliefs and habits of action that keep reweaving (and thus
  altering) as they try to coherently accommodate new experiences" (p.
  575).

## Key results

The results are conceptual.

1. **Synoptic and performative accounts.** Synoptic accounts see change
   from outside, as a succession of states. Performative accounts see it
   from within, through "situated human agency unfolding in time" (p.
   572). Both are needed, and the literature has been dominated by the
   first.
2. **Why closure is temporary.** Organizing needs "closure of meaning"
   (p. 573), but it is only temporary, for two reasons.
   - Interaction with an open-ended world throws up nonprototypical cases
     (Orr's copier technicians, the "wheeled vehicles in parks" statute).
     Applying a category to them is "an imaginative extension" that
     changes it, and "the application of a concept is always a normative
     act" (p. 574).
   - Reflexivity lets actors redescribe their own experience (p. 575).
3. **Illustrations.** Feldman's housing routines, Orlikowski's ITSS, a
   Navy Total Quality programme (Barrett et al.) and jazz improvisation
   (pp. 575–579). Each shows a routine or programme being modified in its
   performance.
4. **Planned change.** Managers are "as much an agent of change as
   everybody else", distinguished by "declarative powers" (p. 579). A
   change programme is "a new discursive template" that amplifies changes
   already under way. It is "a punctuation of the flow of organizational
   life", not its origin.
5. **Inertia.** Ongoing change does not mean organizations constantly
   change. Local adaptations may never be institutionalized. "Organizations
   tend to resist change" is shorthand for that failure (p. 579).

## Concepts

- **Organizational becoming.** The ongoing change of organizations, which
  is the normal condition and not an exception.
- **Synoptic account.** Change seen from outside as an accomplished event
  between states.
- **Performative account.** Change seen from within, as enacted in time.
- **Radial category.** A category with a prototypical core and a
  nonprototypical periphery.
- **Imaginative extension.** Applying a category beyond its prototypes,
  which changes it.
- **Discursive template.** The interpretive codes a change programme
  introduces.
- **Microscopic change.** Change that occurs "through 'creep,' 'slippage,'
  and 'drift'" and may never be institutionalized (p. 579).

## Connections

- **Weick, Orlikowski and Feldman.** The paper builds on their process
  views and says they "do not go far enough" (p. 569).
- **Bencherki, Brummans & Vézy 2024 ([LIT-1902](../literature.d/LIT-1902.md)).** It cites this paper
  as the conception of organizational becoming its own theory "parallels"
  (p. 1351). This paper is human-centred: change is "inherent in human
  action" (p. 570), its engine is "the intrinsically human ability to be
  reflexive" (p. 575), and organization emerges "through the ongoing
  agency of organizational members" (p. 579). Bencherki et al. hold that
  "there are no already-constituted agents" (p. 1358). The two agree that
  organization is a secondary effect of process. They disagree on whether
  the process is carried by agents. Bencherki et al. do not mark the
  difference.

## Limitations

- **No evidence of its own.** The illustrations are other researchers'
  ethnographies, chosen because they show change. No case is described
  in which a routine stays the same under performance.
- **The ontological claim is not testable as stated.** The authors grant
  that organizations often do not change (p. 579). They save the thesis
  by placing change below the level of institutionalization, where it
  "may never acquire the status of formal organizational systems". This
  makes "change is pervasive" hard to fault.
- **Unit of analysis.** The account is about individuals reweaving their
  beliefs. How individual reweaving becomes organizational is left as "a
  different issue" for "empirical research" (p. 579).

## Open questions

- Under what conditions does microscopic change become institutionalized?
  The authors leave this open (p. 579).
- Whether the radial-category mechanism predicts which rules will change,
  for instance that rules with more nonprototypical cases drift faster.
