# NOTE-545: Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person

## Contribution

The paper replaces the then-standard concept of a person (a bearer of both
mental and physical predicates) with one defined by the structure of the
will, and uses that structure to define freedom of the will as distinct
from freedom of action. It then separates freedom of the will from moral
responsibility: acting "of one's own free will" requires acting from the
will one wants, not having a will that is free. This gives a
compatibilist account of responsibility that needs no alternatives.

## Key insight

The question whether a will is free is the question whether it is the will
the person wants. A person is someone to whom that question can matter,
because he has preferences about which of his desires moves him. That makes
freedom of the will a relation among the person's own attitudes, and so
indifferent to determinism and to how the attitudes arose.

## Assumptions

- **"Want" is used broadly** (§I): desires may be unconscious, mistaken,
  ambivalent or unfelt (pp. 7–8). Only one restriction is imposed, on
  strength: the will is the desire that moves "all the way to action" (p.
  8).
- **Second-order volitions entail the first-order desire** in standard
  cases (p. 10); n. 4 flags non-standard cases (wanting one's will to be
  whatever B's is) and does not pursue them.
- **The person/wanton line is partly stipulative.** Creatures with
  second-order desires but no volitions "differ significantly from brute
  animals" and the usage withholding "person" from them "is thus somewhat
  arbitrary" (n. 5).
- **No condition on the content or basis of a second-order volition.** It
  need not be moral and may be "capricious and irresponsible"; "There is
  no essential restriction on the kind of basis, if any, upon which they
  are formed" (n. 6).
- **Persons are rational,** since only reason makes one "capable of
  becoming critically aware of his own will" (pp. 11–12), but the essence
  of being a person "lies not in reason but in will" (p. 11).

## Key results

- **§I Orders of desire and the will (pp. 6–10).** First-order desires are
  "simply desires to do or not to do one thing or another"; second-order
  desires are to have or not have certain desires (p. 7). "An agent's will,
  then, is identical with one or more of his first-order desires", namely
  "an effective desire—one that moves (or will or would move) a person all
  the way to action"; the will is not what one intends, since a settled
  intention can lose to a stronger desire (p. 8). The physician who wants
  to feel a craving for the drug, but not to act on it, "stands at the
  margin of preciosity": his second-order desire is not about his will
  (p. 9). Wanting a desire "to be his will" entails having that desire
  (p. 10).
- **§II Persons and wantons (pp. 10–14).** "Second-order volitions" are
  second-order desires that a desire be one's will, and "it is having
  second-order volitions, and not having second-order desires generally,
  that I regard as essential to being a person" (p. 10). "I shall use the
  term 'wanton' to refer to agents who have first-order desires but who
  are not persons because … they have no second-order volitions" (p. 11).
  "The essential characteristic of a wanton is that he does not care about
  his will" (p. 11). A wanton may deliberate about means; "he does not
  care which of his inclinations is the strongest" (p. 11).
  - *The unwilling addict* wants the desire to refrain, not the craving,
    to be his will, and is "helplessly violated by his own desires" (p.
    12).
  - *The wanton addict* has the same first-order conflict and no
    preference about which desire wins; "he has no identity apart from his
    first-order desires" (p. 13).
  - *Identification.* "The unwilling addict identifies himself … through
    the formation of a second-order volition, with one rather than with
    the other … He makes one of them more truly his own and, in so doing,
    he withdraws himself from the other." This is what lets him say "that
    the force moving him to take the drug is a force other than his own"
    (p. 13). "When a person acts, the desire by which he is moved is
    either the will he wants or a will he wants to be without. When a
    wanton acts, it is neither" (p. 14).
- **§III Freedom of the will (pp. 14–17).** Persons are the beings "for
  whom the freedom of its will may be a problem" (p. 14). Freedom to do
  what one wants is neither sufficient (animals have it) nor necessary
  (someone unknowingly deprived of it keeps a free will) for freedom of the
  will (pp. 14–15). By analogy with free action, a person enjoys freedom of
  the will when he is "free to want what he wants to want … to have the will
  he wants" (p. 15). "It is in securing the conformity of his will to his
  second-order volitions, then, that a person exercises freedom of the
  will", and one who lacks it feels it in "the discrepancy between his
  will and his second-order volitions, or in his awareness that their
  coincidence is not his own doing but only a happy chance" (p. 15).
  - *Second-order conflict.* An unresolved conflict among second-order
    desires, if severe enough "that it prevents him from identifying
    himself in a sufficiently decisive way", "destroys him as a person",
    leaving him "a helpless bystander to the forces that move him" (p.
    16).
  - *Higher orders and their end.* "There is no theoretical limit" to the
    series; refusing to stop would be "humanization run wild" (p. 16). "It
    is possible, however, to terminate such a series of acts without
    cutting it off arbitrarily. When a person identifies himself decisively
    with one of his first-order desires, this commitment 'resounds'
    throughout the potentially endless array of higher orders." The person
    who "without reservation or conflict" wants to be moved by the desire
    to work "can properly insist" that the third-order question "does not
    arise"; the commitment means "he has decided that no further question
    about his second-order volition, at any higher order, remains to be
    asked" (p. 16).
  - *Spontaneity.* Conformity of will to volition "may be far more
    thoughtless and spontaneous"; "The enjoyment of freedom comes easily
    to some. Others must struggle to achieve it" (p. 17).
- **§IV Why it matters, and responsibility (pp. 17–20).**
  - Having both freedoms is "all the freedom it is possible to desire or to
    conceive" (p. 17). Chisholm's agent-causal account fails both
    adequacy conditions: it gives no reason to deny the freedom to a
    rabbit, and no reason to want it (p. 18).
  - "It is not true that a person is morally responsible for what he has
    done only if his will was free when he did it" (p. 18). A free will
    "could have been otherwise", in the sense that the person could have
    constituted it otherwise, but that "has no bearing on the theory of
    moral responsibility" (p. 19).
  - Responsibility requires acting "freely, or … of his own free will":
    having done what he wanted, because he wanted it, moved by a will that
    "was his will because it was the will he wanted". Then "it is quite
    irrelevant to the evaluation of his moral responsibility to inquire
    whether the alternatives that he opted against were actually available
    to him" (p. 19; n. 9 cites the 1969 paper).
  - *The willing addict*, "altogether delighted with his condition", has an
    unfree will, since the desire "will be effective regardless", but takes
    the drug "freely and of his own free will". His desire is overdetermined:
    effective because of the addiction and "because he wants it to be".
    "His will is outside his control, but, by his second-order desire …
    he has made this will his own", and "he may be morally responsible for
    taking the drug" (pp. 19–20).
  - *Determinism and history.* The conception "appears to be neutral with
    regard to the problem of determinism": it may be causally determined,
    or a matter of chance, that a person has a free will (p. 20). Another
    agent may also be responsible for one's having it. Note 10: if the
    willing addict "has been made an addict by the deliberate and calculated
    work of another", it "may be that both the addict and this other person
    are fully responsible" (p. 20).

## Concepts

- **First-order / second-order desire.** A desire to do or not do
  something / a desire to have or not have a desire (p. 7).
- **Will.** The effective desire, which moves one "all the way to action"
  (p. 8).
- **Second-order volition.** Wanting a desire to be one's will (p. 10).
- **Person.** A being with second-order volitions, "for whom the freedom of
  its will may be a problem" (pp. 10, 14).
- **Wanton.** An agent with first-order desires and no second-order
  volitions, who "does not care about his will" (p. 11).
- **Identification.** Forming a second-order volition in favour of a
  desire, making it "more truly his own" (p. 13); decisive identification
  "resounds" through higher orders (p. 16).
- **Freedom of action / freedom of the will.** Doing what one wants / having
  the will one wants (p. 15).
- **Acting of one's own free will.** Acting from the will one wants,
  whether or not one's will is free (p. 19).

## Limitations

- **Identification is asserted, not analysed.** The paper says forming a
  second-order volition makes a desire "more truly his own" (p. 13) and
  gives no argument why the higher-order attitude speaks for the person.
  This is the gap the regress objection presses.
- **Decisiveness is undefined.** "Decisively" and "without reservation or
  conflict" (p. 16) are illustrated by one example, and nothing says what
  makes a commitment decisive rather than merely unopposed.
- **The empirical claims are asserted**: that no animal but man can form
  second-order desires (p. 7), and that very young children are wantons
  (p. 11).
- **Responsibility is treated in two pages** (pp. 18–20), and its relation
  to the 1969 paper is a footnote.
- **The history question is raised and set aside.** Note 10 accepts the
  designed addict as fully responsible without arguing that he is.

## Open questions

- What makes a commitment "decisive"? [LIT-575](../literature.d/LIT-575.md) is reported to answer this
  ("no reason to continue deliberating"), and is unread.
- Is the designed addict of n. 10 responsible? Fischer and Ravizza, Mele
  and Pereboom say no (as [LIT-291](../literature.d/LIT-291.md) reports); the paper asserts it.
- Does freedom of the will, as defined here, need the "could have been
  otherwise" of p. 19, and if so, is the account of free will (as opposed
  to responsibility) neutral on determinism, as p. 20 says?
