# NOTE-769: Bird & Tobin — Natural Kinds (SEP)

## Contribution

An encyclopedia entry that organises the natural-kinds literature by four
metaphysical questions (are our kind classifications natural, are they
into kinds, what are kinds, do they have essences), then tests the
answers against biology, chemistry, psychology and the social sciences,
and closes with the epistemology of kinds and the semantics of kind
terms. It argues for little of its own. Where it does conclude, the
conclusion is that a proposed condition is necessary and not sufficient,
or that an argument assumes what it claims to derive.

## Key insight

"Natural kind" bundles several commitments that come apart: that some
classifications are natural (naturalism), that there are entities that
are the kinds (strong realism), and that kinds have essences. "Intermediate
views tend to be more popular" (§1): natural classifications by kind, with
no kind-entities and with or without essences. Most disputes about whether
something is a natural kind are disputes about which of these is meant.

## Assumptions

- The entry's subject is the kinds of the sciences; that "it is the use of
  natural kinds by the individual ('special') sciences that generates our
  interest in them" (introduction).
- A kind is "natural" if it "reflects the structure of the natural world
  rather than the interests and actions of human beings" (introduction).
- Its scale of commitment (Bird 2018, §1) runs from out-and-out
  conventionalism to irreducible kind-entities with essences.

## Key results

- **Six criteria, mostly necessary only (§1.1.1).**
  1. Shared natural properties: "necessary … but not a sufficient
     condition"; Mill: white objects do not form a natural kind.
  2. Support for induction: also "necessary but not sufficient", and "does
     not go beyond Criterion 1".
  3. Participation in laws: more contentious, and still not sufficient,
     since laws "may concern natural qualities and quantities that do not
     define kinds (e.g. Newton's law of gravitation)".
  4. Forming a kind: needed because 1–3 fail ("negatively charged things,
     nor … things with a mass of 1 kg"), and not clear in what it
     requires.
  5. Hierarchy and 6. categorical distinctness: contentious, and each
     fails for some scientific kinds (allotropes and functional groups in
     §2.2; species in §2.1).
- **Induction runs on properties and relations, not kinds (§1.2.1).** On
  the narrower conception, "the real answer to 'what tends to confirm an
  induction?' is not 'natural kinds and their instances' but is 'natural
  properties and their instances'", and "inductions often concern
  relations … But we do not suppose that objects that enter into a
  natural relation form a natural kind." Quine's liberal view, by
  contrast, counts any set whose members share a natural property,
  including positively charged objects.
- **Cluster kinds (§1.2.2).** Boyd's homeostatic property clusters:
  properties kept together by mechanisms that make deviations unlikely to
  persist, with vague boundaries and no individually necessary property.
  Objections: the mechanisms are not delineated (Boyd), deciding when two
  mechanisms are the same involves convention (Craver 2009), and they
  overlap (Tobin 2017). Slater (2015) recasts it as stable property
  clusters, "sufficiently stably co-instantiated to accommodate the
  inferential and explanatory uses". Chakravartty argues that liberal
  realisms dissolve into conventionalism or into realism about something
  else.
- **Conventionalism with a check (§1.1.2).** Hacking (1999): constructed
  categories can be real or not, and which is "subject to empirical
  investigation". Satanic ritual abuse was a constructed idea whose cases
  were not substantiated; child abuse, a category with a datable origin
  (Denver, 1961), had "real instances … found through empirical
  investigation". Kukla's three dependences: material, causal (Hacking's
  interactive kinds, Merton's bank run), constitutive.
- **Pluralism (§1.1.3, §2.1).** Dupré's promiscuous realism: many
  cross-cutting, equally legitimate taxonomies, each objectively grounded,
  with no hierarchy and no categorical distinctness. Species concepts
  disagree on extensions; pluralism is "the weakest form of realism about
  kinds in the philosophy of biology".
- **Essentialism argued for and against (§1.3).** Kind essentialism does
  not entail that a particular's kind is essential to it (neptunium-239
  decaying to plutonium; speciation by isolation). The Kripke–Putnam
  arguments establish at most "partial essences", necessary conditions
  such as atomic number 79 for gold. Salmon: only trivial essentialism
  follows from the theory of reference, and the reconstructed
  water argument needs an essentialist premise, P3. Fine: essential is not
  the same as necessary.
- **Eliminativism (§1.4).** Hacking (2007): "Delete every mention of
  natural kinds"; Ludwig (2018) likewise, on the ground that the concept
  covers heterogeneous practices and no unitary theory fits all
  sciences. Conix and Chi (2021): the concept still "enables fruitful
  investigation into non-arbitrary classification".
- **The special sciences (§2).** Species may be individuals, not kinds
  (Ghiselin, Hull), and their membership conditions are relational, so
  either species are not natural kinds or kindhood need not be intrinsic.
  Chemical kinds are the current paradigm, with complications (isomers,
  water as a dynamic structure, allotropy). Mental kinds face multiple
  realisability; Griffiths (1997) argues emotion is not a natural kind and
  Machery that "concept" is not one. Social kinds are interactive: "the
  object of classification itself changes in being classified" (Hacking
  1995, 1999). Khalidi (2010): the difference from natural kinds is one of
  degree, "of the degree of control we have over the object of
  classification".
- **Epistemology first, and its critics (§3).** Slater (2015) and
  Ereshefsky and Reydon (2015): judge kinds by their role in inductive
  inference within scientific practice; many successful scientific kinds
  (non-causal, functional, heterostatic) do not count as natural under the
  HPC view. Against them, Lemeire (2018): epistemic success "is
  constrained by deference to the world not only in cases of natural kind
  classification, but also in cases of conventional and pragmatic
  classification", so a purely epistemic approach "fails to give us a
  principled way of distinguishing between the natural kinds and their
  conventional counterparts". Kendig and Grey (2021): the stability of a
  cluster cannot be assessed without the metaphysics that explains it.
  Reydon (2016): kinds are co-created by nature and classifier.
- **Semantics (§4).** The modal, epistemological and semantic (Twin
  Earth) arguments against descriptivism; direct reference and
  externalism; Putnam's same-kind relation anchored to a sample. Rigid
  designation for kind terms may be trivial (Soames, Schwartz, Martí)
  unless kind terms denote only sparse, natural entities. Whether
  essentialism follows from the semantics: Salmon and Burge against;
  Bird (2020) that the semantics carries metaphysical assumptions that
  competent users make tacitly.

## Concepts

- **Naturalism (weak realism).** Some classifications are natural; no
  commitment to kind-entities.
- **Strong realism.** There are entities that are the natural kinds.
- **Essence.** A property or set whose possession is necessary and
  sufficient for membership; a *partial essence* is necessary only.
- **Homeostatic property cluster.** A family of properties kept
  co-occurring by mechanisms, without a necessary member.
- **Stable property cluster** (Slater). The same, judged by the stability
  that suits it to inference, not by the mechanism.
- **Interactive kind** (Hacking). A kind of person whose members respond
  to being classified, which changes the kind.
- **Kinding.** The act of making kinds in scientific practice, in the
  epistemology-first literature.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-028](../questions.d/QUESTION-028.md) is sharpened, not answered.** The question asks when
  "independently generalizable, intervention-relevant relational
  structure" warrants treating a communicative category as a real kind.
  - The entry's §1.2.1 says that natural relations supporting induction
    do not thereby make their relata a kind, and §1.1.1 that inductive
    support is necessary, not sufficient. So the question's qualifiers
    cannot be enough by themselves on the mainstream view; something must
    answer the "kindhood" criterion (4), which the entry calls unclear.
  - Lemeire's point (§3) is the sharpest form of the record's guard,
    [CLAIM-150](../claims.d/CLAIM-150.md): predictive and explanatory success constrains conventional
    and pragmatic classifications too, so it cannot be what separates a
    real kind.
  - The entry also supplies the two most usable positive answers. One is
    Slater's stable property cluster, which would make the question's
    qualifiers sufficient if stability is judged before the data. The
    other is Hacking's test for constructed categories, that real instances
    are "found through empirical investigation". It is close to the
    question's own answer condition: generalisation to realisations not
    used to define the category.
  - Communicative categories are classifications of human acts, so
    Hacking's interactive kinds apply: a category used by speakers can
    change what they do. Any test of the kind the question wants has to
    allow for that, or show the category is indifferent to being named.
- **[THEORY-036](../theory.d/THEORY-036.md).** Consistent with it and independent of it. Dennett's
  compressibility over-admits patterns; on this entry, shared properties
  and inductive support over-admit kinds (Quine's liberal kinds include
  positively charged objects). Both say the excluding work is done by a
  further clause. The entry never mentions information or compression.
- **[THEORY-057](../theory.d/THEORY-057.md).** The entry reports Griffiths (1997) that emotion does not
  correspond to a natural kind, which agrees with [THEORY-057](../theory.d/THEORY-057.md)'s conclusion
  from a different line.
- **The owner's heading.** The work was filed under "Relating K-complexity
  to minimum sufficient statistics". Nothing in the entry concerns
  information, compression or statistics, as the skim ([NOTE-217](NOTE-217.md))
  anticipated; that link is the owner's to make. The positions most
  comparable to "the model class decides what counts as structure" are
  Dupré's promiscuous realism and Reydon's co-creation.
- **Filed from this reading:** [THEORY-273](../theory.d/THEORY-273.md), on inductive and
  explanatory usefulness as necessary and not sufficient.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- A survey. Its reports of Lemeire, Slater, Khalidi and others are
  summaries, and the arguments are not reproduced in full.
- The social-kinds section (§2.4) is short and leans on Hacking and
  Khalidi; nothing in it treats linguistic or communicative categories.
- The 2022 revision was read; the Spring 2024 archive the owner cites was
  not checked against it.

## Open questions

- Whether Lemeire's objection defeats Slater's stability criterion, or
