# LIT-tmpcmegc: Jokes and the Logic of the Cognitive Unconscious

Marvin Minsky (1980), *MIT Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, A.I. Memo No.
603*, November 1980, 25 pp. — https://hdl.handle.net/1721.1/5701. Published
as a chapter in L. Vaina and J. Hintikka (eds.), *Cognitive Constraints on
Communication*, Dordrecht: Reidel, 1984, pp. 175–200 —
DOI-10.1007/978-94-010-9188-6_10. This filing was read from the memo.

## Key takeaways

- **Thesis.** "Common sense logic is too unreliable for practical use. It
  cannot be repaired, so we must learn to avoid its most common
  malfunctions." Humour has a special role in learning and passing on how
  to avoid them.
- **Censors.** These are learned suppressors, in a "B-brain" that watches
  the "A-brain". They recognise the *precursors* of an unproductive mental
  state and prevent it, quickly and without phenomenology, which is why they
  are unconscious. A joke heard before is less funny because a censor has
  since been built.
- **The common element of humour** is "unexpected frame-substitution": a
  scene is described from one viewpoint and then, often by one word,
  re-viewed in another.
- **Positive and negative learning.** General methods cannot be patched
  without losing generality, so their failures must be handled
  *subtractively*, by censors. Particular analogies are handled
  *additively*, by links.
- **Intellect and affect.** The "affect-intellect 'dumbbell'" does more harm
  than good. Cognitive traumas such as the nearness paradox, the liar or
  Zeno create censors much as Freudian taboos do, without needing an
  authority figure.
- **Laughter** disrupts the absurd line of thought and focuses on it, so
  that the censor-learning agency can absorb it. Sexual humour stays funny
  because those censors are "slow learners".

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-01 at the owner's request, as part of Minsky's Society of
Mind programme. Its Note 1, headed "The Society of Mind", places it with
three earlier works: the frame memo ([LIT-tmpf92fs](LIT-tmpf92fs.md)), Plain Talk
([LIT-tmp4jv7c](LIT-tmp4jv7c.md)) and K-lines ([LIT-tmp84goj](LIT-tmp84goj.md)). "The key idea is to reject the
conventional view of the mind as a Single Agent… rather, the mind is
composed of many smaller minds."

It extends the frame memo. Its section III restates frames, terminals,
default assignment and frame-shifts, and builds its account of
commonsense logic and humour on them. Commonsense inference is "learning to
make shifts between frames that have terminals in common", and a joke is an
improper or unexpected shift. It also uses K-lines' partial mental states
(its Note 2) and Plain Talk's ambiguity of thoughts (its Note 7), but it does
not build on them in the same way. The 1986 book and *The Emotion Machine* (2006) are filed alongside by
another pass. How they take up censors, and Note 12's sketch of pleasure as
a "constructed illusion" of coherence, is for those filings to say. I have
not read them.

[NOTE-tmptwjh8](../notes.d/NOTE-tmptwjh8.md) is the close reading of 2026-10-01, from the memo, and it
placed the work: **Active**. It is worth reading for the idea of negative,
anecdotal meta-knowledge and for a computational answer to a hole in
Freud's theory of jokes. As evidence for any of it, it has none.

Within this record:

- **Somatic markers.** The somatic-marker hypothesis ([LIT-384](LIT-384.md)) has learned
  situation–feeling links that covertly prune the options reasoning
  considers. The gambling-task study ([LIT-383](LIT-383.md)) reports nonconscious biases
  steering choice before the reason is known. Minsky's censors are the
  cognitive mirror image. They are learned, unconscious and anticipatory,
  and they prune thought by its precursors, not by feeling. Both reject a
  clean separation of affect from intellect. The pairing is mine.
- **Logic.** In the logic papers held here, paradox is a property of a
  formal system. In this memo it is an event in a developing mind.

The memo carries no instruction for machine-learning practice, and no
anthology topic holds it.
