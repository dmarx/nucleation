# THEORY-tmp0cj7p: Lohmann, Cowlishaw, Ney, O'Donnell and Felmingham find, across 45 cross-sectional studies, that exposure to an intimate partner's coercive control is moderately associated with PTSD and depression symptoms, at strengths comparable to those for psychological intimate partner violence in general and not stronger

Lohmann et al. (2023), [LIT-2678](../literature.d/LIT-2678.md).

## The result

Random-effects meta-analyses of 45 of 68 eligible observational studies:
coercive control and PTSD r = .32 [.28, .37]; and depression r = .27 [.22,
.31]. Psychological IPV in the same studies: .34 and .33. The authors had
predicted coercive control would be "clearly stronger" and found it was not.
Associations were stronger in shelter and service samples (r = .40) than in
community samples (r = .26).

## What it does not show

- **Effects.** "a direct causal link between coercive control and mental
  health could not be established".
- **That domination harms beyond its acts.** The associations are no
  larger than those of psychological abuse generally. The authors suggest
  act-based measures may miss whether a threat is "embedded in a chronic
  pattern of power and control"; that is an explanation of the null, not
  evidence for it.
- **Complex PTSD.** One study only.
