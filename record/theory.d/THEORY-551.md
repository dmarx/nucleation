# THEORY-551: The Russian narrative of a Western information war waged against Russia since the Cold War was politicised by academics, politicians and the public each for their own reasons, scholars promoting their ideas, politicians consolidating power and a public wanting its lost national pride explained, and not directed from the Kremlin; once adopted into rhetoric, doctrine and law, it drives behaviour whatever its origin

## Source

Fridman (2017), [LIT-088](../literature.d/LIT-088.md), "The Russian perspective on information warfare"
(*Defence Strategic Communications* 2, pp. 61–86), read in [NOTE-070](../notes.d/NOTE-070.md). Clark
(2020), [LIT-023](../literature.d/LIT-023.md), read in [NOTE-012](../notes.d/NOTE-012.md), for the same belief in military
writing.

## What was actually shown

**Two strands (p. 62).** Russian writing on information warfare divides into
a neutral, "dual-use" technical literature and an ideological strand in
which information war is a Western technique aimed at Russia (C1,
moderate; no counts).

**The theorists (pp. 63–76).** Messner's "subversion-war", republished after
1991 and inverted from an account of Soviet subversion into one of the
West's ("the Messner formula got it right, but in the exact opposite
way", p. 70). Dugin's "net-centric war" (2007), aimed at "full and absolute
control". Panarin's "informational struggle over the control of the minds
of the elites", with 1991 "a defeat in the informational war, which lasted
for 48 years", though he blames Soviet failure more than US skill (p.
75). Dugin and Panarin share four tenets: continuous Western effort to
undermine the Russian establishment, information war as its method, a
"fifth column", and a patriotic new elite as the answer (p. 76).

**Adoption (pp. 77–79).** Putin (30 June 2016) on "a growing barrage of
information attacks"; Lavrov (April 2015): "We are faced with a
large-scale information war"; the 2015 National Security Strategy on the
"manipulation of public consciousness"; laws 121-FZ, 398-FZ, 97-FZ,
374-FZ and 305-FZ; Levada polls of 2014–2016 (C4, strong for presence).

**The causal claim (pp. 80–81).** Because the theorists predate Putin, the
reading that the Kremlin uses them "seems to be flawed". A demand-side
reading, the Kremlin following the public's "hearts and minds", is
offered. "None of these explanations is completely right or entirely
wrong"; the truth is "somewhere in the middle". Following Tulchinsky, the
discourse about information war is itself information war, aimed mainly at
a domestic audience because it is conducted in Russian (p. 77).

**From the military side.** Clark reports an "institutional worldview that
the US has led the West in an ongoing hybrid war against Russia since the
end of the Cold War" (Clark p. 16). He calls it "deeply paranoid and
frankly hyperbolic" and argues it shapes planning, or that if it began as
propaganda "a fake tail … is now wagging the dog" (p. 18). Both authors
reject the merely-cynical reading, and both say the narrative now drives
behaviour whatever its origin.

**What could have come out otherwise.** The theorists could have appeared
only after official adoption, or the public could have rejected the
narrative. On the article's evidence neither holds, with the exceptions
below.

## What this does not say

- **That the Kremlin played no part.** The conclusion is hedged to
  "somewhere in the middle"; the account denies direction, not
  participation.
- **That the chronology holds throughout.** It holds for Messner's revival
  and for Panarin. By the article's own dates Dugin's net-centric concept
  is from 2007, under Putin (C8, weak–moderate).
- **That doctrine produced the laws.** Four of the five laws (2012–2014)
  predate the 2015 Strategy and the 2016 draft Doctrine they are said to
  be "an outcome of" (C5, weak). One legal citation, 374-FZ supported by a
  2014 story on what is usually 242-FZ, looks mismatched and is unverified.
- **That the public simply absorbed it.** The same polls show majorities
  believing TV censored and trust in TV falling from 79% to 41%
  (2009–2015), which the article does not reconcile (C6). That academics
  played "an equal if not more powerful role" is inferred from those polls
  (C7, weak).
- **That the two sources corroborate each other's evidence.** Clark cites
  none of Fridman's theorists and Fridman none of Clark's military authors
  ([NOTE-070](../notes.d/NOTE-070.md)). They agree on the narrative and on its not being merely
  cynical.
- **Anything after 2016,** for Fridman, or after September 2020, for Clark.
