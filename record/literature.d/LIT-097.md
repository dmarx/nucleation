# LIT-097: The Extended Mind

Andy Clark, David J. Chalmers (1998), *Analysis 58(1), 7–19* — DOI-10.1093/analys/58.1.7

## Key takeaways

- When an external resource is coupled with an organism in a two-way interaction and plays the role an internal process would, it is part of the cognitive process. Clark and Chalmers argue that dispositional beliefs themselves (Otto's notebook) can be partly constituted by the environment, provided the resource is reliably available, easily accessed and automatically endorsed.

*Seeded from the abstract and a skim, not a reading. What follows is what the work says about itself.*

Where does the mind end and the world begin? Rather than the skin-and-skull boundary or Putnam–Burge semantic externalism, Clark and Chalmers defend "active externalism": external features that are coupled to the organism and drive cognition in the here and now are part of the cognitive system. They first argue that cognitive processes can extend into the environment, and then that mental states, specifically standing beliefs, can too. The paper closes by drawing out consequences for language, social cognition and the self.

What a deeper reading should check:

- It is the foundational text for extended-cognition questions about AI tools: whether a user's LLM assistant or memory store can satisfy the availability, trust and endorsement criteria. Birch's [LIT-111](LIT-111.md) and Register's [LIT-138](LIT-138.md) (BCIs as body parts) sit downstream of it.
- The trust and endorsement criteria are the pressure point for AI-mediated cognition. Hallucination-prone tools ([LIT-212](LIT-212.md)) would fail "automatic endorsement" only if users stop endorsing, which cuts both ways.
