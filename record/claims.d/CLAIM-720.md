# CLAIM-720: Inalienability is a limit on normative powers, not on exercise: a person may restrict the exercise of a right or delegate limited authority over decisions, and no delegation, contract, collective decision or success in imposing an arrangement thereby confers authority to determine whether that person keeps the standing from which claims against the arrangement arise, because the holder lacks the power to confer it

## The claim

A85 §2 of the exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b), taking
up the owner's proposal at U37: "An inalienable right is not necessarily a
right whose exercise can never be restricted. Nor does inalienability
necessarily mean that every possible waiver, delegation, or voluntary
commitment is invalid."

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Exercise restriction** | A person agrees or is required not to exercise a particular capability under specified conditions |
| **Delegation of authority** | A person or community grants another agent limited power to make or enforce decisions |
| **Alienation of standing** | A person purportedly loses the underlying normative status from which legitimate claims against power arise |

The first two are ordinary; the third is what inalienability rules out.
The reason, after Locke's Second Treatise §§22–23 and 135 as A85 relays
it, is that "a person cannot convey to another an unlimited arbitrary
power that the person did not legitimately possess in the first place."

A85 §4 stated the limit as an axiom on all agents, ∀a,
¬Authorized(a, ExtinguishStanding(i, r)), and as a Hohfeldian immunity.
A87 §4 corrects this: "A Hohfeldian immunity means that **a specified
other party lacks a particular normative power**", while an unwaivable
right raises another question: "does B itself possess the authority to
surrender, transfer, or extinguish the right?" With P_N(r) the valid
operations on a right r under a standard N, Waive, Transfer and Extinguish
"need not all belong to" it. The claim is filed in that form: the limit
falls first on the holder's own power to confer, and the immunity against
others follows, since what the holder cannot give no recipient can have
been given.

A97 IX puts it most compactly: "**Inalienability is therefore a claim
about the limits of certain normative transformations, not simply a
demand for a permanently fixed outcome.**"

The contract consequence, A85 §4: "the right to contract cannot
automatically include the unrestricted right to contract oneself into
domination". A marks this "a proposed moral argument, not a claim that
every broad waiver or arbitration agreement is legally invalid".

## How it sits with the record

Nothing on the record mentions inalienability, Hohfeld's incidents or
waiver. The nearest entries:

- **[CASE-128](../cases.d/CASE-128.md)** (Active): a person gives a friend the password and is
  later refused it, against the same restriction imposed. It shows a
  delegation can be the person's own and hard to revoke. This claim
  marks where such self-binding stops: it can restrict exercise, and it
  cannot transfer the standing to object to the arrangement itself.
- **[QUESTION-454](../questions.d/QUESTION-454.md)** (Open): what makes authority over another's body
  legitimate when the other cannot decide. This claim limits what any
  answer can license.
- **[TERM-083](../terms.d/TERM-083.md)** (Proposed): moral standing. "Standing" here is narrower:
  standing to make claims against an arrangement that governs one, which
  presupposes moral standing.
- **[TERM-124](../terms.d/TERM-124.md)** (Proposed): domination as an unanswerable capacity to set
  another's terms. An arrangement that purports to own the delegator's
  standing is that capacity made total by consent; this claim says the
  consent cannot make it valid.

[CASE-286](../cases.d/CASE-286.md) is A85 §11's test of the claim: two informed, voluntary
delegations, one limited and one purporting to be unlimited.

## What it does not say

- Not which rights are inalienable, nor how to justify them; that is
  [QUESTION-486](../questions.d/QUESTION-486.md). A85 §12: "We still need to justify the rights
  themselves, distinguish genuinely inalienable standing from rights that
  may legitimately be waived or restricted".
- Not that inalienable means absolute. A87 §4 separates inalienable,
  nonforfeitable and absolute: "a right can be inalienable yet permit a
  justified, temporary restriction in an emergency."
- Not a legal finding. Calabresi and Melamed's inalienability rules (1972)
  are the law-and-economics counterpart, which A does not cite; the
  record has not read them.
- Not that a collective may override it. A85 §8: "A majority's legitimate
  authority to reform government cannot by itself justify extinguishing
  the underlying rights of a minority" ([QUESTION-481](../questions.d/QUESTION-481.md)).

## Note of 2026-11-17: Calabresi and Melamed, read
<!-- inactive-ok-file: THEORY-1962 — new reading and theory of 2026-11-18, Proposed -->

Calabresi & Melamed 1972, read ([NOTE-2070](../notes.d/NOTE-2070.md), [THEORY-1962](../theory.d/THEORY-1962.md)), the source of the exchange's "inalienability rules": it qualifies the claim and does not support its grounding. The paper's inalienability is a limit on the holder's power to transfer, "An entitlement is inalienable to the extent that its transfer is not permitted between a willing buyer and a willing seller" (p. 1092), rules that "may also be viewed as limiting or regulating the grant of the entitlement itself" (p. 1093). That is close to the claim's "the holder lacks the power to confer it". But the paper justifies inalienability instrumentally, by externalities ("moralisms"), self-paternalism ("Ulysses tying himself to the mast", p. 1113), paternalism and distribution, not by the holder's standing; it allows partial inalienability, "prescribing preconditions for a valid sale or forbidding a sale altogether" (p. 1111); and it leaves compensation in place when the entitlement is taken: inalienability does not mean "there will be no compensation to the holder of the entitlement if it is taken from him" (n. 44). So a reader who takes the claim's "inalienability" from this paper gets a rule about transfer chosen for policy reasons, not a limit grounded in standing. The THEORY carries no relation to the claim, since this is neither independent support nor a limit the claim's text cites. No status changes.

## Note of 2026-11-17: Hohfeld, read
<!-- inactive-ok-file: THEORY-1984 — new reading and theory of 2026-11-18, Proposed -->

Hohfeld 1913, read ([NOTE-2071](../notes.d/NOTE-2071.md), [THEORY-1984](../theory.d/THEORY-1984.md)): it bears out the correction the claim is filed in. A87 §4 says an immunity is a limit on another's power and nonwaivability a limit on the holder's; Hohfeld: "a power is one's affirmative 'control' over a given legal relation as against another; whereas an immunity is one's freedom from the legal power or 'control' of another" (p. 55). The holder's inability to transfer would in his scheme be a disability of the holder, a relation he names (the opposite of power) but does not apply to inalienable rights; he notes that "the power of alienation is frequently one of the fundamental elements of a complex legal interest" (n. 67). So "the holder lacks the power to confer it" is expressible in Hohfeld's terms as a disability, but its grounding is not his. No relation is written on the THEORY. No status changes.

## Note of 2026-11-17: Locke, skimmed
<!-- inactive-ok-file: NOTE-2077 THEORY-1959 — new skim and theory of 2026-11-18, Skimmed or Proposed -->

Locke, Second Treatise, skimmed on §§4, 6, 22–27, 95, 135–136, 149, 168, 222 and 240 ([NOTE-2077](../notes.d/NOTE-2077.md), [THEORY-1959](../theory.d/THEORY-1959.md)). The claim is filed "after Locke's Second Treatise §§22–23 and 135". The form is Locke's, which the THEORY's corroborates carries: "a man, not having the power of his own life, cannot, by compact, or his own consent, enslave himself to any one ... No body can give more power than he has himself" (§23); "no body can transfer to another more power than he has in himself; and no body has an absolute arbitrary power over himself" (§135); and the people's judgment of breach is one "they cannot part with" (§168). Two qualifications. (1) Locke's ground is not the holder's standing in general but the absence of power over one's own life, persons being God's "property, whose workmanship they are" (§6); what cannot be conferred is arbitrary power over life, and liberty from it as preservation's safeguard. (2) Locke admits forfeiture: one "having by his fault forfeited his own life, by some act that deserves death" may be held by the one he forfeited it to (§23). The claim's list of what cannot remove standing (delegation, contract, collective decision, success in imposing an arrangement) does not include wrongdoing, and its defeat condition does not address forfeiture; it should say whether standing can be forfeited, or that it departs from Locke here. Since the claim names Locke as its source and the reading narrows what Locke carries for it, the lead may want an ARG from [THEORY-1959](../theory.d/THEORY-1959.md) to the claim (form: argument from a source's authority or from analogy), answering whether Locke's life-based ground extends to standing. No status changes.
