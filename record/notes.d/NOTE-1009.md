# NOTE-1009: Rohde, Di Luca & Ernst — Feeling of ownership and proprioceptive drift do not go hand in hand

## Contribution

Before this study, proprioceptive drift was the standard objective
measure of the rubber hand illusion, on the assumption that synchronous
stroking produces both the feeling of ownership and the drift. The
study adds the two conditions that test the assumption: seeing the
rubber hand with no stroking, and asynchronous stroking broken up by
frequent measurement. Drift appears in both, at the level of the
synchronous condition, without ownership. What had looked like
synchrony producing drift is, on this evidence, prolonged asynchrony
suppressing it.

## Key insight

The usual control condition is not neutral. Seeing a hand near where
one's own hand is felt pulls felt position toward it whatever the
touch is doing; minutes of mismatched touch tell the system that the
seen hand and the felt hand are not one thing, and the pull stops.
Comparing synchronous with asynchronous stroking therefore shows a
difference in drift and a difference in ownership, and makes them look
like one effect when they come from different processes.

## Assumptions

- **A visual probe measures felt position.** Experiment 1 used a
  two-alternative forced choice (dot left or right of the unseen index
  finger) with two interleaved staircases; Experiment 2 had participants
  adjust a dot to the felt finger position, three adjustments per
  measurement (pp. 2–3). Drift is the change from a pre-test in the dark.
  The authors grant that the result may not generalise "to other
  techniques to measure perceived finger location, like inter-manual
  reaches" (p. 6).
- **The questionnaire measures ownership.** Botvinick and Cohen's nine
  items with a German translation, given once at the end of Experiment 1
  (p. 2). In Experiment 2 "No questionnaire ratings were collected", to
  avoid biasing the repeated blocks (p. 3).
- **Robot stroking gives the illusion.** Two PHANToM force-feedback arms
  with paintbrush tips stroked the hands along random, morphed paths
  (pp. 2–3). That this produces a "striking and intense ownership
  illusion" rests on piloting with "Experienced subjects" (p. 2); the
  Experiment 1 questionnaire is the check.
- **Debriefing reports stand in for ownership in Experiment 2.**
  "Anecdotally, the vast majority of participants reported a strong
  feeling of ownership in the synchronous condition, but not in the
  asynchronous condition or vision only condition" (p. 6). That vision
  alone gives no ownership is also supported by citation (Longo et al.
  2008, on visual enhancement of touch, ref. 13).

## Key results

- **Set-up (pp. 2–3).** A left rubber hand 17 cm to the right of the
  hidden real left hand, seen through a semi-silvered mirror; switching
  the light turned the mirror opaque and showed a probe dot instead,
  so that during measurement "The dot was the only thing visible in the
  dark room".
- **Experiment 1 (n = 20, two groups of 10, between subjects; p. 4,
  Fig. 2).** Stroking for 7 min, measurement every 10 s. Questionnaire:
  only Q1–Q3 were positive, and only in the synchronous group (sign
  tests; Q1 and Q3 p = 0.002, p = 0.018 after Bonferroni; Q2 p = 0.022,
  0.195 corrected). Drift from the last 2 min, fitted with a cumulative
  Gaussian: 6.50 ± 2.3 cm synchronous (t(9) = 2.7, p = 0.013, one-tailed)
  and 3.93 ± 1.88 cm asynchronous (t(9) = 2.0, p = 0.039); the difference
  "was not significant (two-sample t-test, t(18) = 0.8, p = 0.425)".
- **Experiment 2 (n = 30, three groups of 10, synchronous and
  asynchronous within subjects, counterbalanced; pp. 3–5, Fig. 3).** 2 min
  of stroking, felt position measured once at the end (1×120), three
  times (3×40) or twelve times (12×10). Synchronous drift reached about
  5–6 cm in every group and "gradually built up over time". Asynchronous
  drift was 0.98 ± 0.69 cm in 1×120 (against 5.9 ± 1.59 synchronous),
  2.75 ± 1.31 cm in 3×40 (against 5.84 ± 1.38) and 5.45 ± 1.48 cm in
  12×10 (against 4.81 ± 1.44). Group × stroking interaction
  F(2,27) = 3.9, p = 0.031; paired tests significant in 1×120 and 3×40,
  not in 12×10 (t(9) = 0.6, p = 0.592). (The text lists the pairs in
  different orders; Fig. 4 shows that in 12×10 the asynchronous mean is
  the larger.)
- **Vision only (p. 5, Fig. 4).** Third block, no stroking, in the 1×120
  and 12×10 groups: 6.47 ± 1.10 cm and 3.09 ± 1.46 cm. In 1×120 it
  differed from asynchronous (t(9) = 4.8, p < 0.001) and not from
  synchronous (t(9) = 0.3, p = 0.76). Synchronous and vision-only drifts
  correlated within participants, r = 0.49, p = 0.030, pooled over 20.
  Mixed ANOVA group × mode, interaction F(2,36) = 7.8, p = 0.002; no main
  effect of mode (p = 0.109).
- **Not response bias (p. 6).** Dots started at random positions centred
  on the real finger, so any bias from the procedure would pull toward
  the real hand, not the rubber one.
- **The account (pp. 5–6).** Asynchrony is evidence against the "unity
  assumption", "the assumption that the proprioceptive and the visual
  sensations belong together", which visuo-proprioceptive integration
  needs; frequent interruption resets the accumulation of that evidence.
  The gradual rise in drift may come from proprioception weakening while
  the hand is still. "The sensation of owning a rubber hand does not
  cause any automatic update of the perceived position of the body in
  space" (p. 6). They leave open that "visuotactile integration may be a
  pre-requisite of the feeling of ownership" and that drift may be a
  prerequisite for ownership (pp. 6–7).
- **Against drift as a proxy (p. 7).** The correlation between drift and
  ownership seen elsewhere could have "A more remote common cause", for
  instance participants who are more "visual". "If it was admissible to
  assess the feeling of ownership by proxy of the proprioceptive drift,
  we could conclude from our results that body ownership is modulated by
  the frequency of measurement in asynchronous stroking", which the
  questionnaire shows is false.
- **On other measures (p. 7).** The same caution covers skin conductance,
  temperature and neural correlates: "a correlation is only meaningful in
  so far as it can be argued to be causally linked to the phenomenon in
  question". Indirect measures are attractive because they would bypass
  "suggestion, variability due to beliefs and top-down influences", and
  "The results presented in this paper demonstrate the dangers of such an
  endeavor."

## Concepts

- **Proprioceptive drift** — the change in felt position of the hidden
  hand toward the rubber hand, here measured by a visual probe rather
  than by reaching.
- **Visual capture of proprioception** — seeing a hand displaced from
  one's own biases felt hand position toward it, with or without
  ownership (refs. 4–7, Holmes et al.).
- **Unity assumption** — Welch and Warren's (1980) premise that signals
  in two senses are taken to come from one object, which integration
  between them requires.
- **Behavioural proxy** — Tsakiris's (2010) phrase for using drift to
  measure ownership: "proprioceptive drifts can be used as a behavioural
  proxy" (quoted p. 2).

## Connections

The study answers Botvinick and Cohen's drift experiment and the models
built on it: Makin, Holmes and Ehrsson's peripersonal-space model, in
which synchrony adds positive feedback to visual weighting, and
Tsakiris's comparator model. Both, the authors say, can be saved only by
giving asynchrony a negative, suppressing role (p. 6). It draws on
Holmes and colleagues' earlier warning that "reaching or proprioceptive
biases are not reliable objective measures of the rubber hand illusion
itself" (p. 7). Abdulkarim and Ehrsson 2016 ([LIT-1268](../literature.d/LIT-1268.md), [NOTE-992](NOTE-992.md))
takes up its open possibility, that drift is a prerequisite of
ownership, and tests it.

## Bearing on the record

- **[NOTE-1006](NOTE-1006.md) and [LIT-1277](../literature.d/LIT-1277.md) (Botvinick & Cohen 1998).** That note
  asks "Is the drift a measure of ownership or only of
  visuo-proprioceptive recalibration?" This study's answer, for drift
  measured by a visual probe, is recalibration: drift of the same size
  occurs without stroking and without ownership. Botvinick and Cohen's
  correlation between reported duration and drift is not contradicted,
  but it no longer shows that ownership moves felt position; a common
  cause, such as how strongly a person weights vision, would give it
  too. The 1998 asynchronous group's drift away from the rubber hand
  (13 mm) fits this account, in which prolonged asynchrony is the
  active ingredient; that reading is mine.
- **[THEORY-765](../theory.d/THEORY-765.md) (Proposed).** Its second refutation condition is
  that "drift toward the rubber hand appears as strongly under
  asynchrony, so that drift tracks visual exposure, not the illusion".
  In Experiment 2's 12×10 group and in Experiment 1 it did, and the
  vision-only condition shows drift tracking visual exposure. So the
  study **meets that refutation condition for the drift clause** of the
  THEORY, with three caveats: groups of ten, a visual probe rather than
  reaching, and the classic difference is reproduced when asynchrony is
  continuous (1×120), so the THEORY's sentence "a small asynchrony
  removes these effects" stays literally true of drift while its causal
  reading, that synchronous brushing shifts felt position, does not. It
  does not touch the first refutation condition or the suggestion
  control: the questionnaire was Botvinick and Cohen's, and expectation
  was neither measured nor matched. It partly meets the first half of
  `promote_when` (sample sizes, named tests and effect sizes for both
  measures; effect sizes only as means and s.e.m.). My proposal, not
  made here: narrow the THEORY to referred touch and ownership, and
  restate the drift as visuo-proprioceptive recalibration that prolonged
  asynchrony suppresses, citing this reading and Abdulkarim & Ehrsson.
- **[THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md) (Proposed).** Its `promote_when` asks for a
  common-cause model that predicts "ownership and drift in held-out"
  conditions. On this evidence drift is not a readout of ownership, so a
  model that fits drift would not thereby fit ownership. The result is
  not against a causal-inference account as such: the authors' "unity
  assumption" is itself a common-cause judgement, but they place it
  between vision and proprioception, separately from the visuotactile
  judgement that gives ownership. A single graded belief that drives both
  measures is what the dissociation bears against. It does not test
  gradedness or the loss of ownership by the real hand. Proposal: the
  promotion test should treat drift as a separate output to be modelled,
  not as a second measure of ownership.
- **[THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md) (Proposed).** Its `promote_when` asks that a
  heartbeat-locked cue increase ownership "on questionnaire and on
  drift". Drift cannot carry that weight: an increase in drift could
  come from changed visual weighting without any change in ownership.
  Proposal: drop drift from that test or require that it come with the
  questionnaire. Nothing here bears on interoception itself.
- **[THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) (Proposed).** Not tested: nothing whole-body was
  measured. By analogy, which is mine and not the paper's, it raises a
  question for the full-body evidence the THEORY leans on. Lenggenhager
  et al.'s walking-back drift ([NOTE-999](NOTE-999.md)) is treated as felt
  self-location; if hand drift can be visual capture without ownership,
  whole-body drift may be too, and in that study the object gave drift
  without self-identification. The THEORY's first promotion test also
  groups "hand ownership and drift" as if they moved together; this
  study says they need not.
- **[THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md) (Proposed).** No bearing: the hand is passive and
  agency is not at issue.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** A clean case of what a behavioural correlate of
  report can and cannot establish. Drift correlated with ownership
  reports across studies, and it was adopted as an objective measure
  that would sidestep suggestion. When conditions were added that pulled
  the two apart, the correlate proved to measure something else. The
  authors' rule, that a correlate counts as a measure only so far as the
  same mechanism can be argued to produce both, is a usable answer to
  part of the question.
- **[NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md), claim C6.** The manipulation of ownership by
  multisensory conflict stands on the questionnaire, which replicated;
  it gets no support from drift.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **Small groups.** Ten per group; standard errors of 1–2 cm on drifts
  of 1–6 cm.
- **Ownership in Experiment 2 is anecdotal.** The vision-only and
  interrupted-asynchrony conditions, which carry the dissociation, have
  no questionnaire; the claim that they gave no ownership rests on
  debriefing and on a citation. Experiment 1 gives the questionnaire
  dissociation only for interrupted asynchrony against synchrony.
- **The vision-only block was always third,** so order is confounded
  with it, as the authors note (p. 6).
- **A visual probe, not reaching.** Felt position was judged against a
  projected dot; whether the result holds for intermanual reaching, the
  1998 measure, is left open by the authors.
- **Robot stroking with random paths,** with a 500 ms pause between
  strokes in Experiment 2, differs from manual brushing. The
  questionnaire in Experiment 1 replicates the illusion, but the
  stimulus is not the classic one.
- **No suggestion control.** As in the 1998 study, the questionnaire's
  predicted items are guessable from the set-up.
- **A slip.** The text says "The main effect of stroking was due to the
  12×10 Group and the 3×40 Group, but not the 12×10 Group" (p. 5); from
  the t-tests that follow, the first should be the 1×120 group.

## Open questions

- Does the dissociation hold for intermanual reaching, and with a
  questionnaire in every condition?
- If drift and ownership are separate, what explains their correlation
  across people? The authors' "more visual" participants is a testable
  common cause.
- Does continuous asynchrony suppress drift by breaking the unity
  assumption, or by some other route, for instance by drawing attention
  to the real hand?
- Is the walking-back drift in full-body illusions also visual capture
  without identification?
