# CASE-tmpnuxjp: The reception of Deborah Smith's The Vegetarian: the Booker judges' task, the Korean critics' task, and one critic's two verdicts

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for: a documented reception
split over one translation, where each side's implicit task can be read
off what it praises and faults, for [CLAIM-tmpnttsk](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpnttsk.md), whose model of a
holistic judgement as a population's default weighting had only a
constructed example.

## The case

Han Kang's 채식주의자 (Changbi, 2007) appeared in Deborah Smith's English as
*The Vegetarian* (Portobello, 2015; Hogarth, 2016) and won the 2016 Man
Booker International Prize. The chair's citation, as reported by Yonhap in
the *Korea Times*, 17 May 2016
(https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/lifestyle/books/20160517/korean-novel-the-vegetarian-wins-man-booker-international-prize),
praised "Deborah Smith's perfectly judged translation". In Korea the
reception went the other way.

**The Korean side, and what only bilinguals see.** Charse Yun, "How the
bestseller 'The Vegetarian,' translated from Han Kang's original, caused
an uproar in South Korea", *Los Angeles Times*, 22 Sep 2017,
https://www.latimes.com/books/jacketcopy/la-ca-jc-korean-translation-20170922-story.html:
"Korean-language media began to report allegations of numerous errors,
omissions and embellishments." Some errors are invisible in English: "Most
English readers will simply glide over them unaware. Few will know, for
example, that Smith confuses 'arm' (pal) for 'foot' (bal)".

**The features both sides perceive.** Yun: "Smith amplifies Han's spare,
quiet style and embellishes it with adverbs, superlatives and other
emphatic word choices that are nowhere in the original", "on virtually
every other page"; the embellishments "create more suspense and interest
for the English reader, but for those who can read the original, it can be
quite jarring."

**One perceiver, two verdicts.** Yun on his first reading: "Here, finally,
was a Korean book that worked spectacularly in English." Then: "I looked
at Han's original text. I was astonished for a second time, but in a much
more sobering way." He ends: "For me, I can still admire the translation.
I just have to keep one eye closed."

**The two tasks, stated.** Deborah Smith, "What We Talk About When We Talk
About Translation", *Los Angeles Review of Books*, 11 Jan 2018,
https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/what-we-talk-about-when-we-talk-about-translation/.
She quotes Daniel Hahn, a judge in 2017, on the prize's judges: they
"aren't comparing the original and the translation and evaluating the
process (the decisions, the ingenuities, the slips…)", but are attempting
"to evaluate the finished English-language work on their own terms." Smith:
"This is one way of assessing translation quality; it is not the only
one." On Korea: "Partly because assessing literary translations in South
Korea does usually involve comparison, some people couldn't understand why
those 'slips' that Hahn mentions hadn't disqualified my translation from
praise and awards." The same feature takes opposite valence: reviewers
called the English "subtle", "precise and spare" and "bone-spare", while
"some people believe that my translation 'overly poeticized' an original
that was spare and understated". Her own weighting: "Quality is yet
another thing translators can choose to be faithful to", and "some of what
was filed under 'mistake' was actually just difference."

**An Anglophone dissenter on the English alone.** Tim Parks, "Raw and
Cooked", *NYR Daily*, 20 Jun 2016,
https://www.nybooks.com/online/2016/06/20/raw-and-cooked-translation-why-the-vegetarian-wins/:
"None of the judges for the prize appear to know Korean. Nor do I." His
task: "I must consider the relationship between content and style in the
English translation." On "Blood ribboned out of her wrist": "The fancy
metaphor of blood 'ribboning' seems totally out of line with our
narrator's expressive abilities." Verdict: "the translation frequently in
trouble with register and idiom." Yun credits him with detecting, "without
knowing a single word of Korean", the dissonance Yun traced to the source.

**The Korean critics' task, as scholarship states it.** Sun Kyoung Yoon,
"Fidelity or infidelity? The mistranslation controversy over *The
Vegetarian*", *Target* 35(2):242–261, 2022, doi:10.1075/target.21001.yoo,
finds the Korean debates "dominated by a preoccupation with fidelity and
literal translation", "regarding accuracy or fidelity as the sole criterion
for a good translation", and traces this to "three levels of political
anxiety" (abstract only).

## What it can show

That real populations bring different default tasks to "how good is this
translation?", and that the tasks can be read off what each side praises
and faults: here they are stated outright, by a judge for one side and by
a translation scholar for the other. The components the profile model
names ([TERM-043](../terms.d/TERM-043.md)) are the ones the parties argue over: lexical accuracy,
register, emphasis, the English style itself. Yun's double verdict is the
clearest documented instance of [CLAIM-tmpnttsk](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpnttsk.md)'s central move: one
perceiver, one rendering, one set of perceived differences, two judgements
under two tasks. That is what [CLAIM-128](../claims.d/CLAIM-128.md) allows and the claim's reply to
[CLAIM-tmpqfgjl](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpqfgjl.md) needs: a holistic judgement that is fidelity under a
default task, and a different task giving a different one.

## What it cannot show

- **Condition (c) is met only in part.** The split is a difference of
  weights only for components both sides perceive: register, emphasis,
  the embellishing adverbs, which Yun, Parks and the English reviewers all
  hear. For other components it is a difference of access. Lexical errors
  such as pal for bal are invisible to readers without Korean ("glide over
  them unaware"). For those, the Anglophone weight is zero by blindness,
  not by choice, and the claim's model, which weights components the
  judges perceive, does not cover them.
- **No weights can be estimated.** The case is qualitative. There are no
  ratings, so no weighting can be fitted on one set of renderings and
  tested on another. It illustrates the claim's model and does not test
  its defeat condition.
- **The strongest reply.** A defender of [CLAIM-tmpqfgjl](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpqfgjl.md) would say the split
  is not two weightings of one profile but two different objects of
  judgement: the judges rate an English novel, the Korean critics rate a
  relation between two texts. Hahn's "on their own terms" supports that
  reading. On it, the judges' "fidelity" is not a fidelity judgement at
  all. The claim can answer that a weighting which puts zero on every
  comparative component is still a weighting, but the case cannot decide
  between the readings. Yoon's "political anxiety" adds a further
  determinant of the Korean weights beyond the task.
- **What would close the gap.** A study in the shape of the discriminating
  study ([CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md)). Take passages of the novel with the source and
  Smith's English, and code each divergence by component, separating
  those a monolingual reader can perceive (register, emphasis) from those
  only a bilingual can (lexical errors). Have bilingual Korean readers and
  monolingual English readers rate fidelity, and also rate each component
  where they can. Fit each population's weights on half the passages and
  test on the rest. The claim predicts that holistic ratings are predicted
  by the fitted weights better than by the best single component, within
  each population, and that the populations' weights differ on the
  components both can perceive.
- **Smaller limits.** Han Kang's approval of the translation is reported by
  Smith, not checked first-hand. The Korean source was not inspected here;
  the divergences are as Yun, Parks and the Korean critics report them.
