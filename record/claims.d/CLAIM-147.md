# CLAIM-147: A trope is a family of observational profiles joined by admissible transformations around a core of preserved relations, so trope instances can separate structural preservation from pragmatic preservation

## The claim

A155 closed on a thesis: "**A trope is a candidate communicative invariant:
a recognizable relational pattern that persists across many realizations,
while its surface content and even its rhetorical function may change.**"
And refined it: "Rather than treating a trope as something that must be
perfectly invariant, we can characterize it as a **family of observational
profiles connected by admissible transformations**, with a distinguishable
core of preserved relations."

So a trope is not one profile but a family of them ([CASE-038](../cases.d/CASE-038.md)). The
admissible transformations are A155's vocabulary: recasting, inversion,
subversion, deconstruction and parody. What makes them admissible is that
they keep the core relations apart from the rest ([CLAIM-077](CLAIM-077.md)), and
transformations and the invariants they keep define each other
([CLAIM-113](CLAIM-113.md)). Because parody keeps the structure and changes the
function, a trope's instances can pull apart the two kinds of preservation
that a translation runs together: of a relational pattern, and of what the
pattern is for. A178's Study B: "Distinguish preservation of the trope's
recognizable relational pattern from preservation of its rhetorical or
pragmatic function." A191 §8: "These interventions separate narrative
structure from narrative function."

The test A155 §5 proposes: "The central test is whether D_trope(W, W′)
remains low even if lexical, visual, or surface-narrative similarity
becomes small." For each source example it generates a surface-altered
version, a relation-altered version, an explicit subversion and a
cross-modal realization, and compares an embedding baseline with transport
measures "on held-out trope families". "The crucial result would be whether
the latter distinguish *same trope, different surface* from *same surface,
different trope*." The distortion is read component by component
([TERM-043](../terms.d/TERM-043.md)): structural preservation in one component, pragmatic in
another. The parody case is the Villon–Henley one ([CASE-010](../cases.d/CASE-010.md)).

It answers [QUESTION-002](../questions.d/QUESTION-002.md) in part, for one kind of material: what survives a
recast is the core of relations, and whether the function survives is a
separate question.

## What it does not say

- It does not say a trope has a definition. A155 §1: "This is not yet a
  formal definition of the trope".
- It does not say a trope classifier would test the theory. A155 §3: "The
  simplest experiment would train a classifier ... But that would miss much
  of what makes this case attractive."
- It does not say the analysis is new in kind. Propp's functions
  ([THEORY-167](../theory.d/THEORY-167.md): "differ in who performs the functions and how") and
  Lévi-Strauss's transformations ([CLAIM-019](CLAIM-019.md)) are the same move on
  folktales and myths. A155 names neither.
- It does not say the catalogue's labels can validate it. A trope's
  description is written to fit its examples, so agreement with the labels
  is editorial agreement ([CLAIM-145](CLAIM-145.md), [QUESTION-005](../questions.d/QUESTION-005.md)). A155 §5's own
  remedy, "a small, independently validated set of tropes" and evaluation
  "on held-out trope families", would meet that objection if it were
  carried out.
