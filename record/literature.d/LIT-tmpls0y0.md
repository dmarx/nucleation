# LIT-tmpls0y0: On Organizational Becoming: Rethinking Organizational Change

Tsoukas, H., & Chia, R. (2002). On organizational becoming: Rethinking organizational change. *Organization Science*, 13(5), 567–582. doi:10.1287/orsc.13.5.567.7810.

Read in full in [NOTE-tmpyz5d5](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpyz5d5.md).

## Key takeaways

- Synoptic, stage-model accounts of change treat it as a series of
  states, and so miss change itself, which "goes on between them" (James,
  quoted p. 571). Synoptic and "performative" accounts are both needed
  (p. 572).
- Organizing generalizes action into institutionalized categories. Those
  categories are radially structured, with a stable prototypical core and
  an unstable periphery. Applying them to nonprototypical cases extends
  and so changes them (pp. 573–574).
- Humans also change their categories reflexively, by interacting with
  their own descriptions (pp. 574–575).
- So "organization aims at stemming change but, in the process of doing
  so, it is generated by it" (p. 577). Inertia is the failure of local
  change to be institutionalized, not the absence of change (p. 579).

## Why this is here

Bencherki, Brummans & Vézy 2024 ([LIT-tmp3mudg](LIT-tmp3mudg.md)) cite it twice. On p. 1349
it is named for drawing on Bergson and James. On p. 1351 the paper says
its theory "parallels existing conceptions of organizational becoming
(see Tsoukas & Chia, 2002), yet explicates the role agency plays in this
process". On p. 1365 it is the example of process research that stresses
that "organizational becoming is an ongoing process" while it "focuses
less on moments when individuation is interrupted, blocked, or halted".

On reading, the citations are fair but thin. The Bergson and James
attribution is accurate. "Parallels" understates a real difference.
Tsoukas and Chia's becoming is carried by human agents: "actors are
conceived as webs of beliefs and habits of action that keep reweaving"
(p. 575), driven by "the intrinsically human ability to be reflexive",
and organization emerges "through the ongoing agency of organizational
members" (p. 579). That is the agent-based starting point the parent
paper rejects. The parent does not say so. The p. 1365 remark is fair:
Tsoukas and Chia treat stalled change as failed institutionalization (p.
579), not as interrupted becoming.
