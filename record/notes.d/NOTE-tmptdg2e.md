# NOTE-tmptdg2e: Stern — Darwall on Second-Personal Ethics

## Contribution

A review article of Darwall's 2013 collections that first sketches the
2006 book's thesis, surveys the later essays (against Wallace's bipolar
view, against Raz on authority, on resentment, respect and relationships),
and objects: obligations to aid a sufferer look obligatory but not
second-personal.

## Key results

Darwall, as Stern reports him:

- (p. 2) Obligation "involves the exercise of legitimate authority over the
  person obliged"; it is "essentially second personal" in that it involves
  "this exercise of authority which makes one person accountable to another
  or others."
- (p. 2) The second-personal reason: "you are entitled to demand of me that
  I get off your foot".
- (p. 3) "whether or not an obligation obtains doesn’t depend on the actual
  demands an individual or a community of individuals might make: if they
  could legitimately make such demands, then an obligation holds".
- (p. 7) Against Raz: "meeting the standards of the normal justification
  thesis is not in fact sufficient to establish that one person has
  authority over another". Following a cooking expert helps you do what you
  have reason to do, "but this doesn’t give that expert any real authority
  over you, as he cannot insist that you comply with what he tells you to
  do, in a second-personal manner."
- (p. 9) Resentment aims "to hold a person responsible for failing to
  honour a legitimate claim"; giving another "second-person standing ...
  thereby makes you answerable to them".
- (p. 11) "respect consists in acknowledging the claims others can make of
  you, while dignity consists in the capacity to make such claims."

Stern's own:

- (p. 12) The triad: "(1) A person’s suffering does not give a
  second-personal reason to act (2) Obligations require second-personal
  reasons (3) The suffering of others can give us obligations".
- (p. 17) Darwall's ideal moral community risks being "a form of divine
  command in disguise".

## Assumptions

Stern assumes that a bystander's duty to aid is a genuine obligation, and
that wronging (bipolarity) can be had without a demand.

## Concepts

- **Second-personal reason**, **second-person standing / authority**.
- **Accountability** as answerability to those with standing to demand.
- **Normal justification thesis** (Raz); **exclusionary reasons**.
- **Honour respect** and **recognition respect**.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-tmpea19q](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpea19q.md)** (when is answerability owed, to whom, and why?):
  Darwall's answer is to those with second-personal standing, because
  obligation consists in legitimate demandability; Stern's triad is the
  case it leaves open, an obligation with no one placed to demand.
- **[CLAIM-tmpnb4v4](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpnb4v4.md)**: corroborates the clause that a demand's being
  asserted gives it no warrant: on Darwall's view obligation tracks the
  demands that "could legitimately" be made, not the "actual demands". It
  qualifies the rest: Darwall makes obligation itself consist in
  legitimate demandability, which the claim's separation of rights from
  demands does not presuppose, and Stern's objection is where the two
  part.
- **[CLAIM-tmpnhesz](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpnhesz.md)**: Darwall is one of the three candidate grounds the
  claim names as unread for why reasons are owed at all. His directed
  relation runs from the claimant's standing, which fits the claim's
  "need not presuppose the actor's legitimate authority"; the sketch does
  not reach the claim's several grounds.
- **[CLAIM-tmpc7shc](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpc7shc.md)**: legitimacy of a demand, not its content's being met,
  is what binds; consistent, not a test.
- **[TERM-tmp4zjar](../terms.d/TERM-tmp4zjar.md)**: Darwall's "answerable" (p. 9) is the directed sense
  the term uses.
- **The exchange's characterization** (A83): "the fact that deferring to
  someone would produce better results may not, by itself, establish that
  the person has the normative standing to command obedience" matches the
  argument against Raz (p. 7), but Stern places it in the 2013 essays, not
  the 2006 book. Raz's *The Morality of Freedom*, [LIT-tmpld793](../literature.d/LIT-tmpld793.md), is the
  work it answers.

## Connections

Stands in for Darwall (2006), [LIT-tmpr2bbq](../literature.d/LIT-tmpr2bbq.md), for the core thesis
(obligation as legitimate second-personal authority to demand; actual
demands not required; resentment as holding to a legitimate claim; respect
and dignity). It does not restate the book's arguments, and its detail on
authority and Raz comes from the later essays.

## Limitations

A critic's review of two later collections; second-hand for the 2006 book.
