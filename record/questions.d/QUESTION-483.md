# QUESTION-483: Does the record's organizational account only supplement contractualism, or does it show an allocation or transformation of normative powers that reasonable rejection cannot explain without importing principles of standing or authority?

## Why it is a question

The owner asked it in U44 of the exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b), 2026-11-17:

> I wonder if rather than Scanlon refining the theory we're developing, our theory might provide a response to Scanlon's approach?

A102 ("From Scanlonian Justification to the Organization of
Justifiability") took it up: "Yes. And I think this reversal may be more
theoretically productive than treating Scanlon as the foundation of our
project." A102 does not name the owner as the source of the reversal.

Scanlon's question is "What principles for governing conduct could no one
reasonably reject?" (A102). The record's is how relationships keep the
capacity to be governed by justified reasons when some parties hold power
over the conditions under which others' reasons can become effective. The
open part is how the two relate: whether the record's account adds
something contractualism lacks as a moral criterion, or only an account of
how its verdicts are realized.

## Candidate answers in the exchange

All three are A's.

1. **Supplement only: a claim about scope** (A102 §10, restated in A106
   §17). A distinguishes "**normative adequacy** from **explanatory and
   organizational adequacy**" and states the strongest defensible claim as:
   "A theory of justifiable principles remains incomplete as a theory of
   legitimate governance unless it is supplemented by an account of the
   capabilities, authority structures, and developmental processes through
   which those principles are made effective and remain open to
   appropriate correction." A marks its limit: "This is a claim about the
   explanatory scope of contractualism, not a demonstration that its moral
   criterion is false." Against it A states the Scanlonian objection at
   full strength, that "All of these organizational facts are simply among
   the facts that people have reasons to care about", and grants that it
   "has real force".
2. **Not a new rejection algorithm** (A106 §8). The record's
   demand-compatibility machinery should not recast reasonable rejection as
   a compatibility problem: reasons "need not share a natural cardinal
   scale", and a reason's "moral role depends on **who has the reason,
   what the reason concerns, and how the parties stand in relation to one
   another**". A reports Scanlon's later *Contractualism and Intuitionism*
   (a 2025 colloquium paper) as holding much the same. This answer says what the reply
   is not.
3. **The stronger answer: relevance depends on relationships under
   examination** (A106 §19). "Consequently, the normative relevance of a
   person's reason may sometimes depend on a relationship whose legitimacy
   is itself under examination." A corporation's appeal to a user
   agreement is the example: the agreement's force depends on "the
   authority to offer and accept the terms", so "We cannot simply infer the
   agreement's normative authority from the fact that the corporation
   regards it as valid." If reasonable rejection must take the relations
   that fix relevance as given, and those relations are what is in
   question, the record's account would do work the criterion cannot. A
   calls this "The strongest challenge to Scanlon"; it is a candidate, not
   a result.

## What would count as an answer

A's discriminating test (A102 §12 and A106 §20) is the condition for the
second alternative:

> A stronger challenge would require showing that some justified allocation or transformation of normative authority cannot be adequately explained through reasonable rejection without importing additional principles that our theory can explain independently.

and A102 §12: "If we find cases where the legitimate allocation and
reproduction of normative powers cannot be adequately captured by
reasonable rejection without importing independent principles of standing
or authority, the argument for a more substantive revision becomes
stronger." A says plainly: "We have not yet established that stronger
claim."

- **Supplement only:** every matched construction A102 §12 proposes
  (identical principles with different capacities to formulate objections;
  first-order authority fixed with control of review varied; two regimes,
  one eroding alternatives) is explained by principles persons could not
  reasonably reject, with no principle of standing or authority imported.
  A106 §20 notes that "a sophisticated contractualist can still recognize"
  the difference such a study shows, so a study of that kind supports
  this answer.
- **More than a supplement:** a case in which the legitimacy of an
  allocation or transformation of normative powers is fixed, and reasonable
  rejection gets it right only by presupposing who has standing or
  authority, which the record's account explains on its own.
- **Neither:** an argument that the two theories answer different
  questions, so that neither can supplement or correct the other.

## Note of 2026-11-17: Scanlon, Wenar and the SEP entry, read

Four readings bear on the question; each THEORY carries bears_on to it. Wenar 2013, read ([NOTE-2038](../notes.d/NOTE-2038.md), [THEORY-1988](../theory.d/THEORY-1988.md)), is a published form of the second option: "Rights of authority explain who can change what we owe to each other and how. "What we owe to each other" is a one-level concept; contractualism must be a multi-level theory" (p. 399), "a theory not only of owing to, but also a theory of authority over". Scanlon's reply, read ([NOTE-2075](../notes.d/NOTE-2075.md), [THEORY-1967](../theory.d/THEORY-1967.md)), answers it: "Contractualism seeks to explain not only first-order claim rights but also powers" (p. 403), the promisee's power of release among them, so an allocation of powers is not obviously beyond reasonable rejection; he concedes that which parts are called rights does not "matter very much" (pp. 403–404). Neither shows the second level for institutional powers. Scanlon's 2025 paper, read ([NOTE-2051](../notes.d/NOTE-2051.md), [THEORY-1977](../theory.d/THEORY-1977.md)), concedes that relevance is "broadly speaking a moral question" depending on "the relation we stand to one another and the decision that is at issue" (pp. 53–54), a place where relations do work in his own account. The SEP entry "Contractualism" ([LIT-2768](../literature.d/LIT-2768.md); [NOTE-2043](../notes.d/NOTE-2043.md), [THEORY-1971](../theory.d/THEORY-1971.md)), read as the neighbour of the unavailable book [LIT-2721](../literature.d/LIT-2721.md), gives the scope, "contractualism covers only the realm of what we owe to one another", and the circularity answer, that only what "happens to individuals" can be fed into the test: the inputs a "supplement only" reading relies on. No status changes.
