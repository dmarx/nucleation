# THEORY-638: Instantiating a formal program is never by itself sufficient for understanding or intentionality, since a person can carry out a program for answering questions in Chinese by hand without understanding Chinese; whatever thinks does so in virtue of causal powers at least equivalent to the brain's, and the reply that the whole system understands is answered only by an asserted internalisation

## Source

Searle (1980), *Minds, brains, and programs*, [LIT-400](../literature.d/LIT-400.md), read in full in
[NOTE-349](../notes.d/NOTE-349.md). Only the target article (BBS 3:417–424) was read, not the
commentaries or Searle's response.

## What was actually shown

**The room.** A man who knows no Chinese follows rules for manipulating
Chinese symbols and so returns answers to questions about Chinese stories.
He is "simply an instantiation of the computer program". "It
seems to me quite obvious in the example that I do not understand a word of
the Chinese stories." So the program is not sufficient for understanding,
and "not the slightest reason has been given to suppose" it is necessary or
contributory; Searle concedes "I have not demonstrated that this claim is
false".

**The replies.** Systems: let the man internalise rules and data; "there
isn't anything in the system that isn't in him", and a Chinese subsystem
would be "simply a part of the English subsystem". Robot: adding causal
links to the world concedes that formal manipulation is not enough, and the
man inside still understands nothing. Brain simulator: water pipes
simulating synapses do not understand. Combination: we would rationally
ascribe intentionality to a humanlike robot "as long as we knew nothing
more about it", and withdraw it on learning it runs a formal program. Other
minds: the question is what is attributed, not how it is known. Many
mansions: redefines AI and concedes the thesis.

**The positive view.** Intentionality is a product of causal features of
the brain, "an empirical fact". "We are precisely such machines"; an
artifact with a nervous system "sufficiently like ours" could think; but
nothing thinks "solely in virtue of being a computer with the right sort of
program". Simulation is not duplication: simulated fires do not burn.

**What could have come out otherwise.** Nothing empirical. The argument
turns on a thought experiment whose verdict is the man's report about
himself.

## What this does not say

- **That the systems reply is refuted.** The internalisation answer assumes
  that whatever the man runs in his head is a part of him whose
  understanding he could report. That is what the reply denies ([NOTE-349](../notes.d/NOTE-349.md),
  C3 and Limitations).
- **That machines cannot think.** Searle grants that they can, and that we
  are such machines. The claim is that no program suffices.
- **That a given system lacks understanding.** The conclusion is about
  sufficiency. Citing the room for "current models do not understand"
  outruns it ([NOTE-349](../notes.d/NOTE-349.md), on [NOTE-176](../notes.d/NOTE-176.md)).
- **Anything about consciousness.** "Consciousness, intentionality, and all
  the rest of it" is mentioned; the argument concerns intentionality and
  understanding.
- **Which causal powers matter.** Left "an empirical question", with no test
  offered.
- **That scale and speed are irrelevant.** The man's slowness and the size
  of the task are idealised away without argument.

## Connections

- **Rival: the intentional strategy ([THEORY-538](THEORY-538.md)).** Dennett makes a
  system a believer if the strategy predicts it reliably and voluminously,
  whatever runs it. Searle's combination reply says the ascription is
  withdrawn once the program is known. Both cannot be right about the same
  robot.
- **Turing ([THEORY-433](THEORY-433.md)).** Searle gives the other-minds reply "only a
  short reply", and calls the test "unashamedly behavioristic and
  operationalistic". Turing's paper does not claim understanding for a
  passing machine.
- **Implementation ([THEORY-609](THEORY-609.md)).** Chalmers answers the room by
  finding two computational structures, and so two minds, in it: the
  man's brain and the marks on paper. That answer presupposes computational
  sufficiency, which the implementation result itself does not establish.
- **Composition ([THEORY-043](THEORY-043.md)).** Searle refuses both the downward move to a
  mind of mindless parts and the upward move to a mind of minded parts, by
  causal powers rather than by anti-nesting or architecture; [THEORY-043](THEORY-043.md)
  names the position as outside its dispute.
- **Psychologism ([THEORY-092](THEORY-092.md)).** Block reaches a related verdict, that
  behaviour does not fix the mental, by a different route: how the
  responses are produced, not what they are made of.
- **Biological naturalism.** Seth ([LIT-135](../literature.d/LIT-135.md)) separates his view from
  Searle's later formulation; this paper is the earlier, causal-powers
  version, and it leaves open whether another chemistry would do.
