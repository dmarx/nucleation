# THEORY-461: If a substantial class of mental states, interoceptive, affective, perceptual and bodily-agentive, depends on an animate body, then today's text-only chatbots lack them and their self-ascriptions of such states are systematically untrue, and users can engage them without false belief by make-believe, as a new kind of interactive, self-involving and blended fictional character

## Source

Roberts (2026), *Talkative AI and the fiction of artificial minds*,
[LIT-207](../literature.d/LIT-207.md), read in full in [NOTE-157](../notes.d/NOTE-157.md) (Philosophical Studies).

## What was actually shown

**The conditional (§2.1).** If "there is a substantial class of mental
phenomena that bear a tight prima facie connection to features of the
thinker's body", and current chatbots lack the relevant bodily
characteristics, that class is "out of bounds" to them. It covers
interoception (thirst, nausea, proprioception), affect with somatic
expression, perception structured by exploratory action and an egocentric
frame, bodily plans and intentions, trying and effort, and the sense of
ownership. Chatbots would then have no episodic memories and "no
first-person empirical knowledge of their surroundings". The argument
avoids the usual semantic premises (no grounding, no meaning, no
assertion), and Roberts presents that as its advantage.

**The consequence (§3).** Chatbot utterances routinely assert or imply such
states, so under the antecedent they are "systematically untrue", and
chatbots are unreliable testifiers about themselves. Their frequency is
asserted from examples, not measured.

**The attitude (§§3–4).** Users may make-believe that the chatbot is an
autonomous agent. The fiction is strongly interactive, in that the user's
utterances make things fictionally true; co-constructed in two stages,
parameter-setting and dialogue; self-involving, since the character
attends to this user; and blended, since live data set it in our world now
and its advice can sway a real user (§4.1–4.3). Dennett's intentional
stance "does not go far enough": it assigns the beliefs a system ought to
have given its perceptual capacities, needs and biography, and a chatbot
has none, so those too must be imagined (§3).

**The upshots (§5).** Make-believe is not subject to the epistemic norms of
belief, so treating an AI as richer than the evidence warrants "need not
be rationally blamed". Fictionalism explains why users confide in AIs and
yet delete them without qualms. Emotions toward AI characters fall under
the paradox of fiction.

**What could have come out otherwise.** The conditional is valid, so only
its antecedent could fail. The claims about users could be false, and the
paper does not check them.

## What this does not say

- **That chatbots have no minds.** The argument aims only "to attenuate the
  range of mental states that may plausibly be attributed" (§2). It grants
  that AI utterances can be "perfectly meaningful", and footnote 8 allows a
  hybrid: realism about the AI as "a disembodied thinker", fictionalism
  about it as a feeling, emotional or agential one. Citing this account for
  a general denial overstates it ([NOTE-157](../notes.d/NOTE-157.md), Bearing on the record).
- **That embodied cognition is true.** It is the premise, at prima facie
  strength. Aizawa's cognitivist reply and Dung & Mogensen's case for
  disembodied AI emotion are acknowledged and not answered.
- **That users are fictionalists.** That is argued by analogy with theatre,
  games and novels, and footnote 10 concedes the worry that make-believe
  may be too close to belief to be told apart.
- **That users ought to take the fictional attitude.** The body argues it
  is permissible and apt; §3 says refraining from engaging is not
  irrational either. Only the Conclusion's "more plausible" is stronger.
- **Anything about robots or multimodal systems.** Embodied or simulated
  bodies are named as realist escape routes and set aside.

## Connections

- **Why fictionalism, and its limit.** Drayson's argument against mental
  fictionalism ([THEORY-646](THEORY-646.md)) targets a motive from skepticism about
  inner representations. Roberts's motive is different, embodiment, so it
  escapes her first argument. Her dilemma would apply to a chatbot
  fictionalist who also wants chatbot talk to track the real causes of
  output.
- **The machine's side.** Bottou & Schölkopf ([THEORY-547](THEORY-547.md)) describe the
  machine as a fiction machine; this account describes the user's
  attitude to it.
- **Felt affect.** [THEORY-045](THEORY-045.md) holds that felt affect is the experience of
  the body's regulatory state. On either of its readings a system with no
  body to regulate has no affect in that sense, which is the condition
  Roberts sets for affective states, and [THEORY-045](THEORY-045.md) says so. Its open case,
  a system that predicts and regulates a simulated or built body, is the
  realist escape route this paper sets aside.
- **Evidence from behaviour.** [THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md) holds that mimicry undercuts
  behavioural evidence for AI consciousness. Both make chatbot
  self-reports poor evidence about their states, for different reasons:
  there, how the reports were produced; here, what the reporter lacks.
- **The self of a role-player** ([THEORY-490](THEORY-490.md)) is Shanahan's account of
  what a chat agent's "I" picks out, a question this paper does not ask.
