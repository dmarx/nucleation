# THEORY-1627: Danieli, Norris and Engdahl report that, among 422 adult children of Holocaust survivors, parents' wartime internment was linked to the children's reported impacts only through the parents' post-war victim style, that the post-war family milieu weighed more than wartime history, and that broken generational linkages, among them rarely counting the murdered grandparents as one's own, had the largest total effect

## The account

The paper tests Danieli's framework, in which "the parent's fixity
becomes the child's biopsychosocial milieu" (p. 640). Adult children rated
their parents' post-war styles, their own "reparative adaptational
impacts" and their family's history. In the final path model (χ²(21) =
25.01, p = .247, CFI = .995), the parents' victim style (stuck in the loss,
overprotective, volatile) predicted the child's impacts (mothers .38,
fathers .34). Internment without hiding or escape reached the child only
through it, with indirect effects of .05 and .06. Family milieu explained
more of the parents' styles (11–25%) than wartime history (1–6%); settling
in Israel had a total effect of −.16. Numb style, which includes the
family's conspiracy of silence, had an effect only through mothers and was
dropped. Broken generational linkages, including "I rarely think of my
parent's murdered parents as my own grandparents", had the largest total
effect (.21) and the only direct one. Having two survivor parents had no
effect beyond each parent's victim style.

## Evidence

The paper ([NOTE-1307](../notes.d/NOTE-1307.md)): 422 respondents recruited on the web through
survivor and Jewish organizations, 72% daughters; style scales derived by
factor analysis of the same data; every measure reported by the child. The
direct path from broken linkages was added after the first model's
residual. The authors grant the effects are "necessary but not sufficient
for establishing causation" (p. 648). There is no comparison group.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-297](../questions.d/QUESTION-297.md), the parenting and identity routes. What passes is the
parent's way of living after the trauma, not the trauma itself, and
silence is not the main channel. The ancestor and the injury come apart:
counting the murdered grandparents as one's own went with fewer impacts,
while "obsession with the Holocaust" is scored as part of the impacts. The
question's phrase "an ancestor's injury becomes part of the descendant's
self" covers both.
