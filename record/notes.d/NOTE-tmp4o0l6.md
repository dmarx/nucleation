# NOTE-tmp4o0l6: A numerical algorithm for block-diagonal decomposition of matrix *-algebras with application to semidefinite programming

## Contribution

Earlier work exploiting symmetry in semidefinite programs (Gatermann–Parrilo, de Klerk et al., Kanno et al.) assumed a known group or *-algebra. The paper gives a purely numerical-linear-algebra method that finds the *finest* simultaneous orthogonal block-diagonalisation of given symmetric matrices, with no symmetry supplied. It therefore finds structure the obvious group misses. Such structure arises from sparsity, commutation (BE = EB), or shared eigenvectors, and would otherwise require identifying "a larger group … difficult to identify in practice" (§2.2).

## Key insight

In a *-algebra with simple components U_j, a *generic* element has eigenspaces that each lie inside one U_j (Prop. 3.2). Two eigenspaces belong to the same component iff the generators connect them, through the transitive closure of Q_iᵀA_pQ_{i′} ≠ O (Prop. 3.3). The projector onto each class is a polynomial in the generic element, so it lies in the algebra and commutes with everything (proof of Prop. 3.3). Within a component, all eigenspaces have the same multiplicity m̄_j. Local rotations P_i that make every inter-eigenspace block a scalar multiple of I then expose the m̄_j identical copies of the irreducible block (Prop. 3.5, eq. 4.5).

## Assumptions

- **The data.** A₁…A_m ∈ Sⁿ, real symmetric, and 𝒯 is the real *-algebra generated by {Iₙ, A₁…A_m}.
- **Genericity.** A(r) is generic: the blocks B_j of (3.1) have simple spectra, and no two blocks share an eigenvalue. This is guaranteed with probability 1 if span{I, A₁…A_m} = 𝒯 ∩ Sⁿ (Prop. 3.4). Otherwise it is guaranteed by first building a basis of 𝒯 ∩ Sⁿ from symmetrised products (Prop. 4.3).
- **Real type.** Implicitly, every irreducible component is of real type, i.e. equal to a full matrix algebra M_{n̄_j}(ℝ). This comes from Theorem 3.1(C); see corrections.
- **Exact arithmetic in the theory.** Numerical zero-tests are handled heuristically (Remark 4.2, and the scaling in Remark 5.1).

## Key results

- **Theorem 3.1 (cited).** (A) Q̂ᵀ𝒯Q̂ = {diag(S₁…S_ℓ): S_j ∈ 𝒯_j}, with 𝒯_j simple. (B) If 𝒯 is simple, Pᵀ𝒯P = {diag(B, …, B): B ∈ 𝒯′}, with 𝒯′ irreducible. (C) "If 𝒯 is irreducible, then 𝒯 = Mₙ", which is false over ℝ in general. Hence (3.1): PᵀAP = ⊕_j (I_{m̄_j} ⊗ B_j), and the subspaces U_j are unique, of dimension m̄_j n̄_j.
- **Proposition 3.2.** For generic A, each eigenspace V_i lies in some U_j, U_j = ⊕_{i∈K_j} V_i, m_i = m̄_j and |K_j| = n̄_j.
- **Proposition 3.3.** The partition K = K₁ ∪ … ∪ K_ℓ is the set of equivalence classes of the transitive closure of i ∼ i′ ⇔ ∃p: Q_iᵀA_pQ_{i′} ≠ O. It is proved in full, with E_j = f_j(A) a polynomial in A.
- **Proposition 3.4.** If span{I, A₁…A_m} = 𝒯 ∩ Sⁿ, then A(r) is generic for r in an open dense set, via resultants.
- **Proposition 3.5.** P can be taken as Q·diag(P₁…P_k)·Π, with local orthogonal P_i ∈ M_{m_i} and a permutation Π.
- **Algorithm 4.1 and Proposition 4.2.**
  - Step 1: random r with ‖r‖ = 1, A = Σ r_pA_p.
  - Step 2: eigen-decompose A.
  - Step 3: form the transitive closure of ∼, giving Q̂.
  - Correctness: this gives the simple components when A is generic.
- **The irreducible stage (§4.2).** Choose a spanning tree T_j of each component's graph. Set P_{i₁} = I, then P_{i′} = (Q_iᵀA_pQ_{i′})⁻¹P_i, normalised by the norm of its first row. This makes every block b_{ii′}^{(pj)}I_{m̄_j} (eq. 4.5), and a permutation then gives (4.1). Remark 4.1 gives a variant with a second random combination.
- **The practical variant (§4.3).** Skip the basis computation, and detect inconsistency: a singular or rectangular Q_iᵀA_pQ_{i′} on a tree edge, or a non-scalar block. On detection, restart with more elements of 𝒯 ∩ Sⁿ, e.g. (A₁A₂ + A₂A₁)/2. With probability 1 there are at most O(n²) restarts. There is "no theoretical guarantee" of finest decomposition if it stops early, though that is "very likely in practice". Example 4.3 shows a case (4×4) where the generators' span is too small, and adding A₃ = ½(A₁A₂ + A₂A₁) fixes it.
- **Relation to de Klerk–Sotirov (Remark 4.3).** Their "block diagonalization heuristic" also uses a random combination, but of the known group's representation matrices T(g). It is not designed to be finest.
- **The T_d cubic truss (§5.1), n_d = 24.**
  - Case 1 (34 members): 9 blocks, 1 + 2 + 3 + 3, matching the group-theoretic prediction. The sign representation's block is absent.
  - Case 2 (30 members, sparser): 12 blocks. Sparsity splits the last block beyond what T_d predicts.
  - In both cases random combinations of the given A_p sufficed, with no extra generators.
- **S₃ examples (§5.2).** Case 1 is generic S₃: B_{p1} ⊕ (I₂ ⊗ B_{p2}), with sizes 3 and 2. Case 2 (BE = EB): a further split into 3 ⊕ 2·1 ⊕ 2·1. Case 3 (C an eigenvector of B, E): the 3×3 block splits further. The method recovers each (Table 2).

## Method

Spectral decomposition of a random element; graph connectivity on eigenspaces; spanning-tree propagation of local orthogonal frames; restarts on detected inconsistency.

## Concepts

- **Matrix *-algebra over ℝ.** Contains I, and is closed under linear combination, product and transpose.
- **Simple component, irreducible component.** The subspaces U_j, and the n̄_j-dimensional blocks repeated m̄_j times.
- **Structural indices ℓ, n̄_j, m̄_j.** ℓ is the number of distinct irreducible types. m̄_j is the multiplicity, which the paper links to eigenvalue multiplicities m_i.
- **Generic in eigenvalue structure.**

## Connections

- **[LIT-309](../literature.d/LIT-309.md) and [LIT-331](../literature.d/LIT-331.md) (Wedderburn, Artin).** Theorem 3.1 is Wedderburn's structure theorem for semisimple (here, *-closed, hence semisimple) algebras, cited through Wedderburn's 1934 *Lectures on Matrices*. The simple components are the Wedderburn blocks, and the error in (C) is precisely forgetting that over ℝ the division algebra can be ℂ or ℍ.
- **[LIT-339](../literature.d/LIT-339.md) (WWW) and [LIT-321](../literature.d/LIT-321.md) (Haag).** The simple-component projectors E_j are central in 𝒯, since they commute with every element (proof of Prop. 3.3). They are the finite-dimensional analogue of superselection-sector projections: the centre Z(𝒯) = span{E_j}. The paper never uses physics language, but this is exactly the map's "types = Z(𝒜)".
- **[LIT-329](../literature.d/LIT-329.md) (Peter–Weyl).** Group-symmetric blocks with multiplicity m̄_j = irrep dimension (the T_d example: 2 and 3). Irrep multiplets appear as eigenvalue multiplicities of a generic symmetric element.
- **[THEORY-017](../theory.d/THEORY-017.md).** The decomposition is basis-free in the sense that the U_j are unique (p. 9). P itself is not unique. The canonical data are the central projectors, not a basis.

## Bearing on the record

- **Map row 6, "the numerical tool for discover semantic types by block-diagonalizing extracted concept operators".** The paper supplies such a tool, and its characterisation holds.
  - *What it provides.* Given a finite set of symmetric matrices (e.g. extracted concept operators), it recovers the *centre* of the algebra they generate, the simple-component projectors, with no prior symmetry. That is literally "types = Z(𝒜)" computed from data. It also recovers multiplicities (m̄_j), which are the map's "irrep multiplets = spectral degeneracies" (row 7).
- **Three cautions for the owner's use.**
  - *Real type.* With real symmetric operators, components of complex or quaternionic type break the §4.2 step. The companion Maehara–Murota paper is needed for the irreducible stage. The simple-component stage (the types) is unaffected: Props. 3.2–3.4 do not use (C).
  - *Exactness.* The theory is exact-arithmetic. Real extracted operators are noisy, and every test "Q_iᵀA_pQ_{i′} ≠ O" becomes a threshold. The paper addresses noise only by rescaling (Remarks 4.2, 5.1), and its examples are small (n ≤ 24) and near-exact. For noisy data, an approximate or error-controlled variant is needed. The paper does not provide one; the search surfaced Maehara–Murota's "error-controlled" follow-up, which I have not read.
  - *Genericity may fail.* With few generators, genericity can fail silently in the simple-component stage (Example 4.3's {2,1,1} vs {1,1,1,1} was caught only at the irreducible stage).
- **ML practice.** It carries nothing established. Using it on LLM concept operators is the owner's proposed experiment, which the map rightly marks as the new part.

## Limitations

- **Real symmetric generators only.** Irreducible components of complex or quaternionic type are mishandled (the Thm 3.1(C) error).
- **No noise model or robustness analysis.**
- **The practical variant has no finest-decomposition guarantee.**
- **Small, structured examples only.** There is no timing or scaling study in the preprint.

## Open questions

- Whether the JJIAM version corrects Theorem 3.1(C) or adds a real-type hypothesis (unverified). How the method behaves on noisy, approximately block-structured operators, which is the owner's actual use case.

## Corrections to the seeded skim

- Seeded from metadata; the text shows the summary is right. The random element is a random *linear combination of the generators*, not a random matrix of the algebra. The paper also offers building a basis of 𝒯 ∩ Sⁿ by symmetrised products, to guarantee genericity (Prop. 4.3).
- **The preprint.** The owner's pointer to "the METR technical-report preprint" resolves to METR 2007-52 (September 2007), not a 2008 number. Its title is "A Numerical Algorithm for Block-Diagonal Decomposition of Matrix *-Algebras", without the published title's "with application to semidefinite programming". I read the preprint, not the JJIAM article.
- **A mathematical error in the preprint (my check).** Theorem 3.1(C), "If 𝒯 is irreducible, then 𝒯 = Mₙ", is stated for real *-algebras generated by symmetric matrices, and credited to Wedderburn's *Lectures on Matrices* and Kojima–Kojima–Hara. It is false over ℝ. The real forms of random complex Hermitian 2×2 matrices are real symmetric 4×4 matrices. They generate an 8-dimensional real algebra (≅ M₂(ℂ)), irreducible on ℝ⁴, with a 2-dimensional commutant, not M₄(ℝ). I checked this numerically. So the algorithm's irreducible stage (§4.2, eq. 4.5, which forces the blocks between eigenspaces to be scalar multiples of I) presumes every irreducible component is of real type. Maehara–Murota's title ("with general irreducible components") is consistent with its removing exactly this restriction. I have not read whether the published JJIAM version of this paper adds the hypothesis.
- **The map's author list.** "Murota–Kanno–Kojima–Maehara" conflates this paper with its companion, as the seed already notes. The preprint confirms the authors are Murota, Kanno, M. Kojima and S. Kojima.
