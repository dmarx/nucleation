# CLAIM-tmpnfjln: An organization can place control over the conditions of action upstream, the practical obligation to secure acceptable outcomes downstream, and blame where failure becomes visible, so that those blamed for a failure can be those with the least control over the conditions that produced it

## The claim

The exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afa2a8d-2598-83ea-8d1a-d9ceb992a99c). The
owner, at U9: "double click on moral crumple zones and privileged
irresponsibility". A17:

> **An institution can concentrate decision-making power upstream,
> concentrate moral obligations downstream, and then assign blame at the
> point where the consequences become visible.**

"Neither concept requires a conscious mastermind." Tronto's privileged
irresponsibility is "a theory of *the unequal distribution of
responsibility*"; Elish's moral crumple zone "a theory of *the
misattribution of responsibility when things go wrong*". A17 §3: the first
works "**before anything goes wrong**", the second "**after something goes
wrong**".

**Split out on 2026-11-16.** A17 §6's mechanism, that unseen compensation
lets the organization take credit for outcomes its members' effort produced
and blame them when the effort lapses, is a separate hypothesis, now
[CLAIM-tmp7zzbd](CLAIM-tmp7zzbd.md). A26 §IV's name for this claim's condition, "asymmetric
moral accountability", is "A systematic divergence between the distribution
of effective control over circumstances, the distribution of practical
moral obligations, and the attribution of responsibility for resulting
outcomes."

## Its limits, A's own

A17 §8 lists three ways the frameworks go wrong:

- **Not every frontline error is systemic.** "The existence of upstream
  authority doesn't itself establish a moral crumple zone. We need to show
  that the person being blamed actually lacked meaningful control over
  relevant conditions, or that the allocation of blame was disproportionate
  to their contribution."
- **Not every unequal division is exploitative.** "Unequal duties are not
  automatically unjust duties."
- **Structure does not dissolve accountability.** "having an explanation
  does not mean lacking responsibility."

## Where it sits

- It rests on [CLAIM-tmpnw7ez](CLAIM-tmpnw7ez.md), from the same exchange: responsibility for the
  conditions of another's conduct is distinct from responsibility for the
  conduct. A17's close says the same for delegation: "**If you have the
  power to determine the conditions under which someone else must exercise
  moral judgment, you cannot automatically absolve yourself of
  responsibility for the consequences merely because they were the one who
  ultimately made the decision.**"
- [CLAIM-tmpyjzi8](CLAIM-tmpyjzi8.md), the implicit moral subsidy, and [CLAIM-tmp7zzbd](CLAIM-tmp7zzbd.md), unseen
  compensation and credit: the upstream mechanism this claim no longer
  includes.
- [CLAIM-614](CLAIM-614.md): an organization can keep harm non-legible by
  the projections it reproduces. Invisible prevention is a non-legibility
  of the organization's own dependence.
- [CLAIM-678](CLAIM-678.md): operational, economic and epistemic power over
  a technology can be held separately. Control "upstream" here is the first
  two; the blame described is a use of the third, control over how failure
  is described (A17 §10: "**Power includes the ability to establish the
  categories through which responsibility is understood.**").
- [CASE-151](../cases.d/CASE-151.md), Wells Fargo's Community Bank: the
  board's investigation traced unauthorized accounts to the sales model and
  its enforcement, while individual employees were dismissed. The nearest
  documented instance in the record. It is cited, not taken as grounds:
  whether those employees lacked meaningful control is not settled there.

## What it does not say

- **That automation causes it.** A19 argues algorithms can
  "industrialize" it, and A19 §9 reports studies against the strong form:
  three Dutch experiments that "did not find the expected general pattern
  of automation bias", and Ozer, Waggoner and Kennedy (2024, [LIT-tmpq41d3](../literature.d/LIT-tmpq41d3.md)), whose
  experiments on blame toward judges using algorithmic advice "did not find
  support for the hypothesized tendency for algorithmic recommendations to
  alter blame in the expected ways". A19's own conclusion: "So the strong
  claim that algorithms reliably allow decision-makers to evade public
  blame isn't established."
- **More than Elish and Tronto support.** Elish ([LIT-tmp4gcqt](../literature.d/LIT-tmp4gcqt.md)) was read on
  2026-11-16 and supports the blame mechanism; Tronto's books
  ([LIT-tmpw46qz](../literature.d/LIT-tmpw46qz.md), [LIT-tmp4yz7x](../literature.d/LIT-tmp4yz7x.md)) are Unavailable and were read through two
  stand-ins, for the concept only.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Ozer, Waggoner and Kennedy, read

The claim's "What it does not say" cites this study, through A19 §9, as evidence against the strong form of the automation thesis. The reading confirms that and sharpens it. In pre-registered vignettes on three US samples, a judge who followed an algorithm's advice before a release that ended in a death was not blamed less than one who decided alone: respondents "did blame the algorithms for the mistake under the agreement" condition, "but this did not reduce the culpability assigned to the judge." Blame rose slightly ("place 9% more blame upon the judge"), mostly because respondents thought she should have used her own judgment. So the visible decision-maker kept the blame, which is consistent with the claim's "blame where failure becomes visible" but does not test it: the design has no organization, no upstream control over her conditions and no compensating effort. The study bounds the claim's automation corollary; it neither supports nor weakens the organizational mechanism. The authors stress the effects are small and fade in the third sample.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Cerrato, read

Cerrato, Sahlgren and Heilmann ([LIT-tmpxzl3z](../literature.d/LIT-tmpxzl3z.md)) give a technical instance of the claim's first two parts. The criteria of an automated decision are set upstream by the deployer (label definition, feature measurement, success metric), and in their simulated hiring screen those choices move the explanation a rejected candidate receives more than the explanation method's own tuning does. A counterfactual explanation then frames the subject as the one who must change: it "preclude consideration of whether the decision-maker ought to have acted differently", and a relational view would not "place onto the subjects the burden of simply adapting to institutional discretion". The mechanism differs from the claim's, which is that compensation which prevents failure leaves no trace. Here the deployer's part is hidden by the form of the explanation. Nothing in the paper concerns credit or blame for outcomes. It supports the claim's placement of control and burden. It does not support the account of how blame settles.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tognazzini and Coates, read

The SEP entry "Blame" reports the complicity condition on standing to blame
and its institutional extension. Watson (2015) and Beade (2019) argue that
since the state is at least partly responsible for social conditions that
partly explain crime, "it suddenly becomes unclear that the state is in a
position to punish certain law-breakers". Lacey and Pickard (2021) reply by
contrasting interpersonal relationships with criminal law. Applied here, an
organization that blames members for failures its own conditions produced
blames as a complicit party. That is a normative verdict on the blame the
claim describes. It is not evidence that blame does settle where failure
becomes visible, which is what the claim asserts and what its `defeated_if`
tests. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Nelkin, read (for Williams)

The SEP entry "Moral Luck" describes the agent-regret of a party who caused
harm without fault, and the virtue of taking responsibility beyond what
justice requires, as appropriate feeling, not differential blameworthiness
(§3.1). The claim describes members blamed for failures the organization's
conditions produced. On the entry's distinction, their taking
responsibility can be fitting while blame from others is not. The entry
does not discuss organizations. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tronto interview (2024), read as a stand-in for Moral Boundaries

In her own words, privileged irresponsibility's "danger ... is to cut off one’s reaction to others"
(p. 279). The Bellamy passage Caring Democracy quotes (2013, p. 44) gives the image: those on top
of the coach, "Once you climb into it, you pretend that you don’t know all those people pushing the
carriage" (p. 278). This bears on the claim's invisibility mechanism: those who benefit stop seeing
those who carry them. It is said of social classes and their indifference, not of organizations
crediting themselves or blaming their members. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tronto (2018), read as a stand-in for Tronto's books

The claim's summary cites Tronto's privileged irresponsibility, unread, for how responsibilities
are distributed before failure. In Tronto's own words (2018, the books being unavailable), the
concept is "the capability of the relatively privileged to refuse to accept unpleasant or
burdensome responsibilities" (p. 8). That is a claim about privileged groups in a society. It
supports the claim's use of Tronto for the upstream half only by extension to organizations, an
extension the exchange makes (A17 §2: "An executive can say...") and Liedo et al. ([LIT-tmpd8i3u](../literature.d/LIT-tmpd8i3u.md))
make for care institutions. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Elish, read

Elish ([LIT-tmp4gcqt](../literature.d/LIT-tmp4gcqt.md)), read in full, supports this claim's **after-failure half** from a source
independent of the exchange. In complex automated systems the operator "may become simply a
component—accidentally or intentionally—that bears the brunt of the moral and legal
responsibilities when the overall system malfunctions", and "the moral crumple zone protects the
integrity of the technological system, at the expense of the nearest human operator" (p. 40). She
adds mechanisms the claim lacks: certification that leaves the human as "the only possible
malfunction, systemically" (p. 50), the operator's legal final authority, and media framing in the
"immediate aftermath of a highly publicized event" (p. 51). She also bounds it: crumple zones "are
not a property of every complex and automated system" (p. 50).

Elish does **not** support the claim's other parts. She says nothing about upstream control
placing the obligation to secure outcomes downstream, and nothing about credit for outcomes
produced by members' compensating effort ("compensation that prevents failure leaves little
trace"). The nearest she comes is that the automation is credited with saving the human (p. 49).
The claim's summary presents the pair as Tronto plus Elish. The reading shows that only the blame
half is Elish's; the mechanism of A17 §6 is A's. A17 §8's own limit, "Not every frontline error
is systemic", matches Elish: "Without a doubt, actions taken by the plant operators led to the
accident" (p. 44).

Relation: **corroborates** (after-failure half only; say so in the THEORY).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Choroszewicz, read

Choroszewicz ([LIT-tmpj3pjx](../literature.d/LIT-tmpj3pjx.md)) corroborates the claim's **allocation clause** from independent
ethnographic evidence. In three generative-AI tool trials, "human oversight and responsibility as
liability were explicitly codified as a user burden" (p. 6) for tools users could not shape, which
"insulates technologies and their developers from accountability" (p. 10). It weakly corroborates
the claim's **different principles for credit and blame**. Poor outputs "tended to be portrayed as
the result of poor user interaction rather than issues with the tool's design and development"
(p. 6), while negative feedback was "frequently dismissed as temporary" (p. 7). It names the
compensation the claim says goes unseen: users' "invisible work to compensate for the limitations
of AI tools" (p. 6). It does **not** support the clause about blame where failure becomes visible:
the study observed trials, and no harm or sanction occurred. A17 §5's "they may be blamed for not
exercising sufficient professional judgment" is A's projection.

Relation: **corroborates** (allocation and attribution of poor outputs; not post-failure blame).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Liedo, Van Grunsven and Marin, read

Bears on the claim's upstream half only. Institutional privileged irresponsibility leaves workers
"the only ones deemed responsible for managing the challenging consequences of a new
technological intervention that produces profit for others" (p. 13). Nothing on blame after
failure. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: NTSB HAR-19/03, read

The case A17 §4 cites, now read ([LIT-tmp3lco2](../literature.d/LIT-tmp3lco2.md)). It **qualifies** the claim's blame clause in one
case. The official investigation did not settle attribution at the point of visibility alone. It
made the operator's distraction the probable cause, but found that Uber ATG's "inadequate safety
risk assessment procedures", oversight and missing countermeasures to automation complacency contributed, "all a
consequence of its inadequate safety culture" (p. vi). It also found that the culture contributed
"specifically to the vehicle operator’s extended distraction during the crash trip" (p. viii).
The operator was nonetheless the one "suspended after the crash" (p. 27). The report disclaims any
assignment of blame ("The NTSB does not assign fault or blame for an accident or incident"), so it
cannot show how blame was distributed outside the investigation. It also meets the claim's own
exclusion, blame on a frontline party who "had adequate time, information and resources": the
NTSB found that attentive, "she would likely have had sufficient time" (p. vii). The case
therefore neither supports nor defeats the claim. A17's use of it is fair, except that A17 calls
the operator's distraction contributing when it was the probable cause, and omits the
pedestrian's impaired crossing.

Relation: none (cite as the case, not as grounds).
