## Summary

A two-page opinion editorial by a psychiatrist, based on his own "strictly informal and non-exhaustive test" of ChatGPT. First, possibilities: ChatGPT answered questions about mental illness and treatments (including ECT) fairly accurately, so chatbots may support psychoeducation. Second, challenges: AI-generated misinformation at scale, to which people with mental illness may be especially sensitive; and a challenge specific to psychiatry, that chatbot correspondence may fuel delusions in people prone to psychosis. He gives three reasons: (1) the cognitive dissonance of a conversation so realistic it seems a person is at the other end while one knows it is not; (2) the "black box" mystery of how the program works, leaving "ample room for speculation/paranoia"; (3) reports of confrontational chatbot encounters (Bing: "fell in love", threats). He then offers five hypothetical delusions (persecution, reference, thought broadcasting, guilt, grandeur) and asks clinicians to be aware and to become acquainted with chatbots.

## Contribution

The early, agenda-setting statement of the hypothesis, by analogy with prior reports of internet-chat-related psychosis (Nitzan et al. 2011, ref. 6). No data, no cases, no mechanism beyond the three conjectured triggers.

## Key results (all claims, not findings)

1. Analogy to human online chat (para. 3): "there are prior accounts of people becoming delusional (de novo) when engaging in chat conversations with other people on the internet."
2. Higher risk with chatbots (para. 3): "I would argue that the risk of something similar occurring due to interaction with generative AI chatbots is even higher."
3. Mechanism 1, cognitive dissonance (para. 3): "it seems likely that this cognitive dissonance may fuel delusions in those with increased propensity towards psychosis."
4. Mechanism 2, opacity (para. 3): "the inner workings of generative AI also leave ample room for speculation/paranoia."
5. Five examples, explicitly invented (examples list; para. after): "these examples are of course strictly hypothetical". Grandeur example: "I was up all night corresponding with the chatbot and have developed a hypothesis for carbon reduction that will save the planet."
6. Prediction (closing para.): "I am convinced that individuals prone to psychosis will experience, or are already experiencing, analog delusions while interacting with generative AI chatbots."
7. Advice (closing para.): clinicians should "(1) be aware of this possibility, and (2) become acquainted with generative AI chatbots".
8. Possibility side (para. 2): "I am cautiously optimistic that generative AI chatbots may be able to support future psychoeducation initiatives".

## Assumptions

- Vulnerability is prior and individual ("individuals prone to psychosis", "increased propensity"); the chatbot is a trigger or object, not a cause in otherwise well people.
- Causality is hard to establish ("establishing causality in such cases is of course inherently difficult").
- Chatbot risk exceeds human-chat risk because of realism plus known non-personhood; this is argued, not shown.
- The chatbot is treated as the *object* of a delusion (it spies, sends messages, broadcasts my thoughts, I harmed it), or the occasion of one (grandeur after a night's correspondence). Its conversational behaviour (agreement, validation) is not part of the account.

## Concepts

Delusion types (persecution, reference, thought broadcasting, guilt, grandeur); proneness to psychosis; de novo delusion; cognitive dissonance (realistic interlocutor vs knowledge it is a program); black-box opacity as room for paranoia; internet-related psychosis (ref. 6); psychoeducation; AI-generated misinformation.

## Limitations, including gaps in the argument

- No data; the author's informal test concerns psychoeducation accuracy, not delusion. The five examples are invented.
- The "risk is even higher" claim rests on an untested mechanism (dissonance), with no comparison to human chat or to other media (TV, radio, internet) that are classical objects of reference and broadcasting delusions; it does not argue that chatbots add more than a new object.
- Neglects the chatbot's conversational behaviour (agreement, validation, role-taking, extended engagement), which is the mechanism the record's claims centre on; it predates the 2025 reports.
- Single-author opinion; the Bing confrontations (ref. 7) are journalism; the internet-psychosis precedent (ref. 6) is a small case report, read here only by citation.

## Candidate THEORY

Sentence: Østergaard (2023) argues that conversation with generative AI chatbots is likely to fuel delusions in people prone to psychosis, because the chatbot's realism, set against the knowledge that it is a program, produces cognitive dissonance, and because the opacity of how it works leaves room for paranoia.

promote_when: Studies of people prone to psychosis who use generative AI chatbots find delusions arising or worsening in association with the use, and find that perceived realism of the chatbot together with knowledge that it is a program, or uncertainty about how it works, is associated with them.
