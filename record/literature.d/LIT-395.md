# LIT-395: The Combination Problem for Panpsychism

David J. Chalmers (2016), in Godehard Brüntrup and Ludwig Jaskolla (eds),
*Panpsychism: Contemporary Perspectives*, Oxford University Press,
pp. 179–214 (volume dated 2017 on the book). DOI
10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199359943.003.0008. Author's draft at
https://consc.net/papers/combination.pdf.

The brief's citation is right in title, editors, publisher and venue. Its
year is the book's; Crossref dates the volume 29 December 2016. Its "earlier
version circulated from 2013" is plausible but unverified: the paper was
written alongside the 2013 Amherst Lecture, and the posted draft is the
April 2015 one.

## Key takeaways

- **Three combination problems, not one** (§3). The *subject* problem: how
  do micro-subjects make a macro-subject? Its sharpest form is
  *subject-summing*: given any group of subjects, it seems possible for
  them to exist without any further subject. The *quality* problem, whose
  sharp form is the *palette* problem: a handful of micro-qualities and a
  vast array of macro-qualities. The *structure* problem, whose sharp form
  is *structural mismatch*: the structure of experience is not the
  structure of the brain. There are also the unity, boundary, awareness and
  grain problems. A solution has to address all of them and should say
  which it addresses.
- **The arguments** (§4): anti-aggregation (James's nihilism about
  composites, easy to reject), subject-summing, a conceivability argument
  for a "panpsychist zombie" world, a knowledge argument, the palette
  argument, the revelation argument and the structural mismatch argument.
  Chalmers rates subject-summing and structural mismatch as the serious
  ones.
- **Non-combinatorial responses** (§5). *Emergent* panpsychism (IIT read as
  a fundamental law is one) and *autonomous* panpsychism avoid combination
  but inherit dualism's problem of mental causation. *Identity*
  panpsychism makes the macro-subject a fundamental entity; its best form
  is *quantum holism*, where an entangled system is the subject, and it
  faces doubt about brain-scale entanglement and a structural mismatch.
- **Combinatorial responses** (§§6–8). Deflating the subject only
  relocates the problem. Combinatorial infusion collapses into identity or
  emergent panpsychism. *Phenomenal bonding* is "one of the more promising
  approaches" but must steer between one giant subject (if bonding is
  transitive) and fragmentary subjects (if not). Functional accounts of
  awareness meet the conceivability objection. Panqualityism avoids
  micro-subjects but leaves all three problems open. For structure, the
  only viable line is that phenomenal structure follows informational
  structure, and it is unclear how that could be constitutive rather than
  a brute law.
- **Conclusion** (§9): the problem may be unanswerable, or answering it may
  point to the right theory.

**On [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md).** The paper is about micro-subjects composing a
macro-subject, not about groups. But three things in it bear directly on
that account.

- *James's argument is a group-mind argument.* The epigraph that names the
  problem is James's twelve men, each thinking one word of a sentence,
  with "nowhere … a consciousness of the whole sentence", and "the private
  minds do not agglomerate into a higher compound mind". Subject-summing
  formalises it (§4.2). Its premise is general: no group of subjects
  necessitates a further subject. If it held, it would refuse
  Schwitzgebel's upward move ([LIT-159](LIT-159.md)) as firmly as any anti-nesting
  principle. Chalmers aims it only at constitutive panpsychism, though.
  It bites where the whole's experience is grounded in the parts'
  experiences. Schwitzgebel grounds a nation's experience in its
  organisation, which is a materialist's claim and is not Chalmers's
  target here.
- *An objection to exclusion that does not mention groups.* Footnote 7
  likens a view Nagel suggested to IIT's exclusion postulate, which it
  glosses as "a system is conscious iff it is not part of (and does not
  contain) a system with a higher degree of integrated information".
  Chalmers's objection is that both make consciousness *extrinsic*:
  intrinsically identical systems might or might not be conscious
  depending on their surroundings. This is an argument against the
  account's first refusal route, anti-nesting, and it is independent of
  what the principle says about nations. It cuts the other way from what
  the account's `promote_when` asks for: it is an independent *objection*
  to exclusion, not an independent justification of it. Schwitzgebel's
  ballot reductio ([NOTE-131](../notes.d/NOTE-131.md), C5) is a concrete case of the same
  extrinsicness, though the footnote does not cite it. My own remark, not
  the paper's: Kammerer's functional principle ([LIT-409](LIT-409.md)) does not
  obviously meet the objection, since it rules on a whole from what its
  parts do, not from what surrounds it, and it leaves the parts'
  consciousness alone.
- *The boundary problem is the same seam.* Phenomenal bonding must bond
  the micro-subjects of one organism and not those elsewhere. Chalmers's
  candidate for a relation that does this is "informational integration
  along the lines of Tononi's hypothesis". He notes that such a relation
  is derivative, not fundamental, so it opens a new combination problem.
  That is [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)'s second route, an architectural criterion,
  reached from panpsychism, and it is left unprincipled here too.

**On Levin's "stand on the combination problem".** Levin ([LIT-439](LIT-439.md),
read in [NOTE-341](../notes.d/NOTE-341.md), C8) says his model "takes a stand on the perennial
'combination problem' and can perhaps be seen as a form of panpsychism",
citing Chalmers 2013. Two findings.

- *Levin is not citing this paper.* His reference list (PMC6923654) gives
  "Chalmers D. (2013). Panpsychism and panprotopsychism. Amherst Lect.
  Philos. 8", that is, Chalmers's companion paper. [NOTE-341](../notes.d/NOTE-341.md) records
  only "citing Chalmers 2013", so the record has not said otherwise, but
  the brief assumed this paper.
- *By this paper's standard it is not yet a stand.* Chalmers asks that a
  proposed solution say which of the subject, quality, structure, unity,
  boundary, awareness and grain problems it addresses (§3), and say how
  the macro-subject is constituted. Levin names no sub-problem and offers
  no constitution relation. His nested goal-pursuing Selves are a
  candidate answer to the *boundary* problem for agency. For experience
  they would need a functional account of awareness, and §6.4 argues that
  such accounts face the same conceivability objection as functionalism.
  The nearest reading of his remark in Chalmers's terms is a
  panprotopsychist view with functional bonding. Chalmers rates that
  family as open, not as solved.

**Neighbours.** IIT 3.0 ([LIT-445](LIT-445.md)) states the exclusion postulate that
footnote 7 objects to, and Barrett et al. ([LIT-185](LIT-185.md)) defend IIT's
panpsychist reading. Chalmers here classifies IIT, read as a fundamental
law, as *emergent* panpsychism (§5.1). Among the core consciousness works
being filed alongside this one, *Panpsychism and Panprotopsychism* is this
paper's companion and supplies its terminology (§2). The hard-problem
conceivability argument is the template for the panpsychist zombie
argument of §4.3. It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.
