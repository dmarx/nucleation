# LIT-2181: How Americans Respond to Idiosyncratic and Exogenous Changes in Household Wealth and Unearned Income

Golosov, M., Graber, M., Mogstad, M. & Novgorodsky, D. (2024). How Americans Respond to Idiosyncratic and Exogenous Changes in Household Wealth and Unearned Income. *The Quarterly Journal of Economics* 139(2): 1321–1395. doi:10.1093/qje/qjad053. First circulated as NBER Working Paper 29000 (July 2021), doi:10.3386/w29000, which is the version read.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-1671](../notes.d/NOTE-1671.md). **The text read is the NBER working
paper of July 2021, not the QJE version of record; every number and page
here is the NBER version's.** The QJE abstract drops the working paper's
second contribution (a "rich life-cycle model" used to bound the effects
of a UBI and of higher top tax rates) and puts in its place a comparison
with earlier lottery studies, concluding that "existing U.S. studies
substantially underestimate wealth and income effects".*

- **Work falls, fast and for good.** "a sizable, swift, and persistent
  change in wage earnings" (p. 10); "for an extra 100 dollars in wealth,
  winners reduce their earnings on average by 2.3 dollars in each of the
  five subsequent years" (p. 16), and the share who stop working grows
  over time (p. 15).
- **About 50 cents per dollar of flow.** Per dollar of annuitized
  unearned income, household labour earnings fall 0.52, labour taxes
  0.11, and consumption rises 0.58 (Table 4.1, p. 27).
- **Consumption is inferred, not seen.** It is the residual of the budget
  identity: the authors "impute the effect of winning a lottery on
  consumption expenditure using the budget constraint identity" (p. 26).
- **Wealth is inferred, not seen.** Assets come from taxable capital
  income at an assumed 5.4% return, and "the imputation of wealth using
  asset returns can only be done approximately" (p. 24). On that basis
  "American lottery winners save most of the windfall for future periods"
  (p. 2).
- **Who responds how.** Higher-income winners cut more per dollar (−0.67
  in the top quartile against −0.31 in the bottom), while "lower-income
  households, however, are more likely to stop working" (p. 18) and
  consume more (MPC 0.73 against 0.50; Table 4.1).
- **No threshold, and no business building.** Per-dollar effects fall
  with prize size, from above 5.5 dollars per 100 for the smaller wins to
  below 1 dollar for the largest (p. 22). Wealth raises entry only into
  self-employment with profits of $15,000 or less; "the marginal
  entrepreneur may be of lower quality" (p. 51).
- **Twice Sweden's.** Against Cesarini et al. (2017), "even after
  adjustment, our estimates are approximately twice as large as those
  obtained for Sweden." (Appendix J)

## Standing in the record

Filed as a case of large, sudden, unconditional and permanent money, the
kind the branch's synthesis, [CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md), singles out as able to slacken
effort. For US households across the income distribution it supports that
clause: work falls, by choice and persistently, while the stock is kept.
It gives no sign of the threshold of [CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md): responses are smooth
and shrink per dollar as prizes grow, and wealth does not turn wage earners
into owners of substantial businesses, a contrast with the asset threshold
of [THEORY-1465](../theory.d/THEORY-1465.md). It bounds the scope of [THEORY-1471](../theory.d/THEORY-1471.md) and
[THEORY-1466](../theory.d/THEORY-1466.md): their small, means-tested transfers in poor countries
left work little changed, while these large, permanent windfalls do not.
Against [CLAIM-601](../claims.d/CLAIM-601.md), winners build the buffer the claim says buys time,
yet few use it to start profitable businesses. It sits beside Cesarini et
al. (2017), the Swedish lottery study filed in the same batch, and finds
twice its effects. Filed from it: [THEORY-1498](../theory.d/THEORY-1498.md).
