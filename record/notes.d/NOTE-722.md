# NOTE-722: Lewis — Many, but almost one

## Contribution

A short essay in honour of Armstrong. It takes Unger's problem of the many
and Geach's paradox of 1001 cats as one problem and solves it twice. One
solution uses van Fraassen's supervaluations. The other is a counting rule:
count by almost-identity, which Lewis takes to be extensive mereological
overlap. Lewis argues that the two are better combined than chosen between,
and shows what each does that the other cannot. Along the way he rejects
three rivals: the dualism of things and their constituting matter (Lowe),
vague objects, and Geach's relative identity.

## Key insight

Identical against not identical is the wrong opposition. Identity is one end of a
spectrum of overlap, and distinctness, meaning no shared part, is the other.
Many candidates that differ only at their edges sit near the identity end.
So "there is one cat" is almost true, and where the near-identity is not
close enough, supervaluation over the undecided boundary still makes it
super-true.

## Assumptions

- **Mereology and perdurance.** Things are aggregates of parts, and persist
  by having temporal parts. Constitution is identity of temporal parts.
  The problem is set out in space, in time ("there are questionable
  temporal parts at the beginning of every human life") and, for those who
  hold it, across worlds.
- **Vagueness is semantic indecision**, not a feature of objects: "Semantic
  indecision will suffice to explain the phenomenon of vagueness." Footnote
  4 adds a condition: there must be many precise candidates to be undecided
  between.
- **Nature is gradual here.** Lewis grants that "Some things are by their
  nature more eligible than others to be referents or objects of thought",
  and that the more eligible referent wins by default. That does not help
  in this case, because "nature is gradual, no handy joint in nature picks
  out one of the c's from all the rest" (footnote 6). The essay therefore
  covers only cases with no natural joint to settle the boundary.
- **The goal of a cooperative speaker is super-truth**, and the
  supervaluation rule is a defeasible presumption that pragmatics can
  suspend.

## Key results

- **The problem (The problem of the many).**
  - "Many surfaces are equally good candidates to be the boundary of the
    cloud." All candidates count, so there are many clouds, or none counts,
    so there are none: "We have many things or we have none, but anyway not
    the one thing we thought we had."
  - The 1001 cats is "another instance of Unger's problem of the many" once
    the hairs are questionable rather than plucked. Without that, temporal
    parts already dissolve Geach's version.
- **Two disqualifications rejected.**
  - *Constituters*: unparsimonious, and the constituters are "all too
    cat-like", so the result is 1002 cats.
  - *Vague objects*: Lewis has no correct conception of one. Three pictures
    (multiplicity, ignorance, fadeaway) each replace the vagueness with
    precision. The precise candidates are still cat-like, and one vague cat
    cannot "spoil their cathood". Footnote 5 adds a point on Evans: a
    consistent theory of vague objects must take vague identity as sharing
    some but not all precisifications, not as a truth-value gap.
- **Supervaluation (One of the many is a cat).**
  - Exactly one candidate is the cat, and which one is arbitrary: "Which
    one deserves the name 'cat' is up to us."
  - "Call a sentence super-true if and only if it is true under all ways
    of making the unmade semantic decisions". Each intended interpretation
    puts exactly one candidate in the extension of "cat", so "there is just
    one cat" is super-true.
  - The cost, granted: "Although it is super-true that something is a cat
    on the mat, there is nothing such that it is super-true of it that it
    is a cat on the mat." This is the horse puzzle.
  - To the objection that the rule forbids even stating the problem: a
    "fanatical supervaluationism" would. The rule is a defeasible
    presumption, suspended when it would make nonsense of what is said.
    "What's intolerable is to be without any good and natural sense in
    which there is only one cat."
  - To the objection that indeterminate reference needs determinate
    reference first: the concept is fixed on imaginary examples set in
    sharper worlds.
- **Relative identity rejected (The many are not different cats).**
  - Geach's "same cat" is a relation of partial indiscernibility. Lewis
    accepts counting by relations other than identity, and cites his own
    roads example from [LIT-446](../literature.d/LIT-446.md). But adding the noun phrase does not settle
    the cats case as it does "same or different newspaper?" or "same or
    different issue?". And Geach
    never relates "same cat" to identity simpliciter.
- **Partial identity (The many are almost one).**
  - "the real opposite of identity is distinctness: not distinctness in the
    sense of non-identity, but rather distinctness in the sense of
    non-overlap". Overlap is a spectrum. Siamese twins sharing a finger are
    near the distinctness end, and any two cat candidates near the identity
    end. Credit to Armstrong (1978).
  - Number statements are translated with "almost identical" in place of
    "identical": "The cats are many, but almost one. By a blameless
    approximation, we may say simply that there is one cat on the mat." It
    is true for most contexts, and stricter standards are possible.
  - Almost-identity is overlap, "not a relation of partial
    indiscernibility". No salient respect is guaranteed to be shared.
  - "the relation of almost-identity, in other words extensive overlap, is
    not in general an equivalence relation. Many steps of almost-identity
    can take us from one thing to another thing that is entirely distinct
    from the first." Whether it is an equivalence among the actual cats
    "depends on the extent to which the cats differ, and on the threshold
    for almost-identity (and both of these are matters that we will, very
    sensibly, leave undecided)."
  - Footnote 9 counts triangles by non-overlap: four small triangles in a
    square with both diagonals.
- **Combination (One solution too many?).**
  - There are two kinds of intended interpretation, one-cat and many-cat,
    and context favours one kind. When attention to the candidates brings
    in the many-cat kind, almost-identity still gives a sense in which
    there is one cat.
  - Almost-identity helps supervaluation: "The supervaluation rule comes
    natural only when the alternative interpretations don't differ too
    much." The 'bank' homonym is the contrast.
  - Supervaluation helps almost-identity in two ways. First, Fred's house
    with and without the garage overlap substantially but not enough: "we
    cannot really say they're almost identical. So likewise we cannot say
    that the two houses are almost one." Second, definite descriptions:
    "The cat on the mat includes hair h17" should be gappy, and relaxed
    Russellian translations split, one true and one false. "Whichever way
    we go, supervaluations give us the gappiness we want. It's hard to see
    how else to get it."

## Concepts

- **Problem of the many.** Many aggregates with equal claim to be one
  thing, because its outlying parts are questionable.
- **Questionable part.** Neither definitely a part nor definitely not.
- **Semantic indecision.** We never decided exactly which candidate a word
  applies to. This is Lewis's whole account of vagueness.
- **Super-true / super-false / super-truth-value gap.** True under all,
  false under all, or true under some and false under others of the ways
  of making the unmade semantic decisions.
- **Partial identity.** Overlap: having some parts in common and some not.
- **Almost-identity.** Extensive overlap. Not similarity, and not in
  general an equivalence relation.
- **Distinctness (Lewis's sense).** Non-overlap, not non-identity. The
  real opposite of identity.
- **Eligibility.** A natural referent's default claim to a word when usage
  leaves it open. Footnote 6 only, and set aside for this problem.

## Bearing on the record

**The check the LIT set: does "almost one" fairly map onto [CLAIM-164](../claims.d/CLAIM-164.md)'s
gap-isolated subspace, or is it only a resemblance?** Partly fair, and only
for one reading of "the many". It is unfair for the other. The fit depends on
which things are taken to be the many candidates.

- **Fair: the many are the perturbed or estimated copies of one subspace.**
  Different noise draws, or partitions that differ in a few marginal
  variables, give many subspaces, none exactly the true one. By
  Davis–Kahan they lie within about ‖δT‖/δ of it, and so within about
  twice that of each other. That is Lewis's situation: many candidates,
  differing a little at the edges, almost one. The gap then supplies two
  things Lewis leaves out. It is a threshold for "almost", which Lewis
  "very sensibly" leaves undecided. And because every candidate is anchored
  to one subspace, there is a bound on how far apart any two can be, so
  the chain of almost-identities that worries Lewis cannot drift to
  something entirely distinct. That bounded chain is the record's
  addition, not Lewis's.
- **Not fair: the many are the vectors inside a degenerate cluster.**
  [CLAIM-164](../claims.d/CLAIM-164.md)'s degenerate case is that "the k-th vector" names nothing,
  because any rotation within V is an equally good basis. These candidates
  are not almost identical. Two of them can be orthogonal, and the
  re-run's 86° maximum drift shows they can be far apart. The almost-one
  route does not apply. Two other parts of the essay fit this case better.
  - **Supervaluation.** Over the choice of basis, *V has a top direction*
    is super-true and *v is the top direction* is gappy, Lewis's horse
    puzzle. On this reading [CLAIM-164](../claims.d/CLAIM-164.md)'s "names nothing" is too strong.
    Lewis would say the term refers indeterminately, not that it fails to
    refer. That is a wording point, and it does not touch the mathematics.
  - **Naming a different object.** The claim's real move is to make V the
    referent instead of any vector. That is the disqualification route
    ("perhaps it is something else altogether, and none of the many are
    cats"), which Lewis rejects for constituters and vague objects because
    the candidates stay "all too cat-like". The objection does not transfer.
    A single vector is not V-like: it has the wrong dimension. So the
    record's route survives Lewis's objection, though not for any reason he
    gives.
- **Overlap is not angle.** Lewis's almost-identity is mereological
  overlap. Two nearby subspaces in general position share only the zero
  vector, yet the angle between them is small. The record's "almost" is a
  metric on subspaces, not shared parts. So the mapping keeps Lewis's
  logic, counting by a relation weaker than identity, and drops his
  definition of that relation. Citing Lewis for the metric would be wrong.
- **The best point of contact is footnote 6, which the LIT did not
  name.** Lewis says the problem bites because "no handy joint in nature
  picks out one of the c's". In [CLAIM-164](../claims.d/CLAIM-164.md)'s terms a spectral gap is such a
  joint: a natural boundary that makes one candidate the more eligible
  referent. So the gap does not illustrate "almost one". It removes the
  problem at the level it isolates, and leaves the problem of the many
  inside the cluster, and at the edge wherever the gap is too narrow for
  [CLAIM-154](../claims.d/CLAIM-154.md)'s threshold. This bearing is the record's.

**For [QUESTION-035](../questions.d/QUESTION-035.md) and [CLAIM-157](../claims.d/CLAIM-157.md).**

- **Duration.** Lewis's temporal version applies directly to [CLAIM-157](../claims.d/CLAIM-157.md)'s
  duration clause ("lasts as long as that block persists"). A couple
  ([CASE-046](../cases.d/CASE-046.md)) does not stop being one system at an instant. The block's
  clearing of τ fades, so there are many candidate end-times, and the
  same goes for start-times. Lewis gives the two remedies. Either
  supervaluate over admissible thresholds, so the song's middle is
  definitely one system and its last bars gappy, or count the many
  candidate durations as almost one. [CLAIM-157](../claims.d/CLAIM-157.md) does not yet say which, or
  that it needs either.
- **Fred's house is the nested-block case.** Where a block and a block
  plus a neighbour both clear the threshold, the candidates differ by a
  whole member, the garage, and are not almost one. For Lewis only
  supervaluation, or context, decides there. The record's analogue is
  [CLAIM-152](../claims.d/CLAIM-152.md)'s target relativity. Lewis's "Context will favour one sort of
  interpretation or the other" plays the role a stated target plays in [QUESTION-035](../questions.d/QUESTION-035.md).
  That resemblance is the record's. Lewis's context is conversational, not
  predictive.
- **Nothing here is evidence for [CLAIM-164](../claims.d/CLAIM-164.md) or [CLAIM-157](../claims.d/CLAIM-157.md).** Lewis's argument
  is about counting and reference in ordinary language. It has no
  dynamics, no metric and no threshold. It is a philosophical precedent for
  counting the record's units by something weaker than identity, and for
  allowing that units may be many under strict standards.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- No formal semantics. The overlap spectrum is not measured, almost-identity
  has no threshold, and "context" is not analysed.
- It assumes perdurance and an unrestricted supply of precise candidates
  (footnote 4). Those who deny either, such as van Inwagen, are pointed to,
  not answered.
- The almost-identity solution is admitted to work "for most contexts"
  only, and not for every instance of the problem.
- The super-truth cost (something is the cat, nothing is super-truly the
  cat) is accepted, not removed.
- The reply on Geach rests on introspective reports of hesitancy ("it seems
  to me").

## Open questions

- What fixes the threshold for almost-identity, and whether it can be
  anything but contextual. For the record, whether a gap-anchored bound
  is a principled threshold.
- Whether almost-identity among the actual candidates is an equivalence
  relation. Lewis says it depends on two matters left undecided.
- How the two kinds of intended interpretation are selected by context, and
  whether a stated target, in the record's sense, is one such context.
