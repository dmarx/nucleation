# NOTE-tmpuvza0: Deci & Ryan — The General Causality Orientations Scale

## Contribution

It turns Deci's (1980) three types of person into three measured
dispositions that everyone has in some degree, and gives the first
instrument for them, the General Causality Orientations Scale (GCOS). It
renames the internal and external orientations "autonomy" and "control" so
that introjects ("I *have* to do that to feel worthwhile") count as control
rather than internal causation (p. 111). It places causality orientations
in a network of other measures and claims a parallel between the three
orientations and the three kinds of event of cognitive evaluation theory.

## Key insight

A person's orientation is to the events themselves: the autonomy-oriented
"interpret their existing situations as more autonomy promoting (i.e., as
informational)", the control-oriented "seek out, select, or interpret
events as controlling", the impersonal experience behaviour "as being
beyond their intentional control" (p. 112). So the same reward or feedback
the experimental literature manipulated is, on this account, read
differently by different people, and "a high level of autonomy orientation
would lead to the same types of behaviors, cognitions, and affects that
tend to occur in informational environments" (p. 113).

## Assumptions

- **Three orientations, scored separately.** "The scale was constructed to
  measure the level of each orientation within a person rather than to
  classify a person as being one of three types" (p. 113).
- **A rank order of self-determination**: autonomy > control > impersonal,
  so a self-determination measure "would be positively correlated with the
  autonomy orientation, slightly negatively correlated with the control
  orientation, and highly negatively correlated with the impersonal" (p.
  115).
- **Locus of causality is not locus of control.** Rotter's internal control
  can be autonomous or controlled; his external control should relate to
  the impersonal orientation (pp. 113–114).
- **Generality.** The scale crosses domains (achievement, relationships) on
  purpose; domain-specific scales are expected to predict better (pp.
  113, 131).

## Key results

### Construction (pp. 117–118)

- 96 items written "by persons familiar with the theory"; over 200
  students; 12 vignettes kept, "5 of the 36 items … were rewritten in an
  attempt to improve their loadings". The revised scale went to 923
  undergraduates and 193 non-students, in varied samples and procedures.
- Example vignette (p. 118): offered a new position — (A) "I wonder if the
  new work will be interesting?" (C) "Will I make more at this position?"
  (I) "What if I can't live up to the new responsibility?"

### Reliability (pp. 118–119, Table 1)

- α (n = 636): autonomy .744, control .694, impersonal .741.
- Mean item–own-subscale (minus itself) r: A .522, C .483, I .508; the
  highest mean with another subscale .147 (I items with C). Lowest single
  own-subscale r .355; highest single cross r .301: "no overlap of the two
  distributions".
- Retest over about 2 months (n = 51): A .749, C .711, I .778.

### Subscale relations (pp. 119–120)

- A–C r = .034; A–I −.248 (p < .001); C–I .273 (p < .001), n = 636.

### Emotions (pp. 123–126, Table 3)

- Ryan et al. 1984 (n = 86): A with interest .24, with shame −.34; C with
  distress .22, guilt .24.
- Ryan 1982's experiment (n = 128; only significant correlations shown):
  I with shame .29, fear .38, guilt .21, relaxed −.36, pressured .30; C
  with hostility A .21, "important to do well" .27, "how hard did you try"
  −.19; A with hostility B −.16, guilt −.26. Correlating across eight
  experimental cells is "a tenuous procedure" (footnote 1, p. 124).
- Kernis (1983, dissertation): control-oriented people were happier and
  less fearful in controlling than in informational conditions (p. 126).

### Behaviour (pp. 126–129, Tables 4, 5)

- Exam grades (n = 88): C r = −.25 (p < .02); A −.04; I .01. The
  predicted negative I relation did not appear.
- Fairness ratings after grades were partialled: A with essay fairness
  β = .51, and I with short-answer fairness −.29, both at p < .07; "the
  findings are quite weak", "since all six B values were in the predicted
  direction, is suggestive" (p. 127).
- Mother–infant attachment (Bridges et al. 1983): n = 4 avoidant, 31
  secure, 6 resistant; the predicted pattern (I highest for avoidant, A for
  secure, C for resistant), "overall significance … marginal (p = .06)"
  (p. 128).
- Cardiac surgery (King 1984, n = 50): A predicted seeing the operation
  as a challenge.

### Norms (pp. 129–130, Tables 6, 7)

- Students (n = 636): A 70.54 (6.62), C 49.05 (8.13), I 39.03 (8.99), on a
  12–84 range. Women higher on A (71.89 vs 68.83), men on C (50.51 vs
  47.83), both p < .001.
- 83 engineers and 19 research scientists: scientists lower on C (40.8 vs
  45.6, p < .02).

## Concepts

- **Causality orientation** — a relatively enduring aspect of people that
  characterizes "the source of initiation and regulation, and thus the
  degree of self-determination, of their behavior" (abstract, p. 109).
- **Autonomy orientation** — choice based on information; seeks
  self-determination; experiences rewards "more as affirmations of their
  competence" (p. 112).
- **Control orientation** — behaviour organized by controls "either in the
  environment or inside themselves"; covers compliance and defiance alike
  (p. 112).
- **Impersonal orientation** — behaviour felt to be "beyond their
  intentional control"; "pervasive sense of incompetence" (p. 112).
- **Locus of causality vs locus of control** — why one behaves vs what
  controls one's outcomes (p. 113).

## Connections

It is the trait counterpart of the experimental work in Ryan 1982 and
Ryan, Mims and Koestner 1983 (LIT-tmpeih61), which it cites as
manipulating "the interpersonal context of rewards or feedback" (p. 110).
The record held causality orientations only as one-line mentions in
NOTE-439 (p. 240 of LIT-558) and NOTE-441 (p. 1562 of LIT-560). The paper
anticipates Deci and Ryan's 1985 book and a causality-orientations-at-work
scale (p. 131).

## Bearing on the record

- **The registration's "the source of Causality Orientations Theory" is
  right**, with the qualification that the paper presents a scale and its
  validity, not a theory with propositions: COT's propositions are stated
  later (Ryan et al. 2022, LIT-tmpd9u4s, cites this paper as COT's
  source and reports one or two meta-analyses).
- **THEORY-054 (graded autonomy, not order).** The paper's renaming is
  evidence for the account's "Not order" half in the SDT sources: inner
  events such as "I *have* to do that to feel worthwhile" "function more
  like external controls" (p. 111), so a motive inside the person can be
  controlled. It is the same position as LIT-558's introjects, ten years
  earlier, and is not a test of it.
- **THEORY-049 (functional significance).** The paper adds a person-side
  route to the same account: whether an event is informational or
  controlling depends on the recipient's orientation as well as the
  event. That makes the account's refutation harder to set up, since an
  event can be controlling for one person and informational for another;
  a test of THEORY-049's clause needs the orientation measured or
  randomized away.
- **QUESTION-071 and CLAIM-016 (history and manipulation).** The paper
  holds that orientations develop from the person's history of contexts,
  but measures only the present disposition, so it does not bear on
  history against present structure.
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- **Its validity summary overstates its table.** "All of the predicted
  relationships appeared" (p. 123), but the predicted negative relation of
  control to ego development was "not quite significant" (−.22, n = 42),
  the predicted I–performance relation did not appear (p. 127), and the
  fairness and attachment results are at p < .07 and p = .06.
- **Small validation samples** (42 to 128) for most constructs, and many
  correlations, with Table 3 reporting only the significant ones.
- **Items written by the theory's own group and rewritten to improve
  loadings**, then validated on overlapping samples; no independent sample
  confirmation is reported.
- **The scale is not printed**, so its content cannot be checked from the
  paper.
- Unpublished manuscripts and dissertations carry several validity
  results (Bridges et al.; King; Kernis; Weil and Ryan; Ryan et al. 1984).

## Open questions

- Do the orientations moderate the effects of manipulated events, as the
  Kernis interaction suggests, in a preregistered experiment?
- Does the autonomy orientation predict behaviour beyond self-report
  outcomes? Ryan et al. 2022 report correlations with outcomes
  "small-to-medium" (Hagger and Hamilton 2021).
