# NOTE-tmpb8btj: Zaibert — Collective Intentions and Collective Intentionality

## Contribution

A close critique of the one place Searle gives an account of collective
intentions, "Collective Intentions and Actions" (1990, [LIT-490](../literature.d/LIT-490.md)). It argues
that the paper never analyses a we-intention: the two collective analyses
it states are rejected by Searle himself, and the analysis he favours
describes an individual's complex intention. It also argues that Searle's
own theory of intentions, in *Intentionality*, makes irreducibly
collective intentions unlikely. For this record its main value is as a
report, with page references, of what the unread 1990 paper argues and
what it concedes.

## Key insight

"Complex" does not mean "collective" (p. 228). An intention to make the
sauce by means of my stirring has the structure of an intention to fire
a gun by means of pulling the trigger. Adding that the end is a joint
action changes the action described, not the kind of mental state.
Searle shows that some collective actions involve complex individual
intentions; he does not show that there are collective intentions.

## Assumptions

- **Searle's theory of intentions is taken as given.** Intentions, unlike
  beliefs and desires, have only actions as their conditions of
  satisfaction, are self-referential, and cannot be directed at what is
  beyond the agent's control (pp. 211–213).
- **Collective intentions and collective intentionality are different
  claims.** Searle's ontology, as Zaibert reads *The Construction of
  Social Reality*, needs we-intentions (CSR 23–26); Searle's arguments
  address collective intentionality in general (pp. 222, 229).
- **"Intentional" is ambiguous.** Zaibert separates intentionality of
  mental states from intentional action, and the latter into a
  descriptive, a normative (blaming) and a loose sense (pp. 216–217). He
  holds that intending X is neither necessary nor sufficient for doing X
  intentionally (p. 214).

## Key results

- **Searle's tone in 1990 (pp. 220–222).** As Zaibert quotes it, the 1990
  paper is "uncharacteristically tentative". Searle asks "how far can the
  theory of intentional action in Intentionality ... be extended to become
  a general theory" (CIA 401). The intuition has two parts: "collective
  intentional behavior is a primitive phenomenon", which he calls obvious,
  and "collective intentions expressed in the form of 'we intend to do such
  and such' ... are also primitive phenomena", which he calls "problematic"
  (CIA 401–402). On reduction he says: "I have not demonstrated that no
  such analysis could ever succeed" (CIA 406), and his arguments
  "suggest that our intuition is right" (CIA 401). His claim of a form of
  collective intentionality that is neither a group mind nor reducible
  "has plenty of problems of its own" (CIA 406). Of the means–end step:
  "the answer to that question is not at all obvious" (CIA 411). And of
  his conclusion: "I am not sure this is the right analysis, but it does
  seem better than the three others we considered" (CIA 412).
- **The postponed analysis (p. 222).** Searle says the individual "I
  intend"s are "derivative from the we-intend's" (CIA 403), and that the
  structure of we-intentions must wait on a prior question (CIA 406). The
  section that answers it opens with "What exactly is the formal structure
  of collective intentionality?" (CIA 408), which Zaibert notes is a
  question about intentionality in general, not intentions.
- **The notation (pp. 223–224).** "i.a. (this i.a. causes: my arm goes
  up)" for raising an arm (CIA 408–409). The hollandaise case, where Jones
  stirs and Smith pours and each could say "We are preparing hollandaise
  sauce" (CIA 410), is written "i.a. (this i.a. causes: sauce is mixed)".
  Zaibert: nothing in this marks it as collective, and with Searle's
  dropped capitals it is formally identical to raising one's arm.
- **The by-means-of relation (p. 224).** Searle calls the "by and
  by-means-of relations" "one of the keys for understanding collective
  intentionality" (CIA 410). Jones's content is "we are making the sauce by
  means of me stirring", Smith's "... by means of me pouring" (CIA 410).
  Zaibert: the two contents differ, so they cannot share a condition of
  satisfaction, and nothing shows the "we are making the sauce" part is the
  content of an intention rather than a desire or belief. A wind-mixed
  sauce would satisfy Smith though not in the way a we-intention requires
  (p. 225).
- **The four analyses (pp. 225–228).**
  - (I) "collective i.a. (this collective i.a. causes: ingredients are
    stirred, causes: sauce is mixed)" (CIA 411), rejected by Searle as
    "collectivist or socialist" because "it leaves out the fact that Jones
    is making an individual contribution to a collective goal". Zaibert
    agrees, and says this is Searle's own general view of we-intentions.
  - (II) "singular i.a. (this singular i.a. causes: stirred, causes:
    mixed)", rejected as "capitalist or individualist" because "it is
    consistent with there being no collective intentionality at all"
    (CIA 411). Zaibert: this begs the question, since whether there is
    irreducible collective intentionality is what is at issue.
  - (III) a collective i.a. causing a singular i.a. Searle rejects it
    because one intention would be in the scope of another; his gun case
    is "only one complex intention, not two intentions where one causes
    the other" (CIA 411–12). Zaibert agrees.
  - (IV) "i.a. collective B by means of singular A (this i.a. causes: A
    stirred, causes: B mixed)" (CIA 412), the one Searle "half-heartedly
    favors". Its individual counterpart is "i.a. B by means of A (this
    i.a. causes: A trigger pulls, causes: B gun fires)". Searle says the
    relation of the collective to the individual intention "is exactly like
    the relation of the intention to pull the trigger and the intention to
    fire the gun" (CIA 412). Zaibert: then (IV) differs from the gun case
    only in that one action described is collective; as to the mental state
    the two are identical, a single individual by-means-of intention
    (p. 228).
- **Tuomela and Miller (pp. 224, 226, n. 16).** Searle calls their account
  "the best he has seen" (CIA 404) and rejects it because "it attempts to
  reduce collective intentions to individual intentions plus beliefs" and
  "no such reduction will work" (CIA 404). His argument, as n. 16 reports
  it, is one case: ethical egoists who each act selfishly, believing the
  others do too, for humanity's good, meet Tuomela and Miller's conditions
  yet have no we-intention, which needs "a pact or agreement of some sort".
  Zaibert replies that the case works only if every collective benefit is
  the aim of a collective goal, and cites Searle's own "It is not of course
  to be supposed that a single counter-example can refute a philosophical
  thesis" (IOQ 120) against him (p. 226).
- **Animals (pp. 219–220).** Searle attributes intentional action to a dog
  chasing a ball (I 101) and collective behaviour to hunting animals.
  Zaibert holds animals have beliefs and desires but not intentions, and
  that groups likewise cannot have irreducible we-intentions, though they
  may have "we-desires or we-beliefs" (p. 220).
- **Conclusion (pp. 229–230).** Searle "has neither shown that the attempt
  to explain we-intentions in terms of individual intentions is doomed nor
  presented a positive account of we-intentions". The gap could be filled
  by a real analysis of collective intentions, reducible or not, or by
  letting collective desires carry the weight: "A definition of a
  collective desire ... seems, after all, a more plausible suggestion than
  a collective intention."

## Concepts

- **We-intention** — a collective intention expressed "we intend to do
  such and such", which Searle holds primitive and irreducible (CIA 401).
- **Intention-in-action (i.a.)** — "the Intentional content of the
  action" (I 84), as against a prior intention; every intentional action
  has one (I 85).
- **By-means-of intention** — one complex intention to do B by doing A,
  not two intentions one causing the other (CIA 411–12).
- **Intentional, descriptive, normative and loose** — Zaibert's three
  senses of "intentional" as said of actions: how an action was done; a
  ground of blame or praise; merely accompanied by some intentional state
  (p. 216).

## Connections

paper's content at second hand. Zaibert's distinction between collective
intentions and collective intentionality bears on what [THEORY-625](../theory.d/THEORY-625.md) calls
collective acceptance: if acceptance is not an intention, Zaibert's
argument against irreducible we-intentions does not reach it, and he
himself leaves collective desires and beliefs open (p. 220). Tuomela and
Miller, Bratman's *Faces of Intention* and Gilbert are cited or named and
not held here.

## Limitations

- **Second hand.** Every claim about [LIT-490](../literature.d/LIT-490.md) rests on Zaibert's
  quotations and his notation. He drops Searle's upper-case expressions
  after Searle does, and some of his formal comparison depends on
  restoring them.
- **The positive thesis is thin.** That groups cannot have irreducible
  we-intentions is argued by analogy with animals and from the uniqueness
  of intentions, not from anything about groups (pp. 219–220). The
  suggestion of collective desires is one paragraph.
- **Section II–III is a separate argument** about the ambiguity of
  "intentional", which connects to collective intentions only through the
  charge that Searle uses "intentional behavior" ambiguously (p. 219).
- **Later Searle is not considered.** The paper reads the 1983, 1990 and
  1995 texts only.

## Open questions

- Does [LIT-490](../literature.d/LIT-490.md) itself, read first-hand, contain more than Zaibert reports,
  for instance an argument against reduction beyond the egoist case?
- Is collective acceptance, as Searle's later work uses it, an intention,
  a desire or a belief? Zaibert's critique reaches only the first.
