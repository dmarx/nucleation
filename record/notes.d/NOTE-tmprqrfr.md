# NOTE-tmprqrfr: Johnson (2006) — Conflict and Control: Gender Symmetry and Asymmetry in Domestic Violence

## Contribution

The dyadic form of Johnson's typology of partner violence, and data for his sampling account of why feminist (agency)
and family-violence (survey) research disagree about gender symmetry.

## Key results

1. Abstract: "In violent resistance, the individual is violent but not controlling; the" "partner is the violent and controlling one." "In mutual violent control, the individual and the partner are violent and controlling."
2. pp. 1005-1006: the classification "most certainly is not based in the nature of the violent acts themselves"; "The distinction lies in the degree of control present."
3. p. 1003: "In heterosexual relationships, intimate terrorism is perpetrated almost exclusively by men," "whereas violent resistance is found almost exclusively among women."; p. 1009-1010, the controlling spouse "is the husband in 94 of the 97 cases."
4. p. 1010: "There were only five such couples (10 individuals) in this data set" (mutual violent control).
5. p. 1010: "In men’s intimate terrorism, wives rarely respond with violence"; median ratio of wife's to husband's acts .17, against .40 in situational couple violence.
6. p. 1011: the general sample "includes almost nothing but situational couple violence".
7. p. 1013: the types "simply cannot have the same causes, developmental trajectory, consequences, or prognosis" for intervention.

## Assumptions

A general motive to control is identifiable from non-violent tactics and splits cleanly into high and low; wives'
reports of both spouses are adequate; one 1970s Pittsburgh sample of 274 women, not representative.

## Concepts

Intimate terrorism; violent resistance; situational couple violence; mutual violent control; dyadic control context;
gender symmetry; sampling bias.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpp8na7: corroborates.** The typology is built on the separation the claim asserts: the types are defined
  by control and not by the violent acts, and a partner can be violent without being controlling while the other
  controls (violent resistance). The support is empirical, that the separation is real and consequential, not that
  responsibility follows control.
- **CASE-tmpwrhkd**: Relationship A is situational couple violence; Relationship B is intimate terrorism with violent
  resistance. The stipulation that Carol strikes more often is atypical in Johnson's data (result 5) but consistent
  with a classification by control rather than count.
- **The exchange** (A3 §2, §5) states the four types and the sampling account accurately; its label "coercive
  controlling violence" is Kelly and Johnson's later (2008) term for intimate terrorism, and it does not say that
  mutual violent control was rare.
- **Connections (as a stand-in):** restates the 2008 book's typology, its definition by control and its sampling
  argument; does not restate the book's additional data sets or its chapters on intervention.

## Limitations

One old sample, wives' reports only, low reliability for some control scales, a binary control cut, and too few
mutual violent control couples to characterize.
