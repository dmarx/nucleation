# LIT-tmp1uy3x: Network Epistemology: Communication in Epistemic Communities

Kevin J. S. Zollman (2013), *Philosophy Compass* 8(1):15–27 —
DOI-10.1111/j.1747-9991.2012.00534.x

## Key takeaways

- **Two lessons** (§1): "the best way for group members to communicate with
  each other depends critically on the epistemic problem", and "our
  intuitions can often be poor guides".
- **Information transmission** (§2). When information passes second-hand
  without loss and links are costly, minimally connected networks are
  optimal: the directed cycle in one model, various minimal trees in
  others. With even slight loss over distance, stars become optimal.
  Unequal centrality is therefore no evidence of distortion. Optimal
  networks are often stable, but the empty network can be too, so groups
  need not find the optimum on their own.
- **Pooling** (§3.1). When agents repeatedly average with neighbours
  (DeGroot), regular networks are best in the long run, and denser ones
  get there faster. In the bounded-confidence version the complete graph
  remains optimal.
- **Active learning** (§4). With a "truth pull" on at least one agent and
  a path to it, any connected network converges. In bandit problems, where
  evidence depends on what agents choose, minimally connected networks
  were best in small simulated groups "even when communication is free"
  ([LIT-tmp8lzwo](LIT-tmp8lzwo.md), [LIT-tmpkqfje](LIT-tmpkqfje.md)).

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-05 at the owner's request. Of the three Zollman papers it
is the one that best backs the essay's sentence as written: research on
epistemic networks shows the effect of communication depends on the
problem, not that less is better. It predates [LIT-tmphw2gz](LIT-tmphw2gz.md), which narrows
the bandit result further.
