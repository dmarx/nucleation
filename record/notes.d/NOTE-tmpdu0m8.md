# NOTE-tmpdu0m8: Freyd (1997) — Violations of Power, Adaptive Blindness and Betrayal Trauma Theory

## Contribution

The first statement of DARVO, inside a short essay on betrayal trauma theory for a symposium on the recovered-memory
debate. The essay frames abuse and its aftermath as questions of interpersonal power and dependence; DARVO takes
about two of its eleven pages (pp. 29-30) and is offered as an observation, not tested.

## Key results

1. p. 22: "What happens when power is used to abuse? These are central questions for feminist psychology."
2. p. 27, adaptive blindness: "When the betrayer is someone we are dependent upon, the very mechanisms that normally protect us — sensitivity to cheating and the pain that motivates us to change things so that we will no longer be in danger — become a problem."
3. p. 29, the theory extended to adults: "In cases of oppression by powerful others, when the victim feels dependent upon the oppressor and betrayal occurs, the theory predicts some degree of information loss about betrayal."
4. p. 29, DARVO: "My proposal, currently very speculative, is that a frequent reaction of an abuser to being held accountable is the 'DARVO' response."
5. p. 29, the caveat: "Thus denial is not evidence of guilt." Freyd adds the untested proposal that "a certain kind of indignant self-righteous, and overly stated, denial may in fact relate to guilt."
6. pp. 29-30, the attack: it "typically includes threats of law suits, overt and covert attacks on the whistle-blower's credibility, and so on."
7. p. 30, the reversal: "Figure and ground are completely reversed."
8. p. 30, consequence: "claims of being victim to false accusations may be more speakable than claims of being victim to sexual and domestic offenses."

## Assumptions

The abuse occurred and the person responding is an actual abuser; dependence on the betrayer is what makes not-knowing
adaptive. The evidence for DARVO is Freyd's own observation; none is reported.

## Concepts

Betrayal trauma; adaptive (betrayal) blindness; DARVO; accountability of "violators of power"; the speakability of
claims.

## Bearing on the record

- **The exchange** (A5 §4) describes DARVO accurately and gives Freyd's own caveat, that denial and counter-accusation
  do not by themselves establish it. Its point that self-presentation as a victim changes how observers assign
  responsibility is a hypothesis here and is tested only in Harsey and Freyd (2023), [LIT-tmpx37pv](../literature.d/LIT-tmpx37pv.md).
- **[CLAIM-tmpp8na7](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpp8na7.md)** shares the essay's framing of abuse as a matter of power ("A focus on betrayal is thus implicitly
  a focus on power", p. 28) but takes no evidence from it. No relation.
- **[QUESTION-tmpgyn11](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpgyn11.md)**: adaptive blindness is a way dependence on a party shapes what the dependent can know about
  that party. The mechanism is attachment, not activated obligation, so it is a neighbour of the question, not an
  answer. No relation.
- Nothing held in the record concerns DARVO or responses to accountability.

## Limitations

Speculative on DARVO and without data; framed for childhood sexual abuse in the recovered-memory controversy. It
offers no way to tell a DARVO response from an innocent person's denial and counter-accusation beyond the untested
"overly stated" marker.
