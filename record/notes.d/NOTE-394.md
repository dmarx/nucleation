# NOTE-394: Haslanger — Gender and Race: (What) Are They?

## Contribution

The paper does two things. It names a method: besides asking what our concept
of gender is (conceptual) or what kind our gender talk tracks (descriptive), one
can ask what concept a theory needs and define the term to fit (analytical). And
it applies the method to give the first unified definitions of man, woman and
racialized group as social positions in a hierarchy, with a non-hierarchical
genus (gender, ethnicity) defined above them so that a just society's categories
can be described in the same terms.

## Key insight

Gender is to sex as race is to "color": the social meaning of a body as it is
read. What all women share is not a body, a psychology or a role but a kind of
position: being subordinated because one is taken to be female. Defining the
category that way answers the objection that women share nothing, and it turns
the objection that any definition is normative into a statement of the
definition's purpose.

## Assumptions

- **The analytical method is legitimate.** "It is up to us to decide what in the
  world, if anything, they are" (p. 34). Two conditions govern appropriating an
  ordinary term: a semantic one, that central functions of the term remain
  (p. 35, n. 6), and a political one that the paper does not state in general.
- **A critical framework.** Four concerns fix the purpose: explaining persistent
  inequality, sensitivity to similarity and difference (intersectionality),
  tracking gender and race in phenomena not obviously about them, and taking the
  agency of women and people of color seriously (p. 36). The paper addresses the
  first two.
- **Oppression as structural.** After Frye and Young: "an enclosing structure of
  forces and barriers", sustained by "the normal processes of everyday life"
  (pp. 39–40). The paper says a satisfactory theory of oppression is not
  supplied.
- **A dominant ideology exists**, as a placeholder for "background",
  "hegemony" or "habitus" (n. 14).
- **"Matters of established fact" about race** (p. 43): there are no racial genes
  for the morphologies and cultural patterns associated with races, and racial
  classification varies by context (Brazil and the US). Asserted, without
  citation.

## Key results

Definitions, with the arguments for adopting them.

- **Three projects (pp. 32–34).** Conceptual (reflective equilibrium on our
  concept), descriptive (the kind our usage tracks, possibly social), analytical
  (what concept serves our legitimate purposes). The paper's is analytical,
  linked to the descriptive claim that gender and race talk tracks social kinds
  "typically obscured by the manifest content" of everyday concepts (p. 34).
- **Woman and man, tentative (p. 39).** "S is a woman iffdf S is systematically
  subordinated along some dimension … and S is 'marked' as a target for this
  treatment by observed or imagined bodily features presumed to be evidence of a
  female's biological role in reproduction." Man is the privileged counterpart.
- **Woman and man, refined (p. 42).** Three conditions: (i) S is regularly and for
  the most part observed or imagined to have such features; (ii) having them
  marks S within the dominant ideology as someone who ought to occupy positions
  that are in fact subordinate; (iii) S's satisfying (i) and (ii) plays a role in
  S's systematic subordination along some dimension. Plus *functions as a woman
  in context C* (pp. 42–43), so that, for example, a Black man targeted for
  violence as male may in that context function as neither (p. 41).
- **Racialized group (p. 44).** A group G is racialized relative to C iff its
  members are all and only those observed or imagined to have bodily features
  presumed in C to show ancestral links to a region, marked by the ideology in C
  for positions that are in fact subordinate or privileged, and subordinated or
  privileged in part because of it. Italians, Germans and the Irish have been
  racialized in the US and are not now (p. 45). Note 17 lists four qualifications
  (passing, Brazil, groups not marked by body type, scattered members).
- **Ethnicity (pp. 45, 50–51)** is the non-hierarchical counterpart of race, an
  "idealized conception".
- **Commonality answered (p. 45).** Women share a kind of position, sexually
  marked subordination, whose content varies. Non-oppressed females, if any, are
  not counterexamples: the category is political by design (pp. 45–46). "It is
  part of the project of feminism to bring about a day when there are no more
  women" (p. 46).
- **Normativity accepted (p. 46).** Choosing which facts are significant on
  explicit values is "both inevitable and important"; the definition is more
  likely to offer "a negative ideal".
- **Terminology as politics (pp. 46–48).** Group terms carry prescriptive force;
  to ask what one should be called is to ask what norms one should be judged by.
  The paper asks readers to "refuse to be gendered man or woman, refuse to be
  raced" (p. 48), and says whether the shift is useful depends on context.
- **Gender as genus (p. 50).** A group is a gender relative to C iff its members
  are regularly observed or imagined to have features presumed in C to be
  evidence of reproductive capacities, these mark them in C's ideology as
  motivating some aspect of their social position, and that plays a role in
  their having it. Man and woman are its hierarchical species; others could be
  non-hierarchical (p. 50; diagram, p. 51).
- **Conclusion (pp. 51–52).** Gender and race are real and social, not chosen,
  hierarchical as we know them, and contingent. "The world itself can't tell us
  what gender is" (p. 52).

## Concepts

- **Analytical project** — defining a concept by "what work we want these
  concepts to do for us" (pp. 33–34); later literature calls this ameliorative.
- **Manifest and operative concepts** — the concept users think they apply and
  the one their practice tracks (n. 5, citing Haslanger 1995).
- **"Color"** — the contextually variable physical markers of race, by analogy
  with "sex" for gender, and including the markers of Whiteness (n. 7).
- **Marked** — taken, within the dominant ideology, to have features that make
  one's position appropriate (pp. 40, 42).
- **Functioning as a woman in C** — satisfying the conditions in a context,
  which can come apart from being a woman (pp. 42–43, n. 15).
- **Focal analysis** — explaining norms, symbols and identities as gendered by
  their relation to the social class (p. 37, n. 9).

## Limitations

- The definitions are stipulated for a purpose and defended by that purpose; the
  paper does not claim they capture ordinary meaning (p. 52).
- The theory of oppression and the notion of dominant ideology are placeholders
  (pp. 39–40; n. 14).
- The empirical claims about race are uncited (p. 43).
- The definitions are intersectional only in that the content of a position can
  vary with race and class (p. 39); there is no account of intersectional
  categories as categories.
- Ethnicity is left vague (n. 19).

## Open questions

- What is the political condition on appropriating a term, stated generally
  (p. 35)?
- Should a feminist antiracism construct non-hierarchical genders and
  ethnicities or abolish them (pp. 50–51)? Left for "another occasion".
