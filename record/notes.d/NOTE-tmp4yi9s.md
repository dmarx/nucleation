# NOTE-tmp4yi9s: Stark and Hester (2019) — Coercive Control: Update and Review

## Contribution

The coercive-control model restated by its originator, with the 2007 book's definitions quoted and page-referenced,
and a review of the evidence that control and violence come apart.

## Key results

1. Abstract: "Coercive control is not a type of violence."
2. Violence and Control: "Stark (2007) defines coercion as" "the use of force or threats to compel or dispel a particular" response (p. 228); control is "structural forms of deprivation, exploitation, and" command that compel obedience indirectly (p. 229); together they produce a "condition of unfreedom" (p. 205) experienced as entrapment.
3. Violence and Control: "In perhaps as many as a third of all cases", abuse elicits entrapment "without any notable incidents of" violence; "Control may continue unabated long after violence has ended."
4. Violence and Control: the research "reinforces a core claim of Stark (2007), that the level of ‘control’ independently" increases a woman's risk of serious injury because it "undermines her autonomous capacity to resist or escape abuse"; coercive control predicts post-separation distress "far better than relationship violence".
5. Incident-based law: abusive men used the act-based definition "to disconnect their violent acts from one another in time and space".
6. Same-sex couples: one study found "almost 3 times as many couples engaged in" mutually violent control as in coercively controlling violence; another found mutual abuse did not characterize what was reported.
7. Scope: in heterosexual relationships coercive control's scope and consequence "are inextricably bound to persistent forms of sexual inequality".
8. Candour: advocacy-driven law making "has spun far ahead of evidence-based research".

## Assumptions

Coercive control has duration and harms autonomy and liberty; in heterosexual relationships it is mainly men's. The
authors have "a stake in the policies we describe".

## Concepts

Coercion; control; condition of unfreedom; entrapment; space for action; incidentalist vs pattern definitions;
mutually violent control; social entrapment.

## Limitations

read.
