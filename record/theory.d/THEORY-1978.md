# THEORY-1978: When an agent divides attention among tasks measured with different precision, incentive pay on the measurable tasks draws effort from the unmeasured ones, so the best incentive for any task falls with the difficulty of measuring the others and can be a fixed wage, with asset ownership, limits on activities and job design acting as substitute instruments

Holmström & Milgrom (1991), [LIT-2753](../literature.d/LIT-2753.md).

## The model

A linear principal–agent model with several tasks. Incentive pay "serves
to direct the allocation of the agents' attention among their various
duties" (§1), and "the desirability of providing incentives for any one
activity decreases with the difficulty of measuring performance in any
other activities" (§1). Under Proposition 1 the efficient rule can be a
fixed wage; under Proposition 2 employees, who do not own the asset, get
muted incentives.

## What it does not show

- **Empirical confirmation.** The evidence is cited, not tested.
- **Robustness to complementary tasks.** The attention model assumes
  efforts are perfect substitutes in cost.
- **Organizational budgets.** It concerns an agent's pay, not how an
  organization allocates resources by observed activity.
