## Summary

A letter. A nonequilibrium steady state is pictured as many cells, each in
local equilibrium at its own inverse temperature β, with β fluctuating in
space and time on a scale T much longer than the cells' relaxation time.
Over long times the system's statistics is the Boltzmann factor averaged
over the density f(β): B(E) = ∫ f(β) e^(−βE) dβ, which the authors call
superstatistics. A χ²/gamma f(β) gives Tsallis statistics exactly;
uniform, two-level, lognormal and F densities give other statistics. For
small variance of β all of them share the same first correction to the
Boltzmann factor, which lets an index q = ⟨β²⟩/⟨β⟩² be defined for any f;
they differ at third order and at large E.

## Contribution

- Names and generalizes the construction (Beck's PRL 87, 180601, 2001,
  ref. [5], had the χ² case for a Langevin particle) to any f(β) and any
  energy E: "after averaging over the fluctuations one can obtain as an
  effective description not only Tsallis statistics but an infinite set of
  more general statistics, which we will call 'superstatistics'" (p. 1).
- The small-variance universality result, with a general proof.
- A q for every superstatistics, read as a coefficient of variation.
- The type-A / type-B distinction, type B reducible to type A.

## Key results

1. **The effective Boltzmann factor is a Laplace transform** (eq. 2,
   p. 3). "the generalized Boltzmann factor of superstatistics is given by
   the Laplace transform of the probability density f(β)" (p. 3).
   Ordinary statistics for f(β) = δ(β − β₀). Three admissibility criteria
   (p. 3): f a normalized density; B normalizable (∫ρ(E)B(E)dE finite);
   reduction to Boltzmann–Gibbs without fluctuations.
2. **Gamma gives Tsallis** (eqs. 11–13, pp. 5–6). With
   f(β) = (1/(bΓ(c)))(β/b)^(c−1) e^(−β/b), "B = ... = (1 + bE)^(−c)",
   "the generalized Boltzmann factor (1 + (q − 1)β₀E)^(−1/(q−1)) of
   nonextensive statistical mechanics ... if we identify c = 1/(q−1) and
   bc = β₀. It is normalizable for c > 1 (if ρ(E) = 1)." "The number 2c
   can be interpreted as the effective number of degrees of freedom
   contributing to the fluctuating β" (p. 5); β is a sum of n = 2c squared
   Gaussians (eq. 12). Checked by quadrature (b = 0.5, c = 2): 0.44444,
   0.027778, 0.00038463 against 0.44444, 0.027778, 0.00038447 at E = 1,
   10, 100.
3. **Other densities** (pp. 4–7). Uniform on [a, a + b]:
   B = (1/(bE))(e^(−(β₀−b/2)E) − e^(−(β₀+b/2)E)) (eq. 5; checked by
   quadrature at a = 0.2, b = 1). Two-level: B = e^(−β₀E)·½(e^(bE/2) +
   e^(−bE/2)) (eq. 9; eq. 7 misprints δ(a) for δ(β − a)). Lognormal and F:
   series only (eqs. 16, 20). The F with v = 2 "is a Tsallis distribution
   in β-space, not in E-space" (p. 6).
4. **Universality at small variance** (eqs. 21–22, pp. 7–8). "one always
   obtains the same result for small σ, B = e^(−β₀E)(1 + ½σ²E² +
   O(σ³E³))" (p. 7), by B = e^(−β₀E)⟨e^(−(β−β₀)E)⟩ expanded in central
   moments. Checked: uniform b²/24 (σ² = b²/12), two-level b²/8
   (σ² = b²/4), gamma cb²/2 = (q−1)β₀²/2.
5. **A q for every superstatistics** (eqs. 24–25, p. 8). "(q − 1)β₀² =
   σ², or equivalently q = ⟨β²⟩/⟨β⟩²"; "√(q − 1) = σ/β₀ is just the
   coefficient of variation of the distribution f(β)" (p. 8).
6. **Maximum entropy only to first order** (p. 8). "For sufficiently small
   variance of the fluctuations they all extremize the Tsallis entropies
   ... However, differences arise if σE is not small ... the densities
   given by B(E) do not extremize the Tsallis entropies in general."
7. **Third order discriminates** (eqs. 26–30, pp. 8–9). g(q) = 0
   (uniform, two-level), −⅓(q−1)² (gamma), −⅙(q³−3q+2) (lognormal): "one
   can distinguish between Tsallis statistics and other superstatistics by
   the third-order term g(q)β₀³E³" (p. 9). Gamma's rechecked from
   −c log(1 + bE).
8. **Large-E behaviour is not universal** (p. 7). "While in general the
   large-E behavior is different for all superstatistics (it strongly
   depends on the function f(β)), we now show that the low-E behavior is
   universal."
9. **Type A versus type B** (p. 9). For E = ½u² and gamma f, "type-A
   superstatistics yields q = 1 + 1/c, whereas type-B yields q = 1 +
   2/(2c+1)" (checked: f̃ ∼ β^(1/2) f is gamma with shape c + ½).
10. **Applications cited, not shown**: Taylor–Couette and Lagrangian
    turbulence, e+e− hadron spectra, cosmic rays, fusion plasmas. "In the
    turbulence applications ... the fluctuating intensive quantity is
    related to the energy dissipation rate of the flow" (p. 9). No data.

## Assumptions

- **Time-scale separation**: "This time scale T is much larger than the
  relaxation time that the single cells need to reach local equilibrium"
  (p. 2).
- β > 0 (Gaussian f called "unphysical", p. 4).
- The long-run density is the average over cells visited (t ≫ T), the
  test particle of eq. (1) with β = γ/σ̂².
- ρ(E) = 1 for normalizability unless stated.
- "The central hypothesis of our paper is that generalized Boltzmann
  factors of the form (2) are physically relevant for large classes of
  dynamically complex systems" (p. 3); f(β) is "a priori unknown",
  "determined by the invariant measure of the underlying dynamical system"
  (pp. 3–4).

## Concepts

superstatistics ("the statistics of the statistics"); effective Boltzmann
factor; Laplace transform of the parameter density; χ²/gamma inverse
temperature with 2c degrees of freedom; Tsallis q-exponential and entropic
index q; q − 1 as squared coefficient of variation; small-variance
universality; third-order discriminant g(q); type-A and type-B
superstatistics; separation of local equilibration from parameter
fluctuation.

## Verdict on the characterization

- LIT-2962's summary ("A superposition of Boltzmann statistics over a
  fluctuating intensive parameter ... gives heavy-tailed, Tsallis-like
  distributions") is **right about the construction, half right about the
  result**. The gamma case gives the q-exponential (1 + bE)^(−c), tail
  E^(−c). But the paper never says "power law", "tail" or "heavy" (grep),
  and its main result runs the other way: Tsallis holds universally only
  at small variance, and "for large variances quite different behaviour
  from Tsallis statistics can be generated" (p. 1). Its uniform and
  two-level cases with a > 0 have exponential tails: eq. 5 decays as
  e^(−aE)/(bE) (9.08e−7 at E = 50, a = 0.2, checked).
- "Heterogeneity across a population alone yields a power law" (LIT-2962,
  from A5) **overstates the paper twice**. (i) The setting is one system
  whose intensive parameter fluctuates in space and time, seen by a test
  particle over times ≫ T, not a population of entities; the mathematics
  is the same mixture. (ii) A mixture alone gives a power law only when
  f(β) has power-law weight near β = 0, as the gamma does (∝ β^(c−1), tail
  E^(−c)). Reader's gloss, not the paper's: a lognormal f vanishes faster
  than any power at 0, so its B(E) falls faster than any power.
- A5's caution quoted in CLAIM-769 and CLAIM-795 ("that does not
  necessarily mean any individual entity follows power-law dynamics")
  **fits**: each cell is ordinary Boltzmann–Gibbs; only the average
  departs.
- "Beck & Cohen (2003)" is fine; the journal reference is unverified.

## Limitations

- A letter: the "central hypothesis" (p. 3) is asserted, f(β) is derived
  from no dynamics, and the applications are cited, not shown.
- Universality is a small-σE expansion; nothing is proved about tails
  beyond the closed forms; lognormal and F have no closed form.
- Read as arXiv v2, not the journal text.

## Candidate THEORY

**Sentence.** Beck and Cohen define superstatistics as the effective
Boltzmann factor B(E) = ∫f(β)e^(−βE)dβ, the Laplace transform of the
density of an inverse temperature that fluctuates slowly compared with
local equilibration; a gamma (χ²) density gives Tsallis's q-exponential
(1 + bE)^(−c), with c = 1/(q − 1) and 2c the effective degrees of freedom
of the fluctuating environment, and for small variance every
superstatistics has the same first correction e^(−β₀E)(1 + σ²E²/2), so
that q = ⟨β²⟩/⟨β⟩² is defined for all of them, while they differ at third
order and at large E, where a uniform or two-level density keeps an
exponential tail.
