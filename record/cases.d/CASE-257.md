# CASE-257: The public theatres of colonial Saint-Domingue, 1764 to the Revolution, as reported: an institution of French cultural prestige to which enslaved musicians, supernumeraries and builders, and a few free performers of colour, are documented as contributing, while they are documented as excluded from standing in it, seated apart or not admitted, black roles given to whites in blackface, with no part in its rules

## The case

As reported. Version 1 was A's report alone; version 2 corrects it from
the readings of 2026-11-12. Powers ([LIT-2528](../literature.d/LIT-2528.md)) and Prest's book
([LIT-2516](../literature.d/LIT-2516.md)) remain Unavailable, so what comes from them is second-hand.

- **The institution.** Public theatres and opera in the French slave
  colonies, Saint-Domingue above all, 1764–1789 (Powers, [LIT-2528](../literature.d/LIT-2528.md)).
  A3: Powers ties the theatre to religious, military and political power,
  and names "the maintenance of peace, the preservation of social
  hierarchy, and the promotion of French culture" as related colonial
  aims. Unverified: the book was not reached.
- **Seating.** Prest ([LIT-2517](../literature.d/LIT-2517.md), read): in Port-au-Prince "the free
  people of colour were required to sit in fifteen second-tier boxes
  (seven on either side of the auditorium and one at the back)"; in Le Cap
  they "sat in the upper boxes at the back of the auditorium." The
  admission of free people of colour was "systematic, although
  restricted". Free Black women in Le Cap were admitted from 1775, during a
  financial crisis, at first standing in the corridor at the back
  (Sabee in the H-France Forum, [LIT-2521](../literature.d/LIT-2521.md)). A3's *paradis*, the upper
  gallery as the nonwhite section that gives Powers's title its irony,
  is A's or Powers's report and is unverified; Prest does not use the word.
- **Performers.** Black roles were "customarily performed by white actors
  in blackface", and "we have no evidence of any actors of colour until
  the 1780s" ([LIT-2517](../literature.d/LIT-2517.md)). The named performers A3 gives, Minette and her
  sister Lise, were free women of colour of mixed ancestry, not enslaved
  ([LIT-2517](../literature.d/LIT-2517.md); [LIT-2527](../literature.d/LIT-2527.md) on Lise). Minette's rise was "significant,
  though ultimately limited".
- **Enslaved contributors.** Enslaved people took part as orchestral
  musicians, supernumeraries, wigmakers and painters, and as the
  carpenters and builders of the playhouses (the chapter abstracts of
  [LIT-2516](../literature.d/LIT-2516.md), read as front matter only; the H-France Forum). The
  forum reports the theatre as "reliant on the world of enslaved people
  of African descent" (Row, quoting the book, p. 187), and their "near
  invisibility in the theatre records" (Williard, quoting p. 175).
  Prest's evidence includes runaway advertisements placed by
  theatre-makers in the same newspapers as their performances.
- **Spectatorship.** Prest's "mitigated spectatorship": enslaved
  domestics and free women of colour with aural and partial visual access
  (Sabee, [LIT-2521](../literature.d/LIT-2521.md)).
- **Staged slave dance.** White performers staged Europeanized,
  non-threatening slave dance, "pas de nègres" and once a "Calenda ou
  Danse negre", by which colonials sought to control a power they
  attributed to it; they never staged vodou dance, and no staged chica is
  documented ([LIT-2527](../literature.d/LIT-2527.md)). The framework is Roque's colonial imitator,
  not Bhabha's mimicry, as A3 had it.
- **Creole performance.** A3, from Leichman and Bénac-Giroux
  ([LIT-2537](../literature.d/LIT-2537.md)): a performance "might challenge metropolitan French
  cultural authority while leaving plantation slavery intact". The
  editors' blog, the only part of the volume read, does not carry this;
  it is A3's inference. "Pale imitations" reads the Créole theatre's slave
  dances as colonial control.

## Observation and interpretation

The owner's review of [#488](https://github.com/dmarx/nucleation/issues/488) on 2026-11-13: the earlier title "combines
documented exclusion with a claim about the causal relationship between
contribution and authority. I would distinguish the observed allocation
of standing from the mechanism that maintained it. The latter is
precisely what the new [QUESTION-461](../questions.d/QUESTION-461.md) asks us to investigate."

- **Observed**, as reported above: enslaved people and a few free people
  of colour contributed to the theatre's productions and buildings; free
  people of colour were seated apart, free Black women admitted late and
  at first to the corridor; black roles went to whites in blackface; no
  source reached shows any of the contributors with a part in the
  theatre's rules.
- **Interpretation, not shown**: that the segregated seating, the casting
  and colonial law are what kept the contributions from giving their
  makers standing in it. Other mechanisms fit the same allocation, among
  them slavery's legal order acting outside the theatre altogether (A6 §5;
  [QUESTION-449](../questions.d/QUESTION-449.md)), and the theatre's financial need widening access without
  standing (the H-France Forum on 1775). Which mechanism maintained the
  allocation, and whether the theatre's dependence on the contributors
  ever moved it, is [QUESTION-461](../questions.d/QUESTION-461.md).

## What it shows, if the reports hold

- **A stable, productive institution whose stability says nothing of its
  terms** ([CLAIM-546](../claims.d/CLAIM-546.md)). A6 §2: "The resulting system might be artistically
  productive and organizationally stable. But none of that establishes
  that the terms of participation were fair."
- **Recognition of an exception without change of the rule** ([CLAIM-403](../claims.d/CLAIM-403.md);
  the shape of [CASE-146](CASE-146.md)). A6 §5: "The recognition of an exception does
  not prove that the rule governing exceptions has changed."
- **A description that serves the institution and merges what its
  workers' claims need** ([CLAIM-348](../claims.d/CLAIM-348.md), [CASE-110](CASE-110.md)). A6 §3: a production
  notice "might preserve the title of an opera, the identity of a
  celebrated performer, or the date of a performance, but provide little
  information about the enslaved people whose work supported the event."
  Here the institution's description is also the historian's evidence;
  see the note of 2026-11-12 on [CLAIM-348](../claims.d/CLAIM-348.md).
- **The question it raises** is [QUESTION-461](../questions.d/QUESTION-461.md): an institution that
  depended on the people it subordinated, and whether that dependence
  ever turned into their authority.

## What it does not show

- **That the theatre produced the dependence.** Slavery's coercion
  predated it. A6 §1: "What we would need to investigate is how
  theatrical specialization altered, reinforced, or occasionally
  challenged those existing relations."
- **That the theatre reproduced the conditions of its non-answerability**
  ([CLAIM-614](../claims.d/CLAIM-614.md), [CLAIM-624](../claims.d/CLAIM-624.md)). A6 §5: proving it "would require more than
  documenting segregated seating or racist dramatic representation", and
  the mechanisms may have been maintained "principally by colonial law
  and state authorities, rather than by theatrical organizations
  themselves", which is [QUESTION-449](../questions.d/QUESTION-449.md)'s question of whose the
  counter-normativity is.
- **That the theatre closed a loop of self-maintenance.** A6 §1: "The
  difficult question is whether this loop actually closes", an empirical
  question ([QUESTION-399](../questions.d/QUESTION-399.md)).
- **Anything of the performers' own view.** A3: the archive "preserves
  the voices of enslavers far more readily than those of enslaved
  performers."
