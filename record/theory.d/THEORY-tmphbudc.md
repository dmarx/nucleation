# THEORY-tmphbudc: Warrant for a type-level causal claim in biomedical and social science comes from eliminating the relevant alternative accounts of its support, not from the kind of study that produced the data, so a randomised trial earns its standing only by eliminating many alternatives at once

## Source

Reiss (2015), [LIT-118](../literature.d/LIT-118.md) (*Philosophy of Science* 82(3)), read in full in
[NOTE-197](../notes.d/NOTE-197.md) from the February 2015 Durham CHESS working paper. Page references
are the working paper's.

## What was actually shown

**Support and warrant (§2, pp. 5–7).** Evidence is two things. Support is a
fact relevant to a hypothesis h. Warrant is a reason to infer h, and comes in
degrees.

**Support (§3, pp. 9–15).** Deductive entailment by h is neither necessary
nor sufficient for support: a "biased coin" hypothesis entails no particular
data set, and a cause acting by two cancelling routes entails no correlation
(Fig. 1). Support is a pattern in the data "we are entitled to expect to
obtain under the supposition that h is true" (p. 12). Direct support bears on
h. Indirect support is a pattern incompatible with an alternative that could
account for h's support.

**Relevance and elimination (§§4.2–4.3).** An alternative is relevant only
with an empirical reason, generic (selection bias for observational data) or
case-specific. Eliminating one is a judgement, not a proof, and values enter
it: when stakes are high, "the standards for rejecting an alternative should
be lower" (p. 23).

**Stopping (§4.4, pp. 24–27).** A default entitlement to other scientists'
claims, and four guidelines: large effects, specific manner and timing, small
inferential distance, and the costs of further inquiry against its benefits.

**Grades (§5, Table 1, p. 29).** Proof, when all relevant alternatives are
eliminated; strong warrant, all salient and some others; moderate, most
including some salient; weak, some. "Proof" is empirical and fallible.

**Against experimentalism (§6, pp. 31–33).** Treating randomised experiments
as basic cannot explain why non-experimental data count at all. The
elimination account explains both why trials succeed, removing "all
alternative causal hypotheses – in one fell swoop", and how they fail, through
measurement, coding and pooling errors that randomisation does not prevent.

**What could have come out otherwise.** Nothing in the paper could have. The
smoking case is an illustration: whether the framework delivers "is, alas, a
matter for future research" (§7, p. 35).

## What this does not say

- **That it holds outside its scope.** It is restricted to type-level causal
  hypotheses in domains where randomised experiments "can be and are
  frequently employed", and excludes most of physics and chemistry, in vitro
  work and the historical sciences (§1; §2).
- **How to settle what is relevant.** What one is "entitled to expect", which
  alternatives to consider and when to stop are handed to context and
  background knowledge, with no account of how disputes over context are
  settled. The four open questions are relocated, not answered.
- **That the grades are principled.** Exactly three grades short of proof is
  "arbitrary" by the author's concession, and grading needs judgements of
  "most" and "some" that the theory does not fix.
- **That there is "no gold standard whatsoever".** That follows from the
  framework and is asserted, not argued independently ([NOTE-197](../notes.d/NOTE-197.md), C8).
- **That probability is the wrong model of credence.** Reiss says so, but in a
  footnote, by citation of Norton (fn. 3).
- **How lowered thresholds are kept from motivated reasoning.** Values may
  lower the bar for rejecting an alternative, and the paper does not say how
  that is kept honest.

## Connections

- Worrall ([LIT-175](../literature.d/LIT-175.md)) reaches an overlapping conclusion from inside the
  randomised-trial debate: telling evidence tells against plausible rivals,
  and randomisation has no special standing beyond that ([NOTE-197](../notes.d/NOTE-197.md),
  Connections).
- The value-laden rejection of alternatives is the indirect role of values
  that Douglas argues for ([LIT-139](../literature.d/LIT-139.md); [THEORY-tmp5dktv](THEORY-tmp5dktv.md)). Reiss does not cite her.
  Whether "values lower the bar" licenses the direct role she forbids is open.
