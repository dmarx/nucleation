# THEORY-322: The sense of agency over an action's outcome draws on prospective signals from action selection and outcome prediction, not only on retrospective comparison of outcome with prediction

## Source

Haggard (2017), [LIT-1009](../literature.d/LIT-1009.md), *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 18(4), read in full
from the accepted manuscript in [NOTE-827](../notes.d/NOTE-827.md): "Action selection"
(manuscript pp. 7–9), "The comparator model of agency" (pp. 9–11),
"Prospective versus retrospective agency" (pp. 11–12), "Brain mechanisms"
(pp. 12–14), "Pathological sense of agency" (pp. 14–16) and Fig. 4.

## What was actually shown

The review reports three kinds of evidence. Sense of agency here means
the experience of controlling a specific motor act and its outcome,
measured mostly by intentional binding, the compression of the perceived
interval between action and outcome.

**Prediction without the outcome.** A voluntary action produced a tone on
some trials. On trials where the tone came, the action was perceived as
shifted further towards it, which is a retrospective effect. On trials
with no tone, the shift was larger in blocks where a tone was likely (75%)
than where it was less likely (50%). So "an additional, prospective
component of the sense of agency is present even if the outcome does not
in fact occur" (pp. 11–12). This could have come out otherwise: no-tone
trials could have shown no block difference.

**Its loss in psychosis.** In a larger patient study, action binding in
schizophrenia "depended entirely on the actual occurrence of the tone",
while in healthy volunteers it "depended on the probability that the tone
might occur" (p. 16, Fig. 4).

**Selection fluency.** Masked primes that eased or hindered the choice of
a keypress raised or lowered explicit judgements of control over a colour
outcome that the primes did not predict. Further experiments ruled out
reaction time as the cue (pp. 8–9). Angular-gyrus activity tracked low
agency ratings only after incompatible primes, and TMS over it at prime
time removed the effect (p. 13). A meta-analysis of seven tDCS studies
found that stimulation of left DLPFC enhanced binding only where
participants chose between actions (p. 8).

Against this the review sets the comparator model, on which agency is the
absence of a prediction error and is computed after delayed feedback
arrives, so that "people experience a sense of agency only
retrospectively" (p. 11). Its conclusion: the circuit "operates both
prospectively ... as well as retrospectively", and the post hoc inference
view "has perhaps been overstated" (p. 18).

## What this does not say

- **It does not say the experience tracks actual control.** The review
  calls the selection-fluency contribution "in a sense, illusory" (p. 9).
  A prospective signal is by its nature present before anything has been
  controlled. The claim is about what the experience is computed from, not
  about its accuracy.
- **It does not say retrospective inference is absent.** Both components
  are reported, and the claim is "not only".
- **It does not locate the computation.** The frontal–parietal
  connectivity proposal (p. 14) is an argument from where activations
  fall, and is not filed.
- **It is a review.** The prospective component rests on one binding
  design and its patient extension, reported, not read here. The review's
  author ran or co-authored most of the studies cited for it.
- **It is about the motor-instrumental sense of agency.** The review sets
  aside self-efficacy and the social attribution of authorship (p. 2,
  p. 6). Nothing here bears on whether a person experiences herself as the
  author of her life or values.

## Connections

No relation is declared: no open question asks how the experience of
agency is computed.

- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** Deficit evidence (schizophrenia) dissociates the two
  components, and the review finds explicit and implicit measures only
  weakly correlated (p. 5). Both bear on what different kinds of evidence
  establish about an experience.
