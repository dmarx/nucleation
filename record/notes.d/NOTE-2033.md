# NOTE-2033: Natali, Naiseh, Cabitza and Frischmann (2025) — Better AI with Designed Friction: Theories, Applications and Research Agenda

## Contribution

A definition of designed friction for human–AI decision-making, a short list of the mechanisms proposed for it in HCI and hybrid-intelligence work, and an agenda for a research community. It is a poster, and it reports no results.

## Key results

1. Abstract: designed friction is "deliberate inefﬁciencies designed to foster engagement, critical reason[ing], and user agency".
2. §1: "intentional design interventions that introduce interaction friction [4] at critical points", against inappropriate reliance; "The challenge lies in calibrating friction effectively to enhance decision-making" without frustration.
3. §2: Frischmann and Vardi's prosocial friction "introduces speed bumps in user interactions to preserve human oversight and prevent passive AI reliance".
4. §2: programmed inefficiencies "require active user engagement before presenting AI recommendations", including pauses before revealing outputs and "intentionally slower algorithms"; in AI provocations "systems challenge rather than obey the user".
5. §3: users in low-context cultures "may perceive additional veriﬁcation steps as bureaucratic barriers" (asserted, not shown).
6. §4: "Designed friction challenges the dominant view that smoother UX is always better", and can help "establish a culture of designer accountability".

## Assumptions

That automation bias is the main cost of frictionless AI; that friction can be calibrated to aid judgement without excessive load; a high/low-context cultural contrast offered without evidence.

## Concepts

Designed friction; interaction friction; prosocial friction; speed bumps; seamful design; programmed inefficiencies; cognitive forcing functions; AI provocations; automation bias; appropriate reliance; deskilling; designer accountability.

## Bearing on the record

- **[TERM-125](../terms.d/TERM-125.md)**: the poster defines friction by inefficiency, added time or effort, which is the delay sense the term sets aside, and never by dependence on another party. Two of its mechanisms are friction in the term's sense all the same: a pause before an output, or a required justification, is a condition the user cannot pass alone because the designer set it. A neighbouring sense the term should list: designed interaction friction.
- **[CLAIM-710](../claims.d/CLAIM-710.md)**: no support. The friction here is cognitive and inside one user's interaction, not procedural friction between parties in an institution (as A23 §7 itself separates them). It does show a case the claim's gloss does not cover. Taking the AI pipeline as the executor, a pause makes the machine's proposal visible to a human before it becomes the decision, which is "exposes" in the owner's sense. Taking the human as the executor, the friction exposes the decision to no one but its maker, and the opportunity it opens is for self-correction.
- **[THEORY-1765](../theory.d/THEORY-1765.md)**: the poster's cited evidence for cognitive forcing functions is Buçinca et al. ([LIT-2283](../literature.d/LIT-2283.md)), which the record holds, with the cost the poster calls calibration: people liked and trusted those designs less.
- **[QUESTION-471](../questions.d/QUESTION-471.md)**: designed friction against automation bias is the human–AI form of the question how an automated record keeps incorporation from outrunning evaluation.
- **The exchange**: A21 §2's description is accurate, though "develops" is generous for a poster. A23 §7 rightly files it under cognitive, not institutional, friction.

## Limitations

A poster abstract: no method, no data, a one-line definition; it does not say who bears the friction or who gains the opportunity to intervene.

## From the reading of 2026-11-17

*Quotations here are carried from the reading of 2026-11-17, whose filer rechecked them against its copy on 2026-11-18; they were not rechecked at the reconciliation.*

- **The problem** (§1, p. 518): "this frictionless approach can lead to
  automation bias—where users uncritically accept AI recommendations".
- **Mechanisms** (§2, p. 519): "Cognitive forcing functions can involve
  checklists, justification prompts, and alternative hypothesis
  generation"; AI provocations prompt users "to justify their own
  AI-assisted decisions".
- **Claimed effects** (§4, p. 519): "frictional AI fosters deliberation,
  strengthens accountability, and preserves human agency by preventing
  passive automation reliance".
- **Concepts it adds**: frictional AI; microboundaries.
- **Assumption it names**: overreliance is treated as a property of the
  individual user; the organizational setting that fixes the user's
  authority, time and incentives is outside the frame.
- **[CLAIM-tmp238zy](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp238zy.md)** (cognitive friction differs from friction that makes the
  institution answerable): corroborates its premise. Every mechanism acts
  on the user's thinking; none gives the user authority to refuse, suspend
  or escalate. The poster also shows the slippage the claim warns of:
  friction "strengthens accountability" without saying whose or to whom,
  and "designer accountability" concerns designers, not answerability to
  the people affected. Evidence of the framing, not an argument against it.
- **Neighbours in the record**: Bainbridge's "Ironies of automation", its
  reference [2], is held ([LIT-2222](../literature.d/LIT-2222.md), [LIT-2225](../literature.d/LIT-2225.md)); [LIT-2224](../literature.d/LIT-2224.md) (AI
  assistance and skill decay) bears on its agenda item on skill retention.
  Frischmann and Benesch, "Friction-in-design regulation" (2023), the
  regulatory form of the idea, is cited and not held; it may be worth
  registering.
