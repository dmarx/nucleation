# LIT-tmpofhpt: Large AI models are cultural and social technologies

Henry Farrell, Alison Gopnik, Cosma Shalizi and James Evans (2025),
*Science* 387(6739):1153–1156, Policy Forum — DOI-10.1126/science.adt9819.
Read from the authors' accepted version.

## Key takeaways

- **The thesis is a classification, not a prediction.** Large models
  "should not be viewed primarily as intelligent agents, but as a new kind
  of cultural and social technology, allowing humans to take advantage of
  information other humans have accumulated." The question of their
  effects is then asked separately, and the paper insists that calling
  them cultural technologies raises, not lowers, their likely impact.
- **Two lineages combined.** As cultural technologies (pictures, writing,
  print, video, search) they give access to information others created.
  As social technologies, in Simon's sense of "artificial systems of human
  society" (markets, bureaucracies, democracies), they also reorganize it.
  Prices, election results, GDP and bureaucratic categories are "lossy
  (i.e., incomplete, selective, and uninvertible) but useful
  representations"; a large model is a lossy JPEG of its corpus in the
  same sense (Chiang's phrase).
- **The case against agency is short.** Agency here means perceiving and
  acting on a changing world, building and revising models of it and
  setting novel goals. Large models do not do this: hallucination is
  endemic because they "have no conception of truth and falsity", and
  scaling "won't change this". This is asserted, not argued.
- **Why we see agents.** Stories pass on information through fictional
  agents that listeners know are not real; "Chatbots are the successor to
  Hercules, Anansi, and Peter Rabbit", and markets and states are likewise
  treated as agents. Behind the interface the human contribution is
  everywhere: corpora, RLHF raters, prompt engineers, users. A cover-letter
  request is "a technically mediated relationship with thousands of
  earlier job applicants".
- **One stated mechanism.** Models reproduce sequence probabilities well
  on average, so they are most accurate where data are common and least
  where rare or novel, which might worsen Weberian homogenization. Against
  this: deliberately diversified "society-like" ecologies of models might
  keep or recombine perspectives. Neither is tested.
- **Political economy.** The old tension between producers and
  distributors of information is sharpened by centralized ownership;
  countervailing institutions (editors, libel law, deposit insurance, the
  SEC) did not arise on their own but from sustained effort.

The record's cluster on LLM selfhood reads large models through the
simulator/simulacra account ([LIT-460](LIT-460.md)) and Shanahan's later papers ([LIT-465](LIT-465.md),
[LIT-466](LIT-466.md)). This paper declines that question and sets the models beside
markets and bureaucracies, which is why it is held here with
`social-science` first. Its explanation of why chatbots look like agents
(fiction's illustrative agents) is close to [LIT-460](LIT-460.md)'s role play but is
reached from the other side: [LIT-460](LIT-460.md) starts from the model and finds a
character; this paper starts from the culture and finds a storytelling
device.

An anthology topic, deployment-and-society, could hold it, and it has
programmatic remarks for AI practice ("thinking in this way might reshape
AI practice"), so it carries `anthology-candidate`. It is kept here
because its argument is social theory.
