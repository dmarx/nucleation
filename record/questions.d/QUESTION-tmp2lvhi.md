# QUESTION-tmp2lvhi: What makes one agent's authority to decide about another's body legitimate when the other has standing but cannot decide: the other's welfare, their earlier wishes, their projected future wishes, or the relation between them, and why do these differ between a parent's consent for an infant and an advance directive for one's own later self?

## Why it is a question

A3 separates three rights that ordinary arguments run together: "A right
against unwanted intervention", "A right to receive care or support" and
"A right to authorize decisions on behalf of another". Infant circumcision
turns on the first and third. A9 §5 adds an asymmetry:

- "In an advance directive, there is an earlier exercise of autonomy that
  might govern later circumstances."
- "In neonatal circumcision, there has been no earlier exercise of
  autonomy by the patient; the authorization comes from a different
  person."

[QUESTION-364](QUESTION-364.md) asks what makes a revised will the continuation of the same
agent's will; the record has nothing on authority that passes between
agents. A9's proposed test: compare medically necessary neonatal surgery
with elective circumcision, holding fixed the inability to consent and the
permanence of the intervention, and varying necessity, expected welfare,
postponability and the parents' reasons, "to identify which differences
actually justify surrogate authority".

What would count as an answer: a ground for surrogate authority that sorts
those cases, and says whether the future adult's interest in deciding
(Feinberg's "open future", which Millum argues reduces to existing rights,
[LIT-tmpapwfu](../literature.d/LIT-tmpapwfu.md)) is a ground of its own.

## Bearing

  of the asymmetry.

## Note of 2026-11-12: formation and maintenance provenance of an authorization, the assistant's, accepted by the owner

The fifth of the ideas offered after filing the exchange, accepted by the
owner on 2026-11-12. [TERM-100](../terms.d/TERM-100.md)'s senses of provenance sort the cases this
question names, if an authorization is treated as a capacity with a
provenance. An advance directive has formation provenance in the patient's
own earlier act and maintenance provenance in others, the proxies and
institutions that keep it in force once the patient cannot. Parental
permission for an infant is formed and maintained by another. An
adolescent's assent beside parental permission is formed partly by the
patient and maintained jointly. A9 §5's asymmetry is then a difference in
formation provenance with maintenance held fixed, and the open-future
argument asks whether formation by another can be legitimate when
maintenance will pass to the patient. [TERM-100](../terms.d/TERM-100.md) notes that only maintenance
provenance can be read off a present organization. If so, a surrogate's
authority cannot be checked from the present arrangement alone, which is
[CLAIM-518](../claims.d/CLAIM-518.md)'s point about histories. An interpretive mapping, not an answer,
so it is filed as a note. The wording is the assistant's (Claude, in this
session); adopting it is the owner's call.

## Note of 2026-11-12: Millum (2025), read

From the reading in [NOTE-tmp4k6g1](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp4k6g1.md)
([LIT-tmpapwfu](../literature.d/LIT-tmpapwfu.md)), the reader's assessment
as checked by the filer: **answers one sub-question and sharpens the
asymmetry.** The question asks whether "the future adult's interest in
deciding" is a ground of its own; Millum argues it is not: what it protects
is bodily integrity plus the child's interests. Two further points bear on
the question's advance-directive half. (1) His ground for autonomy as demand
is present capacity, and he holds that a competent person's earlier consent
does not bind against her present refusal; that gives earlier wishes no
authority over a competent later self. (2) He does not address an earlier
competent self governing a later incompetent one, so the advance-directive
side is left open. He lists pediatric circumcision only as a topic where the
right has been cited (note 4, Darby); he does not analyse it.

## Note of 2026-11-12: Nix (2024), Operationalizing the Intolerable Suffering Criterion in Advance Requests for MAID for People Living with Dementia in Canada, read

From the reading in [NOTE-tmp3t5qf](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp3t5qf.md) ([LIT-tmpbnl6c](../literature.d/LIT-tmpbnl6c.md)), the reader's assessment as
checked by the filer: **Direct bearing**: Nix's answer for Canadian MAID is
that the earlier wish cannot by itself authorize; welfare at the time of
provision (occurrent intolerable suffering) is necessary, and the earlier
wish only selects among actions the duty of care already permits. It is an
instance of the "welfare" answer gating the "earlier wishes" answer, within
one legal framework.

## Note of 2026-11-12: The Brussels Collaboration on Bodily Integrity, read

From the reading in [NOTE-tmpzb2cf](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpzb2cf.md) ([LIT-tmp4zfda](../literature.d/LIT-tmp4zfda.md)), the reader's assessment as
checked by the filer: **bears directly, gives one answer the question lists,
and supplies A9's test variables**. The paper rejects welfare (third-party
benefit–risk judgment) as the ground for nonvoluntary intimate procedures
and grounds the limit in the future person's interest in deciding, a right
"held in trust" (results 5, 7): projected future agency, not earlier wishes.
It sorts A9's comparison exactly by the variables A9 names: medically
necessary, non-postponable surgery is permitted with parental permission;
elective procedures are not, whatever the parents' reasons or expected
welfare. Its asymmetry argument (result 6) is a reason, independent of
welfare, why surrogate authority should default to postponement when a later
decision remains possible. It does not discuss advance directives, so the
asymmetry the question names is not addressed, and it presupposes, rather
than defends, that the open-future interest is a ground of its own, which is
what Millum ([LIT-tmpapwfu](../literature.d/LIT-tmpapwfu.md)) disputes. Its restriction to genital anatomy
rests on that anatomy's social meaning, so it does not offer a general
