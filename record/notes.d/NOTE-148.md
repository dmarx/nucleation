# NOTE-148: Baedke — The Organism (Cambridge Element)

## Contribution

Organisms are central to biology, yet it is hard to say what an organism is. After decades in which the gene dominated, fields such as epigenetics, niche construction theory and evo-devo again treat the organism as a causally efficacious, autonomous and active unit that shapes its own development and evolution. The Element examines this "return of the organism" as integrated history and philosophy of science. It covers conceptual, biotheoretical, historical, sociopolitical and anthropological dimensions, and proposes answers to the challenges facing organism-centred biology in the twenty-first century.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1 Introduction (pp. 1–4): starts from Goodwin's complaint that organisms have vanished as centres of causal agency. It is organised around three bundles of questions: historical organism-first positions, how to ground biology theoretically on the organism, and what organism-centred biology means for human self-understanding.
- §3.3 "Two Conceptual Challenges" (pp. 23–24): the new organism-centred biology rests on two "cornerstones": contextualising genes and cells in development, and organisms' reciprocal construction of their environment (Lewontin). This yields an "Inward Challenge" (what internal organization constitutes individuality) and an "Outward Challenge" (how to separate the organism from an environment it co-constructs).
- §4 (pp. 24–52): compares early-20th-century organicism and holism ("No Vitalism, No Mechanism") with current approaches. Both tend to end in anti-individualistic positions by stressing self-maintenance, which organisms share with other systems, and by neglecting organism–environment boundaries.
- §4.3 (pp. 38ff.): the proposed framework. On the organizational side, organisms are "overcomers" that actively destabilize and reorganize their closure regimes, going beyond the closure view of Montévil et al. 2016, and the account is illustrated with three empirical cases. On the relational side, a model is offered for individuating organisms despite reciprocal interaction with the environment.
- §5 (pp. 52–55): sociopolitical stakes. Postgenomic, organism-centred views undercut genetic determinism, but organismal and plasticity framings can also feed new exclusionary and racist frameworks.
- §6 Epilogue (pp. 55–58): organism-centred biology should expand gene-centred biology, not replace it "in a quasi-Kuhnian manner". The open work is to set the epistemic and explanatory standards for organismal explanations.

## Open questions

- A compact, current reference point for biological individuality and organismal agency, useful to nucleation notes on agency and on individuation. Check the three "overcomer" cases in §4.3 and whether the criterion really picks out organisms uniquely.
- The inward/outward challenge split is a reusable frame for any agency or individuality note, including debates on whether artificial systems are organism-like (compare [LIT-211](../literature.d/LIT-211.md) on definitions of life and [LIT-120](../literature.d/LIT-120.md) on autopoiesis).
- The relational individuation model in §4.3 was not read in detail and should be before it is cited.
