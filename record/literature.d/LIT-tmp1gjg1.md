# LIT-tmp1gjg1: Three types of phenomenal consciousness and their functional roles: unfolding the ALARM theory of consciousness

Albert Newen and Carlos Montemayor (2025), *Philosophical Transactions of the
Royal Society B* 380(1939):20240314, theme issue "Evolutionary functions of
consciousness" — DOI-10.1098/rstb.2024.0314

## Key takeaways

- **Two levels of experience, three functions.** Basic arousal (level 1) is
  signal-driven, uses short-term and procedural memory, and has one
  function, alarming the bodily agent: (i) triggering survival behaviour,
  (ii) caring for the body, (iii) initiating generalised, even one-shot,
  learning. General alertness (level 2) adds selective, goal-directed
  attention and the interaction of short- and long-term memory, and its
  function is advanced learning and decision-making. Reflexive
  self-consciousness is the same level-2 experience with metacognitive,
  self-relational content, and its function is future-directed long-term
  planning that takes one's own or another's mindset into account (Table 1).
- **Basic arousal does not need cortex.** The evidence offered is all cited:
  hydranencephalic children (Merker), cats decorticated in infancy that
  still orient and solve a T-maze discrimination, and macaques under
  anaesthesia that wake to central lateral thalamus stimulation and fall
  back when it stops, responding to pain but unable to do tasks that need
  focused attention (Redinbaugh et al. 2020). More complex consciousness
  needs cortex, modulated by the thalamus. Merker's stronger claim, that
  consciousness never depends on cortex, is rejected.
- **The leading theories cover only general alertness.** IIT (posterior
  cortex), GNWT (prefrontal cortex) and Lau's higher-order theory
  (prefrontal and parietal monitoring) all make neocortex essential. None
  constrains itself by evolutionary function, and so none accounts for basic
  arousal. The authors claim to stay "neutral" on which of the three wins.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, with Maldarelli & Güntürkün
([LIT-tmpulpg8](LIT-tmpulpg8.md)) from the same theme issue, for the record's holdings on the
evolution and function of consciousness. No anthology topic holds it, and it
carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.

**It rivals Veit ([LIT-105](LIT-105.md)) on the original function of consciousness.** Both
are evolutionary, affect-first accounts: the first consciousness was felt
bodily value, and it was an adaptation. They exclude each other on what that
first consciousness did. For Veit's pathological complexity thesis, as read
in [NOTE-187](../notes.d/NOTE-187.md), hedonic valence originated as a common currency for *trade-offs*
in action selection among animals with many degrees of freedom; motivational
trade-offs are his criterion for attributing consciousness. For ALARM, basic
arousal gives the alarm signal "100% weight" and runs "rather rigid
behavioural programmes without choices"; choice first appears with general
alertness ("The various functions of phenomenal consciousness"). An affect whose first job is to arbitrate between options,
and one whose first job is to remove the option, cannot both be the
original function. The two also split on learning: ALARM accepts that basic
arousal brings a new quality of learning, close to Ginsburg and Jablonka's
unlimited associative learning (fn. 1), while Veit holds that unlimited
associative learning explains later features of consciousness, not its
origin. Neither cites the other.

The rest of the record it touches:

- **Damasio & Carvalho ([LIT-388](LIT-388.md)) and [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md).** The closest support. They
  place the first integrated body maps, and feeling, in the upper brainstem,
  not the insula, as basic arousal requires. ALARM's alarm is a special case
  of [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md)'s claim that felt affect is the experience of the body's
  regulatory state, valenced by what it means for staying alive: the case
  where a variable leaves its tolerance range. ALARM sides against
  prediction-only accounts in one sentence: pain alarms "in ways that mere
  predictions concerning statistical probabilities of likelihood cannot
  capture" ("Two framework observations", a). That bears on [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md)'s open question, prediction
  (Barrett, [LIT-542](LIT-542.md)) versus map (Damasio), but it is asserted, not argued.
  Damasio & Damasio ([LIT-387](LIT-387.md), unread) state the same affect-first origin.
- **Maldarelli & Güntürkün ([LIT-tmpulpg8](LIT-tmpulpg8.md))**, in the same issue, argue that
  GNWT's requirements are met by the bird's nuclear, non-cortical pallium.
  That contradicts ALARM's reading of GNWT as needing prefrontal cortex: GNWT
  may need a hub-and-module connectome rather than neocortex. The two papers
  also differ on the mirror test, which ALARM takes as a test of reflexive
  self-consciousness that magpies pass, and which Maldarelli and Güntürkün
  doubt, noting a failed magpie replication.
- **IIT 3.0 ([LIT-445](LIT-445.md)) and Barrett et al. ([LIT-185](LIT-185.md)).** ALARM's objection is
  that IIT's axioms are introspective and unconstrained by evolution or cost,
  and that modular minds sit badly with a measure of integration. This is
  close in spirit to Veit's complaint, in [NOTE-187](../notes.d/NOTE-187.md), that IIT rests on
  unrevisable axioms. It is weaker than Barrett et al.'s, that Φ is
  ill-defined for real systems.
- **Gallagher ([LIT-453](LIT-453.md))** splits the minimal self into a sense of ownership
  and a sense of agency. ALARM uses those two, adds a sense of perspectivity,
  and grades each from a pre-reflexive form (with basic arousal) to a
  reflexive one (with general alertness and metacognition). [THEORY-064](../theory.d/THEORY-064.md), from
  Metzinger ([LIT-568](LIT-568.md)), says that what is experienced as a self is the
  content of a self-model the system cannot recognise as a model. ALARM's
  reflexive self-consciousness is a self-model the system does represent as
  its own, built on the pattern theory of self (fn. 4). The two are not
  rivals: they describe different levels.
- **Chalmers ([LIT-406](LIT-406.md)).** ALARM sets epiphenomenalism aside in its
  Introduction and argues only about what consciousness does. It is an
  answer to easy problems in Chalmers's sense, the functions of
  consciousness, by design.
