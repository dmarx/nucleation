summary: >-
  A web survey of 422 adult children of Holocaust survivors, a convenience sample recruited through survivor and Jewish organizations, tests Danieli's 1998 framework, Trauma and the Continuity of Self, as a path model. The children rated their parents' posttrauma styles (victim, numb, fighter), their own "reparative adaptational impacts" and their family's history. Parents' Holocaust experiences, internment above all, were linked to the children's impacts only indirectly, through the parents' victim style, and those indirect effects were small (.05 to .06). Post-war family milieu mattered more than wartime history. Settling in Israel rather than North America, surviving grandparents, larger surviving families and religious continuity went with milder impacts. Broken generational linkages (rarely thinking of one's murdered grandparents as one's own, and remembering the family's history "only in bits and pieces") had the largest total effect, and the only direct one. There was no comparison group and every measure came from the child.
