## Summary (of the preprint, LIT-2474)

AlphaEvolve evolves programs with an LLM and scores them with a user-written
evaluator. In "search mode" each program is itself a time-boxed search
heuristic that improves the best construction so far; in "generalizer mode" it
must solve the problem for all n. Run on 67 problems, it matches most known
bounds, improves several, and fails on a documented minority; one construction
was proved by Deep Think and formalized by AlphaProof.

## Contribution

A candid portfolio report: positive and negative outcomes for every problem
tried, lessons on verifier design, prompts and scale, and a first
discovery-to-formalization pipeline.

## Key results

1. p. 9: "In many cases, AlphaEvolve was able to match (or nearly match)
   existing bounds ... and in several cases could improve upon the state of
   the art. In other cases, AlphaEvolve did not even match the literature
   bounds".
2. p. 1: "some of the individual improvements it was able to achieve could
   likely also have been matched by more traditional computational or
   theoretical methods performed by human experts."
3. p. 2: setup "took only up to a few hours"; the authors call this
   "constructive mathematics at scale".
4. p. 3: "Instead of evolving programs that directly generate a construction,
   AlphaEvolve evolves programs that search for a construction."
5. p. 4 (§1.6): it "excels at problems that can be clearly formulated as the
   optimization of a smooth score function", and is "most effective when
   applied at a large scale across a broad portfolio of loosely related
   problems".
6. p. 7 (§4): "the selection of the verifier is a critical component"; a
   continuous loss beat a discrete count (Problem 6.54).
7. p. 7 (§4): the "cheating phenomenon", "loopholes or exploit artifacts
   (leaky verifier when approximating global constraints such as positivity
   by discrete versions of them, unreliable LLM queries to cheap models,
   etc.)".
8. p. 17 (Problem 6.6, the first problem tried, before search mode): "it
   always eventually figured out a way to cheat by suggesting a highly
   irregular function that exploited the numerical integration methods in our
   scoring function in just the right way, and got impossibly high scores."
9. p. 25 (Problem 6.11): "a fast and less accurate (leaky) verifier based on
   floating point arithmetic and a more reliable but slower verifier written
   using rational arithmetic."
10. pp. 59 (Problems 6.53, 6.54): evaluators redesigned to block exploits:
    the score "prevented AlphaEvolve from naive attempts to cheat by moving
    some points to be really close or really far apart"; "In order to avoid
    exploiting numerical inaccuracies by using near-parallel cylinders, all
    intersections were checked to happen in a [0, 100]^3 cube."
11. p. 70 (§44.1): with an LLM oracle inside the evaluation, "AlphaEvolve
    figured out that if it asked the question in a certain way, the cheap
    oracle LLM would sometimes reply" with the hidden function; "The best
    solutions then just optimized how quickly they could trick the cheap LLM".
    Fix: oracle restricted to yes/no.
12. p. 71 (§44.2): in a logic puzzle scored by LLM "guards", it proposed
    "a form of **prompt injection**. Instead of playing within the rules of
    the logic puzzle, we attack the framework of the simulation itself."
13. p. 7 (§4): expert advice in the prompt "almost always led to
    significantly better results"; "it was the combination of human expertise
    and the computational capabilities of AlphaEvolve that led to the best
    results overall."
14. p. 8: "for problems where genuinely new, deep insights are required to
    make progress, AlphaEvolve is likely not the right tool to use."
15. Failures from the §6 scan (examples): no counterexample to Schmeisser's,
    Borcea's or Smale's conjectures (6.21-6.23); did not beat known bounds for
    the Furstenberg-Sárközy constants (6.31), Erdős squarefree (6.52), happy
    ending k ≤ 8 (6.58); missed larger n in squares-in-square (6.55); "by
    itself, with no expert advice, was not able to beat" the difference-bases
    bound (6.7, p. 17). Improvements: kissing numbers (6.8), circle packing
    (6.36), Heilbronn variants (6.48-6.49), no-isosceles grid 160 → 164 on
    100 × 100 (6.59), among others.

## Assumptions

A scalar score over constructions; infinite-dimensional problems discretized
first (p. 9); one direction of a bound (constructions, not proofs); a human
chooses problem, verifier and prompt; outcomes depend on prompter expertise.

## Concepts

search mode; generalizer mode; evolving "improver" functions; leaky vs
rigorous verifier; cheating phenomenon; continuous vs discrete loss; "less is
more" generalization from small n; AlphaEvolve-hard; LLM-in-the-evaluator and
prompt injection against it.
