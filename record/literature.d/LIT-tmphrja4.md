# LIT-tmphrja4: Evolving Concepts of Epistemic Injustice

Fricker, M. (2017). "Evolving Concepts of Epistemic Injustice". In I. J.
Kidd, J. Medina and G. Pohlhaus Jr (eds.), *The Routledge Handbook of
Epistemic Injustice*, pp. 53–60. Routledge. DOI 10.4324/9781315212043-5.
Author-posted manuscript.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmp3a0fd](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp3a0fd.md).*

- **Two kinds of epistemic injustice.** Testimonial and hermeneutical
  injustice are "discriminatory": someone is "ingenuously downgraded
  and/or disadvantaged in respect of their status as an epistemic
  subject". She leaves room for a distributive kind, a fair share of an
  epistemic good such as education withheld, which also fits the generic
  definition.
- **Testimonial injustice is not deliberate.** Its cause is "a prejudice
  through which the speaker is misjudged and perceived as epistemically
  lesser". Ripley, who manipulates Greenleaf's sexist prejudice to
  discredit Marge, does not misjudge her and so does not do her
  testimonial injustice. Greenleaf does.
- **Hermeneutical injustice is structural.** Its cause is "a background
  inequality of hermeneutical opportunity—specifically, hermeneutical
  marginalisation in relation to some area of social experience". It
  persists "regardless of individual efforts", so good faith in every
  hearer does not prevent it.
- **Non-deliberate is not non-culpable.** Prejudiced thinking is "almost
  always culpable in some degree". A hearer who only pretends not to
  understand commits a related but distinct wrong.
- **Structural, pre-emptive testimonial injustice.** Following Anderson,
  an un-refreshed list of expert witnesses means some people's views are
  never sought, "owing to a structural prejudice". Such injustice is
  necessarily pre-emptive. Once someone speaks it is transactional as
  well.
