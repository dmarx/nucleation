# NOTE-tmpsbl9z: Witnessing and cultural trauma: the role of flashbulb memories in the trauma process

## Contribution

It names a carrier group Alexander's account leaves implicit: the whole
community, made witnesses by their memories of learning of an event. A
flashbulb memory gives someone who saw an attack on television the
standing of a witness. The authors argue that this standing lets the
trauma process begin among equals, before elites and institutions take
it over, and that it can be handed to the next generation.

## Key insight

What a flashbulb memory carries is not the event but one's own place at
it. Quoting Neisser, these memories "are the places that we line up our
own lives with the sources of history itself and say 'I was there'"
(p. 598). Being able to say so is a mark of belonging: "If you do not
have a flashbulb memory of 9/11, then you cannot be considered a 'proper
American'" (p. 602). And a child who knows a parent's memory is "there,"
by extension (p. 608).

## Assumptions

- **Alexander's trauma process.** Events are not traumatic in
  themselves; a carrier group makes them so for a collectivity, through
  speaker, audience and situation (pp. 591–592).
- **Testimony carries feeling, not facts.** A witness's value is to
  convey "what it was like", which statistics cannot (pp. 593, 599).
- **Authenticity in a weak sense.** Not "undisputed origin" but "being
  faithful to the original" (Varga and Guignon, p. 599). The authors then
  let it rest on belief, not accuracy (pp. 605–606).
- **Test–retest consistency stands in for accuracy** in the studies
  cited, on the assumption that a report made in the first days is
  accurate (p. 604).

## Key results

The paper reports no new study. It argues from the following.

- **Alexander, quoted** (pp. 591–594, cited as 2004a). The trauma process
  is how "members of a collectivity [come to] feel that they have been
  subjected to a horrendous event that leaves indelible marks upon their
  group consciousness" (p. 591). "Trauma is not the result of a group
  experiencing pain. It is the result of this acute discomfort entering
  into the core of the collectivity's sense of its own identity"
  (p. 592). The speech-act triad: speaker (the carrier group), audience
  and situation (p. 592). Carriers are constrained by "the uneven
  distribution of material resources" (p. 594).
- **When speaker and audience coincide** (pp. 594–595). For bounded
  events directly experienced by few but followed by all (9/11, the
  Bataclan, Palme's assassination), most members are witnesses, each with
  "a democratic standing as a witness" (p. 595).
- **Flashbulb memories are community-specific** (p. 601). African
  Americans have them for Malcolm X's assassination and European
  Americans do not; the French for Mitterrand's death and French-speaking
  Belgians do not; Britons for Diana and Thatcher's resignation;
  Catholics for John Paul II's death, more so the more involved they
  are. 9/11 produced them in nine countries at first (Luminet et al.
  2004).
- **And community-defining** (pp. 601–602). In Hirst et al.'s ten-year
  study of 9/11, every participant claimed a flashbulb memory, and 0.5%
  failed to give a detailed one after ten years. A year and a half after
  9/11, US citizens' emotional intensity had declined less, and their
  social sharing was greater, than non-US citizens' (Curci and Luminet
  2006).
- **Inaccurate but confident** (pp. 603–606).
  - Navy SEALs in survival training identified their interrogator, under
    high stress, only 30% of the time (Morgan et al. 2004).
  - Over 32 months, the 9/11 reception memory was more consistent than an
    ordinary memory from the same week, but forgotten at a similar rate
    (Talarico and Rubin 2003).
  - After a year, Americans accurately remembered about 60% of six
    features of their 9/11 reception event (Hirst et al. 2009).
  - Confidence stays high where accuracy falls, and listeners, confident
    of their own memories, believe the teller. So witnesses are
    "authentic" "not because their memories are 'reliable' and 'accurate,'
    but because both the witness and those attending to the witness
    believe they are so" (p. 606).
- **Across generations** (pp. 606–608). Hirsch's "postmemory" of the
  Holocaust and psychologists' "vicarious memories" are named; the
  authors do "not mean to equate" them with passing on a flashbulb memory
  (p. 607). Meyler et al. (2020, then in press) asked children nine or
  younger at the time of 9/11:
  - 89% could report a parent's flashbulb memory. 39% reported it
    accurately and in detail, 51% accurately with minimal detail; 11%
    claimed no knowledge of it, against 2% of parents who thought their
    children would not know.
  - Parents' reports of how they felt correlated strongly with children's
    reports of how their parents felt.
  - The child's age, the amount of 9/11 talk with the parent and the
    child's media use did not affect knowledge or accuracy.
  - The more strongly the child identified as American, the more likely
    they were to know the parent's memory and to report it accurately.
    The authors note the direction is unknown: identification may make
    children attend, or knowing may make them feel more American.
- **Not a necessary step** (p. 609). A flashbulb memory "only underscores
  the legitimacy of community members to serve as authentic witnesses".

## Concepts

- **flashbulb memory**: a vivid, confidently held, long-lasting memory of
  the circumstances in which one learned of a public, emotionally charged
  event (the "reception event").
- **witness / eyewitness**: the authors use "eyewitness" for direct
  witnesses and "witness" for anyone who can say "I was there", including
  by a flashbulb memory.
- **authentic witness**: one whose account, by them and their audience, is
  taken as faithful to what it was like.
- **community-specific / community-defining**: held by one community and
  not others; expected of every member.
- **communicative and cultural memory** (Assmann): the living exchange of
  memories across a few generations, and the institutional memory that
  succeeds it (p. 606).
- **postmemory** (Hirsch) and **vicarious memory**: memories of events not
  lived through but heard from those who did.

## Bearing on the record

- **The question's identity route ([QUESTION-tmp8bttd](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmp8bttd.md)).** The question asks
  whether descendants' distress over an ancestral trauma follows their
  identification with the group, measured apart from knowledge and from
  family transmission. Meyler et al. are the nearest the record holds to
  that test. They do not meet it. These are inferences; the authors do
  not frame their work this way.
  - *What was measured is knowledge, not distress.* Children's knowledge
    of a parent's memory, and their report of the parent's feeling.
  - *The channel is the family.* The transmission is parent to child, the
    confound the question asks to remove.
  - *The direction is open.* Identification and knowledge correlate. The
    authors offer both directions.
  - *It is within living memory.* The authors decline to equate it with
    the transmission of a parent's own trauma (p. 607), and it is far from
    an ancestral injury a century or more old.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)), run backward in time.** The
  paper gives a mechanism for the first step of the extended self into
  the past. A child who knows where a parent was on 9/11 is "there," by
  extension (p. 608), and holding such memories is "an essential marker
  of one's membership" (p. 609). If the descendant's grief over an
  injury is self-regarding, this is how the self could come to include a
  moment it never lived: by taking on another member's place in it, and
  by that, membership. That is an inference. The paper measures no grief,
  and its "extension" is the authors' metaphor.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** Having a flashbulb memory
  is a cognitive mark of membership, not a report of caring, so it is a
  candidate criterion of the self's extent. But it is not cleanly
  separate. Such memories form for events appraised as important and
  consequential (Tinti et al., cited for Catholics' memories rising with
  involvement), so caring helps produce the mark.
- **Batson's objection ([CLAIM-tmpybc4k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpybc4k.md)).** Not addressed.

## Limitations

- **An essay.** The central claims (C1, C3, C4) are argued, not tested.
  The one observation of witnessing in use is the first author's,
  overheard in the 9/11 museum (pp. 599–600).
- **The key study is second hand.** Meyler et al. (2020) were in press;
  their N, measures and statistics are not given. "A strong correlation"
  and "the stronger the identification" have no numbers.
- **Inconsistent shares.** 89% could report the parent's memory, yet the
  bimodal split given is 39% and 51% (90%), and 11% claimed no knowledge
  (pp. 607–608). Rounding, probably; the paper does not say.
- **Self-citation.** The authors' own grant and studies carry much of the
  evidence; Cyr and Hirst (2020) is a manuscript in progress.
- **Typos.**
  - "Jeffery Alexander" for Jeffrey (p. 591).
  - "Olaf Palme" for Olof Palme (p. 595).
  - The reference list swaps Alexander 2004a and 2004b (p. 610).
  - "H. J. Smelser" for N. J. Smelser in the volume's editors (p. 611).
  - "Varga and Guigon" for Guignon, in text and references (pp. 599, 613).
  - "Loftus, E. A." for E. F. Loftus (p. 612).
  - "O. Luminet IV" and "Gabbrieli" in the references (pp. 611–612);
    "Doubleday.." (p. 611); an unclosed quotation mark in Hirst and Merck's
    title (p. 611); "Luminet and A. Curci 2017" (p. 613).

## Open questions

- Does knowing a parent's memory make a child identify more, or the
  reverse? A longitudinal design, or a manipulation of what parents tell,
  would settle the direction.
- Does the same pattern hold for distress, and for events beyond living
  memory? A study of children's and grandchildren's feeling about a
  family's or a community's older trauma, with identification measured,
  would bring this to the question's test.
- Where does communicative memory end and cultural memory begin, for a
  given trauma? The paper names the transition but does not place it.
