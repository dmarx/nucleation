# NOTE-tmp71fl5: Apps & Tsakiris — The free-energy self

## Contribution

Before this paper, the self-recognition literature (mirror and self-face
tasks, self-voice, body ownership) had findings and no theory tied to a
general account of cortical function, and the bodily illusions had a
separate explanation each. The paper puts all of it under the free-energy
principle and predictive coding. Self-recognition becomes ordinary
hierarchical inference about the causes of sensory input, the self is the
cause that best explains correlated input across the senses, and the
rubber hand, enfacement and rubber-voice illusions become one effect. It
also makes motor signals one kind of prediction among others, so that
self-recognition does not need action.

## Key insight

"Me" is a hypothesis, not a label fixed in advance. One's own body is the
object that, alone among objects, is reliably felt when it is seen
touched, moves when one predicts movement, and so on. A brain that
minimises prediction error learns that it is the single best explanation
for all these correlations. Because it is a probabilistic belief, it can
be revised: give another hand or face the same correlations, and some of
the probability moves to it, and away from one's own.

## Assumptions

- **The free-energy principle and predictive coding are taken as given.**
  "The aim of this paper is not to discuss the validity or evidence
  supporting the free-energy principle and predictive coding"; the theory
  "is still in its infancy and therefore is neither largely supported or
  refuted as yet" (§3). Its mathematics is not given.
- **Two classes of neurons.** Representational units carry predictions
  and error units carry prediction errors; unexplained error goes up the
  hierarchy and predictions come down to "explain away" error (§3).
- **Empirical Bayes in a hierarchy.** Beliefs at intermediate levels are
  "empirical priors", optimised by both top-down and bottom-up
  influences; high-level ones are "abstract multimodal beliefs about
  states of the world that change slowly over time", learned from
  associations between congruent sensory events (§4).
- **Motor signals are predictions.** Under active inference, "cortical
  signals that drive movement are descending predictions about the
  proprioceptive consequences of movement", better called corollary
  discharge than efference copy; corollary discharge "is just like any
  other descending prediction" except that it elicits movement (§4).
- **Self-recognition tasks stand in for self-awareness.** Recognising
  one's own face or voice is taken, after Gallup, as a "behavioural
  marker" of self-awareness (§1). The paper is about recognising the
  physical self, not about the self as subject.

## Key results

There are no new data, models or simulations. The paper's claims, by
section:

- **The self as a probabilistic prior (§4).** "One's own body is the one
  which has the highest probability of being 'me' as other objects are
  probabilistically less likely to evoke the same sensory inputs." It is
  recognised as "self" only when streams are integrated: a self-face is
  "a face, that I have seen before, that I am familiar with, and that is
  associated with congruent corollary discharge, vestibular,
  somatosensory and interoceptive information when seen on a reflective
  surface". Touch seen on the skin and felt becomes associated, while
  touch seen between two other objects never evokes a felt touch. "In
  short, the notion that there is a 'self' is the most parsimonious and
  accurate explanation for sensory inputs." The authors add that this
  "parsimonious accuracy" is "exactly the quantity that is optimised when
  minimising free energy". To issue parallel predictions to several
  senses, "there must be a high level representation (of self)".
- **The mirror (§4).** An infant matches felt with seen sensorimotor
  behaviour and so learns what she looks like. A reflection is surprising
  in space (allocentric), in time (it moves with one's corollary
  discharge) and in form, and multisensory areas explain the surprise
  away. One's face becomes "me" because "it is typically the face that is
  processed when looking in a mirror, and that face rarely violates
  expectations instantiated by the agent's actions".
- **What sets the account apart (§4).** Earlier accounts tied
  self-processing to one "self" network (Northoff et al. 2006) or to the
  match of motor efference with reafference alone (Legrand and Ruby 2009).
  Here "information from any system can be used to explain away
  information in any other system", so "information from each and every
  sensory system" can modulate self-recognition.
- **The illusions (§5.1).** Reported from the cited studies:
  - Rubber hand illusion: synchronous touch on congruent locations of the
    hidden real hand and a seen rubber hand produces ownership of the
    rubber hand and a drift in felt hand position toward it (Botvinick
    and Cohen 1998; Tsakiris and Haggard 2005).
  - Full-body versions: Ehrsson (2007) and "Leggenhanger et al (2007)"
    [sic], with synchronous stroking of a seen virtual back making
    participants feel "as if the virtual body was their body". These show
    multisensory input determining a minimal first-person perspective,
    self-location and self-identification, "three conditions that are
    critical for the experience of selfhood (Blanke and Metzinger, 2009)".
  - Enfacement: touch felt on one's face while watching another's face
    touched in synchrony makes participants respond on self-other tasks
    "as if the others face was more like their own face" (Tsakiris 2008;
    Sforza et al. 2010), and report it as more like "me" (Tajadura-Jimenez
    et al. 2012).
  - Rubber voice: synchronous feedback of a stranger's voice while
    speaking is reported as a distorted version of one's own voice (Zheng
    et al. 2011, the only report).
- **One explanation of the three (§§5.1–5.2, Fig. 1).** In each illusion
  there is "considerable bottom up sensory surprise evoked in one system"
  (touch felt where it was not predicted, a felt touch coinciding with a
  seen one). It "will be explained away by top-down effects from
  multisensory areas", and perceptual learning updates the representation
  of one's body, face or voice. After the RHI, participants rated their
  hand and the rubber hand as more similar (Longo et al. 2009). Illusions
  are "Bayes optimal" inference under the prior "that most of our
  sensations are caused by ourselves". The Figure 1 caption: before
  stimulation the rubber hand has "a low probability of being one's own
  hand"; during the illusion "touch on the rubber hand is no longer
  surprising".
- **Ownership as competition (§5.2).** "The driving effect for each
  illusion is an increase in the probability that the other object ...
  will be represented as part of the body and a decrease in the
  probability that one's actual body will be represented as 'self'." The
  decrease is read in reports that the real hand cools (Moseley et al.
  2008; Hohwy and Paton 2010) and its histamine level rises (Barnsley et
  al. 2011), "consistent with the notion that the real limb is being
  partially rejected". Which model wins is compared with binocular
  rivalry (Hohwy et al. 2008): competing generative models, with
  perception "a function of the relative fit".
- **Context as prior (§5.3).** Masked and unmasked self-primes speed
  self-recognition in another domain. Self-face bias differs by culture:
  Chinese participants reversed it in a supervisor's presence (Liew et al.
  2011), and religious participants showed a weaker bias than atheists
  (Ma and Han 2012). The RHI is reduced or absent for a non-corporeal
  object, a hand of another skin tone, or a hand in an incongruent place.
  These are read as priors in force before the stimulus.
- **Three anatomical predictions (§6).**
  1. "No unimodal areas will exclusively be engaged by self-stimuli"
     (§6.1). Face, voice and body areas respond more to self-stimuli, but
     process all faces, voices and bodies; the extra response "may
     therefore reflect the surprise evoked by self-stimuli".
  2. Multimodal areas will be engaged, without being self-specific
     (§6.2). TPJ, IPS, anterior insula and IFG are active in self-
     recognition and in the RHI and full-body illusions, are mutually
     connected, and "may comprise a core circuit which is engaged when
     recognising one's self and creates a sense of ownership over one's
     body". Their connections show they also process other things. The
     anterior insula is called "primary interoceptive cortex".
  3. Predicted self-stimuli will suppress activity (§6.3): one cannot
     tickle oneself, with reduced SI activity (Blakemore et al.); seeing
     one's own face while touched reduces SI and IFG activity compared
     with another's face (Cardini et al. 2011).
- **Relation to Kilner and Friston (§6).** Their free-energy account of
  the mirror-neuron system predicts others' actions from simulated
  corollary discharge. This paper extends it to how a stimulus comes to
  be labelled "self", including "in situations where actions are not a
  driving factor".
- **Caveats (§7).** "There is, as yet, little direct evidence that
  predictions and prediction errors related to self-stimuli are processed
  anywhere in the brain", though "there is also none that refute it".
  The theory is "hard to falsify using standard neuroscientific methods",
  because 3T BOLD mixes prediction and error populations, so that "a
  highly surprising event and a highly predicted event may ... both evoke
  large responses". Whether the self is represented probabilistically,
  not deterministically, has had no direct test. Proposed remedies:
  designs that make error and probability orthogonal, multivoxel
  analysis, laminar high-field fMRI, model-based behavioural tasks and
  dynamic causal modelling.
- **Summary (§8).** "Empirical data neither largely supports nor refutes
  our account of self-recognition."

## Concepts

- **Free energy** — "the upper bound on the level of surprise"; minimised
  by acting to sample predictable input and by updating beliefs about
  causes (§2).
- **Surprise / prediction error** — the discrepancy between predicted and
  actual input; phasic and local at each node, unexplained remainder
  passed up (§§2–3).
- **Explaining away** — top-down predictions from a higher level cancel
  error at the level below (§3).
- **Empirical prior** — a belief at an intermediate level of the
  hierarchy, constrained from above and below; high-level ones are
  abstract, multimodal and slow (§4).
- **Corollary discharge** — a prediction of the sensory consequences of
  movement; set against efference copy, a copy of the motor command (§4).
- **Self-recognition** — recognising a stimulus (face, voice, body part)
  as one's own; distinct here from self-processing in general.
- **Enfacement illusion** — synchronous felt and seen facial touch making
  another's face more like one's own in recognition and report.
- **Rubber voice illusion** — synchronous feedback of another's voice
  heard as a distortion of one's own.

## Connections

The paper builds on Friston's free-energy and predictive-coding papers,
among them Friston, Daunizeau, Kilner and Kiebel 2010 ([LIT-1181](../literature.d/LIT-1181.md)) and
Friston, Mattout and Kilner 2011 on active inference, and on Clark's
"Whatever next?" (then in press). It sets itself against two accounts: a
self-specific cortical network (Northoff et al. 2006) and self-processing
as motor efference matched with reafference (Legrand and Ruby 2009). It
cites Seth, Suzuki and Critchley's interoceptive predictive coding model
of conscious presence (2011) as the interoceptive version of the latter.
Its account of the illusions draws on Tsakiris's own multisensory work and
on Hohwy's predictive-coding treatment of the RHI and binocular rivalry.
It extends Kilner and colleagues' predictive-coding account of the
mirror-neuron system. It cites Botvinick and Cohen 1998, Lenggenhager et
al. 2007 and Blanke and Metzinger 2009, which are being filed alongside it.

## Bearing on the record

- **Metzinger's self-model ([LIT-450](../literature.d/LIT-450.md), [NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md); [THEORY-064](../theory.d/THEORY-064.md)).** [NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md)'s C6,
  that body ownership can be manipulated by multisensory conflict, rested
  on findings the record did not hold. This paper gives those findings a
  mechanism: ownership is a posterior that synchronous stimulation
  shifts, and the real hand loses probability as the rubber hand gains
  it. Its "the notion that there is a 'self' is the most parsimonious
  and accurate explanation for sensory inputs" is close to [THEORY-064](../theory.d/THEORY-064.md)'s
  claim that what is experienced as a self is the content of a
  self-model. The paper, though, says nothing about transparency, and
  does not deny a self. It explains how the body is modelled as one's
  own, not why the model is experienced as a self rather than as a model.
  That is where Metzinger's claim does its work, and it is not tested
  here. Putting the two together is mine; the paper cites Blanke and
  Metzinger 2009 only for the three conditions of selfhood.
- **Active inference ([LIT-1181](../literature.d/LIT-1181.md), [NOTE-960](NOTE-960.md)).** The paper adopts [NOTE-960](NOTE-960.md)'s
  C2 (action fulfils proprioceptive predictions, with no separate motor
  command) and draws a consequence the Friston paper does not: if motor
  signals are only proprioceptive predictions, motor efference has no
  special standing for the self, and self-recognition can run on
  passive, purely sensory correlations. The RHI, with passive stroking,
  is the case.
- **Agency and ownership ([LIT-1009](../literature.d/LIT-1009.md), [NOTE-827](NOTE-827.md); [THEORY-322](../theory.d/THEORY-322.md); [LIT-453](../literature.d/LIT-453.md)).**
  Haggard ([NOTE-827](NOTE-827.md) C1) and Gallagher separate ownership from agency.
  This paper is about ownership and recognition and leaves agency
  aside. Its §6.3 suppression result is the same sensory attenuation
  that Haggard reports the comparator model predicts, and Haggard's
  objection to the comparator (it explains non-agency but gives no
  positive signal, [NOTE-827](NOTE-827.md) C8) has a counterpart here. The account
  gives the self a positive representation, the high-level prior, but
  its own §7 says that representation has not been found. The
  comparison is mine.
- **Interoception ([LIT-542](../literature.d/LIT-542.md), [NOTE-434](NOTE-434.md); [LIT-135](../literature.d/LIT-135.md); [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md)).** The paper
  lists interoception among the inputs to the self-prior, calls the
  anterior insula "primary interoceptive cortex" and cites Seth et al.
  2011. But its worked cases are all exteroceptive, visuo-tactile or
  auditory, and interoception is named, not modelled. Its anatomy differs
  from Barrett's: [NOTE-434](NOTE-434.md) records Barrett placing primary interoceptive
  cortex in the mid/posterior insula and visceromotor predictions in the
  ventral anterior insula, whereas this paper calls the anterior insula
  itself "primary interoceptive cortex". It gives [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md) no new
  support, since it says nothing about affect. The interoceptive side of
  the bridge is better taken from Seth 2013, filed alongside.
- **The scope of the free-energy principle ([LIT-598](../literature.d/LIT-598.md), [LIT-603](../literature.d/LIT-603.md)).** The paper
  states that it does not argue for the free-energy principle (§3), and
  it uses no Markov blankets. The objections in those critiques do not
  reach it. As far as this reading can tell, its arguments use only
  hierarchical predictive coding, not the free-energy bound itself; the
  paper notes the debate over whether predictive coding needs that bound
  (Clark; Friston 2012b) and does not take a side. That reading is mine.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** The illusions are measured by questionnaire, by
  proprioceptive drift, by self-other recognition tasks and by
  physiology (temperature, histamine). The paper treats them as
  converging and does not ask which of them measures the experience.
- **No THEORY holds the predictive account of body ownership.** A grep of
  theory.d, claims.d and questions.d for "rubber hand", "body
  ownership", "self-recognition" and "enfacement" found only [THEORY-060](../theory.d/THEORY-060.md),
  which mentions self-recognition in development and does not bear on
  this account. No instruction for
  machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **A theory with no test of its own.** The authors say there is "little
  direct evidence" for its core claims and that the data "neither largely
  supports nor refutes" it. The evidence they give was gathered under
  other accounts, and they have "largely looked for and reported evidence
  that supports" the theory (§7).
- **Hard to falsify, by the authors' account.** The anatomical predictions
  are weak as stated: "will be engaged" and "not self-specific" are met
  by almost any multimodal finding. The paper names no result that
  would count against it.
- **The physiological evidence for "rejection" is interpretive.** Cooling
  and histamine in the real hand are read as falling probability of
  ownership; no mechanism links the two.
- **A review by an author whose group ran many of the reviewed studies**
  (Tsakiris on the RHI and enfacement). No effect sizes are given.
- **Manuscript slips.** "Leggenhanger" for Lenggenhager, "Clark et al In
  Press" for a single-author paper, and typos ("damming", "somtosensory").
  They do not affect the argument.

## Open questions

- Is ownership graded and probabilistic, as the account says, or
  all-or-none? The authors propose model-based behavioural tasks to
  tell, and report none done.
- What would the high-level self-prior look like in the brain, and how
  would it be told apart from multisensory integration in general?
- If the self is the best explanation of sensory input, why does the RHI
  fail for a non-corporeal object given the same correlations? The
  account answers with a prior, which explains the result but would
  explain the opposite result with a different prior.
- Does the account extend to the sense of agency, where Haggard finds a
  prospective component ([THEORY-322](../theory.d/THEORY-322.md)), or only to ownership?
