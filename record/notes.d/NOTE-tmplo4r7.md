# NOTE-tmplo4r7: King — Two Faces of Desert

## Contribution

King asks what notion of desert each of Watson's two faces of
responsibility carries. Attributability goes with desert as fittingness,
fixed by facts about the agent; accountability, a sanction-like practice,
goes with desert as fairness, which depends also on the blamer and on
comparative and universal fairness. Neither extends to the other face, and
accountability cannot account for praise, so King favours
attributability.

## Key results

King's own:

- (§6, p. 13) "one can have something attributable to them that one is not
  accountable to others for. Watson makes this plain."
- (§4, p. 8) "accountability-blame makes relevant considerations about
  those holding the subject accountable. Thus, if I lack the standing to
  hold you to a legitimate demand, then you cannot be accountable to me".
- (§7, p. 16) "reflections on desert widens the gap between the two faces
  of responsibility".

Watson (1996), as King quotes him by page:

- Attributability is appraisal "of the individual as an adopter of ends"
  (229).
- Accountability "involves a social setting in which we demand (require)
  certain conduct from one another and respond adversely to one another’s
  failures to comply with these demands" (229); it is "a readiness to
  respond in certain ways" (235); to require conduct is to lay it down that
  "unless the agent so behaves she will be liable to certain adverse or
  unwelcome treatment" (236–7).
- Fairness: "[i]t is unfair to impose sanctions upon people unless they
  had a reasonable opportunity to avoid incurring them" (237).
- Standing: Watson's hijacker who holds a hostage "accountable" for the
  others "on pain of death" lacks the standing to do so (237, King n. 33).
- Accountability is a "legal-like practice, an informal institution
  serving the ends of social regulation and/or retributive and
  compensatory justice" (239).

## Assumptions

Moral responsibility entails desert; a fitting-attitude account of value;
accountability-blame is best construed as expressed sanction.

## Concepts

- **Attributability** and **accountability** (Watson's two faces).
- **Desert as fittingness** and **desert as fairness**.
- **Standing to blame**; comparative and universal fairness.

## Bearing on the record

- **QUESTION-068** (does accountability need conditions, such as
  avoidability, that ownership does not supply?): Watson's fairness
  condition, as King quotes it, is one: a "reasonable opportunity to avoid"
  sanction. King adds the blamer's standing. Both are conditions ownership
  of the act does not give.
- **CLAIM-tmpa2l21**: the fairness condition is a route by which coerced
  or structured conduct stays attributable to the actor while
  accountability-blame is reduced. Watson, as quoted, says nothing about
  the arranger's own wrong.
- **CLAIM-tmpciy9q**: King's standing condition bears on an arranger who
  then accuses: one who lacks standing cannot hold the other accountable.
  King does not discuss arranged wrongs.
- **TERM-tmp8pnnr**: its attributability sense ("Whose action ... was
  it?") is thinner than Watson's, which is appraisal of the agent "as an
  adopter of ends".

## Connections

Stands in for Watson (1996), LIT-tmpmsh0d, for the two faces, the
sanction-like character of accountability, its fairness and standing
conditions, and the independence of the faces, in Watson's words as
quoted. It does not restate Watson's own arguments, his treatment of the
self-disclosure view against Wolf beyond a citation, or his conclusion.
King warns he uses the distinction "potentially in ways to which Watson
himself would object".
