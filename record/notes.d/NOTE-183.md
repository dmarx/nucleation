# NOTE-183: Birch — AI Consciousness: A Centrist Manifesto

## Contribution

Birch identifies two urgent challenges. First, very many users will soon attribute human-like consciousness to AI companions and assistants on the strength of mimicry and role-play, and no one knows how to stop this. Second, AI might genuinely achieve profoundly alien forms of consciousness, and consciousness science is too immature to say either way. "Centrism" is the position that both must be taken seriously. They pull against each other: countering misattribution by calling conscious AI impossible undermines the second project, and taking AI consciousness seriously can feed misattribution. The manifesto tries to set out mutually consistent strategies for both.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1–3 (pp. 2–4): phenomenal consciousness as Nagel's "something it's like", treated as "unstable common ground". The two challenges are set out, with Suleyman's 2025 essay cited on the first.
- §4 "The persisting interlocutor illusion" (pp. 4–8): each conversational turn is a separate processing event. Frontier LLMs are widely taken to be Mixture-of-Experts models served across data centres, so consecutive turns may run on different implementations, and the only continuity is the auto-appended conversation history. This makes personal identity implausible and creates the illusion of a persisting companion.
- §7 "Breaking the spell: Anti-shared intentionality" (pp. 10–13): co-remembering, more than faces or voices, drives projection of consciousness. Design nudges must avoid "brainwashing" (forced self-denials, which fuel conspiracy theories) and "lobotomizing" (removing the capacity to build relationships).
- §9 "Flickers and shoggoths" (pp. 14–17): two alien-consciousness hypotheses. One is momentary per-forward-pass "flickers" whose subjective duration may exceed their objective duration. The other is a persisting conscious "shoggoth" behind all the role-played characters.
- §10–11 (pp. 17–23): the "gaming problem": LLMs trained on human talk about minds mimic every behavioural marker, so the parity principle between animals and AI is false and Schneider and Turner's test fails. Architectural indicators (Butlin et al. 2023, e.g. a global workspace) face a "Janus problem": what they show depends on background theory, as the dispute over whether the Perceiver architecture is a workspace (Juliani et al.) illustrates.
- §12–13 (pp. 23–28): the "two-level deadlock" between computational functionalism and biological naturalism is not in-principle irresolvable. Biological-naturalist hypotheses can be assessed by their fit with comparative evidence on how consciousness is distributed among living things, which calls for a mature science of animal consciousness. All sides should agree that any AI consciousness would be profoundly alien and could never vindicate a user's human-like AI partner.

## Open questions

- It is a central current position paper on AI consciousness attribution. It pairs with [LIT-135](../literature.d/LIT-135.md) (Seth, biological naturalism), [LIT-207](../literature.d/LIT-207.md) (Roberts, fictionalism), [LIT-212](../literature.d/LIT-212.md) (Šekrst) and [LIT-206](../literature.d/LIT-206.md) (Goldstein & Lederman on "instance agents"), and it could anchor a nucleation cluster.
- The persisting-interlocutor argument rests on empirical claims about serving infrastructure (MoE routing, multi-datacentre serving) that the paper itself says are guarded secrets. A deeper reading should check how much the identity argument depends on them.
