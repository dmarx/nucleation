# THEORY-2211: Tatel finds that, with infrequent use, accuracy on laboratory motor tasks declines about three times faster than on real-world tasks, and little evidence that the real-world domains differ, though in the posted data only medical and dental tasks decline measurably

## The account

[THEORY-1424](THEORY-1424.md) gives the pooled rate: accuracy declines by about 0.08 SD
a month of infrequent use. That rate averages tasks that decline at very
different rates.

- **Laboratory against real world.** In the dissertation's model
  (Appendix C, k = 829), the accuracy slope is −0.16 a month for
  laboratory or artificial tasks and the interaction for real-world tasks
  is +0.11 (p < 0.01), about −0.05. For speed the interaction is +0.07,
  not significant. "Performance on real-world tasks was better retained
  over time than performance on laboratory/artificial tasks for
  accuracy-based performance measures" (p. 81).
- **Among real-world domains.** "There is less evidence, however, that
  skill decay patterns differ between different types of real-world tasks
  (i.e., medical/dental, military/transportation, sports)" (p. 81). Each
  domain's accuracy slope is shallower than the laboratory slope
  (Appendix D: +0.12 to +0.18).
- **In the posted data** ([CASE-314](../cases.d/CASE-314.md)), each domain's own slope for
  accuracy: medical and dental −0.043 (robust SE 0.012), miscellaneous
  −0.032 (0.013), military and transport −0.009 (0.024), sports +0.015
  (0.021). Only medical and dental tasks have many intervals beyond six
  months (27, up to 22 months).
- **Why.** Tatel offers two explanations: real-world tasks are "more
  representative of the environments that individuals are likely to
  practice and use their skills outside of an evaluation setting and
  participant motivation to perform well is therefore higher" (pp.
  128–129); or complexity, since real-world tasks were rated more complex
  on all three dimensions and "The direction of the relationship remains
  unclear" (p. 129).

## What it does not say

- That real-world skills do not decay. Medical and dental accuracy
  declines measurably, and real-world tasks start lower: their losses at
  short intervals are larger.
- That the decline is linear. In the refit, every type declines faster
  when only short intervals are fitted ([CASE-314](../cases.d/CASE-314.md)); the linear slopes
  are averages over each type's mix of intervals. That finding is the
  refit's, not Tatel's.
- Anything about the article's own moderator analyses, which are unread
  ([LIT-2088](../literature.d/LIT-2088.md)).
