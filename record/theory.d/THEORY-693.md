# THEORY-693: In conversation, evaluating an object, positioning oneself and aligning with another speaker are three aspects of one stance act rather than three kinds of stance, because a stance takes a shared object and answers a prior stance, which are usually supplied by earlier turns; a resonant follow must mark its relation to the lead, with too or either in English

## Source

Du Bois (2007), [LIT-779](../literature.d/LIT-779.md) ("The stance triangle", in Englebretson (ed.),
*Stancetaking in Discourse*, pp. 139–182), read in full in [NOTE-590](../notes.d/NOTE-590.md).

## What was actually shown

**Three questions for any stance (§3).** Who takes it, what its object is,
and what stance it answers. Attested utterances show each answer is
routinely outside the stance utterance: "I would love to go" is about
Nicaragua, 40 lines earlier; "I totally agree" answers a directive 29 lines
back.

**Subjectivity takes an object (§4).** "I'm glad", "I know" and "I agree"
are complete sentences and incomplete stances. "I'm glad" and "I'm so glad"
differ less by *so* than by what each is glad about, which prior turns
supply.

**Marking the follow (§§5–6).** In "I don't like those" / "I don't either",
the second speaker's *either* cannot be dropped without pragmatic anomaly,
and the first speaker cannot use it. Under conditions only partly stated
(a stance follow juxtaposed with a resonant lead, subjective intentional
predicates such as like, love, know, think, want; note 26), a resonant
follow must be marked, *too* for positive and *either* for negative.
Inside a formally parallel follow, differences (a rising *Yeah?*, *want*
for *love*) calibrate alignment, which is scalar.

**The account (49).** "Stance is a public act by a social actor, achieved
dialogically through overt communicative means, of simultaneously
evaluating objects, positioning subjects (self and others), and aligning
with other subjects, with respect to any salient dimension of the
sociocultural field." The triangle (Fig. 1): two subjects and a shared
object, two evaluative vectors, one alignment vector, and positioning
reflected back on each subject. Hence the gloss: "I evaluate something, and
thereby position myself, and thereby align with you."

**What could have come out otherwise.** The second speaker could have been
free to omit *either*, and the first free to use it. In the excerpts, and
by the cited study, neither holds.

## What this does not say

- **That the aspects cannot occur apart.** Co-occurrence is shown in
  chosen examples (C1, moderate). The chapter does not search for
  alignment with no evaluated object, or evaluation that commits the
  speaker to no position. Such a case would break the "one act" claim.
- **That participants use the triangle to infer the rest.** That claim
  (C5) is illustrated only by the analyst's reading.
- **That every stance has a shared object.** The general version rests on
  Tomasello's shared orientation and is deferred (note 29; C6, weak).
- **How often the too/either rule holds.** No counts are reported here,
  the conditions are only partly given, and *too*/*either* are not always
  intersubjective (note 27).
- **Anything about a speaker who is not the stance subject.** Reported
  speech, animated voices and institutional speaking are set aside (note
  13), and Goffman's footing ([LIT-780](../literature.d/LIT-780.md), unread) is where the triangle would
  be tested.
- **Anything beyond American English conversation.** One language, one
  register.
- **That stance is the smallest unit of social action.** Offered
  conditionally (C7).
