# NOTE-tmpgn1lo: Vallor (2013) — The Future of Military Virtue

## Contribution

A first-hand statement, two years before the 2015 paper, of the mechanism that paper generalizes: moral skill is acquired and kept only by practice, so automation that removes the practice removes the skill, whatever the quality of the machine's outputs.

## Key results

1. p. 1 (abstract): virtue "presupposes repeated practice and development of skills of moral analysis, deliberation and action"; such practices "can be made redundant by autonomous or semi-autonomous machines, with a resulting devaluation and/or loss of these skills"; "this deskilling remains a significant risk even with a commitment to ‘human on the loop’ protocols."
2. p. 4: "The virtue of honesty, for example, can only be acquired through repeated practice of truth-telling."
3. p. 6: "moral skills are a necessary if not a sufficient condition for moral virtue."
4. p. 7: "Whether any automated technology produces deskilling, then, is an empirical question that depends upon the particular context of use."
5. p. 7: a widespread loss of moral skill "would not be rendered insignificant by the emergence of machines that produce equivalent, or arguably even better outcomes"; "the intrinsic value of artisanal skills is not only paralleled but dwarfed by the intrinsic value of moral skills."
6. p. 8: "the skill set for supervising, approving or vetoing the decisions of semi-autonomous robots seeking to apply lethal force will be much narrower than the skill set required for humans to make those moral decisions themselves."
7. p. 12, the supervisor's paradox: "An expert supervisor of another’s decision, in order to be worthy of the authority to override it, must have acquired expertise in making decisions of the very same or a similar kind."
8. p. 13, the remedy: "to restrict the deployment of automated methods of warfare to just those contexts in which human judgments are consistently and gravely inadequate", and to use automated systems for moral upskilling (pp. 13–14).

## Assumptions

Aristotelian virtue ethics: virtue is habituated and orchestrated by practical wisdom. Expertise needs repeated practice with feedback. The value of moral skill is intrinsic and not exhausted by outcomes. Whether deskilling occurs is empirical and depends on the context.

## Concepts

- **Moral deskilling**: the loss of moral skills when the practices that cultivate them are made redundant.
- **Moral upskilling**: the use of technology to improve moral skill.
- **The supervisor's paradox** (the filer's name for result 7): authority to override a decision requires expertise in making it, which supervision alone does not give.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmp6wuql** (capability assessed as a trajectory): first-hand support for the premise. The claim needs capacity "learned by doing" that "decays with disuse" and support that "does the work"; results 1, 2 and 7 give that for moral skill. Result 5 draws the claim's consequence in Vallor's terms: equal or better present outcomes do not settle the evaluation. Result 4 matches the claim's own "Not defeated by evidence that a given technology does not deskill its users". Result 8 is capacity-first support in practice. Corroborates.
- **CLAIM-tmpbypt7**: weak. Once the practice stops, the expertise to resume it or override the machine is gone (result 7), which removes the option of returning to unaided practice; Vallor does not frame it as an option set. No relation.
- **CLAIM-tmpsmg1c**: support in spirit. Lost moral skill is hard to restore without practice and exemplars, a reason for restraint before deployment; no burden rule. No relation.
- **CLAIM-558** (decay with disuse, held from the clinical evidence) and **CASE-tmpf71w4** (the proposed study of substitutive against capacity-first assistance): result 4 is the reason the study is needed.

## Limitations

The military domain only. A conference paper argued by thought experiment, with no empirical evidence of deskilling.
