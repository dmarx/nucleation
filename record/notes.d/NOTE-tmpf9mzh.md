# NOTE-tmpf9mzh: Self and Collective: Cognition and Social Context

## Contribution

A short theoretical statement, from the theory's authors, of what
self-categorization theory says about the self. It adds to the 1987 book
([LIT-tmper0q2](../literature.d/LIT-tmper0q2.md), not read) a catalogue of four ways self-categories vary
with context, drawn from the group's studies of 1987–1994. It also adds
an argument from that variability: the self is better treated as a
product of judgment in context than as a fixed, stored structure that
context activates.

## Key insight

Being a "we" is not a departure from the self but one of its normal
settings. Whether I see myself as "I" or as one of "us", which "us" it is,
what that "us" means and who in it is most typical all depend on whom I am
being compared with. They are computed afresh from the comparison at hand
by one principle, metacontrast. A woman who is the only female
psychologist in a room may define herself as a woman against the men, or
as an individual against the other psychologists (p. 455). Neither
definition is the real one.

## Assumptions

- **Categorization is comparative.** A self-category is a grouping of
  self and some class of stimuli as identical, in contrast to some other
  class (p. 454). It is always relative to a frame of reference.
- **Salience = readiness × fit**, following Bruner (p. 455). Readiness
  (relative accessibility) reflects past experience, present
  expectations, motives, values, goals and needs. Fit has two parts:
  - *comparative fit*, the **metacontrast** principle: a collection of
    stimuli is more likely to be categorized as an entity to the degree
    that the average differences perceived between them are less than the
    average differences perceived between them and the remaining stimuli
    in the frame of reference;
  - *normative fit*: the differences also lie in the direction, on the
    content dimensions, that background beliefs about the category
    predict. Catholics must differ from Protestants in the right way, not
    just differ.
- **Fit is part of the explanation, not all of it** (p. 456). The
  authors' work is mostly on fit. They say outright that fit is not a
  sufficient explanation of salience.
- **Personal versus group level is a continuum of competing
  categorizations** (p. 456, after Turner & Oakes 1989). What is salient
  is "rarely a single category"; talk of one dominant self-category is
  shorthand.

## Key results

The paper reports results; it presents none. Each is cited to the
group's own work, several of it in press in 1994.

- **Fit predicts salience (p. 455–456).** Oakes, Turner & Haslam (1991):
  subjects watched six arts and science students discuss. Arts/science
  identities were most salient, judged by attributions to academic field,
  when the students disagreed along category lines *and* the views
  matched the stereotype, that is, under both comparative and normative
  fit.
- **Four forms of context dependence (pp. 456–458).**
  1. *Level.* Self-categorization becomes more inclusive as the
     comparative context widens to include people more different from
     self, so prior others are recategorized as "us" (Gaertner et al.
     1989; Haslam & Turner 1992). Social identity is more salient in
     intergroup than in intragroup comparison: in staged discussions,
     men and women self-stereotyped more by sex when the disagreement ran
     between the sexes (Hogg & Turner 1987). The out-group homogeneity
     effect is explained the same way, and relative *in-group*
     homogeneity is obtained when judgments are intergroup on in-group
     dimensions (Haslam, Oakes, Turner & McGarty, in press). People
     extreme within a context categorize self and others more sharply
     and polarize, and the classic extremist/moderate pattern can be
     reversed by changing their position relative to the majority
     (Haslam & Turner, in press).
  2. *Kind.* Which category becomes salient depends on normative fit
     (Oakes et al. 1991).
  3. *Content.* What a category means is selectively constructed for the
     comparison. In the "Iraq" study (Haslam et al. 1992), Americans
     were seen as more aggressive than the Soviet Union but less
     aggressive than Iraq, and their rank for ambition against Britain,
     Australia and Iraq reversed between the start and the end of the
     Gulf War. Australians judged alone were "happy-go-lucky,
     straightforward, and sportsmanlike"; judged beside Americans, more
     sportsmanlike, less happy-go-lucky, and "pleasure loving" appeared.
     "There is no one-to-one correspondence between the long-term
     knowledge one has about what different kinds of people are like and
     the actual social category that is constructed" (p. 457).
  4. *Internal structure.* Relative prototypicality shifts with context
     (cf. Barsalou 1987). As a group becomes more extreme in an intergroup
     context, its more extreme members gain prototypicality over its
     moderates (p. 458). In-groups can define themselves in opposite ways
     on different occasions (Hogg, Turner & Davidson 1990).
- **Three conclusions about the self (p. 458).** Its variability is
  lawful, not arbitrary; self-categories are "social contextual
  definitions of the perceiver"; and they are veridical, since
  variability is "necessary if self-perception is to be veridical".
- **The self as generated (pp. 458–459).** "We can speculate that
  self-categories are reflexive judgments". The authors "doubt whether
  the idea of self as a relatively fixed mental structure is meaningful
  or necessary". What makes a concept a self-concept may be its use,
  reflexively to define the perceiver, not where it is stored. Stability
  is explained by stable social reality, stable knowledge frameworks,
  stable groups and institutions, and influence processes (p. 460).
- **Influence (p. 461).** Private acceptance is "private category
  change". The same information is accepted from an in-group source and
  rejected from an out-group one. A minority's latent influence depends
  on its being categorized as in-group, and the same minority can be
  either, depending on the comparison (David & Turner 1992).
- **Cognition (pp. 461–462).** The variable self sets what is relevant
  to the perceiver, which background knowledge is normative to apply, and
  whether another's judgment counts as agreement or disagreement. Hence
  the closing claim that "all cognition is social cognition".

## Claims

| C1 | Personal and social identity are different levels of self-categorization, and the social level is as valid and authentic an expression of self as the personal | definitional; argued, not tested | pp. 454, 460 |
| C2 | Shared social identity depersonalizes self-perception (self seen as an interchangeable representative of the category), and this explains group phenomena | moderate as reported: rests on the 1980s programme the paper summarizes | p. 455, citing Turner et al. 1987, Hogg 1992, Turner 1991 |
| C3 | Salience of a self-category is an interaction of perceiver readiness and fit; comparative fit is metacontrast, normative fit is content-consistency | moderate as reported: one study described (Oakes et al. 1991); the authors call fit only part of the account | pp. 455–456 |
| C4 | The level, kind, content and prototype structure of self-categories all vary lawfully with the comparative frame of reference | moderate as reported: several studies by the authors' group, summarized without data; not independently checked here | pp. 456–458 |

## Concepts

- **self-category**: a cognitive grouping of self and some class of
  stimuli as identical and different from some other class (p. 454).
- **personal identity / social identity**: self-categories that define
  the individual by differences from other (in-group) persons, versus by
  shared similarities with members of a category in contrast to other
  categories (p. 454).
- **depersonalization**: defining and seeing oneself less as a differing
  individual and more as an interchangeable representative of a shared
  category (p. 455). Not a loss of self.
- **perceiver readiness**: relative accessibility of a category (p. 455).
- **metacontrast / comparative fit**: see Assumptions.
- **normative fit**: match between category specifications and the
  content of the instances (p. 455).
- **private category change**: the authors' name for what the influence
  literature calls private acceptance, conversion or informational
  influence (p. 461).
- **cognitive choice**: selective representation of phenomena from the
  vantage point of the perceiver (p. 461).

  a stored repertoire. Symbolic interactionism and Stryker's identity

  reverses the order and subsumes role under group.

## Limitations

- **No data.** Every result is reported from the authors' own programme,
  and several were in press. The reading checks what the paper says, not
  what the studies found.
- **"Lawful" and "veridical" are claimed, not specified.** No criterion
  says when a self-categorization is veridical; and since readiness takes
  in motives, goals and needs, fit alone does not fix the prediction.
- **The theory explains its findings after the fact more easily than it
  predicts them.** With readiness, two kinds of fit, and categories
  competing on a continuum, many outcomes can be fitted. Hornsey (2008,
  p. 217) reports the unfalsifiability charge, against the wider approach.
- **C6 is speculation by the authors' own word.** It is argued from
  analogy to category research, not from self data.

## Open questions

- Do the content and prototype shifts replicate outside the authors'
  group, with the comparison frame manipulated and the target held fixed?
  A primary study read in full would settle it for the record.
- Can C6 be distinguished empirically from a stored-repertoire account
  (Markus & Wurf's working self-concept) at all? The paper concedes the
  similarity and claims "real and substantial differences" it has no
  room to spell out (p. 459).
