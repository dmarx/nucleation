# NOTE-tmpdtj68: Ehrsson et al. — No specific relationship between hypnotic suggestibility and the rubber hand illusion

## Contribution

It shows, from the target study's own data, that the size of the rubber
hand illusion's synchrony effect does not depend on hypnotisability: the
least suggestible quarter of 353 participants shows the effect as clearly
as the most suggestible. It also shows that the correlations between
hypnotisability and illusion ratings that Lush et al. report are not
specific to the illusion: they appear as strongly for the asynchronous
control and for a statement describing an impossible experience. It adds
no new data.

## Key insight

The comment turns on one choice of measure. If the rubber hand illusion
is the *difference* between synchronous and asynchronous brushing,
hypnotisability does not predict it; if it is the rating in the
synchronous condition alone, hypnotisability does, modestly. Ehrsson et
al. hold that the difference is the illusion, as the field and Lush et
al.'s own preregistration did, so trait suggestibility touches the
general level of report and not the thing the illusion adds. Everything
then hangs on whether the asynchronous condition is a valid control,
which the comment assumes and the reply denies.

## Assumptions

- **The asynchronous condition controls for everything but timing.**
  This is the load-bearing premise. The comment does not consider that
  participants might expect different experiences in the two conditions;
  it argues instead that if they did, "this effect should be most
  pronounced in highly hypnotizable individuals" (p. 3).
- **A null difference across instruction groups means suggestion does
  not move the illusion.** Figure 1d's three groups were told which
  condition should work best; the comment itself says that "this
  manipulation failed" (p. 1), and the reply reports that the
  instructions did not change expectancies. The comment then uses the
  null as evidence that the illusion "should be resistant to
  suggestions" (p. 3).
- **SWASH measures the capacity in question.** Hypnotisability on the
  Sussex-Waterloo Scale is taken, with Lush et al., as the measure of
  trait response to suggestion.
- **Questionnaire data are ordinal.** Non-parametric tests throughout
  (Spearman, Wilcoxon, Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis), two-tailed, with
  default Cauchy priors for Bayes factors (Fig. 1 caption).

## Key results

- **The target study, as summarised (p. 1).** 353 participants did
  synchronous and asynchronous conditions; a third were told the
  illusion would be stronger in the synchronous condition, a third in the
  asynchronous, a third told nothing. Lush et al.'s preregistered test,
  in the no-instruction group, found "no evidence for a relationship
  between the SWASH scale and the illusion ratings" and "only anecdotal
  evidence" for drift (p = 0.074).
- **Whole-sample reanalysis (Fig. 1a, b).** SWASH against the
  synchronous-minus-asynchronous difference in mean illusion rating
  (statements S1–S3): rho = −0.066, p = 0.219, 95% CI [−0.169, 0.039],
  BF01 = 5.685, n = 353. Against the difference in proprioceptive drift:
  rho = 0.047, p = 0.376, 95% CI [−0.057, 0.151], BF01 = 1.657. The
  first is moderate evidence for the null; the second, by the usual
  threshold of 3, is not evidence either way, which the text does not
  say.
- **By item (Suppl. Fig. 1A–C).** S1, touch felt on the rubber hand:
  rho = −0.137, p = 0.01, BF01 = 0.44, a negative relation, "directly
  against the authors' (one-tailed) pre-registered hypothesis". S2, the
  felt touch caused by the seen touch: rho = −0.022, BF01 = 7.88. S3,
  "the explicit feeling of ownership": rho = 0.003, p = 0.962,
  BF01 = 7.525.
- **Quartiles (Fig. 1c).** In each SWASH quartile (n = 88, 89, 92, 84)
  synchronous ratings exceed asynchronous ones (Wilcoxon V = 2575.5 to
  3198, all p < 0.001) and synchronous ratings exceed zero (all
  p < 0.001). The synchrony effect does not differ across quartiles
  (Kruskal–Wallis χ² = 2.913, df = 3, p = 0.405; Q1 against Q4,
  W = 3879.5, p = 0.574). "Thus, even the least suggestible participants
  showed clear evidence of experiencing the illusion" (p. 1). No effect
  size is given for any of these contrasts.
- **Instructions (Fig. 1d).** The synchrony effect did not differ among
  the groups told synchronous (n = 114), told asynchronous (n = 115) and
  told nothing (n = 124): pairwise Mann–Whitney p = 0.73, 0.836, 0.584.
  Within each, the effect was significant (p < 0.001).
- **Non-specificity of the post hoc correlations (p. 2).** In Lush et
  al.'s exploratory analysis of the synchronous condition alone, SWASH
  "accounted for only ~9% of the illusion ratings' variability"; "a
  similar relationship was found for the asynchronous control condition
  (9%) and a control statement describing an 'impossible' experience of
  visual hallucinations (12%)". Subtracting the control statement (S4)
  from each illusion statement leaves no positive relation
  (Suppl. Fig. 1D–F: S1−S4 rho = −0.189, p < 0.001, BF01 = 0.009;
  S2−S4 rho = −0.063, BF01 = 3.8; S3−S4 rho = −0.013, BF01 = 6.3,
  n = 352). The authors add that the post hoc findings may reflect
  "behavioral compliance or response bias, rather than 'genuine'
  phenomenological control effects".
- **The literature review (pp. 2–3).** Lush et al. justified dropping the
  asynchronous condition by 30 highly cited RHI studies; "28 of the 30
  studies used the asynchronous control to conduct critical statistical
  comparisons with the synchronous condition".
- **The temporal window (p. 3).** Cited studies varied the asynchrony
  and found that "the illusion gradually diminished" and "was abolished
  for asynchronies >200–300 ms", a rule that fits multisensory neurons'
  integration windows and causal-inference models, and that "is not
  explained by phenomenological control theory because suggestions have
  been well controlled in studies using subtle experimental
  manipulations of asynchrony". None of these studies was read here; the
  comment does not say how suggestion was controlled in them.
- **Conclusion.** The results "fit well with the view that the RHI is a
  perceptual illusion driven primarily by multisensory mechanisms"
  (p. 1); Lush et al. add to "the growing literature on individual
  differences in the RHI" without evidence "that fundamentally alters
  our understanding" (p. 3).

## Concepts

- **Phenomenological control** — Lush et al.'s term, adopted here, for
  the capacity to generate experiences that meet expectations, measured
  as hypnotisability.
- **Difference measure** — the illusion operationalised as the
  synchronous rating minus the asynchronous one, "in line with standard
  practice in the field and the authors' preregistered hypothesis" (p. 1).
- **Illusion statements S1–S3 and control statement S4** — S1 touch at
  the rubber hand, S2 felt touch caused by the seen brush, S3 ownership;
  S4 a visual-hallucination statement (Suppl. Fig. 1).

## Bearing on the record

Read against the reply, [NOTE-tmplury8](NOTE-tmplury8.md). The two NOTEs carry the same
verdicts on the THEORYs; this one gives the comment's half.

- **[THEORY-tmpef8lf](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpef8lf.md) (Proposed), the synchrony effect.** Its
  promote_when asks for a first-hand reading of a replication with sample
  sizes, named tests and effect sizes for questionnaire and drift, and a
  suggestion control: "participants' expectations of the synchronous and
  asynchronous conditions are measured or matched, or ... trait
  suggestibility is measured and the synchrony effect survives it". This
  comment **partly meets it.** It is a large replication (n = 353) with
  named tests, and on the questionnaire it shows the synchrony effect
  surviving trait suggestibility, which is the second disjunct in the
  letter. It falls short in three ways: it is a reanalysis of Lush et
  al.'s data, so the replication is theirs and is being read here at
  second hand; it gives no effect sizes for the synchrony contrasts, only
  V statistics and p-values; and for drift it reports only the null,
  inconclusive relation to SWASH, not the synchrony effect on drift
  itself. The reply then shows that the disjunct is met while the first
  one fails: expectations were measured in this sample and differed
  between conditions, and that difference did not vary with
  hypnotisability, so the survival of the effect is predicted by the
  demand account as well. Neither refutation condition is met: no
  condition matched expectations, and no drift under asynchrony is
  reported. My proposal, set out in [NOTE-tmplury8](NOTE-tmplury8.md), is to tighten the
  promote_when so that surviving trait suggestibility no longer suffices
  alone.
- **[THEORY-tmpxdoe8](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpxdoe8.md) (Proposed), graded common-cause ownership.**
  Not met. The comment's strongest argument for a multisensory mechanism
  is the graded temporal window, which is the right kind of parametric
  evidence, but it is cited, not shown, and the causal-inference models
  it names were not fitted here. Supplementary Fig. 1C is worth noting:
  the ownership item's synchrony effect has no relation to
  hypnotisability (BF01 = 7.5), the item the THEORY is about. Nothing
  here bears on the second requirement, the real hand losing ownership.
- **[THEORY-tmpom3z2](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpom3z2.md) (Proposed), interoception.** No direct bearing.
  Indirectly, by its own logic, the comment would defend synchronous
  against asynchronous comparisons like the cardio-visual ones the THEORY
  rests on; the reply is the half that bears on it.
- **[THEORY-tmpuehzc](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpuehzc.md) (Proposed), minimal selfhood.** No bearing. No
  whole-body measure is taken; the comment concerns a hand.
- **[THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md) (Proposed), ownership against agency.** No bearing; the
  hand is passive in every condition.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** This exchange is a worked case of the question.
  Both sides agree on every number and disagree on what the reports are
  evidence of. Each invokes response bias against the other: this
  comment to say that hypnotisability's correlations may be "behavioral
  compliance or response bias" rather than generated experience, the
  reply to say that low-suggestibles' illusion may be the same. The
  behavioural measure, drift, does not settle it here, because its
  relation to SWASH is inconclusive.
- **[NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md), claim C6.** Metzinger's "body ownership can be
  manipulated by multisensory conflict" is the view this comment
  defends. It defends it with the asynchronous control, which is the
  point in dispute, so the manipulation half of C6 stands as the field's
  standard reading and not as settled.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- A reanalysis of another group's data, by authors whose own work uses
  the design under criticism. The comment declares no competing
  interests, which is accurate in the journal's sense.
- The validity of the asynchronous control against expectancy is assumed
  and not argued. The comment's test of it, that demand effects "should
  be most pronounced in highly hypnotizable individuals", is not the
  only prediction a demand account makes, as the reply says.
- Figure 1d is used as evidence that suggestion does not move the
  illusion, though the comment says the instruction manipulation failed.
  If, as the reply reports, the instructions did not move expectation,
  the null cannot show that the illusion resists it.
- No effect sizes for any synchrony contrast; Bayes factors only for the
  SWASH relations. The drift result is called "non-significant" without
  noting that BF01 = 1.66 cannot support the null.
- The temporal-window argument, the one piece of evidence that a demand
  account would have to work to explain, rests wholly on citations.
- These analyses are not preregistered either, as the reply points out.

## Open questions

- Would the synchrony effect survive with expectations matched across
  conditions, for example by an asynchronous condition participants are
  led to expect more from? Neither side reports such a test.
- Do expectations follow the graded temporal window? If participants
  expect the illusion to fade gradually with asynchrony, the cited
  studies do not separate the accounts; if they do not, the window is the
  comment's best evidence.
- Why does S1, touch at the rubber hand, show a negative relation to
  hypnotisability in the difference measure (Suppl. Fig. 1A, D)? Neither
  side explains it.
