# CLAIM-tmp58u4k: An arrangement, a technology among them, can satisfy people's preferences more fully, and raise the well-being they report, while narrowing their opportunities to engage actively with what is worthwhile, so success at satisfying preferences does not show that the lives an arrangement leaves open are going better

## The claim

The exchange "Analyze technological civilization trap" (https://chatgpt.com/share/6af6b47c-8b0c-83ea-ae04-3e3eb2649597, 2026-11-13), A9 §III, after the owner's U7 ("weave in susan wolf"):

> A society could become exceptionally good at identifying and
> satisfying people's preferences while simultaneously becoming worse at
> helping them encounter things worth caring about.

and: "It is that technological systems might become increasingly effective at satisfying desires **without improving our relationship to what is genuinely valuable**." A10 §V, with Sen, makes the same point on the side of agency: a prosperous society offering few opportunities for influence is deficient, and "Sen gives us a reason to regard that as a deficiency even if citizens report high levels of satisfaction."

## Why it holds

Wolf's account (THEORY-tmp4ajaz) separates fulfilment from meaning: meaning "arises when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness", and Sisyphus Fulfilled is worse off in a respect "however fulfilled he feels" (NOTE-tmpzth5f). If a person's fulfilment can come apart from the worth of what fulfils her, an arrangement that changes what fulfils people, and how fully, can raise fulfilment while lowering engagement with worth. Arrangements do change what fulfils people: Marcuse's needs "superimposed upon the individual by particular social interests", whose "satisfaction might be most gratifying to the individual" (THEORY-tmpoxssh), and Illich's industrial tools, beyond a level of which "no amount of industrial productivity can effectively satisfy the needs it creates among society’s members" (THEORY-tmpww16o, NOTE-tmp8ceuj).

## Admission

A separating case, stipulated: two arrangements give the same people equal satisfaction of their preferences and equal reported well-being; in the first their satisfactions come largely from activities of worth they engage in, in the second from activities with little worth beyond the satisfaction itself. Every premise holds: Wolf's distinction is not met by either, and both arrangements are possible on Marcuse's and Illich's accounts. A measure of preference satisfaction ranks them equal; the claim says they differ in how the lives they leave go. That is what the claim adds to Wolf, whose account is of a life, not of the arrangements around it.

## What it does not say

- Not that people's preferences are false or manipulated; A9 §III is explicit that the enjoyment "might be perfectly genuine". Whether a preference's origin matters is CLAIM-tmpx60mo.
- Not which activities are worthwhile, or who may say; that is CLAIM-tmpfyxes.
- Not that satisfying preferences is worthless. Nor is it the agency side by itself: Sen's distinction of well-being freedom from agency freedom is held as a reading (NOTE-260, from Robeyns's entry; THEORY-tmpj32pb, the Tanner Lectures), and this claim uses it only as A10 §V does.

## Where it sits

On the flourishing-and-meaning line (ADR-099). It complements CLAIM-tmpbypt7: that claim measures the freedom an arrangement leaves by what remains open to a non-adopter, this one says that how fully an arrangement satisfies people does not measure how their lives go. It bears on QUESTION-369, whether a need is something functioning requires or something that raises reported well-being, and on QUESTION-tmpvpq3r, the standard by which arrangements are judged.
