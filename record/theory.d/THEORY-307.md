# THEORY-307: Adaptation by trial and error in a large system finishes in a practicable time only if partial adaptations are retained, and retention requires that the system not be fully joined: parts must be separated, at least temporarily, by constancies, and where later learning shares parts with earlier learning it destroys it

## Source

Ashby, *Design for a Brain* (2nd ed., 1960), [LIT-787](../literature.d/LIT-787.md), read in
[NOTE-851](../notes.d/NOTE-851.md): Ss. 10/5–10/9, 11/2–11/12, 12/10–12/17, 13/8–13/13, 15/4–15/11,
16/7–16/15, 17/3, and the proof in S. 22/14.

## What was actually shown

**Exponential against linear (Ss. 11/2–11/6).** With N jointly required
successes each of probability p per trial: all at once, (1/p)ᴺ trials;
one after another, about N/p; all tried, failures retried, a little more
than 1/p. For p = ½, N = 1,000, at one trial a second: about 10²⁹³ years,
about 8 minutes, a few seconds. "What had made the processes … so
excessively time-consuming is that partial successes go for nothing."
Crystallisation is the physical case: impossible if every molecule must
fall into place at once, routine by succession (S. 11/8).

**Retention needs disconnection (S. 11/10).** If adapting one essential
variable leaves others adapted, the step-mechanisms that changed can have
no channel to the parts that serve the others. "For the accumulation of
adaptations to be possible the system must not be fully joined."

**Constancies separate (Ss. 12/10, 22/14).** Two subsystems joined only
through a third are independent and each state-determined if and only if
the third is constant: "a wall of constancies". Parts that go constant
over a threshold give temporary independence; enough of them "can cut a
system to pieces" (S. 12/17). In a richly joined system a near-complete
equilibrium "will almost certainly fall back"; in a poorly joined one the
number of parts at equilibrium "cannot fall with time" (Ss. 13/9–13/10).

**Separate stores for separate situations (Ss. 10/8–10/9, 16/12–16/15).**
For an adaptation to A to survive learning B and be ready when A recurs,
the step-mechanisms must split into non-overlapping sets, with the
situation selecting which set is on the second feedback. In a multistable
system with few parts active per stimulus, different stimuli use largely
different parts, which does this without a dedicated gate; similar stimuli
share parts, and then "new learning does tend to destroy old", more as
the learnings resemble each other (Robinson's numbers, Skaggs's chess
positions). Accumulation and retroactive inhibition are opposed, and an
intermediate degree of connection shows both partially.

**The environment allows it (Ss. 15/2–15/11).** Ordinary environments
are mostly part-functions, constant most of the time and each affecting
few others, so they can be adapted to piece by piece, in their natural
order where they form a chain, provided the links carry little.

What could have come out otherwise: the calculations could have shown
the brain's speed compatible with rich joining. They show it is not, for
independent probabilities.

## What this does not say

- **Not measured.** The times are computed for type-cases with
  independent successes. The match to habituation, ablation and
  interference is qualitative; Ashby leaves measurement to experimenters
  (S. 16/11).
- **Not that less connection is always better.** Joining adds repertoire
  and early warning (Ss. 17/5–17/6); connection must lie "between certain
  limits" (S. 17/7).
- **Not a mechanism for the gate.** How the right store is selected is
  shown only for the random-dispersion case.
- **Not specific to brains.** The results are about state-determined
  systems with step-mechanisms; any system of that kind is covered, and
  nothing more is claimed about which ones the brain uses.
