# THEORY-1947: Automated decisions evade due process

## Source

Citron (2008), Washington University Law Review 85: 1249–1313 ([LIT-2719](../literature.d/LIT-2719.md)), read in [NOTE-2003](../notes.d/NOTE-2003.md).

## What was actually shown

An argument from US doctrine and from documented failures (Colorado's CBMS, CalWIN, TIERS, Florida's
ACCESS, the "No Fly" list, the Federal Parent Locator Service). Automated systems "combine individual
adjudications with rulemaking while adhering to the procedural" safeguards of neither (Abstract).
"Programmers routinely change" rules in translating them (p. 1254); in mixed systems "Automation bias
effectively turns a computer" program's suggestion into a final decision (p. 1272); a Mathews analysis of
one case "will capture all of the costs of improved process but only a tiny fraction of its benefits"
(p. 1285). Remedies: audit trails, training and reasons for hearing officers, a recalibrated Mathews,
open code, testing suites, review boards, and automating only what rules handle better than standards.

Corroborates [CLAIM-710](../claims.d/CLAIM-710.md) in its "exposes" reading: in the cases documented, removing the human step
removed the point at which a decision could be overruled ("Eligibility workers were unable to overrule
this decision", p. 1264), and keeping it under automation bias kept the step without the opportunity.

## What this does not say

Nothing measured: the failures are reports and audits of 2004–2007. Nothing on machine learning. It does
not say automation should be avoided; it says when and under what procedures. It does not support the
"characterizes" reading of [CLAIM-710](../claims.d/CLAIM-710.md): its own remedies restore intervention without delay. It does not
support [CLAIM-705](../claims.d/CLAIM-705.md)'s fixed corrective rate: its point is that correcting code corrects many cases.

## From the second reading (2026-11-17)

The second reading finds three more claims corroborated, none resting on
it: [CLAIM-740](../claims.d/CLAIM-740.md) (notice, hearing and pre-deprivation process are
distinct safeguards that fail differently; process before deprivation is
"surely possible" because the agency controls the system, p. 1288);
[CLAIM-731](../claims.d/CLAIM-731.md) (automation lowers the cost of deciding while a challenge
needs costly decoding of the system, p. 1285); and [CLAIM-410](../claims.d/CLAIM-410.md)'s
"transferred" case (rule-making authority passes to programmers by
"inadvertent rulemaking", "a troubling delegation of legislative power",
p. 1288).

- **That a human in the loop never helps** is not shown: the claim about
  mixed systems rests on the automation-bias literature as it stood in
