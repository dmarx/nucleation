# THEORY-316: Frankfurt-style cases defeat alternative-possibilities conditions on moral responsibility but leave standing the incompatibilist condition on an action's causal history, which is the more fundamental one

## Source

Pereboom (2001), *Living without Free Will*, [LIT-1006](../literature.d/LIT-1006.md), read in part in
[NOTE-823](../notes.d/NOTE-823.md): Chapter 1, "Two incompatibilist intuitions" and "Leeway
vs. causal-history incompatibilism" (pp. 1–6), and the Introduction's
statement of the chapter's thesis (p. xv). The pages are from Cambridge
University Press's published excerpt and front matter.

## What was actually shown

An argument in two steps, the second resting on a concession from the
other side.

**Step 1: alternative possibilities fall.** Principles (1) and (2) say an
action is free in the sense responsibility requires only if the agent
could have done, or chosen, otherwise. In Fischer's case, Black's device
would have made Jones vote for Reagan had he shown an inclination to vote
for Carter. Jones decides for Reagan on his own. He seems responsible,
although he could not have done or chosen otherwise (pp. 2–3). Pereboom
holds that any alternative-possibilities requirement "relevant to
explaining why an agent is morally responsible ... falls to
counterexamples" (p. 1). The pages read state Widerker's objection to
this step and leave its answer for later.

**Step 2: causal history survives.** The case "does not specify that
Jones's action is causally determined". Fischer concedes that the argument
"leaves untouched the view that moral responsibility requires that one's
action not actually result from a deterministic causal process that
traces back to factors beyond one's control" (p. 3). Had Jones's choice
been specified as produced by "Martian neuroscientists", "the intuition
that he could be morally responsible might well fade away". The factors
that remove alternatives "need not be part of the actual causal history".
So condition (3) survives: "An action is free in the sense required for
moral responsibility only if it is not produced by a deterministic
process that traces back to causal factors beyond the agent's control."

**Which is more fundamental.** Condition (3) and Kane's (U) express
origination (O). The production of a decision must be something "over
which the agent has control" (p. 4). Pereboom calls this "the most
fundamental and plausible incompatibilist intuition". It explains why
determinism threatens responsibility, and why an uncaused decision would
too (p. 5). The view is causal-history incompatibilism, as against
leeway incompatibilism.

What could have come out otherwise: Step 2 depends on the intuition
fading when determination by outside factors is specified. A reader whose
intuition does not fade has a counterexample, which is the compatibilist's
position.

## What this does not say

- **Not that determinism rules out responsibility.** Condition (3) is a
  necessary condition that "any incompatibilist should endorse" (p. 3).
  That it is true, against the compatibilist, is argued in Chapter 4 by
  the comparison with covert manipulation, which was not read.
- **Not that every alternative-possibilities condition is false.** Pereboom
  leaves open conditions "necessary for moral responsibility but
  nevertheless irrelevant to explaining why an agent is morally
  responsible" (p. 2). His own robust-alternative condition and his new
  Frankfurt-style case are on pp. 6–23, mostly unread.
- **Not that Step 1 is secure.** Widerker's dilemma is stated as "a test
  for the effectiveness of any Frankfurt-style argument" (p. 10), and the
  answer lies past the pages read.
- **Held at the strength of a partial reading**, of an argument whose
  second step rests on an intuition and a concession, not a proof.

## Connections

No relation is declared. None of the open questions asks which
incompatibilist condition is fundamental.

- **[THEORY-119](THEORY-119.md)** holds that the principle of alternate possibilities is
  false by Frankfurt-style cases. This account accepts that, and adds
  that it leaves incompatibilism standing. The two do not conflict. They
  part where [THEORY-119](THEORY-119.md) joins Fischer's compatibilist conclusion.
- **[THEORY-121](THEORY-121.md)** makes taking responsibility a historical condition on
  owning the mechanism. Condition (3) is a historical condition of a
  different kind: on the causes of the decision, not on the agent's
  attitude to her mechanism.
- **[THEORY-040](THEORY-040.md)** asks whether any historical condition escapes the
  manipulation argument. Condition (3) is the incompatibilist's, and an
  agent deterministically manipulated by outside factors fails it, so
  manipulation cases of that kind do not show it insufficient. Whether it
  is true, and not only consistent with the cases, is what Chapter 4
  argues. Indeterministic manipulation is not discussed in the pages read.

## Note of 2026-10-30: which alternative-possibilities conditions the title means

The title's "defeat alternative-possibilities conditions" means principles
(1) and (2), and any such condition "relevant to explaining why an agent
is morally responsible" (p. 1), as the body says; it does not mean every
such condition. Timpe ([LIT-tmpn8wog](../literature.d/LIT-tmpn8wog.md), read in [NOTE-tmpz05rj](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpz05rj.md)) argues that
the incompatibilist's ultimacy condition entails a weak, possibly
non-robust alternatives condition that Frankfurt-style cases leave
standing (p. 150), and counts Pereboom among those who keep one (p. 147).
That agrees with Pereboom's allowance of conditions "necessary for moral
responsibility but nevertheless irrelevant to explaining why" (p. 2). It
weighs on the title's wording, not its thesis. Fischer ([LIT-tmppshez](../literature.d/LIT-tmppshez.md), read
in [NOTE-tmpxvceo](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpxvceo.md)), writing after the book, again concedes the route Step 2
builds on: determinism may still undermine responsibility through "lack of
'sourcehood'" (p. 331).
