# NOTE-1136: Zorowitz, Momennejad & Daw — Anxiety, avoidance, and sequential evaluation

## Contribution

Exaggerated threat appraisal, fear generalization and avoidance far in
advance of threat had been described in anxiety but not derived from one
cause. The paper shows that a single departure from normative sequential
evaluation produces all three in a Markov decision process: the agent
evaluates each action as if it will not act well afterwards. It also links
that departure to the clinical literature on perceived control and
self-efficacy, which earlier computational accounts of control (Huys &
Dayan 2009) had treated only for one-step choices.

## Key insight

The "max" in the Bellman equation makes approach and avoidance asymmetric:
opportunities for reward propagate back to earlier states, but avoidable
dangers do not, because the best action near them is to avoid. "If danger
can be avoided later, it poses less threat now" (p. 1). Remove the
assumption that one will avoid later and the asymmetry goes: threat value
spreads backward across the state space, and an agent choosing optimally
under that misbelief avoids early and widely. What is wrong is a belief
about one's own future choices, not about the danger.

## Assumptions

- **The model (p. 4, Eq. 3).** State-action values are computed as
  Q_w(s,a) = r(s,a) + γ Σ p(s′|s,a)[w max Q_w(s′,a′) + (1 − w) min Q_w(s′,a′)],
  the β-pessimistic value function of Gaskett (2003). w = 1 is the
  optimal agent; w = 0 expects always to act against its preferences.
- **The belief is false (p. 4).** "Importantly, this belief is false under
  the model": the agent does maximize at each step, but computes values on
  the assumption that it will not later.
- **Where the pessimism sits (pp. 4, 10).** It could be put in the policy
  (misbelief about one's own avoidance) or in the transition probabilities
  (misbelief that the world's response is unpredictable or adversarial),
  or in uncertainty about the current state. The paper simulates only the
  first and says the others "can produce ultimately similar results".
- **Environments (pp. 12–14).** Deterministic, infinite-horizon, discrete
  MDPs; values solved by value iteration except in the free-choice task,
  where a pessimistic temporal-difference learner is used (learning rate
  0.4, 1,000 simulated agents per level of w).

## Key results

All results are simulations; the empirical findings they match are cited.

- **Gridworld (p. 3, Fig. 1).** With w = 1 every state except the harmful
  one has positive value; with w = 0.5 and w = 0 negative value spreads
  from the threat and the optimal path detours further. The authors read
  this as exaggerated threat appraisal, generalization and persistent
  avoidance (p. 4).
- **Balloon analogue risk task (p. 5, Fig. 2A–C).** Pessimistic agents
  (w = 0.6, 0.2) cash out earlier, as anxious people do (Maner et al.
  2007; Ramírez et al. 2015).
- **A unique prediction (p. 6).** Bias should appear only where future
  avoidance matters. The authors cite Fung et al. (2019): trait anxiety
  predicted earlier escape from slow predators but not fast ones.
- **Behavioural inhibition (p. 6, Fig. 2D–E).** In Bach's (2015) sleeping
  predator task, the relative value of approach falls with potential loss,
  more under pessimism; with the added assumption that lower relative
  value slows responses, the model reproduces slower approach under threat
  and its enhancement by anxiety.
- **Aversive pruning (pp. 6–7, Fig. 3).** A pessimistic agent (w = 0.5)
  avoids a branch with a large early loss followed by a larger gain, not
  because it fails to look ahead but because it expects not to recoup the
  loss. They propose a test that would separate their account from Huys et
  al.'s pruning (p. 7).
- **Anxiety to depression (pp. 7–8, Fig. 4).** During learning under
  pessimism, negative value spreads until the agent expects no reward and
  forgoes action, a model of the clinical hypothesis that anxiety precedes
  some depression.
- **Persistence after treatment (p. 8).** Argued, not simulated: because
  threat value has spread far, correcting it takes many steps of
  relearning, and "Frustratingly, these biased estimates of value may still
  remain even after a misbelief in the efficacy of future action is
  corrected for in a course of therapy." They compare it to model-free
  learners' failure to adjust to revaluation.
- **Free-choice premium (pp. 8–9, Fig. 5).** People prefer options that
  lead to further choice even when outcomes are identical. Pessimistic
  learners show a diminished or reversed preference; the authors predict
  the same for anxious people. "Future empirical research will be required
  to test this prediction" (p. 9).

## Concepts

- **Avoidance against escape**: avoidance prevents the onset of a threat;
  escape responds to a threat already near (p. 2).
- **β-pessimistic value**: the mixture of best and worst next action, with
  weight w on the best (p. 4).
- **Free-choice premium**: a preference for options that lead to further
  choices, with outcomes held equal (p. 8).

## Connections

It formalises self-efficacy theory (Bandura & Adams 1977), Barlow's triple
vulnerability model and learned-helplessness accounts of anxiety (Alloy et
al. 1990), and extends Huys & Dayan (2009) to sequential choice. It reads
the Bishop group's uncertainty work (Browning et al. 2015) as compatible:
uncertainty is aversive because it "is resolved (i.e., marginalized) under
pessimistic distributional assumptions" (p. 10). It points to replay and
model-based planning as a possible mechanism, which would make worry
"overcontemplating, or overweighting, certain pessimistic trajectories"
(p. 11). Brown et al. ([LIT-1479](../literature.d/LIT-1479.md)) later doubt that pessimistic policies
capture anxiety rather than normal behaviour under threat.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-446](../claims.d/CLAIM-446.md) (governance-maintained anxiety).** The exchange cited
  the paper for pessimistic beliefs about one's own future control
  spreading avoidance backward. The reading confirms that and adds three
  things the claim must reckon with.
  - **A rival with the danger estimate held correct.** In this model the
    agent knows how dangerous the threat is; what is wrong is its belief
    about its own future avoidance. The claim's test holds "the
    represented threat probability" fixed and varies authority. This model
    would also pass that test with a belief variable alone, by varying w.
    The claim needs its test to hold beliefs about one's own control fixed
    too, or it cannot tell governance from pessimistic self-prediction.
  - **The claim's "defensive authority" and this paper's "self-efficacy"
    are near neighbours.** In the claim, a defensive process is granted
    influence; here, the agent expects its future self to fail. Both make
    avoidance a matter of the agent's organisation of its own future
    action, not of the world. The difference is that this paper puts it in
    a belief, so belief correction should work, while the claim says
    belief correction is not behavioural correction.
  - **Outlasting a corrected belief.** The paper anticipates the claim's
    "the regime can outlast a corrected belief": biased values "may still
    remain even after a misbelief in the efficacy of future action is
    corrected" (p. 8). Its explanation is cached value that needs
    relearning, which is the claim's own defeater, "a habit model with
    matched memory". So the paper offers that alternative explanation for
    the same phenomenon, and the claim has to show something cached
    values would not.
- **[CLAIM-283](../claims.d/CLAIM-283.md) (claimed precedence against granted authority).** Not
  addressed; the model has one valuation process, not several competing
  for authority.
- **[QUESTION-045](../questions.d/QUESTION-045.md).** A formal maintaining link (pessimism about future
  control) and a formal reason for slow recovery, at the level of a model
  without individual data.
- **[THEORY-285](../theory.d/THEORY-285.md).** A second model-level account of self-maintaining threat,
  with a different maintaining variable: there, a precise prior about the
  context; here, pessimism about one's own future policy.
- **Correction to the exchange.** The exchange's "spreads avoidance
  backward in time" is close: the spread is to antecedent states in a
  state space, and with discounting it is also earlier in a trajectory. The
  paper is a set of simulations and has no data of its own.
- Filed: [THEORY-915](../theory.d/THEORY-915.md).
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **No data are fitted.** Every match to anxiety is qualitative, to
  studies cited.
- **The persistence claim is not simulated** (p. 8).
- **Scope stated by the authors (pp. 11–12).** A model of prospective
  cognition, more likely to fit generalized and social anxiety; it cannot
  account for compulsions in OCD or memory disturbance in PTSD.
- **Pessimism can be adaptive.** The authors say these cases "may reflect
  the exaggerative extremes of an otherwise adaptive evaluation strategy"
  (p. 11), as in risk-sensitive reinforcement learning. That leaves open
  where adaptive caution ends.
- **One variant simulated** of the three named.
- **Slips.** "asymptomatic Q-values" for asymptotic (pp. 13–14); "dys-
  reguated" (p. 12); "Bellmare" (p. 11). None changes a result.

## Open questions

- Does the free-choice premium fall with anxiety, as predicted?
- Does trial-by-trial behaviour in sequential avoidance tasks fit a
  pessimistic weight better than an optimal agent with altered threat
  values?
- After correction of the control belief (for example by a successful
  mastery experience), does avoidance of distant states persist, and does
  it decay at the rate cached relearning predicts?
