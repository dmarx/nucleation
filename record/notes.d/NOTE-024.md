# NOTE-024: Rhode Island Hospital subpoena order

## Contribution

The Rhode Island Child Advocate and Rhode Island Hospital (RIH) each moved to quash a DOJ HIPAA administrative subpoena. Issued in July 2025 as part of a nationwide investigation, the subpoena sought years of identifying and clinical records for every minor who had received gender-affirming care at RIH. According to the order, DOJ stopped negotiating with RIH and then, without notice, obtained an enforcement order from the Northern District of Texas. The court finds that the Child Advocate has standing and that the collateral-attack doctrine does not bar relief, because it quashes the subpoena itself rather than the Texas enforcement order. The court also sharply criticizes DOJ's candor toward both courts and toward the negotiating parties.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- Opening (pp. 1-2): the court states that DOJ "has proven unworthy of this trust at every point in this case". It says DOJ misrepresented and withheld information from this court and the Texas court in order to reach a forum it saw as favorable. Both motions to quash (ECF Nos. 1, 28) are granted.
- Background (pp. 2-3): the subpoena sought names, Social Security numbers, addresses, diagnoses, clinical histories and family information. RIH negotiated and made an initial production, DOJ stopped communicating in February 2026, and on April 30 it filed an enforcement petition in N.D. Tex. (No. 4:26-mc-00006-O).
- §II.A-B (pp. 8-13): the Child Advocate has standing. The collateral-attack doctrine does not apply given RIH's lack of notice, the Child Advocate's nonparty status and the target of the challenge.
- §II.C.1 (pp. 14-18): there is no congressionally authorized purpose. The DOJ's FDCA misbranding theory against prescribing hospitals cannot be squared with off-label prescribing being lawful (21 U.S.C. § 396; the Facteau case) or with FDA's and OLC's own prior interpretations.
- §II.C.2-3 (pp. 18-23): the court finds an improper purpose, following seven other federal courts that quashed similar subpoenas. It recognizes an informational privacy right (Whalen v. Roe) and extends relief to all minors' records, because separating out the records of children in state (DCYF) custody would itself expose them.
- Conclusion (pp. 23-24): the court quashes the subpoena, not the Texas order, and enjoins DOJ from receiving, using, retaining or disseminating patient-identifying information or PHI produced under Subpoena No. 25-1431-032.

## Open questions

- The order bears on government access to medical records and on the constitutional right to informational privacy, which are relevant to data-governance and privacy questions around health data.
- It creates an unusual inter-district conflict with the N.D. Tex. enforcement order. The outcome of any appeal to the First Circuit, and any response from the Texas court, is unverified.
- The order's characterizations of DOJ's conduct are the court's findings. DOJ's own filings (e.g., ECF No. 9) would give its position.
