# CASE-tmpd5exw: The LEGO bin: a brick owned in one copy and buried among hundreds of similar ones may as well not be owned, a black brick among white ones needs no bin of its own, and every brick can be found by going through the whole collection

## The case

The owner, 2026-07-02, captured verbatim in Mathematics of Meaning
(`homelab/own-words.md`, "The Lego parable — retrieval, detection, and the
time–space trade", at MOM commit d987e2a):

> the problem is retrieval. you're building something and you want to
> find a specific brick. you have a bin that contains a bunch of different
> similar bricks. the noise is the other bricks.

> If there's a particular brick that you have very few of but use often,
> you can put those bricks in their own tiny bin to make sure you can find
> them and they don't get subsumed and lost in a big pile.

> if I need a particular lego brick that I only have 1 of and I put it in
> a bin with hundreds of other similar legos, I may as well not have the
> brick. I can't access it when I need it.

> if it's a single black brick in a box of white bricks: ok, it's buried
> but its' still detectable. no problem. it can be a unique member of the
> family that goes in that bin. if it's too similar to the other bricks
> though, the difficulty to locate it manifests an imperative to set it
> aside as a new kind in a designated box.

> ultimately, every brick in my collection is detectable if I have the
> time to go through my entire collection, one at a time, checking every
> single brick. but life is short and my time is limited, so if I can't
> find it *quickly enough*, it may as well not be there.

In the same passage he tells the EMT story this record holds as
[CASE-085](CASE-085.md), and ends it on the same line: "if you can't find it, it isn't
there".

## What it can show

- **The cause of a retrieval failure can be similarity, not size.** The
  black brick and the similar brick are equally buried. Only the second is
  lost. Holding the bin fixed and changing only how much the wanted item
  resembles its binmates is the contrast this case is for.
- **What decides a new bin is copy count and use, not evidence.** "Very
  few of but use often" names the cell where a bin pays. [CLAIM-tmp6u639](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp6u639.md) works this
  out: it is the cell where evidence that a distinction exists is weakest.
- **Presence is never in doubt; time is.** Exhaustive search always
  succeeds. So "may as well not have the brick" is a claim about
  retrieval before a deadline, as in [CLAIM-296](../claims.d/CLAIM-296.md) and [TERM-055](../terms.d/TERM-055.md), and not
  about inventory.
- **A bin is made when the item is found.** The practice described is
  setting a hard-to-find brick aside once it has been found, so the
  extra cost of the new bin is small. That bears on [QUESTION-095](../questions.d/QUESTION-095.md):
  sorting spends search once against later retrievals ([QUESTION-tmplg07t](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmplg07t.md)).

## What it cannot show

- How long any search took, how often the brick was wanted, or how the
  owner's bins are in fact arranged. None of it was measured.
- That similarity slows search in general. That is a finding of the
  visual-search literature ([LIT-tmp9g55n](../literature.d/LIT-tmp9g55n.md), [LIT-tmp95pcw](../literature.d/LIT-tmp95pcw.md)), registered here unread.
- That the owner's bins are a good arrangement. [CLAIM-tmpjmtw0](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjmtw0.md) argues that which
  arrangement is good depends on what a builder searches for.

## Standing

Stipulated, as [CASE-085](CASE-085.md) is. The collection and the practice are real;
the record has them only on the owner's account.
