# LIT-129: Epistemic injustice in the clinical care of practitioners of Afro-Brazilian religions

José Eduardo Porcher, Felipe José Santaella (2026), *Philosophical Psychology (online ahead of issue; pp. 1–25)* — DOI-10.1080/09515089.2026.2623889

## Key takeaways

- When clinicians override a patient's religious interpretation of her experience in domains where medicine has no interpretive authority ("interpretive exclusivism"), they commit an epistemic injustice — racialised in the Brazilian case — that the authors say only epistemic humility remedies.

*Seeded from the abstract alone, not a reading. What follows is what the work says about itself.*

The authors build a normative account of epistemic injustice in psychiatry around one case: a twelve-year-old girl, M., whose Afro-Brazilian religious framework for her experiences was excluded in hospital. They name the mechanism "interpretive exclusivism" — clinicians overriding patients' primary meaning-making resources where medicine lacks interpretive authority. Drawing on testimonial smothering and contributory injustice, they show how M. and her mother were denied uptake, which damaged the mother's self-trust as an interpreter and closed off sources of care. They place this within the history of pathologising and criminalising African-derived religions in Brazil, discuss the family's epistemic resistance, and argue that epistemic justice in psychiatry requires epistemic humility.
