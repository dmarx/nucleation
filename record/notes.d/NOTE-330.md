# NOTE-330: Damasio & Carvalho — The nature of feelings

## Contribution

A compact neural account of feeling in three layers. At the functional
layer, feelings are separated from the drives and emotions (action
programmes) that cause them, and given a job: they are valenced proxies of
homeostatic state that support learning and anticipation. At the systems
layer, the paper argues from lesion and stimulation evidence that feeling
is generated in the upper brainstem and only modulated by the insula and
somatosensory cortex. That is a direct challenge to Craig's view that the
anterior insula is the "sole platform" of human feeling and awareness
(refs 60, 111). At the cellular layer it adds a new speculative proposal:
feeling begins in unmyelinated interoceptive fibres.

## Key insight

Feeling is interoception made experiential. The body is mapped centrally
whether or not anything is felt, and most regulation runs on those maps
with no experience at all. A feeling is what such a map is like when it is
experienced. Because body states are good or bad for life, the experience
comes with a valence already attached, and that valence is the
organism's direct reading of its own biological value. On this view the
exteroceptive senses have no valence of their own: they borrow it from
the body (concluding remarks).

## Assumptions

- **Feelings are about the body.** Descriptions of feelings "tend to
  reference states of the body", and introspective reports from antiquity
  on are taken as evidence of this (refs 1–5). Experiences of the
  exteroceptive senses are excluded from "feeling" by definition (Box 1,
  glossary).
- **Feeling requires topographic maps.** The search for substrates is
  restricted to structures with "topographically organized somatic maps".
  This is offered as "sensible", by analogy with vision and hearing, not
  argued. It is what excludes the amygdala, nucleus accumbens and basal
  forebrain from a direct role.
- **Behaviour "consistent with" feeling is evidence of feeling.** It is
  used for decorticate children (refs 112–113), decorticate mammals,
  brainstem stimulation in animals (refs 42, 117) and non-human species
  generally. The authors grant that feeling in non-human species "cannot
  at present be demonstrated", and fall back on parsimony.
- **Valence is intrinsic to homeostatic deviation.** Body states "are
  necessarily valenced". This is stated, not derived.
- **Synchrony scales cellular to systems phenomena.** The model "commonly
  accepted for cognition" (refs 158–159) is assumed to apply to feeling.

## Key results

The paper reports no data of its own. What it argues from:

- **Patient B (Fig. 2; ref 114, the authors' own Cerebral Cortex case
  report).** Complete bilateral destruction of the insula by herpes simplex
  encephalitis leaves pain, pleasure, itch, tickle, happiness, sadness,
  apprehension, irritation, caring, compassion, hunger, thirst and
  bladder and colon distension. Feelings "seem to dominate the mental
  landscape" of such patients.
- **Brainstem lesions.** Damage to the posterior half of the upper
  brainstem gives coma or a vegetative state, in which "feelings and
  sentience are abolished". Damage to the ventral half gives locked-in
  syndrome, with feeling and consciousness spared (refs 17, 115, 116).
- **Unilateral insula lesions** reduce thermal and nociceptive
  discrimination (refs 4, 119, 120). Bilateral parietal lesions covering
  much of SI and SII leave behaviour "clearly indicative of feeling
  states" (refs 124–125).
- **Stimulation.** Brainstem stimulation elicits emotional responses with
  valence, measured by their effect on learning or self-stimulation, in
  mammals (refs 42, 117), and emotions with feelings in humans (ref 118, a
  case of acute depression induced by deep-brain stimulation).
- **Anatomy of interoception (Fig. 1).** The lamina I
  spinothalamocortical pathway and the vagus converge on the NTS, PBN and
  PAG and reach the posterior and then the anterior insula, largely
  through the thalamus. About 80% of the fibres in the main vagal trunk
  are unmyelinated and most of the rest poorly myelinated (refs
  130–132). Interoceptive C and Aδ fibres conduct at 1–8 m/s, against
  14–60 m/s for exteroceptive Aα and Aβ fibres (ref 129).
- **Receptors along the axon.** Some unmyelinated vagal fibres respond to
  ATP, serotonin, acetylcholine and capsaicin along the axonal trunk, not
  only at terminals (refs 152–157; Fig. 3).

## Concepts

- **Action programme.** An innate set of actions, visceral, muscular and
  cognitive, triggered by an internal or external change and aimed at
  homeostasis. There are two kinds: **drives** (hunger, thirst, libido,
  play, care of progeny, attachment) and **emotions** (disgust, fear,
  anger, sadness, joy, shame, contempt, pride, compassion, admiration),
  the latter "mostly triggered" by exteroceptive stimuli.
- **Feeling.** The mental experience accompanying a body state. The same
  word ("fear") names both the action programme and the feeling, and the
  paper insists they are distinct.
- **Interoceptive system.** The vagus and the lamina I pathway, the
  circumventricular organs (the area postrema), and their brainstem and
  cortical targets.
- **Sentience.** Used here for the "cellular proto-phenomenon" that, once
  synchronized across many neurons, becomes a systems-level feeling.
- **Ephaptic transmission.** Sideways coupling between neurons through
  extracellular current, which myelin blocks.

## Connections

- **Seth's biological naturalism ([LIT-135](../literature.d/LIT-135.md), [NOTE-169](NOTE-169.md)).** Both tie
  consciousness to the regulation of a living body through interoception.
  Seth gets there through predictive processing and the free energy
  principle. This paper gets there through body maps, lesions and
  evolution, and makes no use of inference or prediction beyond saying that
  feelings help anticipation. The paper neither cites nor relies on
  predictive processing.
- **Plant sentience ([LIT-193](../literature.d/LIT-193.md), [NOTE-120](NOTE-120.md)).** Terrill and colleagues argue
  from a graded view of sentience. This paper's parsimony argument also
  grades feeling across species, but it anchors feeling in brainstem
  interoceptive maps, which plants lack. So it is no help to the plant
  case.
- **Roberts on animate bodies ([LIT-207](../literature.d/LIT-207.md)).** Its claim that affective states
  need an animate body has here a neural account of why.
- **Damasio's other work filed the same day.** Ref 38 is Bechara,
  Damasio, Tranel and Damasio (1997), cited as decision-making that goes
  on without a felt experience. The paper's claim that cognition "borrows"
  valence from body states restates the somatic marker idea without
  naming it. The comparison of body maps with visual maps of objects is in
  the spirit of the retroactivation framework, which is not cited.
- **Not held in the record.** Craig's insula papers (refs 4, 60, 111), the
  authors' patient-B report (ref 114), Merker on consciousness without a
  cortex (ref 112), Panksepp (refs 42, 117), and Damasio's books (refs 5,
  17, 41). Searched for in literature.d by name.

## Limitations

- **Opinion, not review.** The evidence is chosen to support the authors'
  view. Counter-evidence for an insular or cortical seat of feeling is
  summarized only as the view being rejected.
- **The case against the insula rests on one patient** (and an unspecified
  "some patients" with SI and SII spared) reported by the same authors.
- **Behavioural criteria for feeling** carry the decorticate and animal
  arguments. The paper admits that feeling cannot be demonstrated in
  non-human species.
- **The cellular section is untested.** No observation links myelination,
  or ephaptic coupling, to whether something is felt.
- **The evolutionary claim is an adaptive story.** No alternative, for
  example that feeling is a by-product of mapping, is considered.

## Open questions

- Does the brainstem suffice, or does it only feed a cortex that is needed
  for some feeling? The double dissociation of brainstem lesions is
  consistent with both.
- What are the "physiological conditions" beyond mapping that a map must
  meet to be felt? The paper names intensity and wakefulness and leaves
  the rest open.
- Is there a test that tells the pleiotropy account of unmyelinated
  interoception from the metabolic-threshold account?
