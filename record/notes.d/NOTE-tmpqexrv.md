# NOTE-tmpqexrv: Millikan — Biosemantics (2009)

## Contribution

A compact statement, by its author, of the selection-based theory of
content that the record had cited only as owed. Its distinctive move is
to divide the work. Teleology explains how falsity is possible. A
separate theory of representing, here a mapping theory with producer and
consumer, explains what is represented.

## Key insight

Teleological theories "have in common ... only a view about how
falseness in representations is possible" (p. 2). A false
representation is a product of a system whose biological function was
to make representations, made wrongly: "falsehood is explained by the
simple fact that biological purposes often go unfulfilled" (p. 3). So
"[t]eleological theories, just as such, are not theories of mental
content" (p. 3); "[t]o the shell that is 'teleosemantics' ... it is
necessary to add a description of what successful representing, actual
representing, is like" (pp. 3-4).

## Assumptions

- Functions rest "on a variety of different kinds of selection, the
  most fundamental being natural selection" (p. 3); learning is one of
  them. The account of functions is given elsewhere (Millikan 1984,
  1993, 2002) and only cited here.
- If representations are selected, producing them must benefit the
  organism, so they must be used: the theory of representing "must allow
  us to explain how producing inner representations might sometimes
  benefit an organism" (p. 4). This is what brings in the consumer.
- "Functions are effects, not causes" (p. 7): an icon's function cannot
  be to have been produced by something.

## Key results

- **Producer, consumer, sign** (p. 5). Producer and consumer "have been
  designed by natural selection or by learning to cooperate with one
  another". What the producer does helps the consumer perform functions
  "that loop back to make both the producer and the consumer more likely
  to survive or to maintain their current settings (selection through
  learning) or to proliferate".
- **Content as a mapping rule** (pp. 5-6). The sign "will be true or
  satisfied only if it maps onto some affair or affairs ... in
  accordance with certain 'semantic' rules", rules "instantiated in the
  past when the consumer and producer or their ancestors have succeeded
  in performing their cooperative function(s)". The descriptive content
  is "a condition or state of affairs that must obtain if the consumer is
  to perform its tasks ... in the normal way" (p. 6).
- **Content is not conceptual role** (p. 7): "its content is not
  determined by its conceptual role".
- **Truth does not depend on normal production** (p. 7). A magnetosome
  pointed right by a stray bar magnet "tells the truth by accident"; when a
  magnet points it towards oxygen, "what the magnetosome says is false
  despite its being produced through a normal mechanism". Against Dretske, the
  magnetosome "is not broken or malfunctioning", yet fails to perform all
  its functions: "Very often things fail to perform their functions, not
  because they are damaged, but because the conditions they are in are
  not their normal operating conditions" (pp. 7-8).
- **Three kinds of icon** (p. 8). Descriptive (the producer is
  responsible for correspondence), directive (the consumer is) and
  pushmi-pullyu (both, to different affairs).
- **Natural signs are not true or false** (p. 9). "In the fundamental
  sense, actually representing involves both picturing and carrying
  natural information. As such it is not a matter determined by a history
  of selection. Representing intentionally, however, is a matter of
  having a certain kind of history. Also, attributions of truth and
  falsity ... make sense only by reference to function, hence by
  reference to a history of selection. The terms 'true' versus 'false'
  ... do not apply to natural signs".
- **Distal and abstract content** (p. 9). "The information that a
  natural sign carries intentionally is only the information it is its
  function to carry, the information that its cooperative interpreters
  know how to use."
- **Frequent error is allowed** (p. 9). The rabbit's thump "may be
  elicited more commonly when rabbit danger is absent than present. (What
  matters is the converse --that when danger is present it should usually
  be elicited.)"
- **Novel beliefs** (p. 11). Like a camera, cognitive systems are
  selected not to produce particular products but to vary them with the
  environment.
- **Three replies** (pp. 12-14). To Neander's hoverfly (that content
  would include fertility and not being eaten): the producer must have
  the function of making the correspondence, which needs sensitivity to
  natural signs of those conditions, and there is none. To systems
  selected for false belief: distortion must ride on systems whose job is
  truth, or there are no semantic rules by which the beliefs are false.
  To Pietroski's kimus: a pushmi-pullyu tropism, not a belief, carrying
  local natural information about where snorfs are fewer. Such
  intentionality "is the limiting case of intentionality. It is
  intentionality in the way zero is a number" (p. 14).

## Concepts

- **Intentional icon.** A sign made by a producer for a consumer, both
  designed to cooperate, that maps by a rule onto the affair it
  represents.
- **Normal (in her sense).** The way a function was performed on the
  occasions that explain its selection; not the statistical average.
- **Local natural information.** Information carried by correlations
  that are "not perfect but that are not accidental either, for they must
  persist throughout a spatial-temporal region for a reason" (p. 13).
- **Pushmi-pullyu icon.** One that at once describes and directs (the
  bee dance, the hen's food call).

## Connections

- **The SEP survey** (LIT-tmpppa43, read in NOTE-tmp60j0q) places this
  chapter as one of Millikan's "compressed versions" of the theory
  ("2009" in its §3.2), reads her as consumer-based, and notes she rejects
  that reading and stresses the producer's side in later work. This
  chapter's hoverfly reply, through the producer's function, is the
  later emphasis the survey describes.
- **Pushmi-pullyu.** THEORY-1605 (Salice and Miyazono) uses the term for
  self-transformation in group identification. This chapter is the
  record's first reading of the term at its source.
- **Grice.** Millikan's "natural signs" that cannot be true or false
  parallel Grice's factive natural meaning (THEORY-444), arrived at
  independently; the chapter does not cite Grice.
- **Bickhard** (THEORY-220) argues against etiology from a lion's
  duplicate. This chapter does not discuss Swampman; the survey's §4.2
  reports Millikan's reply (the intuition is false of the real nature).

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-650.** The selection-history candidate for its question (b),
  which it named as "unread here". On question (a) the chapter agrees,
  independently of Grice, that natural signs are not true or false, so a
  false alarm needs a content that is not natural information. It also
  qualifies the split: for Millikan a sign has a content that can be
  false only if some function fixes it, so (a) is not settled apart from
  (b) except where, as in CASE-243, the source is stipulated. The
  support judgement is ARG-tmps4eez.
- **CLAIM-648.** An objection. On this account a norm by which a sign or
  anticipation is false needs a history of selection or learning;
  present upkeep alone fixes none. That is filed as CLAIM-tmp974nn.
  Millikan's "selection through learning", in which producer and
  consumer "maintain their current settings" (p. 5), is the place where
  her account comes closest to an organizational one.
- **QUESTION-128.** Evidence on its third rung (representational norms),
  from the etiological side: representational norms from the
  dynamics of selection, not from present organization. It does not
  touch practical or moral authority.
- **The steam alarm and the magnetosome.** The bar-magnet case is the
  same structure as CASE-243's steam: a mechanism working "perfectly
  properly" that fails to perform its function because its conditions
  are not Normal. A parallel, noted here, with no edge.

## Limitations

- The account of functions on which everything rests is cited, not
  given (pp. 3, 10).
- The section on local natural information says there "is not room to
  unpack" it (p. 8); the replies to the hoverfly and kimu cases depend on
  it.
- Artefacts are not discussed. Whether a designed alarm's content comes
  from its designer (derived proper functions, Millikan 1984) is not in
  this chapter; the SEP survey's §4.4 states the derived-function idea
  for octopus camouflage and learning, not for artefacts.
- The claim that the theory alone explains how representations of
  individuals are possible, "something for which, to my knowledge, no
  other naturalized theory of intentionality accounts" (p. 11), is
  asserted, not argued here.

## Open questions

- Whether "selection through learning" that maintains a system's current
  settings differs, in any case that matters, from organizational
  self-maintenance. CLAIM-tmp974nn states where they would part.
