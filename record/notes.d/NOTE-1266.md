# NOTE-1266: Being one of us. Group identification, joint actions, and collective intentionality

## Contribution

It splits the notion of group identification that the shared-agency
literature borrowed from social psychology into two processes:

- **adopting the we-perspective**: reasoning about what is best for the
  group, as in team reasoning;
- **self-transformation**: coming to understand oneself as a group member.

The two dissociate, and self-transformation is developmentally prior. It
then gives self-transformation a cognitive kind. It is a Pushmi-Pullyu
representation (Millikan): a state that represents a fact and directs
action at once, so identifying with a group is already being moved to act
as a member, with no further desire needed.

## Key insight

Thinking about what is good for one's group is not the same as being one
of the group. A committee member caught in a discursive dilemma can work
out what "we" should conclude while personally disagreeing (§3.2). A
two-year-old can act as one of "us" without being able to take the
group's perspective at all (§3.1). Only the second is identification in
the self-involving sense. It is motivating by its nature: it tells you
both what you are and what to do.

## Assumptions

- **Joint action is explained by shared intention** (Bratman, Gilbert) for
  full-fledged agents. The paper's quarrel is with "lite" versions meant
  to cover infants (Pacherie 2013).
- **Developmental evidence** that adopting a group perspective, shown by
  the black sheep effect, appears around age three and matures by eight,
  later than joint action at 18–24 months (§3.1).
- **Millikan's category of PPRs** is real. The paper does not commit to her
  theory of content (§4).
- **Internalism** about group identification (note 6). Externalists are
  not addressed.

## Key results

- **The two-condition view** (§2). Pacherie's theory of shared intention
  requires (i) a self-conception as a team member, (i′) the belief that
  others have it, (ii) team reasoning from the group viewpoint, and (iii)
  the intention to do one's part. Petersson extends group identification
  to all joint action in the "we-mode", via Bacharach's "agency
  transformation".
- **Team reasoner versus team benefactor** (§2, after Tuomela; Hakli et
  al.). The team reasoner holds "We intend to J", with the "we" in subject
  position. The team benefactor reasons in the first person singular about
  the group's well-being and holds "I intend that we J". Only the first
  is a group member's attitude.
- **Self-transformation without the we-perspective** (§3.1). Precursors of
  the black sheep effect appear by three, mature forms by eight, while
  joint action appears at 18–24 months. So infants' joint action cannot
  rest on adopting a group perspective. The paper calls this
  "conditional and defeasible".
- **The we-perspective without self-transformation** (§3.2). In the
  discursive dilemma (List & Pettit), a member can form "We intend to J"
  while holding "I do not intend to J". This is Goldie's
  "in-his-shoes" perspective-taking applied to a group. What closes the
  gap, turning it into "empathetic perspective-shifting", is
  self-transformation, not more perspective-taking.
- **The social self as a PPR** (§4). A belief "I am a member of us" does
  not motivate, and people can hold it without identifying (Ruble et al.;
  Bennett & Sani). The social self is a PPR: "you are a member of us; act
  as such". Like the bee's waggle dance, it represents and directs at
  once. It is triggered by group cues: Bacharach's list, plus joint
  attention, which the authors propose for children.
- **Three implications** (§5).
  - Minimal joint action needs no complex mentalizing.
  - PPRs spread, which may explain why cooperation is "contagious".
  - Self-transformation is not under direct rational control, though one
    can arrange the situations that elicit it; adopting a perspective is.

## Concepts

- **self-transformation**: transformation in self-understanding, coming to
  understand oneself as a group member (a "social self").
- **adoption of the we-perspective**: framing a situation from the group's
  point of view, as needed for team reasoning.
- **team reasoner / team benefactor**: one who intends as part of "we" ("We
  intend to J"), versus one who intends, as an individual, to promote the
  group's good ("I intend that we J").
- **Pushmi-Pullyu representation (PPR)**: a representation undifferentiated
  between describing a fact and directing action (Millikan).
- **in-his-shoes perspective-taking / empathetic perspective-shifting**:
  imagining oneself in the other's (here the group's) situation, versus
  imagining being the other (Goldie).

## Connections

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).** The paper bears on it at three
  points. These are inferences; the paper does not discuss selflessness.
  - The team benefactor reasons from the first person singular about the
    group's good, which is the thesis's "self-interest on a group" read as
    one would naturally read it. The team reasoner is something else:
    their "we" is in subject position, so the good pursued is the subject's
    own good only if the subject *is* the we.
  - The PPR account says identification motivates directly ("act as
    such"). That fits the thesis's picture that collectivist action flows
    from a self-understanding, not from a separate desire for others'
    welfare.
  - The paper supplies a criterion of self-extension independent of
    caring: the activated social self, triggered by cues. That is what
    [CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md) says the thesis needs.
- **Self-categorization theory ([THEORY-1114](../theory.d/THEORY-1114.md)).** The working definition
  is Turner's (1982): group identification internalizes a categorization
  into the self-concept. The PPR account is a cognitive gloss on
  depersonalization, and Bacharach's group cues (common fate, competing
  group, we-language) are close to fit.
- **Skorich ([THEORY-1112](../theory.d/THEORY-1112.md)).** Both make joint attention and shared
  mental states central to group self-perception, by different routes.
- **Sugden ([LIT-1782](../literature.d/LIT-1782.md))** and List & Pettit ([LIT-391](../literature.d/LIT-391.md)) supply the team
  reasoning and the discursive dilemma.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis.** The team-reasoner/team-benefactor distinction
  gives [CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md) a sharper form. The thesis is strongest for team
  reasoners whose social self is activated, and weakest for team
  benefactors, who care about a group they do not identify with as
  self-transformed members. A dated note is added to the claim.
- **No THEORY filed.** The PPR account is a conjecture argued by
  elimination; it is recorded here.
- **No ML instruction.**

## Limitations

- **Conceptual.** The developmental timeline is borrowed and the authors
  flag its use as defeasible.
- **The doxastic view is dismissed quickly.** That one can believe one is a
  member without identifying shows only that belief is not sufficient,
  not that identification is not a belief plus something else.
- **Externalist accounts are set aside** (note 6).

## Open questions

- Can self-transformation be measured apart from perspective-taking in
  adults? A task that separates "we-reasoning while disagreeing" from
  identification would test C1 directly.
