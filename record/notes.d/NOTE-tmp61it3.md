# NOTE-tmp61it3: Citron (2008) — Technological Due Process

## Contribution

A US administrative-law argument that automation defeats both procedural regimes of the administrative
state at once. Due process protects individuals in adjudication; notice-and-comment rulemaking stands in for
it when policy is general. Automated systems "combine individual adjudications with rulemaking while
adhering to the procedural" safeguards of neither (Abstract, p. 1249). The article then proposes substitute
procedures, "technological due process".

## Key results

1. Abstract, p. 1249: "Code, not rules, determines the outcomes of adjudications."
2. Introduction, p. 1254: "Programmers routinely change" rules when translating them into code; Part I.B,
   p. 1268: CBMS embedded "over nine hundred incorrect rules into Colorado’s public benefits" system, and
   p. 1269 "computer programmers delayed fixing incorrect rules for" extended periods.
3. Part I.A.2, p. 1264: Florida's mandatory online application lacked a category for relative caregivers;
   "Eligibility workers were unable to overrule this decision".
4. Part I.B, p. 1272: in mixed systems, "Automation bias effectively turns a computer" program's
   suggestion into a final decision, and "The distinction between mixed and fully automated systems has
   the" potential to disappear.
5. Part I.B, pp. 1273, 1275: "It took the accused man and his attorney over two months" to clear a false
   child-support match; "At present, there is no assured way to get removed from the “No" Fly” list.
6. Part II.B.1.b, pp. 1283–85: hearing officers' trust in computers does not disqualify them, so "Under
   present conditions, the guarantee of an impartial reviewer" may be illusory; and a Mathews analysis of
   one case "will capture all of the costs of improved process but only a tiny fraction of its benefits",
   since a fix to code serves all later cases. p. 1287: "Correcting only one type of" error systematizes bias.
7. Part II.B.2.c, p. 1297: "rulemaking by code writers is ultra vires even as it is inevitable".
8. Part III.B, p. 1304: "Decisions best addressed with standards should not be automated."
9. Part III.C, pp. 1305, 1310: "automated systems should generate audit trails that record" the facts and
   rules of each decision; "Agencies should maintain testing suites" run before launch and on each change.

## Assumptions

US doctrine (Londoner/Bi-Metallic, Mathews, Goldberg, Zinermon, APA, FOIA, nondelegation, Chenery, Mead).
Evidence is audits, litigation and press reports from 2004–2007. Automation is granted real benefits:
consistency and cost.

## Concepts

Fully automated and three kinds of mixed system; rulemaking by code; automation bias; audit trail; fixed
against variable costs of process; delegation to code writers as a third limb of nondelegation; rules
against standards as the test of what to automate; testing suites as a surrogate for interested parties.
