# NOTE-2029: Cuéllar (2026) — The New Presidential Adjudication

## Contribution

A legal category that gathers scattered presidential actions into one,
presidential adjudication, defined by "distinctions in the agent involved,
the object of the action, and its form." (p. 610). It names a risk,
executive coercion, and proposes a modest doctrinal response, a "harder
look", assembled from existing doctrine rather than a new test.

## Key results

1. Abstract, p. 597: presidents increasingly "adjudicate", that is, act "to
   make fact-specific, high-stakes decisions about" sanctions,
   investigations, contracts and enforcement, with limited judicial
   oversight.
2. Introduction, p. 599: review is limited "even as the importance and scope
   of this practice grows"; p. 604: the new strain reaches "more people and
   organizations, in more specific ways, and in more situations."
3. Part II, p. 617: the norms that constrained it "are quickly eroding."
   Unitary-executive removal doctrine, immunity, partisan oversight, party
   nomination reform, agency capacity loss and polarization all push
   decisions toward the Oval Office (pp. 617–624).
4. Part III, p. 626: the "heightened risk of what could be called
   “executive coercion”: the intentional use" of power against a target to
   extract concessions unrelated to the power's purpose. A Sebelius-style
   test could let presidents impose costs through "mounting yet individual
   discretionary actions." (p. 629).
5. Part III.A, pp. 636–639: the presumption of regularity is judge-made and
   defeasible. Courts have set it aside on pretext (Department of Commerce,
   IRAP, Talbott), and one judge said "In just six months," the President may
   have forfeited it (p. 637).
6. Part III.B, pp. 642–646: a "harder look" for presidential adjudication
   when statutory frameworks appear ignored and statements suggest
   arbitrariness. Its limit, p. 645: it would follow only "the sort of final
   agency action that tends to be necessary for judicial review," which
   "casts aside plenty of words or deeds of a more preliminary nature."
7. Part III.C, p. 647: the White House can "mount seriatim at[tacks]" that a
   court may thwart eventually but that cost "those targeted in the
   meantime."; note 225 (universities): some funding streams were restored,
   yet disruption imposed costs. And "courts lack direct authority to
   enforce" their decisions.
8. Part III.C, p. 648: governors and civil society "can also create friction
   and slow down White House edicts"; "a court decision buys time for broader
   civic deliberations".
9. Conclusion, pp. 651–652: the danger of a "virtually unreviewable, one-man
   judge."; yet "Broad discretion and meaningful accountability can coexist".

## Assumptions

A Weberian frame, in which rational-legal administration and charismatic,
responsibility-driven politics must each be kept from swallowing the
other. Some presidential adjudication is legitimate and needed, above all
in foreign affairs. Courts of mixed appointment will calibrate with care,
and clear opinions shape the elite and public understanding that
constrains presidents.

## Concepts

Presidential adjudication; executive coercion; "harder look"; presumption
of regularity; the foreign/domestic distinction; norm erosion; seriatim
attacks; civic friction; Weber's iron cage and ethic of responsibility.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-462](../claims.d/CLAIM-462.md)**: corroborates it in one polity, independently of the
  exchange. The White House's capacity for effective action grows (tools,
  reach, information, control of personnel) while the relations that
  corrected it (oversight, norms, agency independence, the premise of the
  presumption of regularity) weaken. He also holds that courts depend on
  others to enforce. He does not argue that the two capacities are
  maintained by different relations; that step is the claim's.
- **[CLAIM-464](../claims.d/CLAIM-464.md)**: does not treat the mechanism. Result 7 shows the claim's
  timing condition, harm before an effective remedy, not a rise in the cost
  of the corrective outcome.
- **[CASE-278](../cases.d/CASE-278.md)**: not discussed (it postdates the essay), but it fits his
  account: a presidential announcement on a specific site, a stated
  rationale found "pretextual", and a dispute over whether a final plan
  existed, which is the threshold his own remedy needs (result 6).
- **[CASE-276](../cases.d/CASE-276.md)**: not discussed. Immigration appears only as a field
  where foreign and domestic affairs blur.
- **[TERM-125](../terms.d/TERM-125.md)**: his "friction" (result 8) is spoken of as delay, but his
  instances are dependence (states declining cooperation, litigation that
  can enjoin), which is friction in the term's sense.
- **The exchange** (A25) summarizes him accurately and marks its temporal
  argument as its own ("your observation adds a temporal dimension").

## Limitations

A law-review essay: doctrinal and interpretive, with no systematic data on
how often presidential adjudication occurs or how it fares in court.
Written before the 2026 events in the record's cases. Its remedy is
judicial and civic; it does not address timing, irreversibility or interim
relief.

## From the reading of 2026-11-17

*Quotations here are carried from the reading of 2026-11-17, whose filer rechecked them against its copy on 2026-11-18; they were not rechecked at the reconciliation.*

The second reading was a skim of Parts II and III.A and a close reading of
the rest; it adds these.

- **The risk** (abstract, p. 597): "This development heightens the risk of
  the presidency devolving into a roving tribunal with vast discretionary
  power to make deals and impose arbitrary costs on individuals and
  institutions."
- **Executive coercion in full** (p. 626): "the intentional use of
  presidential power against a specific target with the goal of extracting
  concessions valuable to the President or the administration but
  unrelated to either public policy or the purpose behind the grant of
  power itself".
- **Agency adjudication drawn in** (p. 617): "presidential adjudication now
  threatens to extend to the fact-specific applications of statutory
  authority that were historically meant to be taken up by impartial
  adjudicators."
- **Speed** (p. 627): "Presidential adjudication can lower the cost of
  monitoring and reviewing agency decisions and increase the President's
  capacity to act swiftly in the face of sudden crises and opportunities."
- **The harder look's measure** (p. 643): it "would still call for a lighter
  touch than in the review of intricate, agency-level rulemaking
  determinations."
- **Concepts it adds**: government by dealmaking; roving tribunal; Kadi
  justice; DOGE.
- **[CLAIM-748](../claims.d/CLAIM-748.md)** (litigation as siege): corroborates its mechanism,
  attacks "that might be thwarted by a court eventually but levy a high
  cost on those targeted in the meantime", renewed when one line is
  blocked (p. 647). Cuéllar says the remedy becomes costly, not
  unreachable.
- **[CLAIM-730](../claims.d/CLAIM-730.md)** (a slow rule over a fast process): qualifies it. The
  slow level is the courts, whose harder look comes only after final
  agency action, and civic friction is relied on to "slow down" edicts;
  his subject is the depth of review, not tempo.
- **[CLAIM-727](../claims.d/CLAIM-727.md)**: his case for a defeasible presumption of regularity,
  a court-made condition of challenge, is close to the claim's second
  clause.
- **Neighbours in the record**: [LIT-092](../literature.d/LIT-092.md) and [NOTE-063](NOTE-063.md) (the law-firm
  executive orders) concern his law-firm example (p. 627); [CASE-172](../cases.d/CASE-172.md)
  (grants cancelled and partly reinstated) and [CASE-176](../cases.d/CASE-176.md) (USIP) are near
  his grant and DOGE examples; [CASE-170](../cases.d/CASE-170.md) is not mentioned.
- **The exchange**: placing the essay beside the owner's U13 on the speed of
  executive action is the record's; speed is incidental to Cuéllar, whose
  subject is arbitrariness and the scarcity of review.
