# THEORY-1462: Haushofer and Fehr hold that poverty causes stress and negative affect, and that these in turn make people more present-biased and risk-averse independent of credit constraints and background risk, so that behaviour taken as the poor's own preference may partly be an effect of poverty and may help perpetuate it; they grant that the affect-to-choice link rests only on laboratory inductions, and that no nonlinearity strong enough to make a poverty trap has been shown

## Source

Haushofer & Fehr (2014), [LIT-2152](../literature.d/LIT-2152.md); read in [NOTE-1681](../notes.d/NOTE-1681.md).

## What was actually shown

- **Poverty to affect** (p. 864): correlations across countries, and of
  25 causal studies, "18 studies show a positive effect of poverty
  alleviation on psychological well-being or stress". In the Kenya
  unconditional cash transfer ($0, $400 or $1500, randomised), well-being
  improved with positive transfers, and cortisol "was only reduced in
  those who received large transfers". These could have come out null:
  two of the 25 did.
- **Affect to choice** (pp. 864–865): threat of shock (N = 41) lowered
  risky investment; chronic, not acute, hydrocortisone (N = 36) raised
  risk aversion; a sad prime (N = 189) and hydrocortisone (N = 53) raised
  impatience. Random assignment in the lab rules out credit constraints
  and background risk as the cause here.
- **Economic routes conceded** (p. 863): "the intrinsic time and risk
  preferences of the poor may be identical to those of wealthier people",
  with present bias before payday confined to money.
- **The authors' framing** (p. 862): "we are not suggesting that the poor
  bear blame for their poverty."

## What this does not say

- That stress in poverty changes real choices in the field: "the
  evidence on this link" "is currently restricted to laboratory studies,
  and the literature does little to distinguish between the effects of
  acute and chronic stress" (p. 866). The authors call it "the weakest
  link".
- That poverty is a trap: that needs "a strong nonlinearity", and studies
  of income and psychological outcomes "show no strong signs of being
  nonlinear" (p. 866).
- That relief lasts: "Little is known about whether poverty alleviation
  leads to a permanent or only a temporary increase in psychological
  well-being." (p. 866)
- Anything about effort, hours, labour supply or spending in response to
  transfers, which the Review does not report; nor anything about states
  or projects.
