summary: >-
  The authors use the Upworthy Research Archive, randomized headline tests
  Upworthy ran on its own site (24 January 2013 to 14 April 2015), to
  estimate the causal effect of positive and negative words on click-through
  rate (CTR). Within each test, different headlines for the same story were
  randomly shown, so story content is held fixed. Words were scored with
  LIWC dictionaries (with negation handling); CTR was modelled with a
  multilevel binomial regression with test-level random intercepts (and a
  varying-slopes variant), controlling for length, complexity (Gunning Fog)
  and platform age. In a pre-registered confirmatory sample, negative words
  increased and positive words decreased CTR; robust to alternative
  dictionaries, negation scope, complexity measures, quadratic terms,
  exclusion of image tests and mixed-valence headlines. Secondary: sadness
  increased CTR; joy and fear decreased it; anger null/inconclusive. Effects
  of negative words were strongest for "Government & Economy" stories.

# NOTE-1794: Robertson and others (2023) — Negativity drives online news consumption

## Contribution

A large-scale, field-randomized, pre-registered estimate that negative language in headlines causally raises clicking on news, separating consumption (private clicking) from sharing (public engagement) on which most earlier negativity work relied.

## Key results

1. Results, RCTs: of N = 22,743 tests, "After applying the pre-registered filtering procedure (see Methods), we obtained 12,448 RCTs." (53,699 headlines; 2,778,124 clicks.)
2. Results, main effect: "For a headline of average length (14.965 words), this implies that for each negative word, the CTR increases by 2.3%." Per SD: "increases the odds of a user clicking the headline by 1.5%."
3. Results, positive words: "for each positive word in a headline of average length, the CTR decreases by 1.0%."
4. Results, base rates: "The average CTR across all experiments is 1.39%"; positive words were more prevalent (2.83% vs 2.62% of words).
5. Results, platform age: "Upworthy headlines were most successful when its editorial practices were novel to online users."
6. Discussion: "the positive effect of negative words on consumption rates was strongest for news stories pertaining to ‘Government & Economy’."
7. Discussion, emotions: anger "not statistically significant"; fear effect "in the opposite direction of our pre-registered hypotheses"; they attribute differences from sharing studies to consumption being "a private behaviour".
8. Discussion: "we uncovered a negativity bias in the data even though Upworthy branded itself as a ‘positive news outlet’".
9. Discussion, limits: "we can only draw conclusions at the level of news stories but not at the level of individuals."

## Assumptions

- Headline variants for one story differ only in wording (image tests excluded or checked); randomization within test identifies the effect of wording.
- Dictionary counts (LIWC; NRC for emotions) measure valence of headline language; validated only moderately against human judges (rs = 0.303).
- Upworthy (a click-bait pioneer branded as positive news, 2013–15) is informative for online news generally; the authors argue this, it is not tested.
- CTR measures consumption; no individual-level data.

## Concepts

Negativity bias; click-through rate; randomized headline testing (A/B tests) as field experiments; consumption vs sharing; Registered Report; multilevel binomial regression; LIWC and NRC lexicons; Plutchik emotions and dyads (exploratory: outrage, disapproval associated with higher CTR).

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** A's account ("22,743 randomized Upworthy headline tests: each negative word raised click-through by about 2.3%") is **accurate with two qualifications**: (a) 22,743 is the archive's test count; the analysis used 12,448 tests after pre-registered filtering; (b) the 2.3% is relative (a 2.3% increase of CTR, whose mean is 1.39%), for a headline of average length (~15 words). [CLAIM-656](../claims.d/CLAIM-656.md)'s own gloss, "a causal result about clicking only", is correct and matches the authors' limitation statements.

- **[CLAIM-656](../claims.d/CLAIM-656.md)** (attention-optimization in news favours clickbait and works against investigative journalism): **supports step 1 narrowly (selection at the level of packaging), leaves the rest open.** The paper shows a reliable, causal incentive for negative wording at the headline level, and that a firm optimizing CTR by A/B testing would select for it: the mechanism the claim's first step names. It says nothing about assignments, beats, retained capabilities or investigative journalism, externality or missing demand (steps 2–3), nor about the claim's defeater (attention-revenue dependence predicting loss of reporting capacity). Two details bear on the claim's edge: the decline of CTR with platform age (optimized packaging loses force as readers habituate) and the null for anger (the outrage-clickbait picture is not what drives private clicking here). Use: grounds for "optimization on clicks favours negative framing", not for "works against investigative journalism".
- **[CASE-068](../cases.d/CASE-068.md)** (found on recheck: risk messages in diffusion chains keep and amplify the evaluative signal): a neighbouring negativity-bias result in a different behaviour (recall/transmission vs consumption). Compatible; the authors' consumption-vs-sharing distinction is a reason not to merge the two.
- **[QUESTION-441](../questions.d/QUESTION-441.md)** (named by [CLAIM-656](../claims.d/CLAIM-656.md)'s bears_on): only via the claim; the paper is about reader behaviour, not organizational counter-normativity.

## Limitations

One outlet, 2013–15, click-bait style; story-level not individual-level; dictionary measures of valence with modest validation; effect sizes small in absolute terms (relative change of a ~1.4% CTR); consumption only, no measures of reading, belief or downstream editorial decisions. Supplementary tables not read.
