# NOTE-tmps5rdy: Haspelmath 2011, the indeterminacy of word segmentation

## Contribution

The morphology–syntax distinction is usually taken for granted, but it depends on a cross-linguistically valid notion of the morphosyntactic word. Haspelmath argues no good criteria for such a notion exist. He examines ten criteria and shows none is necessary and sufficient alone, and no combination yields a definition matching linguists' spelling conventions. "Word" can be defined per language or as a fuzzy concept, but the fuzzy version only helps if the affix–word–phrase continuum shows clustering, and there is no systematic evidence of that. So there is currently no good basis for splitting morphosyntax into morphology and syntax.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1–2: the word and the morphology–syntax division. §2 sets aside speaker intuitions, semantic non-compositionality, orthography and phonological words as bases for a morphosyntactic word.
- §3 (§§3.1–3.10): the ten criteria in detail, each shown to fail in either direction. §3.10 covers zero marking, multiple and cumulative exponence, morphomic patterns, and realizational morphology versus constructional syntax.
- §4 "persuasion by test batteries": in practice linguists pick criteria that converge. Table 1 compares nine authors' batteries. The author says this is not rigorous because criteria "can be selected opportunistically".
- §5–6: "word" as a language-specific concept (fine for description, irrelevant to the general question) and as a fuzzy concept (would need evidence of clustering).
- §7–8: the conclusion "Linguists have no good basis for identifying words across languages, and hence no good basis for a general distinction between syntax and morphology". A history of lexicalism and lexical integrity, which re-emerged in generative grammar in the 1970s without any progress on defining the word.
- §9–10: in practice one can keep saying "word" for spaced orthographic units as long as no theoretical difference is implied. Comparative concepts are needed to compare languages, for example on analyticity or syntheticity.

## Open questions

- It bears directly on tokenization. If "word" has no cross-linguistic definition, whitespace or word-level segmentation is an orthographic convention, not a linguistic unit, which supports subword and byte-level approaches (H-Net and similar). The triage asks for the "when in doubt, anthology" test here, since the anthology's `representation-and-encoding` topic may be a better home than a new `linguistics` tag.
- It is the typological foundation that c62 (Krauska & Lau, who cite the 2017 reprint) and c63 (Bruening) build on.
- A deeper read should check §6's clustering argument, since that is the empirical claim a corpus or statistical study could actually test.
