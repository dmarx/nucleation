# THEORY-101: Consciousness began as hedonic valence, which evolved as a common currency for trading off competing options in action selection among animals with many degrees of freedom

## Source

- Veit (2025), [LIT-105](../literature.d/LIT-105.md), read in [NOTE-187](../notes.d/NOTE-187.md): "The Metaphysics of Hedonic
  Valence" (pp. 9–10), "Evaluation and Its Relation to Perception and
  Prediction" (pp. 10–12), "Is Consciousness an Adaptation?" (pp. 12–13)
  and "Disunified Consciousness and Its Neural Basis" (pp. 13–15).

## The claim, as the source states it

**The origin.** In [LIT-105](../literature.d/LIT-105.md) Veit defends the pathological complexity
thesis of his 2023 book. Consciousness evolved, around the Cambrian, to
handle "pathological complexity": the combinatorial trade-off problems of
action selection in animals with many degrees of freedom. Its first form
was hedonic valence, and its other dimensions (sensory, self, temporal,
unity) came later ([NOTE-187](../notes.d/NOTE-187.md), Assumptions and Concepts). He calls this
evaluation-first.

**What valence is.** The reply adds the metaphysics the book left to one
paragraph. Hedonic "feel" is identical with a valence system, "a common
currency of sharing evaluative information across multiple control
systems", and the currency is "not Cartesian but cybernetic" (p. 10). "To
'feel bad' is not to host an entirely private sensation of badness but to
occupy a coordinated regulatory state that ties a multicellular organism
with high degrees of freedom together for goal-directed behaviour" (p. 10).
Phenomenality does not exert causal power "in addition to" this function:
"They are identical".

**The criterion.** Consciousness is to be attributed on high pathological
complexity together with motivational trade-offs that indicate a common
currency (p. 11). It is not attributed on evaluation alone: Veit concedes
that gastropods such as Aplysia, with rich evaluative capacities, may not
be conscious, and he does not count Paramecium or Chlamydomonas.

**Against the alternatives.** Prediction-first accounts are "too narrow"
(warmth, tiredness and aches are not exhausted by forecasting) and too
broad (immune systems, bacterial chemotaxis and plants predict) (pp.
11–12). Ginsburg and Jablonka's unlimited associative learning explains
later features of consciousness, not its origin. Consciousness is an
adaptation (p. 12), and its independent evolution in vertebrates,
cephalopods and arthropods counts for that. Bilaterally integrated
subcortical evaluative structures, the SN–SC–PAG "selection triangle", are
read as "strong evidence here that the evaluative side of experience came
first" (p. 15).

## How this bears on the record's accounts

**It rivals [THEORY-090](THEORY-090.md).** Newen and Montemayor's ALARM theory, in
[LIT-626](../literature.d/LIT-626.md), holds that the first consciousness, basic arousal, alarms
the body by giving an extreme signal "100% weight", through "rather rigid
behavioural programmes without choices", and that choice emerges only
with cortical general alertness. Veit's first consciousness exists to
weigh options against each other; ALARM's exists to remove the weighing.
The two agree on much: both are affect-first, both are adaptationist, and
both place the oldest consciousness in subcortical evaluative structures.
They cannot both be right about what that first consciousness did. On
Veit's criterion, an animal whose only felt states are ALARM's choiceless
alarms shows no motivational trade-off and so gives no evidence of
consciousness at all. On ALARM's, the trade-offs Veit takes as the mark
are a later, cortical achievement. They also disagree on learning, which
ALARM ties to basic arousal and Veit's critique of unlimited associative
learning places after the origin.

Could the two be compatible, with alarm as the limiting case of a
currency, a value so large it swamps every other? That reading is
available for Veit, whose identity thesis is too sketchy to exclude it,
but not for ALARM, whose authors say in so many words that the cost
avoidance of basic arousal "does not involve a choice". The rivalry is
declared on that ground. It would dissolve if ALARM's "100% weight" were
recast as a weight in a common currency rather than an override of it.

**[THEORY-045](THEORY-045.md), compared.** Veit's "coordinated regulatory state" is close
to [THEORY-045](THEORY-045.md)'s claim that felt affect is the experience of the body's
regulatory state, and his attack on prediction-first accounts bears on
that theory's open question, prediction or map. But [LIT-105](../literature.d/LIT-105.md) is about the
origin and function of consciousness, not about what felt affect is of,
and Veit proposes no test that would separate evaluation-first from
prediction-first ([NOTE-187](../notes.d/NOTE-187.md), Corrections). No relation is declared.

## What this does not say

- **It does not rest on a reading of the thesis's main statement.** The
  record holds Veit's reply, which defends the book against five
  commentaries known only through his quotations of them. The book is
  unread.
- **The identity of felt valence with the currency is a sketch.** C2 in
  [NOTE-187](../notes.d/NOTE-187.md) rates it weak: an assertion and one paragraph, with no account
  of the signal's consumers, format or route to action, which Veit
  concedes Frankish ([LIT-103](../literature.d/LIT-103.md)) was right to demand. The origin claim does
  not need the identity claim, and Veit says the thesis is compatible with
  dualism and panpsychism, in tension with it.
- **Its evidence of priority is weak.** That subcortical evaluative
  structures are old is taken as evidence that evaluative experience came
  first (C7, weak); the age of a structure is a proxy for the age of an
  experience. The adaptation argument assumes that a non-adaptive trait
  "would have been selected against" (C6, weak).
- **It does not say any particular animal is conscious.** The criterion is
  a pair of conditions, high pathological complexity and trade-offs, and
  Veit applies it cautiously, withholding it from gastropods.
