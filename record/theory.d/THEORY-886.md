# THEORY-886: Event boundaries are perceived where perceptual prediction error rises transiently; at those points working-memory event models are reset and opened to sensory input, so that information from before a boundary becomes harder to retrieve after it

## Source

Zacks et al. (2007), [LIT-1382](../literature.d/LIT-1382.md), read in full in [NOTE-1084](../notes.d/NOTE-1084.md) from the
author manuscript in PubMed Central (PMC2852534). The theory is in "A
Theory of Event Segmentation"; the evidence in the two sections that
follow.

## What was actually shown

The paper states a mechanism and assembles evidence for its implications.

- **The mechanism.** "Perceptual systems continuously make predictions
  about what will happen next. When transient errors in predictions arise,
  an event boundary is perceived" (Abstract). Event models are "working
  memory representations"; updating "consists of (1) resetting the current
  representations ... and (2) transiently increasing the influence of the
  pathway from sensory inputs to the event models". It is claimed to run
  on several timescales at once.
- **What could have come out otherwise.** A neural network trained to
  predict an actor's body positions (Reynolds, Zacks & Braver, then in
  press) might not have shown larger prediction error at event boundaries,
  or gained from error-gated event models; it did. Readers who met a time
  shift ("an hour later" rather than "a moment later") might have
  recognized what came just before as well as controls; they did not
  (Speer & Zacks 2005). Boundaries marked later might have drawn no
  transient brain response during earlier passive viewing; they did, in
  extrastriate and right frontal cortex. And the anterior cingulate might
  have responded at boundaries during passive viewing, as the theory
  predicts; it did not, which the authors call the one prediction that
  "does not square well with the available data".
- **Supporting regularities.** Unitization is reliable, hierarchical
  across grains, and tied to movement change, goal completion and
  sequential statistics. Boundaries are remembered better, and segmenting
  well predicts later memory.

## What this does not say

- **That prediction error has been shown to cause boundaries in people.**
  It has not been measured. Change, goals and statistics each predict
  boundaries; error is the theory's proposed common cause.
- **Anything about one's own intentions.** "A second limitation is that
  the theory in its current form is a purely passive perceiver." Extending
  event models to represent "one’s own goals" is named as future work. An
  intention lost at a boundary ([CASE-136](../cases.d/CASE-136.md), [CLAIM-421](../claims.d/CLAIM-421.md)) is an extension of the
  theory, not something it states.
- **The doorway effect.** The paper does not mention it. Its memory
  prediction is that what preceded a boundary becomes harder to retrieve.
  The doorway studies find the cost on the object carried across the
  boundary, which they explain by interference between event models
  ([THEORY-888](THEORY-888.md)), a different mechanism.
- **Where it lives in the brain.** The mapping to cingulate, midbrain and
  prefrontal cortex is offered as tentative, and part of it failed.
- **Consciousness.** Event models "are not necessarily accessible to
  consciousness". It shares a prediction-error architecture with
  [THEORY-883](THEORY-883.md) but makes no claim about experience.

  account its authors give of the effect.

  between event models; compatible with segmentation, but not derived from

  conscious access rather than to segmentation.
