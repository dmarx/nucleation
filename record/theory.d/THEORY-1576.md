# THEORY-1576: Heard and Martienssen argue that an effect is transgenerational only in a generation never exposed to the trigger, and that two rounds of epigenetic reprogramming in mammals leave little chance for such inheritance, which is well documented in plants and nematodes and rare in mammals

## The account

Transgenerational effects are those "found in generations that were not
exposed to the initial signal". An exposed pregnant female exposes the
fetus and the fetus's germ line, so "Only F3 individuals can be
considered as true trans-generational inheritance"; an exposed male
exposes his germ line, so only F2 and later count. Everything before is
intergenerational.

Inheritance and reprogramming are "two sides of the same coin". Mammals
reprogram twice, in primordial germ cells and after fertilization, and
"these two rounds of epigenetic erasure leave little chance for
inheritance of epigenetic marks, whether programmed, accidental or
environmentally induced". In plants and C. elegans, epigenetic states pass
for many generations, mostly at transposons and transgenes. The mammalian
epialleles that are bona fide (Agouti viable yellow, Axin fused) sit at
retrotransposons; a diet effect on Agouti "is only transmitted over two
generations and is lost by the third". Environmentally induced changes are
rarely inherited, and more rarely adaptive. Human nutritional studies are
mostly "inter-generational (parental or grand-parental exposure) rather
than truly trans-generational", and bona fide inheritance over several
generations "seems unlikely given the robust reprogramming found in the
mammalian germ line".

## Evidence

The review ([NOTE-1254](../notes.d/NOTE-1254.md)): genome-wide mouse methylation profiling for the
reprogramming claim, which the authors grant is weaker for histones and
RNA; many independent plant and nematode systems; a handful of mammalian
loci, with rarity argued by absence elsewhere. The assessment of human
evidence covers the nutritional literature only.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-297](../questions.d/QUESTION-297.md), the germline route. It sets the standard of what counts:
Holocaust survivors' children are an intergenerational case, and a
germline claim needs a generation never exposed. On present evidence, what
reaches grandchildren travels by other routes. The authors add that human
culture, with Gould, is the inheritance that is Lamarckian.
