# ARG-089: The works read on 2026-11-12 support only the premise that platforms reading demand from activity shape what is produced; they do not show the heaviest users setting what everyone is offered, and they leave the escalation half unsupported

## The inference

From what the readings of 2026-11-12 found to [CLAIM-653](../claims.d/CLAIM-653.md)'s two halves:
that where demand is read from activity or revenue the heaviest users set
the distribution everyone is offered, and that their habituation drags the
mainstream toward the extreme. [ARG-095](ARG-095.md) weighed A's reports of these
works; this argument weighs the works as read.

## Critical questions

- **Do the readings show demand read from activity shaping production?**
  Yes, in one case. Adler ([THEORY-1712](../theory.d/THEORY-1712.md)) documents a platform that
  commissions content from viewing data, "exquisitely calibrated to conform
  to mainstream viewing habits". That is the claim's premise. It is not its
  mechanism: the data are read for the mainstream, not for the heaviest
  users, and Adler says the causal evidence on what viewing does to viewers
  is lacking.
- **Do they show the heaviest users setting the offer?** No, and the one
  direct test points the other way. Amano and Simonov ([THEORY-1681](../theory.d/THEORY-1681.md)),
  with a game's full transaction log, find 90% of revenue from 1.5% of
  players, and simulate that a harder game would multiply revenue many
  times over, driven by those players, while cutting everyone else's play.
  The firm does not do it: by their estimates the design "balances the
  firm's revenue and engagement objectives". Close and colleagues
  ([THEORY-1676](../theory.d/THEORY-1676.md)) confirm that such a minority exists in loot-box
  spending, and that it is disproportionately made up of problem gamblers;
  they do not show that it sets the design. Gan ([THEORY-1668](../theory.d/THEORY-1668.md)) derives
  loot boxes and pity guarantees as optimal against a single buyer, so
  those features need no minority to explain them.
- **Do they show habituation driving the mainstream toward the extreme?**
  No. Ince and colleagues ([THEORY-1690](../theory.d/THEORY-1690.md)) find self-reported escalation
  tied to problematic use, cross-sectionally, with genre escalation low on
  average (about 2.5 of 7). De Roos and Willkomm ([THEORY-1707](../theory.d/THEORY-1707.md)) find
  escalation low and branching into several clusters, not one drift, and
  their "escalation" is a cross-sectional index, not change observed over
  time. Neither touches what is produced. Shor and Liu 2025 ([LIT-2374](../literature.d/LIT-2374.md)),
  by its abstract only, reports rising mostly consensual aggression in
  popular videos over 2000 to 2024; it is unread and carries nothing here.
- **Is there a rival that explains the same observations?** Yes, two. Hu
  and colleagues ([THEORY-1705](../theory.d/THEORY-1705.md)) prove that a recommender can degrade
  what producers make with no change in anyone's taste; and Adler's own
  account, a platform optimizing for the mainstream, explains the spread of
  formerly niche material through catalogue and tagging rather than through
  heavy users.

## Verdict

Proposed. The premise that activity-read demand shapes production stands,
on one reading. The structural half, that the heaviest users set the offer,
is not shown, and the one study able to test it finds a firm deliberately
not following its heaviest spenders. The escalation half is unsupported:
what the readings measure is self-reported, cross-sectional and low. The
claim stays Proposed. [CASE-248](../cases.d/CASE-248.md) remains the test that would decide the
structural half, with Gan's single-buyer result as a control it needs.
