# LIT-tmpotzm0: Liking, wanting, and the incentive-sensitization theory of addiction

Kent C. Berridge & Terry E. Robinson (2016), *American Psychologist*
71(8):670–679 — DOI-10.1037/amp0000059

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, NOTE-tmprj162. Located by section; the manuscript
is unpaginated.*

- **The theory.** "The incentive-sensitization theory posits the essence
  of drug addiction to be excessive amplification specifically of
  psychological ‘wanting’, especially triggered by cues, without
  necessarily an amplification of ‘liking’" (Abstract).
- **'Wanting' is cue-bound, not goal-bound.** "incentive salience ‘wanting’
  is less connected to cognitive goals and more tightly linked to reward
  cues, making those cues attention-grabbing and attractive" (Psychological
  Features of Incentive Salience).
- **It can oppose what the person wants.** It "can occur either in
  opposition to a cognitive desire or even unconsciously in absence of any
  cognitive desire". The recovering addict "has a genuine cognitive desire
  to abstain from taking drugs, but still ‘wants’ drugs, so relapses
  anyway when exposed to drug cues" (after Figure 1).
- **Sensitization lasts, and is reactive rather than tonic.** "Once
  induced, sensitization is very long lasting, and possibly even
  permanent." "A sensitized dopamine system is not always hyper-active,
  but it is hyper-reactive to drug cues and contexts" (Addiction Distorts
  ‘Wanting’ Versus ‘Liking’). During abstinence, "if anything,
  sensitization grows for some time" (Disentangling Reports).
- **'Liking' does not follow.** "‘Liking’, by contrast, need not increase
  with sensitization, and may even decrease" (Addiction Distorts).
- **Beyond drugs, tentatively.** "neural sensitization of ‘wanting’
  mechanisms may in some cases occur without drugs", "through mechanisms
  not yet understood" (Other Behavioral Addictions?). Dopamine agonists
  in Parkinson's disease produce "intense and idiosyncratically diverse
  ‘wants’" without reported pleasure.
