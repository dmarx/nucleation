# LIT-tmpqepho: The Society of Mind

Marvin Minsky (1986), *The Society of Mind*, New York: Simon and Schuster
(hardback, ISBN 0-671-60740-5; Library of Congress 86020322, call number
BF431 .M553 1986; 339 pp., illustrated, bibliography pp. [326]–332, index).
A Touchstone paperback (ISBN 0-671-65713-5) followed. Google Books dates
it 15 March 1988, and the Harvard Book Store listing agrees.

## Key takeaways

*Registered from the publisher's description and the contents list, not a
reading. What follows is what the book says about itself.*

- The publisher's description, verbatim: "Marvin Minsky -- one of the
  fathers of computer science and cofounder of the Artificial Intelligence
  Laboratory at MIT -- gives a revolutionary answer to the age-old question:
  'How does the mind work?' Minsky brilliantly portrays the mind as a
  'society' of tiny components that are themselves mindless. Mirroring his
  theory, Minsky boldly casts The Society of Mind as an intellectual puzzle
  whose pieces are assembled along the way. Each chapter -- on a
  self-contained page -- corresponds to a piece in the puzzle. As the pages
  turn, a unified theory of the mind emerges, like a mosaic."

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-01 at the owner's request for Marvin Minsky's "Society of
Mind" programme. The theory holds that a mind is a society of many simple,
mindless agents. It was developed with Seymour Papert from the 1970s
through this book to *The Emotion Machine*. No anthology topic holds the
book. Its subject is the architecture of minds, human and artificial, and
it carries no instruction for machine-learning practice. It is the
programme's central statement, and it goes on the record so that the
programme's other works have something to point at.

`Deferred` because it was not read. This record files books from their
publisher's description and contents when no lawful text can be read, and
it does not rebuild a book from what others say about it. So the takeaways
above stop at the description and the contents list. What the record knows
of the book's content comes from two readings filed with it:

- Push Singh's *Examining the Society of Mind* ([LIT-tmpxp9b8](LIT-tmpxp9b8.md)), a sympathetic
  exposition by Minsky's student. It quotes the book (§§2.5, 6.12, 10.4,
  20.1, 30.8 and the Postscript) and lays out its agents, K-lines, nomes and
  nemes, frames and transframes, censors, and A- and B-brains. It is
  secondary, and it is a student's.
- The 2005 draft of the sequel, *The Emotion Machine* ([LIT-tmpr3qe6](LIT-tmpr3qe6.md)), read in
  full. Its introduction says it replaces this book's "agent" with
  "resource". Many readers had taken an agent to be "a personlike thing (like
  a travel agent)", and that was not the intent. Its §9-5 says plainly that
  many readers took this book to propose a "community view" of the mind, and
  "that was not my intention". When the book is read, that reading should
  check whether the 1986 text invites the community reading the sequel
  disowns.

Minsky defended the book against its first scientific reviewers in
*Artificial Intelligence* in 1991 ([LIT-tmpi03n5](LIT-tmpi03n5.md)). The earlier memos and
papers that led to it (the frames memo of 1974, "Plain Talk About
Neurodevelopmental Epistemology" of 1977, "K-lines" of 1980 and the 1980
jokes memo) are being filed in this record at the same time by another
reader. Singh traces the agent idea to the procedure-bearing frames of the
1974 memo. He quotes the 1977 paper, which already names the theory "The
Society of Minds", and the 1980 K-lines paper, which pictures the mind as
"Divisions" of agents. By its title, the book's chapter "Censors and Jokes"
takes up the jokes memo's theme. Singh ties the book's censors and humour to
that memo.

The book bears on what this record holds in three places. These are my
inferences from the description, the contents and the two readings above,
not claims the book was read to make:

- The record's work on wholes made of parts and on collective
  intelligence. Pilgrim et al. ([LIT-046](LIT-046.md)) analyse collectives of capable
  individuals. Minsky's agents are too specialised to be individuals in
  that sense, and Singh says so.
- Group minds: Schwitzgebel's argument that the United States may be
  conscious ([LIT-159](LIT-159.md)).
- The record's emotion works. The book has a chapter called "Emotion",
  which is why `emotion-and-affect` is among its tags. Its sequel's account
  of emotion is set against Damasio's ([LIT-384](LIT-384.md), [LIT-388](LIT-388.md)) in the reading of
  that sequel ([NOTE-tmp95bt1](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp95bt1.md)).
