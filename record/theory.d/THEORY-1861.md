# THEORY-1861: Tools replacing labour enslave their users

## The account

Illich holds that "used for this purpose, machines enslave men", and that
"People need new tools to work with rather than tools that “work” for
them". "I consider conviviality to be individual freedom realized in
personal interdependence and, as such, an intrinsic ethical value." And:
"I speak about radical monopoly when one industrial production process
exercises an exclusive control over the satisfaction of a pressing need,
and excludes nonindustrial activities from competition"; "That motor
traffic curtails the right to walk, not that more people drive Chevies
than Fords, constitutes radical monopoly."

## Evidence

The excerpt read in [NOTE-1921](../notes.d/NOTE-1921.md), results 1–11.

- **Corroborates [CLAIM-677](../claims.d/CLAIM-677.md)**: radical monopoly harms the
  non-industrial alternative, not adopters' choice among brands, which is
  the claim's measure; the city shaped around cars is its infrastructural
  mechanism. "Cars create distance" is a further mechanism the claim
  does not list: adoption enlarges the need it serves.
- **Corroborates [CLAIM-675](../claims.d/CLAIM-675.md)**, the distinction only: tools to work with
  against tools that "work" for people, at the cost of persons reduced to
  "mere consumers". No dynamics.
- **Bounds [CLAIM-682](../claims.d/CLAIM-682.md)**: "Neither a dictatorial proletariat nor a
  leisure mass can escape the dominion of constantly expanding
  industrial tools" places the constraint in the tools, whoever controls
  them, outside a test for domination by controllers.

## What it does not say

- That alternatives become "unimaginable", the second watershed, or the
  burial case: none is in the excerpt. They rest on Cayley's reading
  ([THEORY-1862](THEORY-1862.md)).
- How much political control of tools suffices, or who carries the burden
  of a change in them.
