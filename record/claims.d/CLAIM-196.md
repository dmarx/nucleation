# CLAIM-196: A person's roles in contexts that do not meet cannot contradict one another: role conflict needs an overlap, which segregating audiences prevents and collapsed contexts create

## The claim

**The passage.** MoM's v1 "Sheaf Semantics of Context" §11.5, "Identity as
Global Section", says: "Not only words but **selves** are contextual". It
takes a person's roles in family, professional and social contexts, and
continues: "If these local identities glue coherently, the sheaf has a
global section: a stable self. If they do not (contradictory roles,
fractured coherence), identity fails to unify."

**As written it is wrong, in the way [CLAIM-197](CLAIM-197.md) identifies.** If the
family context and the professional context do not meet, the roles agree
on every overlap because there is none, and they glue. A person who is
deferential at home and authoritative at work holds no contradiction by
that fact.

**The defensible version.** Roles conflict only on an overlap: an occasion
on which audiences from two contexts are present together and the person
must present one way to both. The cover is not given. People build and
maintain it.

- **Segregating audiences keeps the contexts disjoint.** Different styles
  for different groups do no harm while the groups are kept apart.
- **Scripting manages the overlaps that must happen.** Weddings, "in which
  context collisions are common, are frequently scripted to make everyone
  comfortable" ([LIT-1001](../literature.d/LIT-1001.md), p. 38).
- **Collapse creates overlaps nobody chose.** Broadcast and networked
  media bring the audiences together. Carmichael "was able to maintain
  distinct styles as long as he was able to segment social groups, he ran
  into trouble when electronic media collapsed those social groups"
  ([LIT-1001](../literature.d/LIT-1001.md), pp. 36–37; [CASE-052](../cases.d/CASE-052.md)).

So a presented self ([TERM-045](../terms.d/TERM-045.md)<!-- ref::uses: TERM-045 -->) is a global section relative
to a cover the person partly controls. Integrity of presentation can be achieved two
ways: by sections that agree where contexts meet, or by keeping contexts
from meeting. The second is not deception. boyd makes the point that
distinct contexts are how people "properly locate their performances".

## Why this record holds it

It is the synchronic partner of [CLAIM-198](CLAIM-198.md). There, interests over time
conflict only where self-prediction makes an overlap. Here, roles across
audiences conflict only where audiences meet. [CLAIM-153](CLAIM-153.md) supplies the
mathematics once overlaps exist: with pairwise overlaps, two contexts
always glue, and the obstruction is a loop.

The sheaf picture adds one thing to the sociology. It says where conflict
can be: on an overlap. That makes a prediction the sociology states but
does not derive. Segregation strategies should target the overlaps, the
shared channels and the occasions where audiences meet, and should not
target the roles themselves.

## What it does not say

- **That role conflict is rare.** Overlaps are common, and for networked
  publics boyd describes them as constant.
- **Anything about the other senses of "self".** The manuscript's
  "identity" is the presented self, sense 7 of [TERM-045](../terms.d/TERM-045.md). A person whose
  presentations collide is not thereby less a subject, less integrated or
  less the same over time.
- **That contradiction does no harm unseen.** A person may feel the
  contradiction while the audiences are apart, by remembering one audience
  in front of the other. That is an overlap made by memory, as for the
  film joke in [CASE-048](../cases.d/CASE-048.md), and the defeat condition is written to allow it.
