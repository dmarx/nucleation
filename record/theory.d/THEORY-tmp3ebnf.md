# THEORY-tmp3ebnf: Miyazono and Inarimori argue that self–other merging is best read as group identification, so that in empathic helping the other's welfare becomes a constituent of one's first-person plural welfare, and the helping is altruistic at the individual level and egoistic at the group level

## The account

Cialdini et al.'s "merging" is group identification: in empathizing
with someone one comes to conceive of her as part of one's social self.
It needs no belief in being two bodies, since identification is a
non-doxastic Pushmi-Pullyu representation (after Salice and Miyazono).
Egoistic helping uses the other's welfare as a means; identified
helping is the reverse: "Reynie's first-person plural welfare is
constituted by, but is not causally influenced by, Elaine's third-person
singular welfare" (p. 9). Caring for a whole includes caring for its
parts for their own sake, so the act is altruistic toward the other and
egoistic toward "us". Identification can occur without empathy, as in
minimal groups (p. 5).

## Evidence

Argument (NOTE-1277). Behavioural adequacy is an analogy with
minimal-group favouritism; Batson's results against merging are not
examined ("conducted experiments that cast doubt on SMH", p. 4). The
reply on strangers infers identification from a similarity the empathy
itself creates.

## What it bears on

QUESTION-300 asks whether any motivation aims at another's good and
at the good of no self. The paper answers for empathic helping: it aims
at another's good for its own sake and also at the good of a "we" the
helper belongs to. It gives no measure of identification apart from
empathy, which is what the question's "criterion of the self's extent
that can come apart from caring" asks for.
