# NOTE-tmpfar7p: Delusion

Lisa Bortolotti, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, revised 2026.

## Contribution

A survey organised by the continuity thesis: how far delusions differ from ordinary irrational beliefs, and what makes them pathological.
- **§1:** six candidate answers to what makes delusions pathological, none chosen.
- **§2:** the DSM definition and its critics, and attributing delusion as "epistemic gatekeeping".
- **§3:** formation. One-factor (Maher), two-factor (Davies, Coltheart, McKay) and prediction-error (Fletcher and Frith, Corlett) accounts; bottom-up against top-down; explanationism against endorsement.
- **§4:** irrationality, belief status, and overlap with self-deception and conspiracy belief.

Bortolotti argues in her own voice in §4.1 and §4.2.

## Key results

- **Resistance is the mark, not falsity** (preamble): delusions "resist counterevidence and are not easily given up in the face of challenges", and may be true.
- **The cultural clause** (§2.1): the DSM excludes a belief "ordinarily accepted by other members of the person's culture or subculture". Coltheart asks whether a belief held by a whole community could still be delusional.
- **Two factors** (§3.2, and §4.3 for conspiracy belief): "The first factor would explain the theme of the delusion or conspiracy. The second factor would explain why the belief is endorsed and maintained despite its prima facie implausibility."
- **Prediction error** (§4.1, quoting McKay 2012, p. 18): with "an excess of prediction error signal, inappropriately heightened salience is attached to the data, and belief revision is excessively accommodatory – biased towards explanatory adequacy". This is aberrant salience in substance; the term is not used.
- **Maintenance** (§4.1, Coltheart, Menzies and Sutton 2010): information undermining the delusion "does not present itself as disconfirming evidence to the person with the delusion".
- **A need for closure** (§4.1): people with delusions may "have an accentuated need for closure which comprises a desire for clarity and structure" (Kruglanski 1989). It is one citation, not developed.
- **Degree, not kind** (§4.1): delusions "can be irrational to a higher degree than normal beliefs… they do not seem to be irrational in a qualitatively different way from nondelusional beliefs".
- **Doxasticism** (§4.2): anti-doxastic arguments "rely on an idealization of belief states, and impose constraints on delusions that not even typical beliefs would meet".
- **Motivation splits the cases** (§4.3): for misidentification delusions "there is no fundamental role for motivational biases"; "A different analysis might be appropriate for delusions of jealousy or persecution", which may be self-deception.

## Assumptions

- Clinical delusion is the topic; everyday and shared delusion are mentioned only through the DSM's cultural clause.
- The phenomenological tradition (delusional mood, Conrad's apophany) is almost absent.

## Concepts

The continuity thesis; doxasticism and its rivals (imaginings, acceptances, in-between states); one-factor, two-factor and prediction-error accounts; explanationism and endorsement; epistemic gatekeeping; the epistemic benefits of delusion (cited, not set out).

## Bearing on the record

- **The gamed-evaluator question** (CLAIM-tmpcv6i6, CLAIM-tmpu6fff, CLAIM-tmpu6c7h): the entry's split by motivation has the form of CLAIM-tmpu6c7h. Its unmotivated cases are a miscalibrated signal, as CLAIM-tmpu6fff says. What is sought in the motivated cases is the comfort of a content, not the feeling of insight.
- **The delusion thread:** the two-factor template separates the theme from its endorsement. That gives DISTINCTION-tmpwhh30 a third axis, below.
- **Absent:** folie à deux and shared delusion, apophany, felt certainty, AI, mathematics.

## Limitations

A survey that argues little. "Degree, not kind" has no measure of degree. The social and affective turn of the 2026 revision is cited only.
