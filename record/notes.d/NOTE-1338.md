# NOTE-1338: Ward & Marco — pragmatic-similarity judgments

## Contribution

Before this there were measures of semantic and prosodic similarity between
utterances and none of pragmatic similarity, and no human judgments to train
or test one (§§1–3, pp. 154–155). The paper collects them. Each stimulus is a
seed utterance from a recorded dialog (mostly the DRAL corpus) followed by a
re-enactment, made by one of four re-enactors or a synthesizer under one of
six conditions that withhold different information (Table 1, p. 157). The
release has the stimuli, seeds, re-enactments, source conversations and all
ratings (p. 158). The paper also reports the protocol, three pilot studies
and post-hoc factors behind agreement.

## Key insight

Pragmatic similarity can be rated directly, from audio, with no taxonomy of
functions, and trained judges converge on it about as well as people
converge on most subjective audio judgments. Their agreement depends on
what is being compared. Judges agree less when the two utterances differ in
words, and they disagree on which cues count, rate of speech for one,
endings for another (p. 156). The authors chose to "simply accept that
people differ in how they included and weighed the possible factors
underlying these judgments, and not try to constrain them in some way to
increase agreement scores" (p. 156).

## Assumptions

- **Within-language pairs.** Seed and re-enactment are always in the same
  language. There are no cross-language pairs; the Spanish session is a
  separate collection (§5.2, p. 157).
- **Isolated utterances.** Stimuli are played without context (pilot 4,
  p. 156), back to back, seed first (p. 157). Seeds are 3 words to 6
  seconds, average 3 seconds, "mostly clear in intent and meaning when heard
  in isolation", with no reported speech (p. 157).
- **One holistic instruction.** "How pragmatically similar are the two
  clips, in terms of the overall feeling, tone, and intent", ignoring
  speaker, word and insignificant prosodic differences, and "Maybe
  consider" likely contexts, "how a listener would likely respond", the
  speaker's feeling and the dialog activity (Fig. 1, p. 158).
- **A selected, socially connected panel.** Judges were invited from
  earlier eager participants and the lab; "Most were in their early 20s"
  (p. 157), and all were connected to at least one other judge. The first
  author judged in Sessions 1 and 2, the second author in Session 3
  (p. 157); the authors were also two of the four re-enactors (p. 157).
- **Discussion during rating.** "for the first ten stimuli, and
  periodically throughout, we had them compare their ratings and discuss the
  factors that had affected their judgments" (p. 158). Judges were told
  "that convergence was not expected" (p. 158).

## Key results

- **Size (Table 2, p. 158).** Session 1: 220 English pairs, 9 judges, 1,980
  ratings. Session 2: 238 English pairs, 9 judges, 2,142. Session 3: 235
  Spanish pairs, 6 judges, 1,410. Total 5,532 (checked). From 80 English and
  40 Spanish seeds (p. 157). The repository README gives 1,872 ratings for
  the second English session and 689 stimuli in all; I did not reconcile
  these with the paper.
- **Agreement (Table 2).** Mean pairwise Pearson correlation between judges:
  0.45, 0.72, 0.66. The abstract states the last two: "as high as 0.72 for
  English and 0.66 for Spanish" (p. 154). Pearson, because scatterplots
  "did not reveal any nonlinear patterns" (p. 158).
- **Session 1 in detail (Table 3, p. 160).** 36 pairwise correlations from
  0.18 to 0.80, mean 0.447 (recomputed); per-judge means 0.29 to 0.53. The
  judge at 0.29 gave "ratings that were mostly just integers" (p. 159).
- **What lowers agreement (§8.2, pp. 159–160).** Judge identity; inexperience
  (Session 1 to 2, though "the least-agreeing judge did not return for that
  session", p. 159); low-rated stimuli (correlation of per-item SD with mean
  rating −0.60, 0.00, −0.39); different words: "for Session 2, excluding
  those stimuli gave an average inter-judge agreement of 0.80, significantly
  above the 0.72 seen for all stimuli" (p. 160); duration differences (SD
  correlates 0.30 with duration difference in Session 1). Of the 27 Session
  1 stimuli with SD > 1.0, many were Context-Only re-enactments, seeds with
  laughter or breathiness, or seeds with unusual warmth (Table 4).
- **Conditions as a check (§8.1, p. 159).** Voice re-enactments were rated
  most similar and synthesized ones only weakly, as designed. Two
  expectations failed: Words-Only beat Context-Only, and Words-Only beat
  Words+Context; Lexically-Distinct was barely above Words-Only.
- **Drift.** Mean rating per stimulus correlates with serial position 0.04,
  0.09, 0.16 by session (p. 159).
- **Reliability of the panel mean, my computation.** If judges are
  exchangeable, the Spearman–Brown reliability of a k-judge mean at mean
  inter-judge r is kr / (1 + (k − 1)r): 0.88 for Session 1 (r = 0.45,
  k = 9), 0.96 for Session 2, 0.92 for Session 3 (k = 6). The paper does not
  report these, and the authors themselves "would be mostly using the
  averages of everyone's judgments" (p. 158).

## Concepts

- **Pragmatic similarity** — as instructed to judges: similarity "in terms of
  the overall feeling, tone, and intent" (Fig. 1). It includes, by
  suggestion, likely contexts and likely listener response.
- **Seed and re-enactment** — a clip from a real dialog, and a new
  production of it under one of six conditions: Voice, Words+Context,
  Lexically Distinct, Context-Only, Words, Synthesized (§5.1).
- **Inter-judge agreement** — the mean of pairwise Pearson correlations
  between judges' ratings over a session's stimuli (p. 158).

## Connections

- **Avila and Ward 2023**, the DRAL corpus the seeds come from and a
  prosodic distance metric over 100 features that this data could evaluate
  (p. 155). Not held.
- **Pragst 2022**, the one earlier attempt at pragmatic similarity, text only
  and on artificial data (p. 155). Not held.
- **TyDiP** ([LIT-1799](../literature.d/LIT-1799.md), read the same day) reports agreement for a
  narrower judgement, politeness, as pairwise Spearman: 0.46 in the target
  language. The two numbers are close but measure different things.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-185](../cases.d/CASE-185.md): its "main threat" sentence misreads the 0.45.** The case
  says "PragSim's judges agreed at a mean correlation of 0.45 ([LIT-1812](../literature.d/LIT-1812.md)),
  so Stage 0's reliability targets must be met before any transport readout
  is interpreted". The conclusion stands; the premise is narrower than it
  reads. (a) 0.45 is Session 1 only; the other sessions gave 0.72 and 0.66,
  and the abstract reports those. (b) It is the agreement between two single
  judges. The case's criterion is the panel's choice, and the mean of nine
  judges at r = 0.45 has an estimated reliability of about 0.88 (my
  Spearman–Brown, assuming exchangeable judges). (c) In the other direction,
  the figure is inflated by things a translation study would not have:
  judges discussed their ratings in the room, knew each other, and two were
  authors; and pairs sharing words agree best (0.80 against 0.72). Every
  translation pair differs in its words, so the agreement that matters for
  the case is the one for the Lexically-Distinct and Context-Only pairs,
  which the paper says is lower and does not report.
- **[CASE-055](../cases.d/CASE-055.md), Stage 0: a protocol to borrow, and a reliability figure that
  is not the one Stage 0 asks for.** Stage 0 wants written definitions, a
  fixed format, agreement between judges and between two passes of one
  judge, and known-groups validation. The paper has a written instruction
  (Fig. 1), a fixed continuous format with anchors (p. 158) and inter-judge
  agreement. It has no second pass by the same judge. Its re-enactment
  conditions are a rough known-groups test, and it half passes: Voice above
  Synthesized as built, Words-Only above Context-Only and Words+Context
  against the design. The paper's open admission that judges weigh different
  cues (p. 156) is the measurement problem [CLAIM-227](../claims.d/CLAIM-227.md) names, made visible.
- **[CLAIM-202](../claims.d/CLAIM-202.md): a warning that the "static" baseline may not be static.** The
  claim asks whether context-indexed response adds to the static pragmatic
  profile. Ward and Marco's judges were told to "Maybe consider … Similarity
  in how a listener would likely respond" and "Similarity in the contexts
  where they would likely appear" (Fig. 1). A similarity metric trained on
  these ratings would fold expected uptake into the baseline. A comparison
  under [CLAIM-202](../claims.d/CLAIM-202.md)'s defeat condition would have to say which baseline it
  beats.
- **[CLAIM-226](../claims.d/CLAIM-226.md): in-session calibration is the opposite of what the claim
  asks.** The paper averages a socially connected panel that discussed its
  ratings, and treats disagreement as something to accept and average
  (p. 158). It does observe structured differences between judges (strict
  against generous, integer users, extremes; p. 159), which supports the
  claim's premise that judges differ in a structured way.
- **[CLAIM-257](../claims.d/CLAIM-257.md): no bearing.**
- **Should produce [THEORY-1148](../theory.d/THEORY-1148.md)** (Proposed): single-judge agreement on
  pragmatic similarity is moderate and depends on lexical overlap and
  experience, and a panel mean is far more reliable.
- **Machine-learning practice: an anthology candidate.** §8.3 tells modelers
  to use "the Session 1 data for training and the Session 2 data for
  evaluation" and to z-normalize each judge before averaging (p. 160). That
  is an instruction for training and evaluating a metric. The LIT carries
  `anthology-candidate`.
- **Correction to the LIT's description.** The summary's "nine judges rated
  220 pairs … with a mean inter-judge correlation of 0.45" describes
  Session 1 only. The collection is 693 pairs in three sessions with 6 to 9
  judges each.

## Limitations

- **Within-language only**; nothing about pragmatic similarity across
  languages, which is what translation needs.
- **No test–retest**, and no agreement figure by re-enactment condition.
- **A small, homogeneous panel**: mostly early-20s, connected, from one lab's
  circle in El Paso, with the authors among them.
- **Post-hoc analysis.** The authors say "the data was not collected to
  support a systematic analysis; the rest of this section only reports the
  factors identified using post-hoc tests" (p. 159).
- **Isolated clips**, which the authors chose knowingly: context "would likely
  boost the inter-annotator agreement" but cost time (p. 156).
- **Counts differ between the paper and the repository README** (2,142
  against 1,872 ratings for Session 2).

## Open questions

- What is inter-judge agreement for lexically different pairs alone, by
  session? The released ratings can answer it.
- How consistent is one judge across two passes on the same pairs?
- Would cross-language pairs, a seed and a translated re-enactment rated by
  bilinguals, reach the same agreement? That is the measurement
  [CASE-185](../cases.d/CASE-185.md) needs and nobody has collected.
