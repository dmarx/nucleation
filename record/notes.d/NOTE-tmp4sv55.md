# NOTE-tmp4sv55: Hyzen, Van den Bulck, Puppis, Kulig and Paulussen — Epistemic welfare and algorithmic recommender systems

## Contribution

It proposes epistemic welfare as a normative frame for the "epistemic
crisis" of a datafied, algorithmic and platformized public sphere. The
frame is wider than misinformation or exposure diversity: it covers the
production, distribution and acquisition of knowledge. It extends
Majcher's legal notion of a right to trustworthy information to
knowledge. It then uses news organizations' recommender systems as a test
case for Goldman's truth-linked standards.

## Key insight

Epistemic agency needs two things: conditions, which are structural, and
capabilities, which are individual. The capabilities come in stages. On
the tool's account, information, media and digital literacy "turn
availability into access" [exact], and "scientific literacy, critical
thinking and problem-solving turn access into knowledge acquisition"
[exact]. Without the right conditions, people "may not have the
opportunity" to use the capabilities they have. So being able to
evaluate information does not give access to it, and access does not give
knowledge.

## Assumptions

- Veritistic social epistemology: "oriented towards the pursuit,
  verification and promotion of true beliefs" [exact]. Five epistemically
  valuable states, from true belief to knowledge, and heavy dependence on
  testimony, journalism included.
- Goldman's standards, "truth-linked standards, any or all of which can be
  used to appraise social institutions and practices" [exact]. "Power" is
  epistemic problem-solving, not political power.
- Welfare, after Kaun and colleagues: "fundamentally about how societies
  are organized and what shared values prevail" [exact]. Governance
  includes self-regulation, co-regulation and design, not only state
  regulation.
- The case is legacy media's recommenders, not platforms. "Unlike
  platforms, media's control over and standards for production provide
  more guarantees" [exact], so the analysis is of distribution.

## Key results

- **Definition.** "We define epistemic welfare as creating and maintaining
  the conditions and capabilities" [exact] "for individuals' epistemic
  agency in the public sphere." [exact] Epistemic agency is described as
  "control over knowledge acquisition: how true beliefs are formed and
  revised" [exact]. Capabilities are the "skills, competencies and
  critical understanding required to access, appropriate and disperse
  knowledge" [exact].
- **Conditions.** Political systems, platform governance and designers'
  intentions; authoritarian regimes "often restrict information access"
  [exact].
- **The standards applied to recommenders.** Reliability: "how many false
  beliefs (errors) and justified beliefs (truths) the system disseminates"
  [exact], including whether corrections reach users, since they "may not
  receive visibility or distribution due to low user engagement" [exact].
  Power: connecting users to relevant and diverse answers. Fecundity: the
  "capacity for many users to attain epistemically valuable states"
  [exact]. Speed, which can cost verification. Efficiency, which counts
  hidden costs such as personal data, advertising and environmental
  impact. Popularity and advertising objectives can weaken power,
  fecundity and reliability.
- **Governance.** Two kinds. By algorithms: design that embeds the
  standards, for instance keeping commercially unattractive but diverse
  content discoverable. Of algorithms: regulation. Under the EU AI Act,
  media recommenders carry only transparency duties, and "transparency
  might not be helpful in assessing the design and quality of a
  recommender system" [exact]. The paper favours self- and co-regulation
  given media freedom, treats public service media as the non-commercial
  model, and warns against making individuals responsible for protecting
  themselves.
- **Conclusion.** "epistemic welfare cannot be ensured if algorithmic
  recommender systems are primarily optimized for profit-oriented goals"
  [exact]. The authors grant that conditions and capabilities do not
  guarantee that agency is used well, and say that epistemic welfare is
  not the same as democracy.

## Concepts

- **Epistemic welfare**: as defined above. It is a goal of welfare
  provision, not a right held by an individual.
- **Conditions** versus **capabilities**: structural enablers versus
  individual competences, both "prerequisites" [exact] of agency.
- **Governance by and of algorithms**: design as governance, versus
  regulation of the systems.

## Connections

It builds on Goldman's veritistic social epistemology, Majcher on epistemic
welfare in law, Coeckelbergh on epistemic agency and AI, Kaun and
colleagues on the welfare state, and the literature on recommender
diversity.

## Limitations

- **Not first-hand.** Every quote passed through a summarizer.
- **Conceptual.** The standards are applied to recommenders by argument,
  with no evaluation of any system.
- **Media, not platforms.** By design. Its conclusions for platforms are
  stated as contrasts only.

## Open questions

- How are the five standards to be traded against one another when they
  conflict, for instance speed against reliability? The skim found no
  rule.
