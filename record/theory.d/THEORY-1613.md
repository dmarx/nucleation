# THEORY-1613: Hirst, Cyr and Merck argue that flashbulb memories make members of a community who learned of an event at a distance into witnesses who can carry its trauma process, and report a study in which most children knew a parent's flashbulb memory of 9/11 and knew it better the more strongly they identified as American

## The account

Alexander's trauma process needs a carrier group. The authors add one:
ordinary members of a community, made witnesses by memories of where they
were when they heard. Quoting Neisser, such memories "are the places that
we line up our own lives with the sources of history itself and say 'I was
there'" (p. 598). They are specific to a community and can mark membership:
"If you do not have a flashbulb memory of 9/11, then you cannot be
considered a 'proper American'" (p. 602). Where nearly everyone has one,
speaker and audience coincide, each member with "a democratic standing as
a witness" (p. 595). The memories are inaccurate in detail but held with
confidence, so witnesses are "authentic" "not because their memories are
'reliable' and 'accurate,' but because both the witness and those
attending to the witness believe they are so" (p. 606).

They pass to children. In Meyler et al. (2020), 89% of children nine or
younger at 9/11 could report a parent's flashbulb memory; the more
strongly the child identified as American, the more likely they were to
know it and report it accurately. The authors note the direction is
unknown.

## Evidence

The essay ([NOTE-1305](../notes.d/NOTE-1305.md)): interpretive argument from Neisser and Alexander,
cross-group studies of flashbulb memory for specificity, and studies of
confidence and accuracy for the authenticity claim. The intergenerational
result is one correlational study, reported second hand and then in press.
A flashbulb memory, the authors grant, "only underscores the legitimacy of
community members to serve as authentic witnesses" (p. 609).

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-297](../questions.d/QUESTION-297.md), the identity route. It ties a group's memory of an injury to
its members' identification across a generation. It falls short of the
question's test: it measures knowledge, not distress; the channel is the
family; and the direction of the association is unknown.
