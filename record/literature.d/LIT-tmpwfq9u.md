**What it is said to argue**, attributed: *per the publisher*, Boehm
"finds existing explanations lacking" (nepotism, reciprocation, group
selection) and argues "that our moral sense is a sophisticated defense
mechanism that enables individuals to survive and thrive in groups". The
risk is "being punished for our misdeeds by those around us": "bullies,
thieves, free-riders, and especially psychopaths … are the most likely to
suffer this fate". "This social type of selection … singles out
altruists for survival", "bred the first stirrings of conscience" and
"led to the fully developed sense of virtue and shame".

**Bearing on morality-as-cooperation**, as far as these sources show.
Boehm's account is a mechanism, social selection by group punishment and
reputation, more than a map of domains. On the description it bears on
MAC's exchange domain (free riders, thieves) and on its contest domains
from the opposite side: where MAC predicts that deference to the powerful
is moralized, Boehm's groups punish bullies. The "reverse dominance
hierarchy" that Gintis's commentary on [LIT-047](LIT-047.md) invokes ([NOTE-034](../notes.d/NOTE-034.md)) is
Boehm's term from his earlier work; whether this book treats the
dominance–deference question directly is for a reading to check. The
publisher's line that he "finds existing explanations lacking" places
him against kin selection and reciprocity as sufficient accounts, which
is MAC's own starting point.

**Neighbours.** Alexander ([LIT-tmp0b4eb](LIT-tmp0b4eb.md)) also explains morality by
reputation within groups. Trivers ([LIT-tmppstp9](LIT-tmppstp9.md)) gives the moralistic
aggression that Boehm's group punishment generalizes. Fitouchi, André &
Baumard ([LIT-101](LIT-101.md)) explain puritanical norms by reputational concern for
self-control, a neighbouring reputational account. Nowak ([LIT-tmpbmh7b](LIT-tmpbmh7b.md))
holds that punishment is not itself a mechanism for cooperation, which a
reading should set against Boehm's punishment-driven social selection.

**Topic.** `moral-psychology` first. `social-science` for its
anthropology of foragers, `natural-sciences` for the primatology and
evolutionary argument, `emotion-and-affect` for shame and conscience as
its explananda. `ethics` is not added: the epilogue on "humanity's moral
future" may be normative, and a reading should decide.
