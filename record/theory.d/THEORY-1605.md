# THEORY-1605: Salice and Miyazono argue that group identification, as philosophers of shared agency use it, runs together two separable processes, adopting the group's perspective and self-transformation into a member, and that self-transformation is a Pushmi-Pullyu representation that describes and motivates at once

## The account

Thinking about what is good for one's group is not the same as being
one of the group. A committee member caught in a discursive dilemma can
work out what "we" should conclude while personally disagreeing; a
two-year-old can act as one of "us" before being able to take the
group's perspective. Only self-transformation is identification in the
self-involving sense. It is not a belief, which would not motivate, but
a Pushmi-Pullyu representation in Millikan's sense, triggered by cues
such as joint attention and not under direct rational control. The team
reasoner holds "We intend to J", with the "we" in subject position; the
team benefactor reasons in the first person singular about the group's
well-being.

## Evidence

Argument, not data ([NOTE-1266](../notes.d/NOTE-1266.md)). The developmental dissociation rests
on borrowed timing (the black sheep effect from about three, joint
action at 18–24 months), which the authors call "conditional and
defeasible". The Pushmi-Pullyu account is argued by eliminating a
doxastic view and by analogy.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-300](../questions.d/QUESTION-300.md) holds team reasoning to make "what is good for us" a
mode "that is neither self-interest nor altruism", and asks whether any
motivation aims at another's good and at no self's. The team
benefactor, who promotes a group's good without identifying as a
member, is a case of the first kind; the activated social self is a
proposed criterion of the self's extent that is not a report of
caring.
