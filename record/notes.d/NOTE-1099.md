# NOTE-1099: Statistical ranking and combinatorial Hodge theory

## Contribution

A method for ranking from incomplete, imbalanced, cardinal pairwise data,
HodgeRank, with a built-in diagnostic of whether the data admit a global
ranking at all. Its authors call their "elementary treatment a minor
expository contribution" on the mathematics' side (p. 5). The new part is
its application to ranking.

## Key insight

A ranking is a potential and the comparisons are its gradient. Data
consistent with a ranking form a gradient flow. What is left after the
best gradient fit is a cyclic flow. Cycles are not noise to be discarded:
"cyclic inconsistencies are not necessarily due to error or noise in the
data but may very well be an inherent characteristic of the data" (p. 3).
"Even a single individual making his own preference judgements could face
such dilemma — if he uses multiple criteria to rank the alternatives"
(p. 3).

## Assumptions

- **Cardinal comparisons.** "We are primarily interested in cardinal data
  based on scores or ratings though our methods also give specific
  insights on ordinal data" (abstract). Curl-freeness, X_ij + X_jk + X_ki
  = 0, is a cardinal condition (p. 30).
- **A pre-processed flow Ȳ.** Raw data must first be turned into one of
  four averages (§2.2.1, p. 10): mean score difference, log score ratio,
  binary comparison, or log odds. The result depends on which is chosen.
- **Weights.** w_ij defaults to the number of comparisons of the pair
  (eq. 8). "This choice of W is not intended to be rigid. One could for
  example define W to incorporate prior knowledge of the relative
  importance of the paired comparisons as judged by the voters" (p. 9).
- **l2 geometry.** Orthogonality and the least-squares solution use the
  weighted inner product on edge flows (§5.1). l1 variants are treated in
  §6 and lose orthogonality.

## Key results

- **Definition 2.3, p. 12.** X is *globally consistent* if X = grad s for
  some score function s, and *locally consistent* if it is curl-free on
  every triangle of the comparison graph.
- **Theorem 4.7 (Hodge Decomposition Theorem), p. 18.** For a simplicial
  complex K, C^k(K, ℝ) = im(δ_{k−1}) ⊕ ker(Δ_k) ⊕ im(δ_k*), orthogonally,
  with ker(Δ_k) = ker(δ_k) ∩ ker(δ_{k−1}*). A proof is included (pp. 18–19).
- **Theorem 4.8 (Helmholtz Decomposition Theorem), p. 19.** "Let G = (V,
  E) be an undirected, unweighted graph and KG be its clique complex."
  Then C¹(K_G, ℝ) = im(grad) ⊕ ker(Δ₁) ⊕ im(curl*), and ker(Δ₁) = ker(curl)
  ∩ ker(div). "The equation (21) says that an edge flow is harmonic iff it
  is both curl-free and divergence-free."
- **Theorem 5.1, pp. 21–22.** (i) Solutions of (7) satisfy ∆₀s = −div Ȳ,
  and the minimum-norm solution is s* = −∆₀† div Ȳ. (ii) The residual R* =
  Ȳ − δ₀s* "is divergence-free, i.e. div R∗ = 0", and splits orthogonally
  into proj_im(curl*) Ȳ, which "is a local cyclic ranking accounting for
  local inconsistencies", and proj_ker(Δ₁) Ȳ, which "is a harmonic ranking
  accounting for global inconsistencies". On a complete graph with
  unweighted inner product, s* is "the well-known Borda count" (p. 22).
- **Theorem 5.2, p. 24.** If the 3-clique complex has no 1-loops (β₁ =
  0), "every locally consistent pairwise ranking is also globally
  consistent". There is no ordinal analogue: "If we instead have
  “triangular transitivity” in the ordinal sense", "then there is no
  result analogous to Theorem 5.2."
- **Theorem 7.1, pp. 31–32.** For binary Y ∈ {±1}, least squares over the
  Kemeny class, a linear programme over the convex hull of permutations,
  weighted l1 projection onto the transitive region, and minimum feedback
  arc set are equivalent. "It is known that the minimum feedback arc set
  problem in (iv) is NP-hard, and therefore, so are the other three"
  (p. 32).
- **Inconsistency indices, p. 35.** The "cyclicity ratio, is a measure of
  global inconsistency given by" C_p = ‖R*‖²_{2,w} / ‖Ȳ‖²_{2,w}. Relative
  curl compares a triangle's curl with the gradient on one of its edges.
  On the Netflix example the binary-comparison flow "has the smallest
  cyclicity ratio, 0.30" (p. 36).

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Any pairwise-ranking flow decomposes orthogonally into gradient, curl and harmonic parts | strong | Theorems 4.7–4.8, proved |
| C2 | The least-squares ranking is the gradient part; the residual is the cyclic part | strong | Theorem 5.1 |
| C3 | On complete, balanced, binary data it is the Borda count | strong | eq. (26), §7.1 |
| C4 | A small residual certifies a global ranking; a large one says no ranking explains the data | moderate | interpretive; the l2 share is a convention |
| C5 | Harmonic components are rare on realistic comparison graphs | weak | Kahle's random-graph result, applied by analogy (§5.2) |

## Concepts

- **gradient / curl / harmonic flow** — globally acyclic, locally cyclic,
  and locally acyclic but globally cyclic pairwise rankings.
- **cyclicity ratio** — the residual's share of the flow's squared
  weighted norm.
- **certificate of reliability** — the authors' name for the residual's
  magnitude.

## Connections

Candogan et al. ([LIT-1442](../literature.d/LIT-1442.md)) cite this paper and apply the same
decomposition to games, where curl vanishes. Sen's liberal paradox
([THEORY-027](../theory.d/THEORY-027.md)) is the ordinal intransitivity this paper sets beside its
cardinal inconsistency (p. 30). Kendall and Smith's circular triads, the
ordinal index, are the binary case of its curl (p. 12).

## Bearing on the record

**It fits the conflict record directly.** [QUESTION-268](../questions.d/QUESTION-268.md)'s first
measure is an organization's decisions on the pairs where its perspectives
disagree. HodgeRank takes exactly such data: alternatives as nodes,
contested pairs as edges, and the direction of each decision as a binary
comparison averaged over occasions, Ȳ_ij = Pr(j chosen over i) − Pr(i over
j) (p. 10). Two flows must be kept apart.

- *The decision flow*, built from what the system did. Its gradient share
  says whether the system's contested decisions admit a global ordering.
  That is [TERM-076](../terms.d/TERM-076.md)'s *common currency*: whether the negotiation has a
  potential at all.
- *The perspectives' flows*, one per perspective, built from what each
  would have chosen. Aggregating them with HodgeRank gives a Borda-like
  collective ranking. That is a choice rule applied to the perspectives,
  not the outcome of their negotiation, and it must not be mistaken for
  it.

Which ordering prevails is then a comparison between the two. One
candidate, the record's and not the paper's, is the weighted correlation
⟨grad s*, Y^v⟩_w / (‖grad s*‖_w ‖Y^v‖_w) between the decision flow's
gradient and perspective v's flow, on the contested pairs. Edges should be
restricted to contested pairs. Otherwise pairs on which every perspective
agrees inflate the gradient share, and [CLAIM-440](../claims.d/CLAIM-440.md)'s first consequence
fails: with no frustration there is nothing to prevail over.

**Can a binary conflict record be fed to it? Yes, but its cyclic share
is then not ambivalence.** The record's check: a perfectly transitive
binary record, the complete tournament in which the higher-ranked option
always wins, has cyclicity ratio (n − 2)/3n. That is 1/9 at n = 3, 1/6 at
n = 4 and 0.27 at n = 10, rising toward 1/3. It was computed for n = 3 to
10 and matches the formula at each. The least-squares ranking recovers the
order exactly. The residual is not intransitivity. It is the mismatch
between ±1 data and any gradient, since equal-magnitude wins cannot be
differences of scores. The authors flag the general point: their
inconsistency is cardinal and the impossibility theorems are ordinal
(p. 30), and curl-freeness has no ordinal counterpart (p. 24). So
"ambivalence = cyclic share" fails on binary data unless:

- the share is read against the transitive baseline for the same graph,
  or
- the ordinal index is used, circular triads, |curl| = 3 on a binary
  triangle (p. 12), counted among contested triples.

The second is the honest measure of ambivalence on a who-won record. The
first is a calibration of the cardinal share.

**What it assumes.** Three choices fix the share before any data are
seen:

- an aggregation into Ȳ, binary or score difference or log ratio, which
  give different rankings and ratios in the paper's own example (Table 1,
  p. 37);
- a weighting w, which by default counts how often a pair was contested,
  so frequently contested pairs dominate whatever their stakes; and
- the l2 inner product.

An intensity read from it is a property of the system plus these choices.
[CLAIM-436](../claims.d/CLAIM-436.md)'s warning that an ordering need not be a currency applies
here too. The measure turns the ordering into a currency by fiat.

**Gradient share as intensity.** On the decision flow, the gradient share
measures how coherent the contested decisions are, not how strongly a
given ranking overrides the others. A system whose decisions follow a
stable compromise among its perspectives scores as high as one in which a
single perspective dictates. Intensity in [TERM-076](../terms.d/TERM-076.md)'s sense, how much
of what the others would decide the prevailing ranking overrides, needs
the alignment term above, weighted by how far the overridden perspectives'
flows were from it. The gradient share alone cannot supply it.

**Local and global inconsistency.** On a complete comparison graph the
harmonic term "is always zero" (p. 35). A conflict record is sparse,
holding only the pairs that happened to be contested. Harmonic
inconsistency there may be an artefact of which pairs were contested
rather than a fact about the system. Curl, which is local, is the more
robust indicator. The paper's food example (p. 4), coarse consensus with
local cycles, is a natural picture of split ascendancy within a group of
nearby options.

**A lexicographic override.** On a binary record, a lexicographic override
and a compensatory trade-off that rank the options faced in the same order
produce the *same* transitive tournament and the same shares. The
distinction [CLAIM-440](../claims.d/CLAIM-440.md)'s fifth consequence and [CLAIM-435](../claims.d/CLAIM-435.md) rely on
is invisible to a who-won record. Telling them apart needs magnitudes,
such as how much of one dimension was traded for another, which is the
cardinal data this method is built for. A threshold override with a
discrimination threshold, a lexicographic semiorder ([CLAIM-436](../claims.d/CLAIM-436.md) part
3b), does show up. Its choices cycle, and the cycle appears as circular
triads or a non-zero curl on the relevant triangles.

**Persistence and resumption.** HodgeRank is static. Applied over
successive windows, the gradient share and the alignment term become time
series, which is where [QUESTION-268](../questions.d/QUESTION-268.md)'s attractor-strength and
hysteresis tools would act. The paper does none of this.

**An anomaly in the arXiv text.** Theorem 5.1 makes R* orthogonal to the
gradient part under the same weighted inner product, so ‖R*‖² ≤ ‖Ȳ‖² and
C_p ≤ 1. Yet Table 1 (p. 37) reports a cyclicity ratio of 1.15 for the
score-ratio flow. Either the table uses a different normalization or it
has an error. The published version was not checked. The record should
not take the paper's C_p values as calibration without recomputing them.

## Limitations

The l2 choice is a convention, and §6 shows that l1 alternatives change
the solution. Harmonic components are argued to be rare by analogy with
Erdős–Rényi graphs, which the authors do not defend as models of real
comparison graphs. Ties are excluded from the binary case "to keep the
discussion simple" (§7).

## Open questions

Whether an ordinal decomposition exists with the gradient/cyclic
geometry, one invariant to monotone transformation of the data. If not,
whether the circular-triad count, normalized by the number of contested
triples, is the best ordinal index of ambivalence the record can have.
