# NOTE-145: Douglas — Inductive risk and values in science

## Contribution

Epistemic values are widely accepted in scientific reasoning, but non-epistemic values have mostly been confined to science's "external" parts: which hypotheses to pursue, ethical limits on methods, and how results are used. Douglas argues that inductive risk, the risk of error, makes non-epistemic values necessary wherever the non-epistemic consequences of error ought to be considered. Using dioxin studies, she shows how such consequences can and should enter the internal stages of science: choice of methodology, characterization of data, and interpretation of results.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1 targets the normative "value-free" ideal (free of non-epistemic values) for large areas of science. §2 recovers the 1948–65 inductive-risk literature (Churchman, Rudner, Hempel, Jeffrey).
- §3 (The Structure of Values in Science) follows Longino in placing uncontroversial value roles in problem selection, in the use of knowledge, and in ethical limits on methods. Douglas extends Hempel's inductive risk from hypothesis acceptance to the whole process.
- §4: the conventional choice of a statistical-significance level trades false positives against false negatives, and in dioxin toxicology that trade-off has non-epistemic consequences.
- §5 uses the ambiguous rat-liver-tumour slides from the TCDD bioassay (Brown 1991 re-reading) to show that evidence characterization carries inductive risk. §6: whether the data show a carcinogenic threshold depends on which evidence one emphasizes.
- §7: values play an indirect role, through consequences of error, not a direct one. The role vanishes when uncertainty is negligible or errors have no outside consequences (atom-beam coherence research), leaving a grey area in between. "I want X to be true, therefore X is true" remains a bad argument.

## Open questions

- The canonical source for the inductive-risk argument. It applies directly to ML evaluation choices (thresholds, significance levels, labelling ambiguous data, what counts as a capability or a harm) wherever errors have social consequences.
- Check how Douglas separates the legitimate indirect role of values from an illegitimate direct role; her later book (Science, Policy, and the Value-Free Ideal, 2009) develops this.
- Check responses (e.g. Betz; Steel) arguing that uncertainty reporting can avoid value-laden acceptance decisions.
