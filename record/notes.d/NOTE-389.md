# NOTE-389: Ritchie — Social Structures and the Ontology of Social Groups

## Contribution

It puts two kinds of social group into one ontology without collapsing them.
Teams, courts and committees (organized social groups) and races, genders and
classes (feature social groups) are both defined through social structures,
but the first are wholes structured from inside and the second are positions
in structures that relate them to other positions. It supplies a definition of
"social structure" (constitutive dependence on social factors) and three
distinctions among structures, and shows how the framework can keep arbitrary
collections of people from counting as groups.

## Key insight

"Group" names two different things. A baseball team is a concrete whole: its
members fill roles defined by relations among themselves. "Women" is not a
whole of that kind; it is a place in a gender structure defined by its
relations to other places (subordination to "men"), with requirements on who
can occupy it. Both are social because the structures are, and the
difference between them is internal versus external structure.

## Assumptions

- **Structures as in structuralism.** Complexes of relations that make nodes
  available, with possibly null further requirements on occupiers (pp.
  405–406, after Koslicki). Structures are treated as universals that exist
  only when instantiated, for convenience (n. 9).
- **"Social" is not defined.** It is characterised by examples (marriage,
  money, recessions, driving on the right, the popularity of athleisure), and a
  non-circular definition is said to be unlikely (p. 404, n. 5).
- **Constitutive dependence, disjunctive (p. 407).** S constitutively depends on
  social factors iff (i) defining S requires reference to social factors, or
  (ii) social factors are metaphysically necessary for S, or (iii) social
  factors ground S's existence. Merely causal dependence (lactose tolerance and
  dairy farming) is not enough (p. 407).
- **The Goldilocks Constraint (p. 403)** is a desideratum, motivated partly by
  common sense (n. 3); it is not an argument against universalism about
  arbitrary sets or sums of people (p. 403).
- **A restriction (p. 410).** Only social structures that "(a) intuitively
  exist or (b) figure in causal or other explanations" are in scope. This, with
  the conditions on node occupiers, is what keeps arbitrary collections out.
- **Views of gender are not argued for.** Haslanger's, MacKinnon's and Ásta's
  are used as illustrations (pp. 409, 416–417).

## Key results

- **Social structure (p. 407).** "A social structure is a structure that is
  constitutively dependent on social factors."
- **Three distinctions (pp. 408–410).** Overt or covert, from Griffiths: whether
  the dependence on the social is or could be openly acknowledged (the three
  branches of US government are overt, gender covert). Intentionally or
  unintentionally created, and intentionally or unintentionally instantiated,
  which can come apart (an experimenter instantiating an unplanned class
  structure). Internal or external relative to a realization: internal if every
  node is occupied by elements of the system and every element occupies a node;
  external if the system's elements occupy only some nodes. The American League
  structure is internal to the league and external to each team (p. 410).
- **Organized social groups (pp. 410–414).** Structured wholes, from Ritchie 2013.
  Membership: some things X are the members of a group with structure S at t
  and w iff together X occupy the nodes of S, that is, are functionally related
  "or at least normatively bound" as S requires (p. 411). "Occupy the nodes" is
  collective, so two groups can share a structure (n. 22, an amendment to the
  2013 definition). Identity: G1 = G2 only if their structures co-vary across
  times and worlds and the same things occupy the same nodes (p. 412), a
  necessary condition only; persistence is not given. Coincident clubs with
  the same members are distinct (p. 412). Conditions: (i) the structure is
  social; (ii) node occupiers are people, social creatures or structured groups
  of them (p. 413); and often (iii) overt and (iv) intentionally created and
  instantiated (p. 414). The Supreme Court meets all four; a family meets (i)
  and (ii) with a structure that can be covert and unintended (pp. 412–413).
  Excluded as organised groups: people in an X shape, the moons of Jupiter,
  amoebas in a dish, women, African Americans (p. 414).
- **Groups within groups (n. 24).** Organised groups can be members of organised
  groups: Congress with the House and Senate as members.
- **Not every social entity is a group (p. 411).** Major League Baseball has
  corporate entities, stadiums and bank accounts as node occupiers, and a game
  includes a field and a ball; these are other social entities a general
  structuralism would treat with other restrictions.
- **Feature social groups (pp. 414–420).** Groups whose members share a feature
  or cluster of features, broadly construed (being judged to have ancestry,
  self-identifying, being bound by common norms) (p. 415). They are social kinds
  (p. 415), and on the proposal they are nodes in social structures (p. 416).
  Haslanger's and MacKinnon's accounts of gender read as identifying *women*
  with a node related to *men* by dominance and subordination, with the bodily
  marker as a further requirement (pp. 409, 416–417); Ásta's conferralism reads
  as a node whose occupation requires a conferred status (p. 417).
  Conditions: (i) defined at least in part by social factors; (ii) occupiers
  restricted to people or social creatures; (iii) multiple occupiers allowed
  (p. 419), which excludes single roles such as the US President.
- **Intersectional groups (pp. 417–419).** Not by joining a gender structure to a
  race structure, which would make Asian women people in one group related to
  people in another, but by distinct, more complex structures (class; class and
  race; class, race and gender), none a proper part of another.
- **Covert, unintended structures and oppression (pp. 419–420).** Feature groups
  that are nodes in covert, mainly unintended structures may be the ones
  involved in widespread oppression. Offered as a possibility ("it might be").
- **Goldilocks met (p. 420).** There is no actual social structure with a node
  for people whose left ear is larger than their right, or for people who have
  visited Boise.
- **Extensions (p. 421).** Artefacts as wholes with overt intentional structures,
  money and property as nodes, individual roles as kind-level nodes. Gestures
  only.

## Concepts

- **Social structure** — a structure constitutively dependent on social factors
  (p. 407).
- **Overt / covert** — whether a structure's social dependence is or would on
  reflection be acknowledged (p. 408).
- **Internal / external structure** — relative to a realization: whether the
  system's elements occupy all the nodes and only nodes, or only some nodes
  (pp. 409–410).
- **Organized social group** — a structured whole realizing an internal social
  structure with people or social creatures as node occupiers (pp. 410–414).
- **Feature social group** — a social kind that is a node in a social
  structure, defined partly by social factors, occupied by people or social
  creatures, allowing many occupiers (p. 419).
- **Goldilocks Constraint** — include common-sense and explanatory social groups,
  do not overgenerate (p. 403).

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md): no change.** The structures here are social roles and power
  relations, not causal or informational organisation, and the paper offers no
  criterion for agency, awareness or experience.
- **For the group-agency strand: a scoping result.** The distinction says which
  groups the agency and consciousness questions are about (organised ones), and
  that the entity Schwitzgebel asks about is not a group in this sense. It does
  not say whether any organised group is an agent or a mind.
- **For the social-construction strand.** It is the held bridge between
  Haslanger's and Ásta's accounts of social categories and the metaphysics of
  groups.
- No ML instruction. Nothing belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- It is a framework to "motivate", not a demonstration; the author says more
  work is needed to show it is the most viable model (pp. 414–415).
- The Goldilocks Constraint is met partly by the scoping restriction of p. 410,
  which is not derived from structuralism; any ontology could adopt it.
- Constitutive dependence is a disjunction of three different relations
  (definitional, modal, grounding), so "social structure" may cover structures
  that differ in how they depend on the social.
- Persistence of organised groups is left open (p. 412).
- Feature groups are shown to be compatible with three views of gender; the
  paper does not show that every feature group is a node.

## Open questions

- What are the persistence conditions of organized social groups through change
  of structure?
- Are feature social groups properties or classes? The paper allows both
  (p. 420).
- Can a structure be both internal to an organised group and external to a
  feature group, as with an organisation of the members of a feature group, and
  what follows for collective action by such groups?
