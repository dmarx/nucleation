# THEORY-1478: In a global review of 514 conservation translocations of terrestrial vertebrates, practitioner-judged success was similar for captive-bred and wild-sourced stock and for soft and hard release, but population growth after release was negative on average for captive-bred stock and lower for soft than for hard release, and success rose with numbers released up to about 20–50 individuals

## Source

Morris, Brook, Moseby & Johnson (2021), [LIT-2163](../literature.d/LIT-2163.md); read in
[NOTE-1657](../notes.d/NOTE-1657.md), from the preprint "Lessons from a century of conservation
translocations" (EcoEvoRxiv, 2021-02-12).

## What was actually shown

- **Source** (3.4): wild animals "did not result in higher success rates
  (wild 70.2%, captive 76.0%)"; growth "wild +2.2, sd 5.7; captive -2.8,
  sd 12.2". Captive sourcing ranks first or second among predictors of
  growth (Table 3).
- **Release method** (3.4): "soft 79.8%, hard 75.0%" on reported success;
  "hard +5.6, sd 9.8; soft -1.3, sd 6.9" on growth.
- **Numbers released** (3.1): "success increased up to about 20-50
  individuals and declined slightly above a few hundred individuals
  (although the evidence of this decline is weak because of few
  datapoints)".
- **Samples** (2.4, 2.5): reported success on 257 translocations, 148
  entries per one-per-species subsample; growth on 87, 40 per subsample.
- The design could have come out otherwise: the growth measure could have
  agreed with reported success on source and release method.

## What this does not say

- That captive breeding or soft release causes lower growth: neither was
  randomized, and both may be confounded with species, region and threat.
- Why the measures disagree: leniency in success criteria is the authors'
  conjecture.
- That 20–50 is an optimal number to release; the authors say it is not.
- Anything about the published version's figures, the supplement, or the
  coefficients' sizes, none of which was read.
- Anything about predator training, feeding after release, habituation or
  the withdrawal of support.
