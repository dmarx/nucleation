# CLAIM-tmp1ugth: Ordinary and legal attributions of agency to an artifact cannot serve as the independent marks that would defeat the record's agency test, because they turn on whether its mechanism is understood: a known mechanism invites "This is all just programmed!", an inscrutable one invites fear, whatever the description lengths

## The objection

[CLAIM-174](CLAIM-174.md) makes a factor an agent at a level when it is one system
there, when a goal-directed description beats a device description by
more than the Occam cost, and when its policy would adapt if its
actions' consequences changed. Its `defeated_if` names two kinds of
counterexample. One is a factor that passes the tests and is
nonetheless, "by independent marks, not an agent there".

Boyle states, and returns to, what he calls the inscrutability paradox:

> If something that looks like General AI emerges from transparent,
> well-understood programming and technology, that may lead us to doubt
> that it could have autonomous consciousness. The machine is merely
> doing or saying that which we have programmed it to do! (p. 30)

> If we know how you were designed, if we understand how you "think"
> because we programmed you to do so, how can we see you as anything but
> a mannequin or a wind-up doll rather than an entity with free will?
> (p. 54)

He also states the other horn. An inscrutable system meets "fear and
incomprehension" (p. 54), and "our fears may overshadow our respect"
(p. 272). In the Conclusion he calls the paradox "the central
catch-22" (p. 246).

If he is right, then for artifacts the marks a reader would reach for,
what people and courts will grant, are not independent of the test. They
track what is known about how the thing works. A system that passes
[CLAIM-174](CLAIM-174.md)'s three tests and whose mechanism is published will meet
"This is all just programmed!" (p. 246). That verdict would look like the first kind of
counterexample, but it is evidence about the judges, not about the
system. So the defeater needs marks that are not mechanism-sensitive,
and for artifacts it is not clear what they would be.

## What would tell them apart

[CLAIM-174](CLAIM-174.md) can also lose an agency verdict when a mechanism becomes known,
but only where knowing it makes a device description shorter than the
goal-directed one. A chess engine whose code is public still has a
shorter goal-directed description (it plays to win) than any account
of its search. [CLAIM-174](CLAIM-174.md) keeps it an agent. The paradox predicts that
many people would not.

So the two separate on systems whose mechanism is known and whose
behaviour is still best compressed as goal-directed. A study that held
behaviour fixed and varied only what subjects were told about the
mechanism would test the paradox. [CLAIM-174](CLAIM-174.md) predicts no change in such a
study unless the information changes which description is shorter.

## What it does not say

- It does not say [CLAIM-174](CLAIM-174.md)'s test is wrong, or that agency is in the eye
  of the beholder. It is about evidence for defeating the claim, not the
  claim's truth.
- Boyle's paradox is about consciousness, free will and personhood, not
  agency as such. It reaches [CLAIM-174](CLAIM-174.md) most directly through its
  willed-agent clause ([CLAIM-155](CLAIM-155.md)). Its extension to agency in the thin
  sense is this record's step, not Boyle's.
- Boyle offers the paradox as a forecast with examples from fiction, the
  Chinese Room debate and current chatbots. He reports no study. The
  warrant is interpretive.
- It leaves [CLAIM-174](CLAIM-174.md)'s second kind of defeater untouched: a clear agent,
  such as a person, that fails one of the tests.
