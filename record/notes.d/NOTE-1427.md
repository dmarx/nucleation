# NOTE-1427: Apt — Potential Games (slides)

## Contribution

Teaching slides on potential games for a course on strategic games. They
give the definitions after Monderer and Shapley, the theorem linking
generalized ordinal potentials with the finite improvement property,
Rosenthal's theorem that congestion games are potential games, worked
traffic examples, the Braess paradox, and bounds on the price of stability.
Nothing in them is new; their value here is a clear second statement of
the definitions and the theorem [THEORY-896](../theory.d/THEORY-896.md) attributes to Monderer and
Shapley.

## Key insight

A potential is a single function on joint strategies that every player's
improving move climbs. Asking only that it climb, not that it track the
size or even the exact sign pattern of payoff changes, gives exactly the
games in which improvement cannot go round in circles.

## Assumptions

- **A game** G = (S₁, …, S_n, p₁, …, p_n) with payoff functions p_i on
  S = S₁ × ⋯ × S_n (slide 3). Cost functions are payoffs negated
  (slide 16).
- **Finite** strategy sets for the theorem (slide 15) and for the
  termination of best-response dynamics (slide 9).

## Key results

- **Best-response dynamics (slides 3–5).** Repeatedly let a player not
  playing a best response switch to one. With strictly dominant
  strategies it ends within n steps. It can fail to find an equilibrium:
  a matching-pennies game with a third strategy E has the equilibrium
  (E, E), which the dynamics can miss.
- **Exact potential (slide 6), "Monderer and Shapley '96".**
  P : S → ℝ with p_i(s_i, s_−i) − p_i(s′_i, s_−i) = P(s_i, s_−i) −
  P(s′_i, s_−i) for all i, s_−i, s_i, s′_i. The prisoner's dilemma has one
  that counts the defectors (slides 7–8).
- **Termination (slide 9).** "For finite potential games all best
  response dynamics terminate", since the potential strictly increases
  along each best-response path.
- **Ordinal potential (slide 10).** p_i(s_i, s_−i) − p_i(s′_i, s_−i) > 0
  iff P(s_i, s_−i) − P(s′_i, s_−i) > 0. A modified prisoner's dilemma has
  an ordinal potential and no exact one (slide 11).
- **FIP (slide 12).** s′_i is a better response given s if
  p_i(s′_i, s_−i) > p_i(s_i, s_−i). A path is a sequence of joint
  strategies each differing from the last in one player's strategy; it is
  an improvement path "if it is maximal and for all k > 1,
  p_i(s^{k+1}) > p_i(s^k), where i deviated from s^k". "G has the finite
  improvement property (FIP), if every improvement path is finite." "If G
  has the FIP, then it has a Nash equilibrium."
- **FIP without an ordinal potential (slide 13).** The game with rows T,
  B, columns L, R and payoffs (T, L) = (1, 0), (T, R) = (2, 0),
  (B, L) = (2, 0), (B, R) = (0, 1) has the FIP. An ordinal potential would
  need P(T, L) < P(B, L) < P(B, R) < P(T, R), "but p₂(T, L) = p₂(T, R), so
  P(T, L) = P(T, R)".
- **Generalized ordinal potential (slide 14).** p_i(s_i, s_−i) −
  p_i(s′_i, s_−i) > 0 implies P(s_i, s_−i) − P(s′_i, s_−i) > 0. The same
  game has one: P(T, L) = 0, P(T, R) = 3, P(B, L) = 1, P(B, R) = 2. (The
  potential table is labelled C, D, carried over from the prisoner's
  dilemma slides; checked, each strict improvement raises it.)
- **The theorem (slide 15).** "Theorem (Monderer and Shapley '96) Every
  finite game has a generalized ordinal potential iff it has the FIP."
  *(⇒)* "The generalized ordinal potential increases along every
  improvement path." *(⇐, sketch)* "An improvement sequence: a prefix of
  an improvement path. Assign to each joint strategy s the number of
  improvement sequences that terminate in it. Because the game has the
  FIP this number is finite. This defines a generalized ordinal
  potential."
- **Congestion games (slides 17–26).** Rosenthal (1973): every congestion
  game is a potential game, with P(s) = Σ_j Σ_{k=1}^{x_j(s)} d_j(k), so
  every congestion game has a Nash equilibrium. Fair cost sharing is the
  case d_j(k) = c_j/k (slide 27).
- **Braess paradox and price of stability (slides 34–47).** Adding a
  zero-cost link raises everyone's travel time from 65 to 80; price of
  stability at most 4/3 for linear delays (Roughgarden and Tardos) and
  at most H(n) for fair cost sharing (Anshelevich et al.), both stated.

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | A finite game has a generalized ordinal potential iff it has the FIP | moderate as presented | slide 15: (⇒) proved in a line; (⇐) sketched; credited to Monderer and Shapley 1996 without lemma number or page |
| C2 | A game can have the FIP and no ordinal potential | strong | slide 13, a checked two-by-two counterexample |
| C3 | In a finite potential game every best-response path terminates, so a pure equilibrium exists | strong | slide 9, potential strictly rises |
| C4 | Every congestion game is an (exact) potential game | moderate | slide 26, Rosenthal's potential stated; general proof referred to Tijs's notes |
| C5 | Price of stability ≤ 4/3 for linear congestion games and ≤ H(n) for fair cost sharing | weak here | stated, slides 43 and 47, with citations |

## Concepts

- **Better response** — a strictly payoff-improving change of one's own
  strategy (slide 12).
- **Improvement path** — a maximal path of better responses; the FIP says
  every one is finite.
- **Improvement sequence** — a prefix of an improvement path (slide 15).
- **Exact, ordinal and generalized ordinal potential** — as above; each
  is weaker than the one before.

## Connections

Monderer and Shapley (1996, [LIT-1462](../literature.d/LIT-1462.md)) is the source the slides cite and the
record cannot read. Candogan et al. ([LIT-1442](../literature.d/LIT-1442.md)) restate the exact
definition. [THEORY-896](../theory.d/THEORY-896.md) states the same equivalence and proves it in the
record with a different potential, the length of the longest improvement
path ending at a profile, where the slides count the improvement
sequences ending there. Both are finite for the same reason: with the FIP
there is no cycle, and a finite game has finitely many profiles, so every
improvement sequence has fewer than |S| steps.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-896](../theory.d/THEORY-896.md): partly.** Its promote_when asks for Monderer and Shapley
  read first-hand: the lemma's wording, number and page, the paper's
  definitions of the FIP and of generalized ordinal potential, and
  agreement with the record's proof. These slides do not meet that: they
  are not the paper, and they give no lemma number or page. What they do
  give:
  - **The class.** [THEORY-896](../theory.d/THEORY-896.md) notes that its two restatements disagree on
    whether the equivalence is with generalized ordinal or with ordinal
    potentials. Apt states it for generalized ordinal potentials, as
    Duersch, Oechssler and Schipper do, and slide 13 shows a finite game
    with the FIP and no ordinal potential, so an equivalence with ordinal
    potentials is false. Biggar and Shames's weaker sentence, that ordinal
    potential games have the FIP, is consistent with this.
  - **The definitions** of the FIP and of generalized ordinal potential
    agree with [THEORY-896](../theory.d/THEORY-896.md)'s, except that Apt's improvement paths are
    maximal; finiteness of every path is the same condition either way.
  - **The proof.** Apt's sketch and the record's proof differ only in the
    potential chosen, and both work.
  So the mathematics and the class are corroborated by a second source;
  the attribution's exact form still waits on [LIT-1462](../literature.d/LIT-1462.md).

## Limitations

- **Second-hand attribution.** "Monderer and Shapley '96" with no lemma,
  page or wording.
- **The converse is a sketch.** "Because the game has the FIP this number
  is finite" needs the finiteness of the game as well: without cycles and
  with finitely many profiles each sequence is bounded in length. That a
  step s → t raises the count is left to the reader (every sequence ending
  at s extends to one ending at t, and t alone is one more).
- **Slide slips.** The generalized ordinal potential table on slide 14 is
  labelled C, D for a game with rows T, B and columns L, R; slide 44 writes
  the harmonic number as Σ 1/n for Σ 1/i.
