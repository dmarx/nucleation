# CLAIM-tmpd6uxt: One party can wrongfully bring about circumstances in which another incurs a genuine obligation, so that the second does right in discharging it and is still wronged, and the manipulation works through the target's sound moral reasoning rather than a defect in it; the obligation so created is no larger than the circumstances support, whatever the manipulator claims

## The claim

The exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afa2a8d-2598-83ea-8d1a-d9ceb992a99c). The
owner, at U7: "double click on normative manipulation". A13 opens:

> The most striking thing about **normative manipulation** is that it can
> work *because the target's moral reasoning is sound*, not because it's
> defective.

A13 §1's case. Bob, who takes animal welfare seriously, declines to look
after Alice's dog; Alice leaves town anyway, "abandoning the dog at Bob's
house without arranging another caretaker". Bob "may genuinely have a
moral obligation to care for the animal", because "Alice has deliberately
created a situation in which fulfilling his moral duties requires doing
what Alice wants". A13 attributes the structure to Keller (2016), who
"argues that someone can wrongfully place another person under a real
moral obligation and exploit that obligation for their own ends", and
draws it out:

> *Bob ought to care for the dog. But Alice ought not to have made caring
> for the dog Bob's responsibility.*

"In other words, **an obligation can be genuine while the circumstances
that produced it are unjust**." Bob has done right and "has a legitimate
complaint against Alice".

The limit, A13: "**An exploitative attempt to create an obligation does
not establish that the obligation is unlimited—or even that it exists in
the form the manipulator claims.**" Bob may be permitted to call a
shelter; appeals to identity ("*A conscientious doctor wouldn't walk
away.*") try "to transform a broad moral value into an absolute obligation
to perform some specific act", and "The inference is frequently invalid."

## Where it sits

- [CLAIM-404](CLAIM-404.md) (Active): an organization can be governed from
  outside through the evidence its procedures receive or the criteria by
  which they weigh it. Normative manipulation in A13's sense is neither:
  Bob's evidence is accurate and his criteria are his own and sound. What
  the manipulator sets is the situation those criteria are applied to. A11
  and A13 call the evidence route "epistemic" manipulation; this is a third
  route, through circumstances.
- [THEORY-235](../theory.d/THEORY-235.md), the record's reading of covert
  influence, places the harm of manipulation in making the target "opaque
  to herself". Here the target can see the whole manoeuvre and still be
  bound, so whatever wrong is done is not that one. The claim does not deny
  [THEORY-235](../theory.d/THEORY-235.md), which is about covert influence; it marks a case the reading
  does not reach.
- The same exchange's [CLAIM-tmpnw7ez](CLAIM-tmpnw7ez.md) (responsibility for the conditions of
  another's conduct): there the structured party does wrong, here it does
  right. Both say the structuring party answers for the conditions.

## What it does not say

- **That Bob may refuse.** The dog's welfare still matters: "Alice's
  wrongdoing doesn't magically erase Bob's reasons to prevent suffering."
- **What Keller says.** The paper ([LIT-tmp55sg8](../literature.d/LIT-tmp55sg8.md)) is registered and unread; the
  attribution and the case's closeness to Keller's own are A's report.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Anderson, read

The SEP entry on coercion reports White (2017): in making conditional
threats, coercers "make the coercee responsible for the coercer’s execution
of their threat, a responsibility that in many cases they should not have
to bear". This holds even when the threatened act is not itself wrong, as
in "emotional blackmail" and threats of self-harm, where "forcing another to
regard this outcome as their responsibility to prevent generates an unfair
burden" (§3.1). That is close to this claim's wrongfully created
obligation. But White treats the burden as unfair to impose, and the entry
does not say whether the responsibility so created is genuine, which is the
claim's distinctive thesis. Second-hand; White not read. Relation:
bears_on is not available, since this is a claim. Note only, no relation.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tessman, read

Tessman's response to critics (LIT-tmpq5p0l), read as a stand-in for Moral Failure, gives the claim an
argument independent of Keller's, from Karnein (2014): another's wrongdoing can leave an agent
genuinely bound to an innocent third party. In her coercive-household case, A "is wronging B in some
very serious ways that go beyond simply being unfair", yet "A’s noncompliance functions like luck for
the purpose of the relationship between B and C" (p. 14), and "A owes all kinds of reparations to B".
Her name for it: "There are moral requirements that it is unfair for us to be bound by, but that
nevertheless are binding on us" (p. 16). That supports the claim's first two clauses.

It also qualifies them. "It is crucial to the case ... that the wrongdoer, A, is not the same person
as the dependent, C" (p. 15); where the dependent is the wrongdoer, "Part of the wrongdoer’s being
accountable for the wrongdoing may mean forfeiting the care of the person whom he has wronged". So the
claim holds where the obligation runs to an innocent third party (Keller's dog, his teachers' students)
and may fail where the manipulator is also the one owed (Keller's patient who misses his bus on
purpose). Tessman also says the bound party need not, all things considered, meet the obligation
(p. 15); she may rightly protect herself at the price of unavoidable failure. Nothing here bears on
the claim's limit, "no larger than the circumstances support". Relation: corroborates (THEORY-tmp3poko),
with the two-party exception recorded.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Keller, read

The paper the claim attributes the structure to (LIT-tmp55sg8) was read in the author's pre-print.
It supports the claim's first two clauses in Keller's words: "you can have a genuine moral obligation
because you have been treated wrongly" (p. 21), and the victim's complaint, "I see that it is my moral
obligation to perform this act, but it is morally wrong that I have this moral obligation", is
"perfectly legitimate" (p. 26). It supports the claim's point against THEORY-235 directly: "It may
even be common knowledge between us that I have deliberately manipulated your circumstances. But for
all that, my manipulations may succeed" (p. 23). A13's Alice and Bob is Keller's own opening case
(pp. 1-2), not one "remarkably close" to it.

Two qualifications. First, Keller locates the mechanism in "the victim’s moral sensibility" (p. 22),
and what matters is that the victim believes the alternatives unacceptable and is motivated to avoid
them (p. 21); he does not say it works through sound reasoning rather than a defect, and his account
covers a victim whose belief is mistaken. Second, the claim's last clause ("no larger than the
circumstances support, whatever the manipulator claims") is A13's, not Keller's; Keller does not
discuss limits on the obligation created. The claim's summary attributes the structure to Keller,
which is right; the limit should not be read as his. Relation: corroborates (THEORY-tmp5b8ou).
