# LIT-1160: Observed universality of phase transitions in high-dimensional geometry, with implications for modern data analysis and signal processing

David L. Donoho and Jared Tanner (2009), "Observed universality of phase transitions in high-dimensional geometry, with implications for modern data analysis and signal processing", *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A* 367(1906): 4273–4293 — [ARXIV-0906.2530](https://arxiv.org/abs/0906.2530), [doi:10.1098/rsta.2009.0152](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2009.0152)

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-933](../notes.d/NOTE-933.md).*

- **The Gaussian theorem, restated (Theorem 2.1).** For A n × N with iid
  N(0, 1) entries, n = δN, k = ρn and N → ∞, the expected fraction of
  k-faces of the simplex T or cross-polytope C that survive projection by
  A tends to 1 for ρ < ρ(δ; Q) and to 0 for ρ > ρ(δ; Q). By Lemmas 2.1–2.2
  that fraction is the fraction of k-sparse vectors recovered by (LP)
  (nonnegative) or (P1) (ℓ1 minimization). It was proved in the authors'
  earlier papers; this one gives no formula for ρ(δ; Q). The figures print
  ρ(δ; C) values, e.g. 0.189 at δ = 0.1, 0.3848 at 0.5, 0.6773 at 0.9.
- **Two curves, not one.** ρ(δ; T) > ρ(δ; C) for δ ∈ (0, 1). The paper
  checks both, with 8 non-Gaussian ensembles each: Bernoulli, partial DCT,
  three ternary densities, partial Hadamard, Expander and Rademacher.
- **Strict universality rejected, weak universality kept.** Over 16,984
  settings and 2,948,000 linear programs at N = 200, 400 and 1600, the
  two-sample Z-scores against the Gaussian are mostly within ±2, but their
  means trend significantly with δ. The trend terms decay as N^(-1/2)
  (γ = 1/2 gives the best R², 0.976 and 0.982, Table 8), and terms that do
  not decay add nothing (F = 0.7467, p = 0.7475, §5.5).
- **Finite-N width.** For the Gaussian, success falls as
  Φ̄((ρ − ρ(δ; Q))/w) with √N · w between 0.62 and 0.73 (Table 5).
- **Sparse matrices are late joiners.** Ternary (1/10), Expander and
  Hadamard have their 50% point about one transition width below the
  curve at N = 200 to 256, because sparse matrices at small n have sparse
  null vectors (an all-zero column, null vectors with 6 nonzeros). At
  N = 1600 the effect is gone (§5.7).
- **Decoder-specific.** Other recovery algorithms show other transitions
  (§3.7). Cyclic polytopes do better than random matrices, and the
  all-ones matrix is a trivial counterexample.
- **Status of the claim.** Observed, not proved. The authors pose the
  open problem of characterizing the ensembles that share the Gaussian
  transition, and they note that Adamczak et al. (2009) proved a success
  region for some ensembles without matching the Gaussian one.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-14 for [QUESTION-120](../questions.d/QUESTION-120.md) and read the same day in
[NOTE-933](../notes.d/NOTE-933.md). Toy Models of Superposition asks whether its phase changes
relate to the Donoho–Tanner transition ([NOTE-278](../notes.d/NOTE-278.md)), and the record could not
follow the question without the transition itself. The question says
Donoho and Tanner "observe that the sparse-recovery transition does not
depend on the ensemble". The reading corrects that: they observe it for
eight ensembles and only as N grows. At finite N the transition does
depend on the ensemble, and for sparse matrices by a full transition
width at small N.

For the question the reading gives three things. The Donoho–Tanner
threshold is an exact limiting location from face counting, not a
log-family-size bound from a union bound, so it does not fit the
question's proposed shape. A comparison with the toy model must use
δ = m/n and ρ = k/m and reckon with the paper's statement that the curve
belongs to the ℓ1 decoder. And the sparse ensembles closest to the fly's
and SDM's maps were the ones that failed at small size. The
`mathematical-statistics` tag is for its reading of the transition as a
hard limit on what can be inferred from undersampled data, which the
abstract states.

Filed as [THEORY-391](../theory.d/THEORY-391.md), Proposed, because the universality it reports is observed at three problem sizes.
