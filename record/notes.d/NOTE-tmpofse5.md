# NOTE-tmpofse5: Feldman and Pentland — Reconceptualizing organizational routines

## Contribution

It keeps the standard definition of a routine, "a repetitive,
recognizable pattern of interdependent actions, involving multiple
actors", and argues that the definition itself forces a two-part
ontology. The habit, program and gene metaphors that dominated the
literature treat a routine as a fixed object and so cannot explain the
variation and change that field studies keep finding. Splitting the
routine into ostensive and performative aspects, recursively related,
gives change a source inside the routine rather than only in external
shocks.

## Key insight

A routine is never only its script. Borrowing Latour's distinction for
power, "in principle" and "in practice", the ostensive aspect is "the
routine in principle" and the performative "the routine in practice".
No rule fixes its own application (Wittgenstein, via Blau), so every
performance improvises; performances then feed back into what the
routine is taken to be. Whoever can "anoint performances as legitimate"
can "turn exceptions into rules" (p. 110). Stability and change are two
outcomes of the same loop.

## Assumptions

- The routine involves several participants whose information, goals
  and interpretations differ, so the ostensive aspect is "not
  monolithic" and "distributed unevenly" (pp. 101, 104).
- Actions are interdependent, so each performance is collective and
  each participant's options depend on others' prior actions (pp.
  104–105).
- Structuration (Giddens) and practice theory (Bourdieu) supply the
  frame: structure is reproduced by action and constrains it.
- The unit of analysis is a routine within a given organization; the
  argument is not made for routines copied between organizations.

## Key results

The paper's results are conceptual.

- **Ostensive to performative (pp. 105–107).** Three uses: *guiding*
  (a template that never fixes the details), *accounting* (legitimating
  or delegitimating actions after the fact), *referring* (a gloss that
  lets people invoke "hiring" without knowing its details).
- **Performative to ostensive (pp. 107–109).** Performances *create*
  the routine (a pattern must repeat and be recognized as one),
  *maintain* it (an unperformed routine decays, like an old DOS manual
  or a medieval recipe) and *modify* it (variations may or may not be
  retained).
- **Endogenous change (pp. 112–114).** Campbell's variation and
  selective retention applied inside one routine: performances are the
  variations, interpretation into the ostensive aspect is the selection,
  and the story of "how we do hiring" is the retention. The authors
  disclaim adaptiveness: the claim is only that "a given routine,
  within a given organization, has the inherent, endogenous capacity to
  generate and retain novel patterns of action".
- **Against the genetic reading (p. 114).** "If the gene is changing
  itself, then the information it carries is not stable and unchanging,
  as required in ecological theory"; whether this matters for
  organizational ecology "is an empirical question".
- **Method (pp. 111–112).** Survey measures of task variety and observed
  action sequences gave opposite rankings in Pentland's bank study;
  the paper reads the first as measuring the ostensive and the second
  the performative.

## Concepts

- **Organizational routine**: "a repetitive, recognizable pattern of
  interdependent actions, involving multiple actors".
- **Ostensive aspect**: "the ideal or schematic form of a routine ...
  the routine in principle", including participants' divergent
  understandings and any artifacts (procedures, forms, past ads).
- **Performative aspect**: "specific actions, by specific people, in
  specific places and times ... the routine in practice".
- **Endogenous change**: change in a routine that results "from
  engagement in the routine itself".

## Connections

The paper sets itself against Nelson and Winter's routines-as-genes and
against Hannan and Freeman's structural inertia, and inside Giddens's
duality of structure. Marquis and Tilcsik ([LIT-1144](../literature.d/LIT-1144.md), [NOTE-tmpegckt](NOTE-tmpegckt.md))
review imprinting, the opposite emphasis: features fixed in a brief
sensitive period that persist. They list routines among the "building
blocks" that can be imprinted, which this paper would expect to drift
through performance unless performance keeps re-enacting the imprint.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-329](../claims.d/CLAIM-329.md).** A6's question for this paper was whether it separates
  "the continuity of a *particular organization* from reproduction of an
  *organizational kind*". It does not. Its whole analysis is of one
  routine in one organization, and its persistence story is
  maintenance: an ostensive aspect survives only while it is performed,
  and fades when it is not. That is a form of the claim's historical
  persistence: what carries the routine across a change of performers is
  the chain of performances and the shared understanding they keep
  alive, not similarity to a type. The paper's one move toward the
  kind/individual question is negative: it doubts routines can serve
  ecology as unchanging genealogical carriers between organizations
  (p. 114), and leaves the matter as empirical. So the literature A6
  hoped would already hold the claim's distinction does not hold it
  here; the claim's distinction remains the record's own.
- **[QUESTION-118](../questions.d/QUESTION-118.md).** The paper offers one candidate for "what must stay
  causally continuous": the ostensive aspect as maintained by ongoing
  performance and by those with power to say which performances count.
  It does not discuss total turnover of performers, so it does not test
  whether a routine survives when every participant changes.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- No new data; the hiring example is illustrative and hypothetical (the
  snowstorm videoconference).
- "Ostensive" is defined to include participants' subjective,
  divergent understandings and also artifacts, so it is not one
  observable; the paper gives no measure of it beyond the survey versus
  observation contrast.
- The claim that endogenous change happens is supported by citation;
  the paper does not say how often or how fast, or when a routine
  resists it.
- **Slips.** The text cites Emirbayer and Mische (1998) as "Emirbayers
  and Mische" throughout, including in the reference list, and spells
  Narduzzo "Naduzzo" twice and correctly once (pp. 95, 100, 102),
  as the OCR reads the page images.

## Open questions

- Does a routine survive a complete change of its performers, and if so
  what carries it: artifacts, overlapping tenure, or the people who
  certify performances?
- When the same routine appears in an unrelated organization, is that
  the routine persisting or a new one of the same kind? The paper's
  doubt about genealogical transmission suggests it would say new, but
  it does not say.
