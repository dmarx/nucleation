# THEORY-050: Psychological inflexibility, in which verbal relations give private events aversive functions and make experiential avoidance a general strategy, is a transdiagnostic process in psychopathology

## Source

- Hayes, Luoma, Bond, Masuda and Lillis (2006), [LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md), read in full in
  [NOTE-431](../notes.d/NOTE-431.md) from an early manuscript whose numbers differ from the
  published article.
- Kashdan and Rottenberg (2010), [LIT-553](../literature.d/LIT-553.md), read in full in [NOTE-436](../notes.d/NOTE-436.md).
- Barnes-Holmes, Barnes-Holmes, McHugh and Hayes (2004), [LIT-541](../literature.d/LIT-541.md), read in
  full in [NOTE-430](../notes.d/NOTE-430.md).

Hayes et al. 1996 ([LIT-551](../literature.d/LIT-551.md)), which defined experiential avoidance as a
functional dimension, the RFT book ([LIT-549](../literature.d/LIT-549.md)), and Macri and Rogge's 2024
meta-analysis ([LIT-543](../literature.d/LIT-543.md)) are filed unread. They are named here and carry
nothing.

## The claim in three parts

1. **Mechanism.** In humans, stimulus relations are learned in both
   directions and can be derived without being taught. Once a word or
   thought stands in such a relation to an event, it can take on that
   event's psychological functions ([NOTE-430](../notes.d/NOTE-430.md), C1–C3). A report of a shock
   can become aversive, and a child told that dentists are like doctors
   can fear a dentist she has never met. So private events (thoughts,
   feelings, memories) can be as aversive as the events they relate to.
2. **Process.** Avoiding those private events then becomes a general
   strategy. ACT calls this experiential avoidance, "the attempt to alter
   the form, frequency, or situational sensitivity of private events even
   when doing so causes behavioral harm". Cognitive fusion, "excessive or
   improper regulation of behavior by verbal processes", supports it, and
   contexts of literality, reason-giving and emotional control foster
   fusion. Avoidance makes the avoided events more salient, and behaviour
   narrows ([LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md), pp. 6–8).
3. **Transdiagnostic.** The same process appears across disorders. Kashdan
   and Rottenberg ([LIT-553](../literature.d/LIT-553.md)) find rigidity in depression (rumination,
   emotion context insensitivity, flattened physiological variability) and
   avoidance of arousal, strong emotion or exposure across panic,
   generalised anxiety and social anxiety ([NOTE-436](../notes.d/NOTE-436.md), C2).

## What was actually shown

- **Correlation.** The ACT manuscript's meta-analysis finds moderate to
  strong correlations between its process measure, the AAQ, and distress.
  The manuscript has 21 studies, with a misprinted point estimate likely
  meant to be .30. Kashdan and Rottenberg cite the published version as 32
  studies with r = .42. Only one two-wave panel addresses direction: the
  AAQ predicted later distress and job performance, and not the reverse
  ([NOTE-431](../notes.d/NOTE-431.md), C2–C3).
- **Components.** Small randomised analogue studies (N = 8 to 60) show
  that acceptance and defusion instructions change discomfort, the
  believability of negative self-referential thoughts, pain tolerance and
  avoidance under CO₂ challenge (C4).
- **Mediation.** Seven studies, mostly self-report, report the targeted
  processes as mediators of ACT's effects. The authors note that the
  mediators were often measured after outcomes had begun to improve (C5).
  By the standard of [THEORY-053](THEORY-053.md), that is the right kind of evidence
  with the order not established.
- **Mechanism.** The RFT mechanism rests on laboratory transformation-of-
  function studies the record has not read. The 2004 paper labels its
  clinical reading of ACT's processes "no more than a heuristic" (p. 369).

Kashdan and Rottenberg add two caveats. Whether inflexibility causes
psychopathology "is unknown" ([NOTE-436](../notes.d/NOTE-436.md), C4). And the global self-report
ACT relies on cannot capture a capacity that "unfold[s] over time" across
situations (C6). Both are reasons for Proposed.

## How it bears on mental causation and on language

- **It is an account in which thoughts are not causes by their content.**
  Functional contextualism, ACT's philosophy, limits causal analysis to
  manipulable events. "Thoughts and feelings do not cause other actions,
  except as regulated by context." Fusion is the name for the contexts
  in which they do ([LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md), p. 5). This is a third position beside the
  record's mental-causation holdings. Pernu ([LIT-147](../literature.d/LIT-147.md)) asks whether
  intentions cause behaviour as mental or as neural. Kendler
  ([THEORY-048](THEORY-048.md)) keeps psychological functions in the explanation.
  ACT relocates the cause to the person's history and context, which
  decide whether a thought's content governs action at all. What the
  record does not hold is any comparison of the three.
- **It is a claim about meaning as behaviour.** RFT treats derived
  relating as a learned operant ([NOTE-430](../notes.d/NOTE-430.md), C2), so the account is also a
  theory of how symbols acquire the functions of what they stand for. That
  is why `philosophy-of-language` is tagged. [ADR-021](../decisions.d/ADR-021.md) records that
  language-as-behaviour has no word of its own yet.
- **Literality is what self-model theory calls transparency.** ACT's
  "contexts of literality" treat a symbol "as one would their referents":
  the thought "life is hopeless" is responded to as a hopeless life.
  Metzinger ([LIT-450](../literature.d/LIT-450.md), [NOTE-372](../notes.d/NOTE-372.md)) calls a representation transparent when
  its being a representation is unavailable, so that it is experienced as
  the thing itself. He writes that "the phenomenology of transparency is
  the phenomenology of naïve realism". Fusion is then a thought that
  functions transparently, and defusion ("I am having the thought that I am
  no good") makes it opaque. Neither source cites the other, and the
  identification is the record's. It is developed for the self in
  [THEORY-044](THEORY-044.md).

## What this does not say

- **Not that inflexibility is the only process, or a latent cause.** It is
  compatible with [THEORY-058](THEORY-058.md). Avoidance that makes the avoided event
  more salient is a self-reinforcing loop, and on Borsboom's terms it would
  be a constituent of many symptom connections, not a disease behind them.
  The two conflict only if inflexibility is read as a cause that sustains
  symptoms whatever their connections. Neither source reads it that way,
  so no `rivals` is declared.
- **Not that it is the same construct as Salkovskis's "psychological
  flexibility".** [LIT-538](../literature.d/LIT-538.md) uses the phrase for meaning-making flexibility
  and cites none of the ACT literature. Whether the two are one construct
  is open ([NOTE-428](../notes.d/NOTE-428.md), [NOTE-437](../notes.d/NOTE-437.md)).
- **Not that ACT outperforms other treatments.** The ACT review does not
  claim it ([NOTE-431](../notes.d/NOTE-431.md), C7).
- **Not that emotion is the problem.** The account locates the trouble in
  how private events, emotions included, are related to and avoided, not in
  having them. Gross's process model of regulation ([LIT-554](../literature.d/LIT-554.md)) intervenes
  elsewhere, on how an emotion is generated. This account does not take a
  side on emotion theory.
