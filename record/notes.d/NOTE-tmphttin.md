# NOTE-tmphttin: Social Identity and Close Relationships: What is the Connection?

## Contribution

A theoretical chapter that sets close-relationship research and social
identity research against each other. Brewer restates her tripartite
self (individual, relational, collective), argues that relational and
collective bonds serve different adaptive functions and are regulated
separately, reviews the evidence that people tell them apart, and
extends optimal distinctiveness theory to all three levels. It ends with
a prediction about culture: individualism favours the collective self,
relational collectivism the relational self.

## Key insight

There are two ways to include others in the self, and they are not
substitutes. "The relational and collective levels of self ... represent
two different forms of social identification, i.e., processes by which
the individual self is extended to include others as integral to the
self-concept" (p. 3). The relational self takes in particular others who
are "not interchangeable" (p. 5). The collective self takes in a symbolic
group "independent of personal relationships within the group" (p. 3).
Humans need both, "belonging with and belonging to" (p. 24).

## Assumptions

- **Evolutionary functions** (pp. 4–5). Close bonds give tailored,
  privileged mutual aid but are few (about 15 close ties, 150 in a
  network, citing Dunbar). Symbolic group identities provide
  "depersonalized" cooperation at scale.
- **The self as a set of regulatory systems** (pp. 14–15), each with an
  assessment, a comparator and an activation function (Pickett and
  Gardner 2005), on the analogy of hunger and thirst.
- **Optimal distinctiveness** (pp. 12–13): identity needs come in opposed
  pairs that hold each other in check.
- **The evidence is others'**: the chapter reports no new data.

## Key results

- **The tripartite model, restated** (pp. 2–3). Brewer and Gardner 1996
  replaced the independent/interdependent and personal/social dichotomies
  with individual, relational and collective self-representations, "not
  simply three aspects of a single self system, but rather three separate
  systems with different identity properties, locus of agency, and
  motivational concerns".
- **People tell the two kinds of belonging apart** (pp. 5–7).
  - Group attachment (Smith, Murphy and Coats 1999) correlated only .46
    (anxiety) and .33 (avoidance) with interpersonal attachment, and
    uniquely predicted affect and engagement with in-groups.
  - The UCLA Loneliness Scale, in more than 2,500 students, has three
    correlated factors, isolation, relational connectedness and
    collective connectedness (Hawkley et al. 2005), replicated in older
    adults.
- **Gender** (pp. 7–11).
  - Women give more relational, men more collective self-descriptions on
    the Twenty Statements Test, with the same total of interdependent
    ones (Gabriel and Gardner 1999).
  - Men were more likely to choose to "benefit a collective over
    self-interest", women "to sacrifice self-interest for the sake of a
    relationship" (p. 9).
  - Primed with "we", men and women trusted an in-group stranger equally
    over an out-group one; only women trusted more an out-group stranger
    with an indirect personal connection (Maddux and Brewer 2005).
- **Not substitutes** (pp. 11–12). In almost every study relational bonds
  are more important than collective ones for both sexes; the sexes
  differ only in collective identity's weight beyond them.
- **Optimal distinctiveness at three levels** (pp. 13–15, Table 1):
  uniqueness against similarity (individual), autonomy against intimacy
  (relational), separation against inclusion (collective). Satisfying one
  level can transfer to another, and the levels can conflict.
- **Two collectivisms** (pp. 17–23).
  - Relational collectivism: obligation to specific persons in a network.
    Group-based collectivism: obligation to the group as a whole,
    "subordinating individual interests to those of the collective".
  - Because relational obligations are costly, relational collectivists
    should bound their in-groups tightly. Cited: Chinese and Japanese
    participants applied equality to friends and equity to strangers more
    than Americans did.
  - Individualists, whose obligations are weak, need large inclusive
    groups: "individualists are more likely to focus on the collective
    level of the social self than are relational collectivists" (p. 22).
    Cited: Americans view their country more favourably than Japanese do;
    American football fans show more in-group bias than Japanese fans at
    equal identification.

## Concepts

- **relational self**: the self extended to particular close others and
  the networks built from those dyads; personalized.
- **collective self**: the self extended to a symbolic group as a unit;
  depersonalized.
- **optimal distinctiveness**: identities chosen to balance a need for
  inclusion against a need for differentiation.
- **relational versus group-based collectivism**: obligation to specific
  persons, or to the group as a whole.
- **belonging with and belonging to**: personalized and depersonalized
  interdependence.

## Connections

- **Brewer and Gardner 1996 (LIT-tmplxaqm)**, unread. The chapter's
  framework. It is restated, not re-evidenced.
- **León's personalized "we" (THEORY-tmpsm03n).** Brewer gives the same
  line from psychology. The relational self is a we whose members are not
  interchangeable, kept apart from the depersonalized collective self.
  León cites Brewer and Gardner for it (NOTE-tmpuba7m).
- **Self-categorization theory (THEORY-tmpo85ke).** Brewer accepts its
  account of the collective self but says it relegates relationships to
  "personal" identity (p. 2). On her model a close relationship is a
  social identity too.
- **Aron et al. (LIT-tmp9rujh) and Cialdini et al. (LIT-tmpfxss5)**:
  their self–other inclusion is the relational self's kind of extension.
- **Markus and Kitayama (LIT-tmp8zvjf)** are among the dichotomies the
  tripartite model replaces.
- **The group entity versus a category (TERM-tmpko4rw).** Brewer's line
  falls between personalized and depersonalized bonds, not between a
  whole and a set of like members. Families and teams fall on the
  relational side.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** These are inferences; Brewer
  does not discuss selflessness.
  - Brewer states the thesis's premise in her own words: social
    identification is the self "extended to include others as integral to
    the self-concept" (p. 3). She gives it two forms.
  - That splits the thesis. "Collectivism is self-interest on a larger
    self" reads differently for each collectivism. Group-based
    collectivism subordinates the individual to the group as a whole;
    relational collectivism is obligation to particular people. The
    larger self of the first is a category, of the second a network of
    individuals.
  - Her motivational account fits the thesis. Groups are joined to meet
    the individual's needs for inclusion and distinctiveness, so the
    extension itself is driven by the person's own needs.
  - Her sources still speak of sacrifice: men choosing to "benefit a
    collective over self-interest", women to "sacrifice self-interest for
    the sake of a relationship" (p. 9). Self-interest there means the
    personal self's.
- **Batson's objection (CLAIM-tmpybc4k).** Empathic helping of a
  particular person is the relational self's territory. If the thesis
  needs an extended self there, it is the relational self, and the
  oneness measures (Aron's circles) are its measures. Brewer adds nothing
  on empathy.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** Brewer's criteria for
  the self's level are self-descriptions (the Twenty Statements Test) and
  importance ratings (the RIC scale asks what "is most important in
  life", p. 9). The first is a criterion apart from caring; the second is
  caring. The chapter does not test whether they come apart.
- **A dated note should be added to CLAIM-tmpjylq8.** Proposed text:
  "Brewer (NOTE-tmphttin) calls both close relationships and group
  memberships ways the individual self is 'extended to include others',
  but they are different extensions: the relational self takes in
  particular, non-interchangeable others; the collective self, a symbolic
  group. She distinguishes relational collectivism, obligation to
  specific persons, from group-based collectivism, which subordinates the
  individual to the group as a whole. The thesis needs to say which larger
  self it means, or that it means both."
