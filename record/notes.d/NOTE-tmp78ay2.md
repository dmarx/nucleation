# NOTE-tmp78ay2: Cunha et al. (2025): Instruments to Assess Bidirectional Intimate Partner Violence

## Contribution

A map of the instruments behind estimates of bidirectional violence (BV), and an argument that act-counting with single-partner reports cannot establish what "bidirectional" means for a couple.

## Key results

1. Abstract: "Fifty studies published between 2012 and 2024 met the inclusion criteria." "The Conflict Tactics Scale emerged as the most used instrument for evaluating BV, demonstrating robust psychometric properties."
2. Abstract: "BV prevalence rates ranged from 2% to 98.4% and remained generally consistent across different instruments, except in the case of male reports."
3. Abstract: "However, the limited number of studies on interpartner agreements and the reliance on single-partner self-reports pose challenges to the accuracy of these estimates."
4. Introduction: "comparing the frequency of violence can lead to couples being perceived as having BV, even when one partner is merely acting in self-defense".
5. Discussion: "Like other self-report tools, the CTS does not capture information about who initiates the violence, making it difficult to distinguish between acts committed in self-defense or retaliation and those initiated without provocation."
6. Results: "Only five studies incorporated reports from both partners and included some evaluations of interpartner agreement."; "Conroy et al. (2022) found that 60.2% of couples agreed on physical IPV victimization and perpetration in their relationship."

## Assumptions

BV is defined by acts: "BV occurs when both partners engage in violent behavior, either simultaneously or alternately." Reliability is read from Cronbach's alpha. Prevalence is compared statistically across heterogeneous studies.

## Concepts

Bidirectional violence; Johnson's four types; the CTS and its blindness to initiation, motive and consequence; interpartner agreement and chance-corrected indices. Self-report as narrative: "what is actually being measured are subjective narratives about violence, rather than the violent acts themselves".

## Connections

Stands in for Machado, Sousa and Cunha (2026) (LIT-tmp5jqvq), unread. It restates that review's operational problem: act-counting misses motive and initiation. It does not restate the conceptual review of the terms "mutual", "reciprocal", "bidirectional" and "victim-perpetrator overlap", or the proposed minimal definition with "motivational asymmetry". It stands in partly for Stover et al. (2025) (LIT-tmpcxq1w), unread: on the wide prevalence range and the scarcity of dyadic agreement data. It does not cover Stover's 34 US dyadic studies or the finding that counting a couple as bidirectional when either partner reports violence may inflate rates.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpp8na7**: corroborates the claim's first half from measurement practice. The instrument that produces most figures on mutual violence counts acts. It cannot tell retaliation or self-defence from initiation, so a tally of acts does not show who controls whom. It says nothing about responsibility.
- **CASE-tmpwrhkd**: the case's warning that counting assaults could make Carol look like the more violent partner is the CTS misclassification documented here.
- **The exchange** (A5 §2) on CTS-derived instruments ("much less equipped to determine whether an act represents intimidation, retaliation, self-protection, coercion") is supported.
- The authors also counsel "avoiding the reinforcement of unilateral blame". This agrees with what CLAIM-tmpp8na7 says it does not say.

## Limitations

Half the studies are American, and only three languages were searched. Few instruments other than the CTS, and few dyadic studies, appear. Study-level statistics compare heterogeneous designs.
