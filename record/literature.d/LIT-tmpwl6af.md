# LIT-tmpwl6af: The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental Theory

David J. Chalmers (1996), *The Conscious Mind: In Search of a Fundamental
Theory*, New York: Oxford University Press (hardback ISBN 0-19-510553-2;
paperback ISBN 0-19-511789-1, 1997; Library of Congress 95036036). It began
as the author's PhD dissertation at Indiana University (1993), according to
his book page.

## Key takeaways

*Registered from the author's posted contents, introduction and jacket copy,
not a reading. What follows is what the book and its author say about it.*

- **Aim, from the introduction.** Three stated constraints: "take
  consciousness seriously" (no redefining the explanandum as a function),
  "take science seriously" (physics is causally closed and behaviour is
  physically explicable), and treat consciousness as "a natural phenomenon,
  falling under the sway of natural laws". The book says it does "not solve
  the problem of consciousness once and for all", but tries "to rein it in".
  It frames the issues in terms of supervenience and its modal strength,
  "largely eschewing the notion of identity".
- **Parts and chapters, from the table of contents.**
  - *I Foundations*: 1 Two Concepts of Mind (the phenomenal and the
    psychological); 2 Supervenience and Explanation.
  - *II The Irreducibility of Consciousness*: 3 Can Consciousness be
    Reductively Explained?; 4 Naturalistic Dualism (an argument against
    materialism, the objection from a posteriori necessity, "Is this
    epiphenomenalism?"); 5 The Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment.
  - *III Toward a Theory of Consciousness*: 6 On the Coherence between
    Consciousness and Cognition; 7 Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing
    Qualia; 8 Consciousness and Information: Some Speculation ("Is
    experience ubiquitous?").
  - *IV Applications*: 9 Strong Artificial Intelligence ("On implementing a
    computation", "The Chinese room and other objections"); 10 The
    Interpretation of Quantum Mechanics (sections on the Everett
    interpretation and objections to it; the introduction says the chapter
    lends support to a "no-collapse" interpretation).
- **Claims the introduction makes for the chapters.** Ch. 3: standard
  methods of reductive explanation cannot account for consciousness. Ch. 4:
  "materialism is false, and … a form of dualism is true". Ch. 5 is "largely
  defensive", about the relation between consciousness and our judgments
  about it. Ch. 7: consciousness is an "organizational invariant". Ch. 8 is
  "by far the most speculative chapter". Ch. 9: "the implementation of an
  appropriate computer program will give rise to a conscious mind".

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request for David Chalmers in this
record. It is the book-length statement of the hard problem and of
naturalistic dualism. No anthology topic holds it, and the anthology does
not hold it (checked by grep).

`Deferred` because it was not read. This record files a book from its
publisher's description and contents when no lawful text can be read, and
does not rebuild a book from what others say about it. Chalmers posts the
contents, introduction and jacket copy but no chapter drafts. The Internet

Where it bears on the record. These are my inferences from the contents
and the introduction, and from the two papers read alongside it, not
claims the book was read to make:

- **The hard problem.** The papers carry the argument. [LIT-tmpbxqmy](LIT-tmpbxqmy.md) (read)
  states the hard problem and naturalistic dualism, and defers to this book
  for the anti-materialist argument and the reply to a posteriori
  necessity, which are chapter 4 here. A reading of chapter 4 is what would
  turn the record's citations of the hard problem from "stated" to
  "argued".
- **The society-of-mind bridge ([THEORY-tmp31lxe](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp31lxe.md)).** Chapter 7 is the
  book version of [LIT-tmp0swyc](LIT-tmp0swyc.md) (read). That reading's answer, that
  organizational invariance licenses the replacement cases and not
  Schwitzgebel's upward move ([LIT-159](LIT-159.md)), is drawn from the paper. Chapter
  8's "Is experience ubiquitous?" is the double-aspect principle that List
  ([LIT-tmp6phwb](LIT-tmp6phwb.md)) rejects as too unrestricted.
- **The meta-problem ([LIT-tmpn694x](LIT-tmpn694x.md)).** The later paper says its own first
  solution to the meta-problem was developed here (pp. 184–8 and 289–92),
  and that the book said "if I were a materialist, I would be an
  illusionist" (p. 189). Chapter 5, The Paradox of Phenomenal Judgment, is
  where that problem is first posed at length.
- **AI consciousness ([THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md)).** Chapter 9 defends strong AI by way of
  an account of implementing a computation. Another reader is filing
  Chalmers's papers on implementation and AI in this record at the same
  time. A reading of chapter 9 should be set against theirs.
- **Quantum foundations.** Chapter 10 applies the theory to the
  measurement problem and favours a no-collapse interpretation. Hence the
  `quantum-foundations` tag, which a reader browsing that topic would
  expect from a contents entry titled "The Interpretation of Quantum
  Mechanics". [LIT-tmpbxqmy](LIT-tmpbxqmy.md) §5 dismisses consciousness-collapse
  interpretations as explaining nothing about consciousness.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.
