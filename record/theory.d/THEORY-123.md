# THEORY-123: In ad hoc groups of students negotiating a bonus, a confederate's randomly assigned pleasant or unpleasant mood spread to the other members, by their self-reports and by blind coders, and the more pleasant mood a group caught, the more cooperative and the less conflicted coders rated it, a correlation with measured contagion rather than a manipulated effect; members' moods also converged without a confederate, and members did not credit their mood for how effective they judged themselves; the numbers are from the 2001 manuscript

## Source

- Barsade (2002), [LIT-706](../literature.d/LIT-706.md), read in full in [NOTE-551](../notes.d/NOTE-551.md), from the
  August 2001 revise-and-resubmit manuscript Wharton deposited in Penn's
  repository. The published article may differ in numbers, wording or
  analyses. Every figure below is the manuscript's, and page numbers are
  its pages.

## What was actually shown

**The spread is causal (Study 1).** Barsade, [LIT-706](../literature.d/LIT-706.md), put 94
undergraduates in 29 groups for a 30-minute leaderless, mixed-motive
negotiation over a bonus pool. A trained confederate, one male drama
student, was randomly assigned to show pleasant or unpleasant mood, with
high or low energy. Members' self-reported pleasant mood rose by .41 with
the pleasant confederate and fell by .26 with the unpleasant one (χ²(1) =
9.97, p < .005). Video-coders who saw only the members, and were kept
unaware of the conditions, rated members' mood at the end 2.75 against 2.33
(χ²(1) = 10.30, p < .005) (pp. 26–27). At group level, aggregated self- and
coder ratings, coders' ratings of overall group pleasantness and members'
ratings of the group's pleasantness all followed the confederate's valence
(Table 3b). This could have come out the other way: two independent
measures of a randomised manipulation.

**The link to group process is correlational.** Hypotheses 4 to 6 regress
outcomes on *measured* contagion, not on the manipulated condition.
Individually, a member's mood change predicted self-rated cooperativeness
(b = .89, p < .001) and peer-rated cooperativeness (b = .47, p < .05). At
group level (Table 5, N = 26), coder-rated contagion correlated with
coder-rated cooperativeness (r = .44, p < .05), with a more even split of
the bonus money (r = −.37 with its standard deviation, p < .05), and
against coder-rated conflict (r = −.42; −.48 for self-reported contagion)
(pp. 28–29). Mood change also predicted self-rated performance (b = .44, p
< .001), and only marginally peer-rated performance. The abstract's "this
contagion improved cooperation, decreased conflict" states as an effect
what was measured as an association.

**Convergence without a confederate (Study 2).** In 113 MBA students in 26
randomly formed groups, the intraclass correlation of members' pleasant
mood was .00 before the exercise and .24 after (p. 35). Contagion
predicted self-rated cooperation (b = .48, p < .01) and self-rated but not
peer-rated performance. Group contagion correlated with members' ratings
of group cooperation (r = .49, p < .01) and conflict (r = −.49, p < .01),
but not with the spread of the funds. Barsade offers this as evidence that
contagion "is a group phenomenon and not due to the dyadic nature of the
relationship between the confederate and the group member" (p. 36). It
replicates the convergence and the correlations; it has no manipulation,
so it adds nothing causal.

**What members noticed (General Discussion, p. 37).** In both studies
members "did significantly relate how pleasantly they behaved in the
experiment to the way other group members acted", and Barsade herself
calls this a "recognition that other group members could influence the
degree of pleasantness they felt". What they did not do was link their mood
to their effectiveness: they "indicated absolutely no awareness of the
relationship between the mood they were in and their attributions for
their degree of effectiveness", which they put down to the money won and
their own abilities. So the unawareness is of the mood's *influence on
their judgments*, not of the contagion itself, and it rests on attribution
items, not a designed test.

**What failed.** Unpleasant mood did not spread more than pleasant mood
(χ² < 1). Energy cut both ways, marginally: high-energy unpleasantness
spread more than low-energy (−.44 against −.07, p < .10), but low-energy
pleasantness spread more than high-energy (+.66 against +.16, p < .10), the
opposite of the prediction (pp. 27–28).

## What this does not say

- **It does not say pleasant contagion causes cooperation.** It is
  consistent with that and with a third factor driving both a member's mood
  and conduct. A test that compares process across the manipulated
  conditions is not reported.
- **It does not say members are unaware that others affect their mood.**
  By Barsade's own account they partly recognised it. [NOTE-551](../notes.d/NOTE-551.md)'s C4
  and [LIT-706](../literature.d/LIT-706.md)'s status note, which say members did not recognise that
  their mood was caught, state this more strongly than the manuscript does.
- **It does not identify the mechanism.** Mimicry with afferent feedback
  (Hatfield, Cacioppo and Rapson) and conscious social comparison are both
  invoked, and nothing separates them.
- **It does not generalise beyond the setting.** Ad hoc, competitive
  student groups; one confederate in all four conditions; Time 2 mood
  recalled after the exercise for its second half. Ongoing teams may
  habituate to one another's moods or grow more sensitive to them; the
  Study 2 correlation between friendship and contagion (r = .44, p < .05)
  hints at the second.
- **It is about mood, not emotion with an object.** That matters for how it
  stands to the appraisal account, [THEORY-055](THEORY-055.md), on which an appraisal of an
  event's pertinence to one's concerns elicits an emotion. Contagion by
  mimicry and feedback produces mood without an appraisal of any event, but
  [THEORY-055](THEORY-055.md) is a claim about emotions, and Barsade's object is mood, so
  the two do not conflict. Her alternative description, that contagion
  carries "a type of group appraisal of events", would bring them closer.
  The feedback route is nearer the bodily-state account of felt affect,
  [THEORY-045](THEORY-045.md); that comparison is the record's, not Barsade's.

## Connections

- **The convergence account, [THEORY-126](THEORY-126.md), extends this one.** That
  account defines collective emotion as convergence of affect on one object
  and treats contagion as one route to it among several, with shared
  appraisal and group membership. Barsade's Study 2 ICC is its definition
  operationalised; her Study 1 is the causal test of its contagion route.
  Barsade's groups test the I-mode end only, because they were strangers
  competing for a bonus.
- **It bears on no group subject.** Study 2's "group phenomenon" is a claim
  about level of analysis, not a claim that the group bears a mood. It is
  the empirical floor any account of a group's own emotional state must
  stand on, and it does not reach that question.
