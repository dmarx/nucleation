# NOTE-tmpzarar: Scanlon — Reply to Leif Wenar

## Contribution

Scanlon's own statement that contractualism explains the Hohfeldian
structure of moral rights, powers included, without rights as basic; a
two-part account of why promissory obligation is directed; and an explicit
refusal to say which moral phenomena are rights.

## Key results

- **Moral rights constrain institutions** (p. 401): they "place demands on
  what laws and other institutions must be like. They therefore cannot
  derive their authority from these institutions".
- **Free expression** (p. 402): "The conclusion supported by this reasoning
  is an immunity: a limit on the powers of government."
- **Powers explained** (p. 403): "Contractualism seeks to explain not only
  first-order claim rights but also powers, as well as the prior
  liberties". "It also explains why a promisee has the power to release the
  promisor from her obligation": both parties "have reason to insist on a
  principle that includes the condition "unless B consents to X's not being
  done," rather than a principle that would make promisory obligations
  unalterable once they are undertaken."
- **Scope** (p. 403): "I believe that a contractualist theory can explain
  all of the moral phenomena that Wenar would count as rights". Wenar's
  rights-based account "seems to be just a description (in terms of rights)
  of the moral facts to be explained, rather than an explanation of those
  facts."
- **Rights not basic** (pp. 403–404): "I don't think that we need to appeal
  to the idea of a right to explain why our moral situation has this form,
  and don't think it matters very much exactly which parts of this
  structure we denominate as rights."
- **Directedness** (p. 404): "promisees are singled out in two ways: by the
  central role that their interest in assurance has in justifying
  principles of fidelity and by the way in which those principles must make
  promisory obligations sensitive to their wills." Contractualism "is
  committed to neither" will nor interest theory "as constituting the
  essence of rights".
- **Wrongs without a right** (p. 405), under the Principle of Established
  Practices: "It would be wrong not to do these things, but it does not
  seem to me that other citizens have a right that we do them".

## Assumptions

Principle F (What We Owe to Each Other, p. 304, quoted in n. 3) as the
principle of fidelity; Hohfeld's analysis as the description of moral
structure; legal and institutional rights draw their force from the
institutions that define them.

## Concepts

Rights as constraints; Hohfeldian elements; moral power; power of
release; directed obligation; wronging without a right; Principle F;
Principle of Established Practices.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmplidg6.** Its premise is this reply's: contractualism explains
  claims and powers without rights as basic (pp. 403–404). The step the
  claim adds, that rights once justified organize later justification, is
  not Scanlon's: he treats the promisee's power as justified once, by a
  principle. His p. 401 ("cannot derive their authority from these
  institutions") is near the claim's priority of rights to institutional
  authority. The claim's "claims, powers, liberties, and immunities" holds,
  but immunities come from the free-expression section, not the
  contractualism section.
- **CLAIM-tmpssfc1.** For promises, the release is a power the promisee
  holds because the principle makes the obligation "sensitive to their
  wills" and not "unalterable once they are undertaken". Nothing here on
  institutions that cease to count a demand.
- **QUESTION-tmpk7u3e.** Scanlon claims contractualism already explains
  powers; the question's second option, an allocation of powers reasonable
  rejection cannot explain, must meet this reply. He concedes that
  contractualism does not say which phenomena are rights, which Wenar
  (LIT-tmpretfe) presses.
- The exchange's A100 is faithful: Scanlon's phrase is "a basic element of
  moral thinking" (abstract), where A100 says "morally primitive".

## Limitations

Six pages, a reply. It does not meet Wenar's argument that contractualism
must also be a theory of authority over obligations, except through the
promise case, and does not discuss institutional powers.
