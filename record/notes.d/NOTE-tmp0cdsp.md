# NOTE-tmp0cdsp: Preregistered Replication and Extension of "Moral Hypocrisy: Social Groups and the Flexibility of Virtue"

## Contribution

A large preregistered replication of a well-known small study. It finds
the original effect is not there, and that intergroup bias in moral
judgment takes different forms in minimal and partisan groups. For the
record, it is the only preregistered evidence held on whether minimal
groups produce favouritism at all. The answer is: only among those who
identify with the minimal group.

## Key insight

A minimal group is not a group for everyone assigned to it. Labelled
"Overestimators" after a dot task and a chat, participants identified with
their in-group more than the out-group (d = .64), but not uniformly. Bias
followed identification. Politics, a group people care about (d = 1.30),
produced derogation of the other side rather than favouritism toward one's
own.

## Assumptions

- **The original paradigm** (Valdesolo & DeSteno 2007), moved online.
  Participants self-assign an easy ("green") or hard ("red") task, leaving
  the other to a future participant, or use a randomizer. Others judge the
  fairness of an assigner who takes the easy task.
- **Pay was equalized** across tasks, so the easy task stayed preferable
  (p. 12).
- **Intent-to-treat** was preregistered. The authors made a post hoc switch
  to excluding fair and altruistic choosers, as the original did, because
  their inclusion confounds the Self condition (p. 21).

## Key results

- **Samples.** Experiment 1: minimal groups, 610 recruited, 590 analysed,
  nationally representative. Experiment 2: political groups, 606
  recruited, 577 analysed, half Democrats and half Republicans. Power
  simulations assumed d = .4 (pp. 9–10, 21, 25).
- **H1, the original contrast** (self and in-group versus other and
  out-group): Experiment 1 F(1, 536) = 0.15, p = .70, with the 95% CI
  below the equivalence bound η² = .01, so it is "statistically
  equivalent to zero". Experiment 2 F(1, 528) = 1.86, p = .17.
- **H2, self versus others:** not significant once fair choosers are
  excluded (Experiment 1 p = .26; Experiment 2 p = .23). It is significant
  only when they are included, which the authors call uninterpretable.
- **H3, in-group versus out-group:**
  - Minimal groups: F(1, 536) = 3.22, p = .07.
  - Political groups: F(1, 528) = 9.04, p = .003, η² = .02, driven by
    out-group derogation.
  - Pooled: F(1, 581) = 11.69, p < .001.
- **H6, moderation by identification:**
  - Minimal groups: the interaction is b = −.39, p < .001. In-group
    fairness rose with identification (b = .30) and out-group fairness
    did not change.
  - Political groups: no moderation, perhaps because identification was
    uniformly high (M 2.95 against 1.04).
- **H5, stronger in natural than minimal groups:** not supported (p = .44).
- **Choosers.** In the Self condition, 47 (Experiment 1) and 40
  (Experiment 2) used the randomizer, and 4 and 6 took the hard task
  themselves. In the original, 17 of 19 took the easy task.

## Concepts

- **moral hypocrisy**: judging one's own transgression, or an in-group
  member's, as fairer than the same act by others.
- **collective identification**: here, the difference between in-group
  and out-group scores on three items (value, pride, importance).
- **intent-to-treat**: analysing participants as randomized, whatever they
  chose.

## Connections

- **Tajfel et al. 1971 (LIT-tmp6t6ny)**, unread. This is not a
  replication of that experiment; it uses the same overestimator
  induction. The finding that favouritism tracks identification is
  consistent with Hornsey's report (NOTE-tmp3dav9) that SCT effects appear
  under high salience or identification.
- **Rubin & Hewstone (LIT-tmpv3dgs)**, unread, and the self-esteem debate:
  not addressed here.
- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** About a third of choosers, with
  pay equalized and no one watching, used a randomizer or took the hard
  task. That is fair or altruistic choice in a minimal setting where no
  group was at stake in the choice. It is not evidence about motive, but
  it is a base rate a selfishness thesis must explain.

## Bearing on the record

- **The minimal-group effect.** It is the record's only preregistered
  evidence on it, and it qualifies the 1971 claim that mere
  categorization suffices: here it sufficed only for those who identified.
- **THEORY-tmpo85ke.** It fits the account's emphasis on identification
  and salience.
- **No THEORY filed.** It is a replication of a moral-hypocrisy effect,
  outside the record's current questions.
- **No ML instruction.**

## Limitations

- **The post hoc switch** from intent-to-treat to exclusion was made
  openly and matched the original.
- **Online setting and chat-room induction**; Prolific workers only.
- **Supplementary materials were not read**, including the high-identifier
  subgroup analysis cited in the discussion.
- **A typo** in the H5 comparison names both groups "Experiment 2" (p. 28).

## Open questions

- Does a minimal-group induction that reliably produces identification
  produce the original moral hypocrisy? The paper suggests the induction
  failed for many participants. A manipulation check that selects
  identifiers in advance would test it.
