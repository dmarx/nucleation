# NOTE-tmpkouzt: Vestring and colleagues, read for whether a drug changes the weight objective threat has on approach while the threat itself is held fixed

## Contribution

The first comparison of ketamine with lorazepam on a validated human
approach-avoidance conflict task, with threat probability and loss varied
independently. The reading is THEORY-tmppv8yl.

## Key results

1. **The task** (Methods): a predator's attack probability is "signaled
   by the color of the screen frame, with three levels of threat
   probability (0.02, 0.04, or 0.06 per 20 ms bin outside the safe
   zone ...)"; "While participants were informed that different colors
   represented different threats, they were not told the specific
   probabilities and had to learn them from experience." 240 test trials
   per session.
2. **Design** (Experimental setup): a drug-free first visit, then
   ketamine and lorazepam "in a counterbalanced, within-subject design",
   tested 90–150 min after dosing. "Personnel responsible for behavioral
   data collection were blinded to drug condition"; participants and
   physicians were not.
3. **Approach** (Fig. 3): "Both ketamine and lorazepam showed
   significantly higher approach rates than control (ketamine vs. control:
   β = 0.31, p = 0.007; lorazepam vs. control: β = 0.48, p = 0.001)."
4. **Threat sensitivity** (Fig. 3B): "Ketamine significantly reduced
   threat sensitivity compared to the control condition, i.e., a flatter
   decline with increasing threat (ketamine × threat probability vs.
   control: β = 0.13, p = 0.005)." Loss sensitivity rose (β = −0.17,
   p = 0.001).
5. **The authors' reading** (Results): "ketamine sharpens sensitivity to
   explicit loss magnitude while reducing reliance on probabilistic threat
   cues, whereas lorazepam attenuates adaptive adjustments to both threat
   probability and loss magnitude".
6. **Missing measure** (Discussion): "Session-level mood, dissociation,
   and anxiety ratings were not collected systematically".

## Assumptions

The objective attack probability is taken as the threat the participant
faces; the paper does not estimate what each participant learned it to
be. Effects are fixed effects in mixed models with z-scored threat and
loss, Bonferroni threshold p < 0.0125.

## Concepts

- **Approach-avoidance conflict task** (Bach et al. 2014): passive
  avoidance of a token under an experimenter-set threat.
- **Threat sensitivity**: the slope of approach on threat probability.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-446, near miss of a second kind.** The objective threat is held
  fixed by design within each level and the drug acts on the arbitration
  between threat and gain, which is near the claim's authority variable g.
  It does not meet the claim's test. The represented threat is neither
  measured nor fixed: the probabilities are learned, so "reducing
  reliance on probabilistic threat cues" could be a lower learned
  estimate (belief), a weaker weight on an unchanged estimate
  (authority), or a change in how uncertainty about the estimate counts.
  The task offers no route to evidence that avoidance withholds: the
  attack is visible either way, so the identifiability step is absent.
  The authors' own phrase "reliance on" matches the claim's weighting
  without testing it. It neither supports nor counts against the claim.
  No relation written.
- **QUESTION-325, background.** The paper's reading that ketamine
  down-weights "uncertain threat cues" is in the territory of the
  uncertainty account (THEORY-910), but uncertainty is not estimated, so
  it does not bear on which rival holds.

## Limitations

- The drug-free session was always first; the authors' order and
  repetition checks do not remove the confound.
- No ratings of threat probability, anxiety or dissociation.
- 34 healthy participants; no anxious sample.
- Ketamine also shortened approach and withdrawal latencies, which
  reduces time at risk and so the realized threat; part of the gain
  effect is motor.
