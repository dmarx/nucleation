# NOTE-1373: Marquis and Tilcsik — Imprinting: toward a multilevel theory

## Contribution

Imprinting had been used since Stinchcombe (1965) at every level from
industries to individual careers, usually undefined. The review gives a
general definition precise enough to exclude neighbouring ideas, sorts
the scattered literature into one framework, and extends the concept in
three ways: sensitive periods are transitions, not only foundings, so an
entity can have several; imprints persist without being permanent, and
can decay or grow; and an entity can take imprints secondhand from
others it emulates.

## Key insight

Imprinting is history entering at a few moments and then staying. "A
process whereby, during a brief period of susceptibility, a focal
entity develops characteristics that reflect prominent features of the
environment, and these characteristics continue to persist despite
significant environmental changes in subsequent periods" (p. 201). An
organization is then "layers of imprints", like strata at a dig, laid
down at its sensitive periods and not swept away by later ones.

## Assumptions

- The three features hold "across levels of analysis", but the
  mechanisms at different levels are "only analogous, rather than
  identical" (p. 201).
- Persistence at the organizational level is carried by inertia and
  institutionalization (Hannan and Freeman; DiMaggio and Powell); at
  the collective level by later entrants copying earlier ones; at the
  individual level by reduced susceptibility outside transitions.
- Sensitive periods are times of transition and uncertainty (founding,
  going public, mergers, career entry).

## Key results

A review's results are its distinctions and its framework.

- **Definition (pp. 201–204).** Sensitive period; stamp of the
  environment; persistence of imprints.
- **Against path dependence (pp. 205–206).** Imprinting involves
  "prominent environmental conditions rather than historical accidents",
  "short sensitive periods rather than long-term event chains", and "the
  stability of stamped-in features rather than the increasing dominance
  of a pattern".
- **Against cohort effects (pp. 206–207).** A cohort effect is an outcome
  (similarity within a cohort), imprinting a process. Not all cohort
  effects are imprinting, and imprinting need not produce a cohort
  effect, since members of one cohort can be imprinted differently
  (different mentors, different founders' blueprints).
- **Table 1 (p. 208).** Twelve cells. At the collective level imprints
  persist because "subsequently founded organizations" imitate earlier
  ones (Stinchcombe's industries, Marquis's local corporate networks); at
  the organizational level by inertia and institutionalization (density
  at founding and mortality; founders' blueprints in Baron, Hannan and
  Burton); at the building-block level, e.g. Burton and Beckman's
  "position imprints", where a job's first incumbent shapes the turnover
  of successors to the third and fourth; at the individual level, early
  career conditions and mentors (Higgins, McEvily et al.).
- **Imprint transfer (p. 216).** Founders bear imprints from the firms
  they came from and pass them to new firms (Phillips on women's
  promotion in law firms; Burton, Sørensen and Beckman).
- **Dynamics (pp. 222–225).** Multiple sensitive periods; layering, or
  "sedimentation"; persistence and decay, with cases of amplification
  (Eisenhardt and Schoonhoven; Sullivan, Tang and Marquis).
- **Origin and current utility (pp. 225–228).** Following Gould, an
  imprinted capability may stay adaptive, become maladaptive or be
  exapted; Marquis and Huang's banks reused coordination capabilities
  built for dispersed branches to manage acquisitions.
- **Secondhand imprinting (pp. 228–229).** "The social transmission of
  imprints, a process whereby an actor takes on aspects of an imprint
  borne by another actor"; a new firm that emulates older ones takes on
  "imprinted elements from earlier eras that survived in the
  architecture and routines of older firms".
- **Figure 1 (pp. 229–231).** A general model: a sensitive period, a long
  period of imprint maintenance (with decay, secondhand transmission to
  others, and adaptive, maladaptive or exapted consequences), and a
  second sensitive period whose imprint is "layered upon the existing
  imprint". The authors call it "more of a road sign for further
  research than a detailed map".

## Concepts

- **Imprinting**: as defined above, three features.
- **Sensitive period**: "temporally restricted", of "high susceptibility
  to environmental influence"; here, any transition.
- **Imprint**: the characteristics formed in a sensitive period.
- **Secondhand imprinting**: taking on another actor's imprint by
  learning from or emulating it.
- **Exaptation**: an imprinted capability put to a use other than the
  one it formed for.
- **Organizational building blocks**: units, routines, capabilities,
  jobs, occupations.

## Connections

Feldman and Pentland ([LIT-1124](../literature.d/LIT-1124.md), [NOTE-1386](NOTE-1386.md)) put the opposite stress
on routines, which they treat as changing through every performance;
this review lists routines among the building blocks that carry
imprints. The two can be squared only if performance keeps re-enacting
an imprint, which neither paper shows. The review's "sedimentation"
comes from Cooper, Hinings, Greenwood and Brown (1996).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-329](../claims.d/CLAIM-329.md).** The review gives the claim's distinction between
  transmission and resemblance a vocabulary the organizational
  literature already uses, at the level of features rather than of
  identity. Secondhand imprinting is causal transmission of a
  characteristic by emulation; a cohort effect is shared resemblance
  from shared conditions; and the review insists the two are different
  (pp. 206–207, 228–229). It also gives a case where a feature persists
  across total turnover in one position: Burton and Beckman's position
  imprint constrains the third and fourth successors. What it does not
  do is say when an entity that carries another's imprint is that
  entity continuing; every case it discusses keeps the bearer's identity
  fixed by its legal or institutional existence and asks only which
  features persist. So it supports the claim's half that transmission
  and resemblance differ, and says nothing on its half about which
  transmission makes the same collective.
- **[QUESTION-118](../questions.d/QUESTION-118.md).** The layered-imprint picture, in which a later
  sensitive period adds strata without removing earlier ones, is one
  candidate answer to what stays continuous through change: the older
  strata. The review does not test it.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- It is a review and a programme: its extensions (multiple sensitive
  periods, layering, secondhand imprinting, intersecting imprints) are
  proposals, with the authors' own note that the influence of
  coexisting imprints "has received little empirical attention".
- The evidence it surveys is mostly correlational: present features
  regressed on founding-era conditions. The review does not discuss how
  those studies separate imprinting from selection, in which entities
  founded in some conditions survive only if they have certain features.
- The definition requires a "brief" sensitive period and then allows any
  transition to be one, which risks the elasticity the authors warn
  against at the outset.
- No errors of fact found in what was read. "Sinchcombe" for
  Stinchcombe on p. 199 and "Stinchcome" on p. 203 are typos.

## Open questions

- When does an entity that bears another's imprint count as that entity
  continuing rather than a new entity with an inherited trait?
- How would a study separate imprinting from survivorship among entities
  founded in a given period?
