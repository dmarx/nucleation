# NOTE-tmp460sz: Blame

## Contribution

The record's first source on the philosophy of blame as such, as opposed to
the conditions of responsibility (LIT-691). It asks two questions, "Which
reactions and interactions constitute blame?" and "When is it appropriate to
respond in these ways?". It answers the first with a four-way taxonomy and
the second with three kinds of fact. For this filing it stands in for
Scanlon's Moral Dimensions and adds to LIT-691 on Strawson.

## Key results

- **Taxonomy (§1).** "we get four categories: cognitive, emotional,
  conative, and functional accounts of blame".
- **Cognitive (§1.1).** Smart's grading. Watson's (1996) aretaic blame,
  where the action "represents their evaluative standpoint, their practical
  identity, what they “stand for” (Watson 1996: 234)". "Watson does not take
  aretaic blame to be a general analysis of blame." Against cognitive
  accounts: "they risk conflating blaming with judging blameworthy".
- **Emotional (§1.2).** Strawson's paper "contains little sustained
  discussion of blame as such" but is the genesis of emotional theories.
  "Regarding others as morally responsible agents, for Strawson, is not a
  matter of judgment but of emotional response." Wallace develops it.
  Objections: Sher (blame without emotion) and Hieronymi's force objection.
- **Scanlon (§1.3.2).** Blame's cognitive component is "a judgment that
  another has acted in a way that impairs meaningful interpersonal
  relations". To blame is further "to take your relationship with him or her
  to be modified in a way that [a judgment of blameworthiness] holds to be
  appropriate" (2008: 128–29). Criticisms: Wallace, that it "leaves the
  blame out of blame", because "blame has a quality of opprobrium that is not
  captured by the considerations about the normative significance of
  impaired relationships". Wolf: one can blame without impairment, in a
  hot-headed but loving family. Sher: strangers. "Scanlon, in response
  (2008, 2013), insists that all rational agents stand in the “moral
  relationship” to one another", but "whether this kind of relationship is
  sufficient to explain the blame of strangers is unclear".
- **Functional (§1.4).** "According to one functional account of blame, the
  function of blame is protest." Smith (2013) holds that Scanlonian
  modification counts as blame only when it is protest.
- **Facts about the blamed (§2.1.2).** "We are all vulnerable to coercion,
  manipulation, situational pressures, and varying degrees of temptation or
  compulsion", and "the extent to which these factors rob us of our freedom
  is the extent to which we may not be deserving of blame". Even so, "just
  because someone has earned blame doesn’t mean that blame is necessarily
  the right response".
- **Facts about the interaction (§2.2).** Proportionality: "there will
  likely always be some responses that take the transgression too
  seriously, and some that don’t take it seriously enough". Evidence:
  "there is some epistemic standard that must be met before blame is
  appropriate".
- **Facts about the blamer (§2.3).** Friedman: "not everyone is
  blamerworthy". Complicity (§2.3.3), quoting Cohen: "you ordered me to do
  it, you asked me to do it, you forced me to do it, you left me with no
  reasonable alternative, you gave me the means to do it". "The superior
  officer who orders a subordinate to do something morally reprehensible is
  not in a position to blame the subordinate for carrying out the order,
  even if civilians are." Watson (2015) and Beade (2019) apply this to the
  state: given its share in the social conditions of crime, "it suddenly
  becomes unclear that the state is in a position to punish certain
  law-breakers". Lacey and Pickard reply. Meddling: Radzik's restricted
  standing for wrongs "committed within special relationships, such as
  romantic partnerships, familial bonds, and friendships". On Scanlon's
  account, "some wrongs are none of our business because they don’t impair
  any of our relationships". Moral luck: Watson on Robert Harris, "I too am
  a potential sinner", a worry of "subjunctive hypocrisy". Claimant
  injustice (Carbonell 2019): it "occurs when social prejudices or
  structural inequalities undermine a moral agent’s ability to engage in
  felicitous moral address".

## Assumptions

- Interpersonal blame, not causal blame ("to blame" as "is to blame").
- The entry endorses no theory of blame. Its norms of appropriateness are
  stated "at a level of abstraction that floats free of substantive
  commitments".

## Concepts

- **cognitive / emotional / conative / functional** accounts of blame.
- **impaired-relationship account** (Scanlon); **moral relationship**.
- **aretaic blame** (Watson).
- **standing to blame**; **hypocrisy**; **complicity**; **meddling** (the
  Business Condition); **subjunctive hypocrisy**.
- **claimant injustice** (Carbonell).

## Bearing on the record

- CLAIM-tmpnw7ez: the complicity condition is an independent statement of
  the claim's structure from the blamer's side. A party who "forced" another
  or "left [them] with no reasonable alternative" loses standing to blame
  them, without the actor ceasing to be blameworthy (civilians still may).
  Corroborates.
- CLAIM-tmpnfjln: an organization that blames members for failures its
  conditions produced is, on this condition, a complicit blamer. Watson's
  and Beade's argument about the state is the institutional case. This is a
  normative verdict on the blame the claim describes, not evidence that
  blame does settle where failure becomes visible. Note only.
- CLAIM-tmpp8na7: Scanlon's account, as reported, lets a person withdraw
  from a relationship on the impairment without settling who is worse. That
  matches A7 §4's use. Note only.
- QUESTION-tmpgyn11 and CLAIM-tmpyjzi8: no direct bearing.
- Epistemic-injustice entries (A7 §6): Carbonell's claimant injustice is
  the blame-side counterpart. The filer should check the record's
  epistemic-injustice holdings.

## Limitations

Strawson in one paragraph. The book's other chapters (permissibility,
meaning) are not reported. Complicity and claimant injustice get a
