# LIT-439: The Computational Boundary of a "Self": Developmental Bioelectricity Drives Multicellularity and Scale-Free Cognition

Michael Levin (2019), *Frontiers in Psychology 10, 2688* (Hypothesis and
Theory; published 13 December 2019) — DOI-10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02688, open
access (CC BY)

## Key takeaways

- **A Self is defined by its goals.** "The scale and types of goals that a
  system can pursue defines … the boundaries and content of the putative
  agent." Goals are meant in the cybernetic sense: a feedback loop that
  drives a state toward a set point.
- **The cognitive light cone.** An agent's boundary is "the most distant
  (in time and space) set of events that this system can measure and
  attempts to regulate". It is drawn on a diagram with time on one axis
  and space on the other (Fig. 2), and is called "semi-quantitative". No
  procedure for measuring it is given.
- **Nesting is the norm.** "Individuals can overlap – the same biophysical
  system can support a number of coexisting, coupled Selves with different
  cognitive borders" (Fig. 2C). "Most biological systems consist of
  multiple, nested selves [not one, as implied by Integrated Information
  Theory (Tononi, 2008)]."
- **Binding is information sharing.** Cells joined by gap junctions share
  one picture of the world and so form a larger Self with larger goals,
  such as an organ's shape. Too little sharing fails to bind the parts;
  too much gives "a homogenous soup".
- **Cancer is the Self shrinking.** A cell cut off from bioelectric
  coupling reverts to single-cell goals. This rests on cited experiments:
  depolarising a cell population makes melanocytes metastatic in
  genetically normal tadpoles, and hyperpolarisation stops human
  oncogenes from forming tumours.
- **Consciousness is set aside.** No claims about first-person experience
  are made "except for a few brief remarks at the end". Those remarks
  suggest there may be "something-it-is-like to being a tissue or organ",
  and say the model "takes a stand on the perennial combination problem
  and can perhaps be seen as a form of panpsychism".

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 for the owner's bridge between Minsky's society of
mind and Schwitzgebel's conscious United States. [NOTE-341](../notes.d/NOTE-341.md) is the
close reading, and it placed the work: **Active**, on the **composition
side** of the seam.

What it gives the bridge is a criterion of individuation under which a
whole built from agents is itself an agent and its parts stay agents.
Minsky's *Society of Mind* ([LIT-429](LIT-429.md), read here through Singh's
exposition [LIT-444](LIT-444.md)) decomposes a mind into agents that are mindless.
Levin's parts are the opposite. Cells are "competent", goal-seeking Selves
in their own right, and a body is a Self because their goals are scaled up
by sharing information. Schwitzgebel's [LIT-159](LIT-159.md) (read in [NOTE-131](../notes.d/NOTE-131.md)) argues
that a whole made of sensing or conscious parts can be conscious (its C3).
Levin supports the **agency** half of that claim, and his closing section
gestures at the experience half without arguing it. He proposes, too, that
"multi-human systems could have their own degree of cognition" and that
reinforcement-learning and information-theoretic tools could test whether
a social group is "a coherent goal-seeking individual". That is the
functional version of Schwitzgebel's telescopic view of the United States,
reached independently: Levin does not cite Schwitzgebel.

On the anti-nesting principle the paper is **against** IIT, but only by
assertion. The bracket naming IIT is the whole of the argument. Nothing
in the paper engages the exclusion postulate as stated in IIT 3.0
([LIT-445](LIT-445.md)), which is about phenomenal consciousness, not goal pursuit.
So it **supports C3** in the agency sense, **is neutral on C4** (Putnam is
not mentioned), and **does not support C5**: it contests IIT's conclusion
without giving the reductio, or any argument, against the postulate.

Neighbours. It cites Hofstadter's *Gödel, Escher, Bach* ([LIT-437](LIT-437.md))
twice, for collectives that compute "meta-system properties" and for
agents that "transcend one system". Its pragmatic rule, that the right
level of description "is the one that facilitates prediction and control
with the least effort", is the intentional stance it cites (Dennett 1987),
and so sits with Dennett's [LIT-220](LIT-220.md). It names causal-emergence measures
(Hoel et al.) as the tool for finding the most efficacious level, which
[LIT-025](LIT-025.md) reviews. The record's two other Levin works, [LIT-195](LIT-195.md) and [LIT-211](LIT-211.md),
assume this paper's continuum of cognition. [LIT-120](LIT-120.md) (autopoiesis) and
[LIT-167](LIT-167.md) (the organism as an individual) are the record's other accounts
of biological individuality, and [LIT-046](LIT-046.md) is its computational account of
collective intelligence.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice. Its one remark
about engineering is a prediction that "adaptive, useful robotics will
require components that are themselves competent and goal-seeking", which
is a conjecture, not a method. It is not held in the anthology, and nothing
here needs to move there.
