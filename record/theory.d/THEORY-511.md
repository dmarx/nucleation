# THEORY-511: Illocutionary acts that are not essentially conventional, such as warning, entreating and objecting, are individuated by a wholly overt, audience-directed intention, Grice's three intentions with a fourth that the audience recognise the second, and are performed when that intention is understood; acts such as verdicts and umpires' calls exist only within rule-governed procedures and fall outside the scheme

## Source

Strawson (1964), [LIT-1043](../literature.d/LIT-1043.md) (*The Philosophical Review* 73(4):439–460),
read in full in [NOTE-736](../notes.d/NOTE-736.md). It amends Grice (1957), [LIT-1042](../literature.d/LIT-1042.md), read in
[NOTE-735](../notes.d/NOTE-735.md). Its quotations from Austin are the record's only text of *How
to Do Things with Words* ([LIT-785](../literature.d/LIT-785.md), unread).

## What was actually shown

**Not every illocution is conventional (pp. 443–445).** Introducing,
surrendering, the foreman's "guilty" and the umpire's "out" are
conventional. A skater told "The ice over there is very thin" can be warned
"without its being the case that there is any statable convention at all".
What makes "Don't go" an entreaty is "something ... relating to X's
situation, attitude to Y, manner, and current intention". An objection in
debate has no constituting convention.

**Grice amended (pp. 446–448).** S intends (i1) a response r in A, (i2)
that A recognise (i1), and (i3) that this recognition be part of A's reason
for r. In the watcher case S plants evidence knowing A watches, and A does
not know S knows: all three hold, and S is not trying to let A know. So add
(i4), that A recognise (i2). Strawson grants this opens "a regressive
series of intentions that intentions should be recognized".

**Uptake (pp. 448–449).** To understand the utterance is to take S to have
the complex intention, so (i2) and (i4) must be fulfilled, not (i1) or
(i3). This is identified, tentatively, with Austin's uptake. After Hart's
objections (a will no one reads), "the aim, if not the achievement, of
securing uptake is essentially a standard, if not an invariable, element".

**What the scheme explains (pp. 449–454).** Explicit performatives make an
overt intention explicit. Showing off is not an illocution, because
recognition would defeat its effect. Insinuating is not, because its
intention "is essentially nonavowable".

**Where it strains (pp. 454–458).** Entreaty differs from request by a
recognised "complex attitude". An order needs an intended recognition of a
social context with rules and sanctions, held "with some strain". For the
umpire, the jury, the judge, a bridge redouble and a marriage, the intended
response drops out and no single audience can be isolated: "Such acts
could have no existence outside the rule- or convention-governed practices
and procedures of which they essentially form parts." A "redouble" let slip
is a redouble, but "the case is essentially deviant or nonstandard".

**The common element (pp. 458–459).** "The illocutionary force of an
utterance is essentially something that is intended to be understood."

**What could have come out otherwise.** The skater's warning or the
entreaty could have needed an identifiable convention. On Strawson's
analysis they do not.

## What this does not say

- **That the conditions are sufficient.** They are offered as necessary,
  with the regress acknowledged.
- **That the audience is only evidence of the act.** For a
  non-conventional act "the act of communication is performed if uptake is
  secured", and an order's intention must refer to social rules. The
  relation to the audience enters through the content of the intention.
  [NOTE-736](../notes.d/NOTE-736.md) corrects [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md) on this point: on Strawson the speaker's
  states are not "alone".
- **Anything about teasing.** Strawson does not discuss it. Where parental
  reproach falls (with orders, where standing enters the intention) is the
  reader's placement, and whether that content counts as a relation is
  left open.
- **That institutional acts are explained by intention.** They are
  conceded to procedure. That end of the scale is Searle's status
  functions ([LIT-482](../literature.d/LIT-482.md)).
- **That a general account is available.** Intermediate cases (advice
  given "in a 'take it or leave it' spirit", an official as "mouthpiece")
  are left schematic (p. 460).

## Connections

- **Extends [THEORY-444](THEORY-444.md).** The same mechanism, an effect meant to work
  through recognition of the intention, refined by a fourth intention and
  widened from meaning to force. Its source list carries [LIT-1042](../literature.d/LIT-1042.md) second
  because the amended analysis is Grice's analysis with one condition
  added.
- **Answers [QUESTION-073](../questions.d/QUESTION-073.md) in part.** It is the intention-based rival the
  question names, read. It makes a prediction: for acts not essentially
  conventional there is no case with the overt intention fixed and the
  act changed by standing or uptake. [CASE-069](../cases.d/CASE-069.md) (Labov's sounding) would
  count against it if Labov's attribution of the ritual aim is accepted
  as the speaker's. And it takes uptake into the intention-based account,
  weakened to an aim, so uptake does not separate the two sides as
  cleanly as the question supposes. Goffman and Bach and Harnish remain
  unread, so the question stays open.
- **[CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md), [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md).** The three-way division (overt intention alone;
  overt intention referring to social rules; procedure) is the shape
  [CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md)'s scope turns on.
- Bach and Harnish (1979; [LIT-1040](../literature.d/LIT-1040.md), unread) build their taxonomy on the
