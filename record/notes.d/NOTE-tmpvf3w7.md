# NOTE-tmpvf3w7: Harnish — Commitments and Speech Acts

## Contribution

A co-author's restatement of the expressed-attitude theory of illocutionary
acts (EA-TIA) of Bach and Harnish (1979), with a critical survey of the
alternative that commitment, not expressed attitude, makes an utterance the
act it is. It finds Searle's and Alston's normative accounts unexplained or
false at the point that matters. It proposes that the two kinds of theory
may pick out the same facts. The hard question, what undertaking a
commitment amounts to, is left "to another occasion" (abstract).

## Key insight

An intention-based theory and a commitment-based theory of illocution may
not be rivals at all. If expressing the belief that P is the same as taking
responsibility for P being the case, both theories say the same thing in
different vocabularies (pp. 34–35, 38). A choice between them would then
have to rest on something other than the illocutionary facts.

## Assumptions

- **The utterance of a sentence in a context is not sufficient for a
  speech act**; theories differ by what they add (abstract).
- **Belief-desire psychology** as the frame for explaining communication:
  the Gricean programme hopes to put "communicative uses of language
  within the reach of traditional psychological explanations" (p. 16).
- **"Internal" and "external" apply to force, not content.** Harnish
  accepts "certain forms of externalism with respect to content" (p. 15,
  n. 9).
- **Expressing in the non-achievement sense.** One need not have the
  attitude one expresses: one expresses "the belief that P", not
  necessarily "one's belief that P" (pp. 17, 34).

## Key results

- **The Austinian paradigm's shortfalls (pp. 12–14).** No analysis of
  convention, no principled taxonomy, no link to successful communication,
  no explanation of why speakers say what they say or how hearers
  understand, and no tie to the structure of a language.
- **EA-TIA (pp. 15–16).** The Gricean answer goes "internal". "Suppose a
  central class of illocutionary acts is communicative in nature, and
  suppose that the performance of illocutionary acts in this class can be
  defined in terms of the propositional attitude expressed by the speaker,
  and the understanding of such communicative acts can be defined as the
  recognition of such propositional attitudes by the hearer." Recognition
  "is guided by a shared system of inference strategies, presumptions, and
  contextual information" (p. 15). Then "kinds of (communicative)
  illocutionary acts can be defined in terms of the kinds of attitude(s)
  expressed", and "successful illocutionary communication can be
  characterized as the recognition of the expressed propositional
  attitude" (p. 15).
- **Expressing (pp. 16–22).** Bach and Harnish: "For S to express an
  attitude is for S to R-intend the hearer to take S's utterance as reason
  to think S has that attitude" (1979, p. 15, quoted p. 19), where to
  R-intend is to intend that the intention be recognized. Kemmerling's
  attempt at a stronger, "cp-analytical" notion fails, because lying,
  sarcasm and microphone tests are normal, and so "collapses into something
  'empirical' like Bach and Harnish" (pp. 20–22).
- **Objections to EA-TIA (pp. 22–23).** Among them, that it is not a
  single category: Austin, Searle and Alston give homogeneous theories,
  whereas "Strawson (1964) suggested a spectrum of conventionality, and Bach
  and Harnish (1979) sharply distinguished the two categories", the
  communicative and the institutional (pp. 22–23). Another objection is
  that illocution involves commitment, which expressed attitudes do not
  capture (p. 23).
- **Normative theories (pp. 23–24).** Alston: the utterance is made the act
  it is "not by any 'natural' facts about the speaker ... but by a
  'normative' fact about the speaker – the fact that he has changed his
  normative position in a certain way by laying himself open to the
  possibility of censure, correction, or the like in case the conditions in
  question are not satisfied" (Alston 2000, pp. 70–71, quoted p. 24).
- **Searle on commitment (pp. 25–28).** Searle (2001) says speech acts
  create desire-independent reasons by "imposing conditions of satisfaction
  on conditions of satisfaction". Harnish finds the phrase
  "quasi-mysterious", and "exactly how does imposing (cosocos) create
  commitment? ... we are just told that it does" (p. 27). Appealing to
  constitutive rules regresses, since it leaves open how speaking by rules
  creates commitment (pp. 27–28).
- **Alston on rule subjection (pp. 28–33).** Alston analyses taking
  responsibility (D5–D8) as subjecting one's utterance to a rule "in force
  within a community", with rules existing when deviations "evoke
  incorrectness judgments" (pp. 28–30). Harnish's counterexamples are
  conversational implicature, where no illocutionary rule licenses "I will
  be in London" only if one will miss the party, and parents'
  responsibility for their children, where community judgements "would be
  just symptoms of having undertaken a responsibility" (pp. 31–32). He
  concludes: "A speaker does not take responsibility for a certain set of
  conditions because his sentential act is subject to an illocutionary rule
  requiring it. Rather, S performs a certain illocutionary act because he
  takes responsibility for those conditions being the case" (p. 32).
- **Expressing and taking responsibility linked (pp. 33–38).** Harnish
  proposes "(E : C) In uttering e, S is expressing the belief that P : In
  uttering e, S takes responsibility for its being the case that P" (p. 35).
  He meets the objection that commitment comes in degrees and expressing
  does not (pp. 37–38). Conclusion: normative notions "do, however, seem
  connected to notions of being liable to criticism and blame. However, we
  suggested, Gricean notions of expressing an intentional state are
  likewise liable. So normative theory might at this level be simply
  another way of describing the same illocutionary facts" (p. 38).

## Concepts

- **EA-TIA**: the expressed-attitude theory of illocutionary acts, Bach
  and Harnish's Gricean theory (p. 15).
- **N-TIA**: any normative theory of illocutionary acts, using
  commitment, responsibility, blameworthiness (p. 23).
- **Expressing an attitude** (Bach and Harnish): R-intending the hearer
  to take the utterance as reason to think the speaker has it. It is
  non-achievement: the speaker need not have the attitude (pp. 17, 19).
- **R-intend**: intend reflexively, that is, intend that the intention be
  recognized (p. 18).
- **R'ing that P** (Alston): taking responsibility for its being the case
  that P; in D5, knowingly taking on liability to blame if not-P (p. 28).
- **Internal and external, of force**: whether what makes an utterance an
  illocution lies in the speaker's states or outside them (p. 15, n. 9).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md): partly. It confirms the rival's shape and adds a twist, but
  says nothing on teasing.** The claim describes the intention-based line
  as individuating communicative acts by speaker-internal states, and
  conceding institutional acts to convention. A co-author's text bears both
  out. EA-TIA goes "internal" (p. 15). Acts are defined by the attitude
  expressed (p. 15). Bach and Harnish "sharply distinguished" communicative
  from institutional acts (pp. 22–23). The 2026-10-13 correction, that
  uptake is a condition and not only evidence, holds here too:
  communication succeeds by recognition (p. 15). But what individuates the
  act is the attitude the speaker R-intends to be recognized, and
  recognition only measures success. The new point is Harnish's argument
  that the normative alternative may be equivalent (p. 38). Alston's
  normative theory makes the act a matter of the speaker's liability "to
  censure, correction, or the like", a relation to others (p. 24). If
  Harnish is right, that relation and the expressed attitude pick out the
  same acts, and the dispute [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md) describes may be partly verbal. The
  article does not mention teasing, reproach or standing. What [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md)
  waits on for teasing, Bach and Harnish's own treatment, is not met.
- **[QUESTION-073](../questions.d/QUESTION-073.md): partly.** The question asks whether teasing and reproach
  are individuated by communicative intention or partly by a jointly held
  frame and standing. On the evidence here, EA-TIA answers "intention" for
  every communicative act. It handles context as "contextual information"
  and mutual presumptions that guide the hearer's inference (p. 15), not as
  part of the act. That is the intention-based answer stated by its author,
  with no case of a fixed intention and a changing act. Its suggestion
  that liability to censure (a relation) may coincide with the expressed
  attitude bears on the 2026-10-13 note's open point: whether standing that
  enters through the intention counts as a relation. The article does not
  settle it.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **Not the book.** EA-TIA is summarized in two pages (pp. 15–16). The
  book's inference schema, its presumptions and its taxonomy are not
  presented.
- **Assertion-centred.** The positive argument (E : C) is made for belief
  and assertion only, though EA-TIA covers all communicative acts (p. 33).
- **Left open by the author.** "The really hard work is determining what
  undertaking a commitment or responsibility amounts to. That we must leave
  to another occasion" (abstract).

## Open questions

- Does (E : C) extend from assertion to directives and to acts like
  teasing, whose expressed attitude is not a belief?
- If liability to censure and the expressed attitude coincide, are they
  equivalent for an audience that does not share the speaker's standing?
  That is the case [QUESTION-073](../questions.d/QUESTION-073.md) asks for.
