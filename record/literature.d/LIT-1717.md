summary: >-
  Six experiments (N = 76, 101, 64, 32, 131, 92; U.S. undergraduates, MBA students and university staff) on how the valence of what is passed on affects concern for one's legacy and generosity to future others. Framing climate change as leaving burdens, rather than using up benefits, raised a generativity scale built from items about being remembered and living on (4.90 vs 4.10), direct concern with one's legacy, felt responsibility and affinity toward future generations, and ethical concern; recalling one's own past burden allocation raised moral emotions. In allocation tasks people favoured themselves more with benefits than with burdens. The difference appeared only when the recipient was in the future (not in a dictator-game version with a present recipient), and only when the allocator was a party to the trade-off (not when allocating between two other people). One item on ethical considerations statistically accounted for the first of these. The authors read the pattern as legacy concern, which they define as the wish to extend the self beyond death, engaged more by leaving burdens than by withholding benefits. Legacy concern was measured only in the attitude studies, never in the allocation studies.
