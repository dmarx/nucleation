# CASE-tmpa82q4: Wonder-working power: one hymn phrase in a national address, heard as faith by those who sing it and as boilerplate by others

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for: a change of audience
without translation in which the wording is identical, the condition
[CASE-tmpgdy8a](CASE-tmpgdy8a.md) fails because its token is a photograph. The owner suggested
slang and coded in-group language, and TV Tropes's lists, as places to look.
The lists are described below as a pool of items, not as evidence.

## The case

**The source wording.** "Power in the Blood", by Lewis E. Jones (1899), is a
gospel hymn printed in 467 hymnals. Its refrain begins "There is power,
power, wonder working power" and goes on "in the blood of the Lamb"
(hymnary.org, refrain first line).

**The same wording before a new audience.** George W. Bush, State of the
Union, 28 January 2003, on the White House's transcript: "Yet there's power,
wonder-working power, in the goodness and idealism and faith of the American
people." The phrase is verbatim from the hymn. It was spoken to a national
audience that was not a congregation.

**Uptake by the in-group.** James Heflin, the son of a Baptist minister,
writing in April 2003, recognised the line ("I know it well") and took it as
Bush "stealthily passing the message to the flock, to my flock".

**Uptake by the out-group.** Timothy Noah (*Slate*, October 2004), who
learned the reading from others, wrote that the phrase refers to "the power
of Christ, though Bush used it in a seemingly secular context", and that
such phrases deliver "a religious message over the heads of plodding secular
humanists like me." Jennifer Saul's analysis (2016 draft, p. 4): "To a
non-fundamentalist this is an ordinary piece of fluffy political
boilerplate, which passes without notice. But a fundamentalist Christian
will hear the dogwhistle." She separates two in-group messages, a religious
content and the fact "that Bush does speak their idiolect—indicating that he
is one of them."

**Uptake measured.** Bethany Albertson (*Political Behavior*, 2015) tested
"multivocal and obvious religious appeals experimentally with two national
samples: an ingroup that understands the religious connotations in a
multivocal appeal and a religiously diverse outgroup that does not."
"Religious appeals are persuasive for the ingroup ... while the religious
meaning in a multivocal appeal eludes them" (abstract). Norma
Mendoza-Denton (*Dædalus*, 2023) reports her result on this phrase: "89
percent of Pentecostals recognized the reference as coming from a well-known
church hymn ... only 9 percent of a more general subject population." That
figure is a secondary report; the article itself was not read.

What is held fixed is the wording: one sentence, and in it a phrase carried
verbatim from the hymn. What differs is the audience: those who sing the
hymn, and the rest of a national audience.

## What it can show

That with the wording held fixed, which act a hearer takes an utterance to
perform depends on the hearer's relation to a practice the words come from.
For the hymn's singers the sentence is an appeal to shared faith and a sign
of membership (Saul's second message). For others it is secular praise of
the public. Both audiences' uptake is documented independently of the
speaker and of the record, and the split was measured.

That is [CLAIM-tmpcq79k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpcq79k.md)'s point met without translation and without any change
of token. [CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md) version 2 answers the objection by making the
constituents relations to participant roles, so that a new audience in the
same role keeps them. Here the two audiences hear one token at once, and the
role the speaker addresses ("the flock") is held only by those who know the
hymn. Nothing in the words tells a hearer which role he is in. It is the
residue the objection keeps open, whether the norms that define the role are
shared, shown inside one language and one broadcast.

It is a variant of [CASE-tmpgdy8a](CASE-tmpgdy8a.md) in two respects: the token is words, not an
image, and the speaker made the split on purpose. It illustrates and bears
no weight.

## What it cannot show

**The condition it meets only partly: a change of audience.** The phrase
moves from congregational singing to a national broadcast, but the speech's
audience is mixed at once rather than changed afterwards, as Lindsey Stone's
was. And the split is designed. A dog whistle is a deliberate collapse of
contexts, not an accidental one.

**The strongest reply.** The speaker intended both readings. An
intention-based account ([CLAIM-tmpc25sn](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpc25sn.md)) says this was one act, a multivocal
appeal, and the two audiences recovered different parts of it. On that
reading the audiences differ in what they recognise, not in a verdict on
which act was done, and the case shows that uptake moves with the audience,
not that the audience's role constitutes the act. Albertson measures
recognition and persuasion, not act category.

**The outgroup uptake is thin as testimony.** Noah writes as an outsider who
had been told the reading; Saul's sentence is an analyst's claim. Only the
experiment measures the outgroup, and its stimulus and figures are
unverified (see Sources).

**What would close the gap.** A reader test that holds the sentence fixed
and varies only whether the reader knows the hymn, as a change of situation,
do(S), rather than an instruction, do(I) ([CLAIM-130](../claims.d/CLAIM-130.md)): one group hears the
hymn's refrain first, one does not, one is told "this is a hymn phrase".
Raters judge the act category (religious appeal, secular praise, sign of
membership) separately from recognition of the source and from the intention
they attribute. The sentence, with and without its hymn context, could be an
item in the no-rendering arm of [CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md).

## A pool of items: TV Tropes's audience-split lists

TV Tropes ([CASE-038](CASE-038.md)) keeps lists of lines whose force splits by audience.
tvtropes.org returned 403 to scripted requests on 2026-10-10 and the
archives were unreachable from the search's environment, so the lists were
read on Tropedia (tropedia.fandom.com), a fan-run fork of TV Tropes, through
its ordinary API. Whether Tropedia's text matches today's TV Tropes is
unverified. Counts are example lines at the first and second level of the
page.

- **Have a Gay Old Time.** "Language drift ... causes a word or phrase
  originally intended as wholly innocuous to be potentially taken as
  startling, confusing or just plain funny in a different time or place."
  Named for the last line of *The Flintstones* theme. Identical wording, an
  audience changed by time. About 440 and 290 lines.
- **Parental Bonus.** "A joke on a children's TV show that children of the
  appropriate age would likely never get, but which their parents would."
  One token, two audiences at once, split by age. About 130 and 330 lines.
- **Getting Crap Past the Radar.** Lines meant to "sneak some manner of
  profanity or other forbidden material past the network censors". The
  censor is the audience that misses it. Examples are mostly on subpages,
  not read. Tropedia's *Round the Horne* page files Julian and Sandy's Polari
  here, the slang case the owner had in mind; the documentation for it is
  Paul Baker's work, not read.
- **Bilingual Bonus.** Messages "available only to those viewers ... that
  have sufficient knowledge of the language in question". One token, split
  by language.
- **Values Dissonance.** Tropes that "don't travel - or age - very well". An
  index, not item-level.

What they offer: hundreds of naturally occurring lines with identical
wording and a named pair of audiences. What they lack: any record of either
audience's uptake. Each split is asserted by an editor who belongs to the
knowing audience, few entries cite anything, and descriptions are written to
fit examples ([CLAIM-145](../claims.d/CLAIM-145.md)). They are candidates, not evidence. For
[CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md) they could supply items for an arm with no rendering: lines
drawn by a stated sampling rule, the hearer's membership in the knowing
audience varied as a situation, and the act labelled by raters who did not
select the items. Parental Bonus and Bilingual Bonus are closest to
[CLAIM-tmpcq79k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpcq79k.md) (one token, two audiences at once); Have a Gay Old Time is
cleanest on identical wording.
