# NOTE-tmpsn285: Basic psychological need theory: Advancements, critical themes, and future directions

## Contribution

An editorial review introducing two special issues of Motivation and
Emotion (19 papers, Table 1). Its lasting content is the explicit list of
criteria for a basic need, which turns the functional definition of 2000
([LIT-tmpc4275](../literature.d/LIT-tmpc4275.md)) into tests a candidate can pass or fail, and the
reorganization of the theory around need frustration as well as
satisfaction.

## Key insight

A need is defined by what its satisfaction and frustration do, so the way to
argue about a new need is to test each criterion. Beneficence is the worked
case: its satisfaction adds to well-being, but its frustration "failed to
predict incremental variance above the frustration of autonomy, competence,
and relatedness", so it is classed a "well-being enhancer", not a need
(p. 8). The same criteria make self-esteem, security and power derivative:
they surface when other needs are frustrated (pp. 5, 7).

## Assumptions

- **The organismic metatheory** of SDT, restated: people "naturally develop in
  the direction of increasing adaptation, integration, and coherence where
  possible" (p. 4).
- **Need experiences are measurable by self-report**, chiefly the Basic
  Psychological Need Satisfaction and Frustration Scale (BPNSFS; p. 10).
- **Universality is a claim about effects**, not about mean levels or about
  which practices work (pp. 18–19).

## Key results

- **Definitions (p. 3).** Autonomy is "the experience of volition and
  willingness", with "a sense of integrity as when one's actions, thoughts,
  and feelings are self-endorsed and authentic"; frustrated, "a sense of
  pressure and often conflict". Relatedness: warmth, bonding, care;
  frustrated, alienation, exclusion, loneliness. Competence: effectiveness
  and mastery; frustrated, ineffectiveness, failure, helplessness.
- **Nine criteria (Table 2, pp. 4–7).** Basic: psychological; essential;
  inherent (evolved); distinct (not derivative of another need's
  frustration); universal (regardless of demographics, personality, culture or
  need strength). Associated: pervasive (many outcomes, many levels including
  neural); content-specific (concrete experiences, present in natural
  language); directional (people seek need-satisfying settings); explanatory
  (needs mediate between contexts and outcomes). SDT thereby "provides an
  antidote to relativism" (p. 7).
- **Theme 1, candidate needs (pp. 7–9).** Novelty, novelty-variety and
  morality passed an initial test; beneficence did not (frustration not
  unique). Facets within a need (giving versus receiving relatedness) are
  proposed as an alternative to adding needs (p. 9).
- **Theme 2, need frustration (pp. 9–12).** Satisfaction and frustration are
  asymmetric (p. 9). Frustration predicts amotivation, disengagement,
  stress, depressive symptoms, suicidal ideation beyond depression, and death
  anxiety in older adults (p. 10). Three kinds of cost: direct ill-being;
  compensatory behaviour (rigid routines, contingent self-worth, oppositional
  defiance); need substitutes (extrinsic goals, a drive for muscularity)
  (pp. 10–11). Proposed as transdiagnostic, in line with "the continuity
  hypothesis" that there is "no abrupt demarcation" between growth and
  psychopathology (p. 12).
- **Theme 3, physical needs (pp. 13–14).** Need frustration predicts poor
  sleep two years later (Uysal et al. 2020); three nights of sleep restriction
  eroded need satisfaction only after the third night (Campbell et al. 2018c);
  under poverty and unsafety, the three needs still predicted well-being, and
  safety did not moderate them (Chen et al. 2015b), against Maslow's
  prepotency (p. 14).
- **Theme 4, need-relevant practices (pp. 14–17).** Autonomy support and
  structure are not opposed. A circumplex (Aelterman et al. 2019, Fig. 2)
  orders teaching and coaching practices; its "attuning" and "guiding" areas
  correlate most with need satisfaction and its "abandoning" and
  "domineering" areas with need frustration (p. 16), and the "participative"
  approach is "merely need-enabling" (p. 20). The figure's full set of labels
  did not survive the text layer. Interventions can train
  teachers, parents and coaches to be more need-supportive (p. 15).
- **Theme 5, universality (pp. 17–20).** Interactions explain little next to
  main effects; there are no cross-over interactions; more moderation is
  found on the input side (which practices satisfy) than the output side
  (what satisfaction does) (pp. 19–20). Ghanaian adolescents did not benefit
  from parental provision of choice, but did from perspective taking
  (Marbell-Pierre et al. 2019, p. 20).

## Concepts

- **basic psychological need** (BPNT): "a psychological nutrient that is
  essential for individuals' adjustment, integrity, and growth" (p. 1).
- **need frustration**: an "active threat" to a need, not its mere absence
  (p. 9).
- **need substitute**: a preference that develops to cope with chronic
  frustration and promises to relieve it, e.g. extrinsic goals (p. 11).
- **input side / output side**: contexts → need experiences, versus need
  experiences → outcomes (Fig. 3, p. 18).
- **universality without uniformity**: needs universal in effect, variable in
  how they are met (p. 17).

## Connections

- **Deci & Ryan (2000), [LIT-tmpc4275](../literature.d/LIT-tmpc4275.md)**, which it extends, and Ryan & Deci
  (2000), [LIT-tmpckq0r](../literature.d/LIT-tmpckq0r.md).
- **ACT and psychological flexibility ([LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md), [LIT-553](../literature.d/LIT-553.md); [THEORY-tmpdbe64](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpdbe64.md)).**
  Both programmes now propose a transdiagnostic process: psychological
  inflexibility there, need frustration here. They meet in the same
  phenomena: rigid routines that bring short-lived relief and keep
  self-worth contingent (pp. 10–11) are what ACT calls experiential avoidance
  and inflexibility. Neither literature is cited by the other here. The two
  claims are compatible (need frustration could produce avoidance), but each
  names a different process as the one to target, so they compete for the
  same explanatory role.
- **Process-based therapy (Hofmann & Hayes, [LIT-544](../literature.d/LIT-544.md)).** This paper's hope
  that interventions "oriented towards ameliorating the origins of
  psychological need frustration" would yield "deep-level, enduring, and
  multiple benefits" (p. 12) is a process-based programme in Hofmann and
  Hayes's sense, with need frustration as the process. Its evidence is
  correlational, so it does not yet meet their standard of a process shown to
  mediate change.
- **Frijda ([LIT-540](../literature.d/LIT-540.md)).** No connection drawn in the paper. Emotion regulation
  appears only as an outcome of need satisfaction (p. 5).

## Limitations

- **Selective by design.** "Our review is necessarily selective" (p. 2); it
  introduces papers by the special issues' authors.
- **Written by the theory's authors.** Criticisms of SDT appear only as
  positions the review answers (cultural relativism, Maslow).
- **Self-report on both sides.** Most need-frustration findings correlate a
  self-report of frustration with a self-report of distress.
- **The criteria are not shown to exclude anything except by example.**
  Beneficence is the one candidate rejected, on one study.

## Open questions

- Does need frustration precede the symptoms it is said to explain, measured
  apart from distress? The paper asks for "longitudinal designs" (p. 12).
- Would a fourth need survive the frustration test that beneficence failed?
