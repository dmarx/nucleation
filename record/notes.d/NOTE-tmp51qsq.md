# NOTE-tmp51qsq: Mazinani and O'Connor (2026): An Updated and Revised Dyadic Model of Partner Violence

## Contribution

Updates Bartholomew and Cobb's (2011) dyadic model of partner violence with meta-analytic evidence. It adds victimization as well as perpetration, and gender comparisons. It also states what a dyadic framing does and does not commit one to.

## Key results

1. Abstract: "Eight peer-reviewed meta-analyses published between 2000 and 2024 formed the core synthesis." It is "a selective review and conceptual synthesis of meta-analytic evidence, rather than a systematic or structured review" (§3.1).
2. Abstract: "Across the reviewed meta-analyses, the most consistent risk markers of IPV clustered within the relationship-context level, particularly prior partner aggression, emotional abuse, and relationship dissatisfaction." "Communication skills and empathy were inversely associated with IPV risk."
3. §5.1: "Other relationship-context markers included demand/withdraw interaction patterns (r=0.37), controlling behavior (r=0.30), and greater perpetrator power within the relationship (r=0.18)."
4. §5.3: "Spencer et al. (2022) found that male partners’ controlling behaviors were not significantly associated with women’s IPV perpetration."
5. §7: "the present synthesis does not constitute empirical validation of the model’s integrated temporal or causal pathways".
6. §2.2: "This relational framing does not imply equivalent agency, mutuality, or shared responsibility; responsibility for a violent or coercive act lies with the person who commits it."
7. §7: "In such cases, a victim’s resistance or constrained responses should not be treated as equivalent to the perpetrator’s controlling violence."
8. §7: "Effect sizes based primarily on the occurrence of physical acts may not capture motive, coercive control, fear, chronicity, severity, injury, sexual violence, or constraints on safety and leaving."

## Assumptions

That risk markers from separate meta-analyses can be placed on the model's levels by one author's mapping (§3.3). It also assumes the source meta-analyses can be read together despite overlapping primary studies (§7.3). The scope is physical IPV in adult heterosexual relationships.

## Concepts

The dyadic model's four levels (background and dispositional, relationship context, situational context, pattern of violence); actor and partner effects; Dutton's nested ecological theory as the individual-focused contrast; Johnson's typology as complementary: "The frameworks therefore address different questions and can be used together". Bidirectional violence in a minimal sense: "The term does not imply equivalence in motive, coercive control, severity, consequences, or responsibility."

## Bearing on the record

- **The exchange** (A5 §6) summarizes the review accurately. Its contrast between a "systemic perspective" that "can risk obscuring asymmetrical responsibility" and the coercive-control perspective is A's. This paper anticipates the risk and disclaims it.
- **CLAIM-tmpp8na7**: corroborates, as an independent statement of the separation the claim makes, in a source that is not part of the exchange. It is the authors' framing, not a finding. Relational analysis of how violence arises is kept apart from responsibility, and resistance is not equated with controlling violence.
- **CASE-tmpwrhkd**: qualifies its realism, not its coherence. In pooled data, men's controlling behaviour was not significantly associated with women's perpetration (result 4), so the case's pattern is not shown to be a common route.
- **CLAIM-tmpnw7ez**: the paper affirms the first-order half (responsibility stays with the one who acts) and is silent on responsibility for conditions.

## Limitations

Selective and unregistered, with an unreproducible search. One coder did the mapping. The source meta-analyses were not appraised and overlap. The evidence is physical IPV in Western heterosexual samples, and correlational: no actor, partner or cross-level effects were tested.
