# NOTE-024: Rhode Island Hospital subpoena order

## Contribution

This is the first ruling I found in which a court quashed an administrative subpoena that another district court had already ordered enforced, ex parte, without notice to the recipient. The court says it is "unaware of any similar case" (p. 10 n.7). It is also the order in the DOJ gender-affirming-care subpoena campaign that rests quashal on the minors' Fourteenth Amendment right to informational privacy as an independent ground. It adds a remedy-scope holding: relief extends to all minors because separating out state wards would itself disclose the protected fact.

## Key insight

The court avoids a direct collision with the Texas court by attacking the subpoena, not the enforcement order. Because a § 3486 subpoena is "the product of the DOJ's own statutory authority" and not issued by any court, the court reasons by analogy to APA vacatur. On that reasoning it may "effectively vacate (i.e., quash)" the agency instrument even though doing so "necessarily implicates" another court's judgment premised on it (p. 12). The Texas court then used exactly that framing to keep its own order alive. It read the Rhode Island injunction as binding only DOJ and ordered RIH to deposit all responsive records with the Texas court in camera (N.D. Tex. ECF 26, May 18, 2026).

## Assumptions

The authorities and standards the order rests on:
- **Enforcement standard for § 3486 subpoenas (First Circuit):** DOJ must show (1) a congressionally authorized purpose, (2) relevance, (3) adequate description, and (4) proper procedures. U.S. DOJ v. Ricco Jonas, 24 F.4th 718, 726 (1st Cir. 2022), quoting Sturm, Ruger, 84 F.3d 1, 4 (p. 14). The order treats this enforcement test as governing a motion to quash.
- **Standing:** Lujan (injury, causation, redressability) and First Choice Women's Res. Ctrs. v. Davenport (2026) (p. 8). The Child Advocate's statutory role comes from R.I. Gen. Laws § 42-73-7 (p. 11).
- **Collateral attack and preclusion:**
  - Pratt v. Ventas (6th Cir.) and Verogna v. Johnstone (D.N.H.) for the doctrine;
  - Baella-Silva and In re Iannochino (1st Cir.) for res judicata elements;
  - Martin v. Wilks, 490 U.S. 755 (1989), and Hansberry v. Lee: nonparties are not bound. The order acknowledges in n.8 that Wilks was superseded by statute as to consent decrees;
  - Taylor v. Sturgell: the adequate-representation exception;
  - Postal Telegraph Cable Co. v. Newport (1918) and Blonder-Tongue: no preclusion without an opportunity to be heard (pp. 9–13).
- **FDCA law:**
  - United States v. Facteau, 89 F.4th 1, 15 (1st Cir. 2023): off-label prescribing of a cleared device is lawful;
  - In re Celexa & Lexapro, 915 F.3d 1, 5 (1st Cir. 2019);
  - 21 U.S.C. § 396;
  - 21 U.S.C. § 353(b)(2): the prescription-dispensing exemption from § 352;
  - Gonzales v. Oregon: state regulation of medicine;
  - R.I. Gen. Laws §§ 5-37.8-1, 23-101-2;
  - FDA public statements (2018; Jan. 2025 guidance);
  - OLC, 43 Op. O.L.C. 81, 85 (2019) (pp. 14–17).
- **Improper purpose:** United States v. Powell, 379 U.S. 48, 58 (1964); United States v. Comley (1st Cir. 1989); United States v. Gertner, 65 F.3d 963, 970 (1st Cir. 1995), under which quashal is warranted when improper purpose is the *sole* purpose (p. 18).
- **Informational privacy:**
  - Whalen v. Roe, 429 U.S. 589, 599 (1977);
  - NASA v. Nelson (2011), which assumed the right without deciding it;
  - Dobbs (2022), cited for describing Whalen's "right to shield information from disclosure";
  - Borucki v. Ryan (1st Cir. 1987);
  - Vega-Rodriguez (1st Cir. 1997);
  - Arroyo González v. Rosselló Nevares (D.P.R. 2018) (pp. 20–21).

  The court reserves whether the Fourth Amendment independently applies (n.10).

## Key results

What the order holds (ECF 38, pp. as paginated; ECF 44 is identical in substance except the injunction):
- **Standing (§II.A, pp. 8–9):** the Child Advocate has standing. The children's informational-privacy rights "will surely be injured", the injury is caused by the subpoena, and it is redressable "notwithstanding the Texas court's Order".
- **Collateral attack (§II.B, pp. 9–13):** the doctrine does not bar relief.
  - (a) The Child Advocate was not a party in Texas, and its interests differ from RIH's. The Texas court itself said RIH had not shown harm to itself "rather than its patients, who are third parties" (N.D. Tex. ECF 9 at 9, quoted p. 11).
  - (b) Quashing an agency-issued subpoena is analogous to APA vacatur, so it is not a collateral attack even though it "necessarily implicates" the Texas order (p. 12).
  - (c) RIH is not precluded, because the Texas order was entered before RIH had notice or an opportunity to be heard. Reconsideration in Texas would have demanded "extraordinary relief" (p. 13).
- **Ground 1, no congressionally authorized purpose (§II.C.1, pp. 14–18):** DOJ's theory runs as follows. An off-label prescription changes "intended use" (21 C.F.R. § 201.128), which makes labeling inadequate under § 352(f)(1), which exposes anyone in the distribution chain, including hospitals and (via Park) officers, to liability under §§ 331, 333(a). The court holds the theory cannot be squared with Facteau and Celexa or with § 396. Because prescribing is lawful, "it is therefore logically impossible to construct an aiding-and-abetting, facilitation, or conspiracy theory predicated on nothing more than the physician's own lawful prescribing act" (p. 16). DOJ's position also conflicts with FDA statements and OLC's 2019 opinion. Independently, § 353(b)(2) exempts prescription-dispensed drugs from § 352(f) (p. 17). Theories of conspiracy, billing fraud and healthcare fraud "are also tethered to off-label use and fail for the same reasons" (n.9).
- **Ground 2, improper purpose and bad faith (§II.C.2, pp. 18–19):** the Administration publicly characterized gender-affirming care for minors as abuse, directed DOJ to end it, and celebrated hospitals curtailing programs (citing ECF 20-1). The court rejects DOJ's Gertner argument: a mixed purpose requires "a genuine legitimate investigative purpose, not merely a legal theory". Otherwise DOJ could "immunize any coercive investigation from the improper purpose doctrine simply by appending an untenable legal theory to it" (p. 19). It follows seven listed decisions.
- **Ground 3, informational privacy (§II.C.3, pp. 19–23):** full enforcement "would violate the right to informational privacy of the children". DOJ failed to explain relevance or safeguards, and conceded it would try to locate and question children and caregivers (n.12).
- **Scope of relief (pp. 22–23):** the court rejects DOJ's proposed DCYF-only limitation. Identifying DCYF wards among gender-affirming-care patients "would itself expose precisely the sensitive intersection of information the constitutional right is designed to protect". Relief extends to all minors "not from any expansion of the Child Advocate's standing, but from the practical impossibility of implementing a narrower remedy".
- **Findings on DOJ conduct (pp. 1–7):**
  - DOJ told the court RIH "never complied and never moved to quash" (ECF 9 at 2) and omitted that it had not answered RIH's February search-term proposal.
  - DOJ asked RIH on April 28 to confer "this week", then filed ex parte in N.D. Tex. about 48 hours later. The court calls this "a subterfuge".
  - The Hsiao declaration's statement that the last communication was Feb. 4, 2026 was "clearly misleading, if not utterly false".
  - The declaration's claim that identifying data was necessary omitted that DOJ had accepted anonymized data elsewhere: "at best, deceptive".
  - DOJ could not say when the investigation moved to N.D. Tex.
  - The court credits RIH over DOJ counsel on whether anonymization was discussed (n.3).
- **Disposition (pp. 23–24):** both motions to quash (ECF 1, 28) are granted. The subpoena, not the Texas order, is quashed on "independent grounds". DOJ is enjoined as described above. Judgment was entered the same day (ECF 39).

## Concepts

- **HIPAA subpoena**: the colloquial name for an administrative subpoena under 18 U.S.C. § 3486, issued in investigations of federal health care offenses (n.2).
- **quashal vs. vacatur of an order**: as the order uses them, quashal acts on the agency's instrument, and the court treats it as distinct from setting aside a court's enforcement order (pp. 12, 23).
- **collateral attack doctrine**: as framed here, rooted in res judicata, and therefore limited by party identity and by the need for a prior opportunity to be heard (pp. 10–13).
- **informational privacy**: the "individual interest in avoiding disclosure of personal matters" (Whalen, quoted p. 20).

**Relation to the Texas order, in short:** two circuits hold live, facially conflicting district orders on one subpoena. Texas says enforce, and records are now held in camera there. Rhode Island says the subpoena is quashed and DOJ may not seek or receive the records. Each court frames its order so as not to vacate the other's. Neither appellate court had resolved the conflict as of the last docket entries I could see.

## Bearing on the record

The order bears on government compelled access to health records and on the constitutional right to informational privacy. Its remedy reasoning is that segregating a protected subgroup can itself disclose membership in that group. That point is a familiar one in data de-identification and is stated here as a legal finding. It carries no instruction for ML practice, so there is nothing for the Anthology of the SOTA. For a privacy or data-governance reading in this record, cite it with its appellate status. Do not cite it as settled law on informational privacy or on § 3486 purpose review.

## Limitations

- It is a district-court order on an expedited timetable. The court ruled one day after the hearing and one day before the Texas compliance deadline.
- Its collateral-attack analysis is expressly tentative ("appear", "seems", "seems likely"), and the court says it knows of no analogous case.
- The informational-privacy holding does not state a balancing standard or address federal (rather than state) action. It relies on a right the Supreme Court has only assumed (Nelson).
- The improper-purpose ground largely adopts other courts' reasoning, and at least one of those courts has since been reversed on that ground.
- The order's rhetoric about DOJ, including the footnote quoting the Texas judge's Federalist Society remarks on forum shopping (n.1), goes beyond what the holdings require. The court says its "decision is based solely on its application of the law".
- Citation errors: § 3846 on p. 12, and the date of Texas ECF 12.
- The order does not resolve the conflict with the Texas order. It left RIH subject to both, and the later Texas and First Circuit orders show that the conflict continued.

## Open questions

- Will the First Circuit affirm any of the three grounds? The Dunlap concurrence and QueerDoc suggest the purpose-based grounds are the most exposed. The Child Advocate is pressing the privacy ground as independent. The decision after the Oct. 7, 2026 argument will settle this.
- How will the Fifth Circuit rule on the enforcement order and on the RIH July notice of appeal? Unverified.
- What happens to the in camera records if the circuits disagree? Only a Supreme Court decision, or one side's appeal failing, seems likely to resolve that; this is speculative.
- What is the status of the other six decisions the order relied on? Unverified. A Fourth Circuit appeal in Children's National exists (No. 26-1104), but I did not check its docket.
