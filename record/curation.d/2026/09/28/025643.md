## The account, piece by piece, and where each piece comes from

Each claim below is marked **first-hand** (read in a Sen text here) or
**second-hand** (reported by a secondary source, which is named).

1. **The negative argument (first-hand, 1979).** Three measures of advantage
   are each insufficient, even for the needs-based part of equality:
   - *utilitarian equality* (equal marginal utility), because
     sum-maximisation gives the less efficient converter of income into
     utility *less* income, pp. 203–204;
   - *total-utility equality*, completed as leximin, because a contented
     disabled person with low aspirations shows no utility deficit,
     pp. 217–218;
   - *Rawls's primary goods*, which are "fetishist" because they take goods
     "as the embodiment of advantage, rather than taking advantage to be a
     relationship between persons and goods", p. 216.

   The disabled person's claim is tracked by none of them.
2. **The positive proposal (first-hand, 1979).** "Basic capability
   equality", meaning "a person being able to do certain basic things"
   (p. 218). Sen offers it only as "a partial guide" to the equality part of
   morality, and says the negative thesis stands without it (p. 220). The
   index of capability bundles yields only a *partial ordering*, completed
   by culture-dependent conventions (p. 219).

   The 1979 text never uses "functioning", "capability set", "agency" or
   "freedom". Accounts that credit it with the full later vocabulary, or
   with *replacing* utility and primary goods, read later Sen back into it.
   Robeyns does this (p. 76 n. 39; [NOTE-tmpmfu9t](../../record/notes.d/NOTE-tmpmfu9t.md)), and so does the IEP entry
   in places.
3. **The mature vocabulary (second-hand).**
   - Functionings are achieved beings and doings; capabilities are the
     real opportunities to achieve them; the capability set is the menu
     (per the SEP and the IEP).
   - Conversion factors are why equal resources yield unequal capabilities
     (per all three secondary sources).
   - Adaptive preferences are why utility misleads (per the IEP, quoting
     *Commodities and Capabilities*).
   - Well-being freedom is distinguished from agency freedom, and freedom
     from achievement. This is attributed to the Dewey Lectures (1985) by
     the SEP (§2.5) alone; the IEP never mentions it. No Sen text for it
     was reachable.
4. **The social-choice grounding (first-hand, 1998).**
   - *What escapes Arrow is interpersonal comparability.* Even ordinal
     comparisons break the exact impossibility, and partial comparability
     often suffices. Capabilities enter only as *one* candidate answer to
     "comparison of what?", which Sen declines to make exclusive.
   - *The liberal paradox is different.* Sen proved it in 1970, and the
     reader checked the proof. It is explicitly *not* resolved by
     comparability (p. 199).
   - *A correction to the scoping report.* It ran these steps together.
5. **The "no fixed list" stance and public reasoning (second-hand).** Sen
   refuses "one pre-determined canonical list" (SEP §3.3, quoting "Human
   Rights and Capabilities" 2005, p. 158). The two overviews disagree about
   what replaces the list:
   - the IEP credits Sen with a social-choice procedure of "intersection" and
     "ranges of weights" (§3b);
   - the SEP says he has "never explained in detail" how selection should
     work (§3.3).
6. **Justice (second-hand, via two readers of the 2009 book).**
   - *Comparative, not transcendental.* Sen argues for a comparative,
     "realization-focused" approach against "transcendental
     institutionalism" (Valentini; Zwolinski).
   - *Positional objectivity* and the impartial spectator (Zwolinski).
   - *Freedom* is "an inescapably plural idea" (Zwolinski, IoJ p. 305).
   - *Human rights* whose realisation need not be the state's task
     (Zwolinski, IoJ p. 364).

   The two readers agree on what the book says and disagree on whether it
   matters. The SEP adds that the capability approach is not itself a theory
   of justice (§4). The IEP calls Sen a "comprehensive consequentialist",
   and the SEP calls that contested.

## The critiques as the record now holds them

- **Nussbaum: the list and the threshold.** The approach yields no theory
  of justice without a definite, revisable list of central capabilities,
  owed up to a threshold. She gives ten. "Freedom" is not a general good,
  since some freedoms are trivial or unjust. She endorses Sen's arguments
  against GNP, utility and resources. Her threshold is named but not
  specified in this paper, and Sen's side appears only as her reports of
  his oral remarks.
- **Valentini: method.** Each of Sen's complaints against transcendental
  theory either misreads Rawls or is true but inconsequential. "If there are
  transcendental institutionalists, Rawls is not one of them." She says
  nothing about capabilities.
- **Robeyns: the modular reply.** The core is eight "A-module" commitments;
  particular theories fill seven compulsory-but-open modules. She uses this
  to argue that the standard critiques (no list, individualism, power,
  liberalism) hit particular capability theories, not the approach. The
  approach is committed to ethical, not ontological, individualism. She is
  an advocate and Sen's former student; read her as a reconstruction that
  generalises Sen, including dropping his "reason to value" clause.
- **Pogge's resourcist critique is not represented.** None of the nine texts
  states his argument. The scoping report expected Robeyns 2017 and the SEP
  to carry it, and both readers found that neither does. Pogge 2002 itself
  is paywalled.

## Errors found in the secondary sources (checked against Sen 1979)

- **The SEP inverts Sen's pleasure-wizard.** In Sen the pleasure-wizard is
  person B, not the disabled person A (p. 203). I checked this against the
  text when filing. The SEP also merges his argument against utilitarianism
  with his separate argument against total-utility equality (§§1.1, 2.6).
- **Robeyns over-attributes to the 1979 lecture.**
  - She has it argue that almost any theory of justice is egalitarian in some
    space (p. 94 n. 2). The lecture makes no such argument; the reader
    thinks the thesis is from *Inequality Reexamined* (1992), unverified.
  - She counts it as endorsing her whole A-module (p. 76 n. 39).
- **The IEP** is internally inconsistent on when the approach began ("1980s"
  against 1979), and drops Sen's condition from the utilitarian argument.

## What is missing, and why

**Sen's own statements.** None of these is reachable legitimately, and all
are represented only second-hand through the SEP and the IEP:

- the Dewey Lectures (1985), the source for agency freedom;
- *Capability and Well-Being* (1993);
- *Capabilities, Lists, and Public Reason* (2004);
- *Elements of a Theory of Human Rights* (2004);
- *Human Rights and Capabilities* (2005);
- *Development as Capability Expansion* (1989).

**Critics.** Pogge (2002) and Anderson (1999) are also unreachable.

**Blocked hosts.** Sen's Harvard page, the Tanner Lectures site, PhilArchive
and Open Book Publishers' own site refused automated requests. The owner may
be able to fetch copies in a browser. The Dewey Lectures and the 2004
*Lists* paper would most improve the account.

**Dating conventions.**

- Valentini's `published:` is the November 2010 working paper, which is her
  first appearance ([ADR-002](../../record/decisions.d/ADR-002.md)).
- Zwolinski's issue date is inferred and unverified.
- Nussbaum was read from her own preprint, so her page numbers are not the
  journal's.

**Existing record links.**

- [LIT-202](../../record/literature.d/LIT-202.md) (preference formation) is cited for adaptive preferences, but only
  thematically: it cites Elster and Khader, not Sen.
- [LIT-146](../../record/literature.d/LIT-146.md) treats Nussbaum's capabilities as a value theory for animals.
