# THEORY-tmp85a21: Kilduff, Elfenbein and Staw report that rivalry among NCAA men's basketball teams, rated by student sportswriters, varied mostly with the particular pair of teams, grew with proximity, similarity, repeated play and above all a history of even contests, and was largely reciprocated, one-sided rivalry running from lower-status to higher-status teams

## The account

Rivalry is a subjective competitive relationship that raises the
psychological stakes of competing with a particular opponent, holding
objective stakes fixed. It is a property of the pair, and competition is
"path-dependent" (p. 948): contests long since decided keep shaping how
much the next one matters. Once the historic head-to-head balance is in
the model, similarity in all-time winning percentage drops out.

## Evidence

From NOTE-1292: a round-robin design analysed with the social
relations model, consistent across all six conferences; reciprocity
r = .64 raw and .85 net of each team's general tendencies; where
rivalry was one-sided, lower-status teams felt it toward higher-status
ones. The design is cross-sectional, and scheduling could follow
rivalry.

## What it bears on

QUESTION-298 asks whether, when fans perceive their rivalry as
reciprocated, the rival's fans in fact return it. Its note of 2026-10-29
names this work as partial evidence: rivalry measured from both sides
was largely returned, at the level of teams, not fans. It predicts
where fans' perceived reciprocity would be wrong if fans follow their
teams.
