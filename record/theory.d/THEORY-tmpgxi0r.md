# THEORY-tmpgxi0r: Told in the third person, a transfer of memories and character between two bodies reads as the persons exchanging bodies; told in the first person, no predicted psychological change removes the subject's reason to fear pain to his own body, and the step that completes the exchange happens only to the other man, so the two tellings support opposite criteria

## Source

Williams (1970), "The Self and the Future", *The Philosophical Review*
79(2): 161–180, [LIT-462](../literature.d/LIT-462.md), read in full in [NOTE-387](../notes.d/NOTE-387.md).

## What was actually shown

**The third-person telling (pp. 163–167).** A and B are told that one
person emerging from the process will get money and the other torture, and
they choose selfishly. Whatever they choose, the reactions afterwards
(complaints, satisfaction, recalled choices) make "a strong case for saying
that the B-body-person really is A, and the A-body-person really is B"
(p. 164). So "to care about what happens to me in the future is not
necessarily to care about what happens to this body" (p. 164).

**The first-person telling (pp. 167–171).** I am told I will be tortured;
then that I will first forget being told; then that I will have total
amnesia; then a new character; then "memories" matching another living
person, copied from his brain. "Fear, surely, would still be the proper
reaction" (p. 168). The principle: "my undergoing physical pain in the
future is not excluded by any psychological state I may be in at the time,
with the platitudinous exception of … unconsciousness" (p. 169). Fear of
heights is the contrast: there, predicted change does remove the reason
(p. 169). Pain is chosen because it is "absolutely minimally dependent on
character or belief" (p. 170).

**The series (pp. 172–174).** (i) amnesia; (ii) plus a new character; (iii)
plus fictitious "memories"; (iv) character and "memories" fitting a real
person B; (v) produced by copying from B's brain, B left unchanged; (vi)
the same, with the reverse done to B. No step from (i) to (v) gives A a
reason to stop fearing. Step (vi) "is absolutely no difference at all in
what happens to him, the only difference being in what happens to someone
else" (p. 174). If A is the B-body-person in (vi), then A "just does not
exist" in (v), though the A-body-person there is the same "in character,
history, everything" (p. 174).

**Against the escapes (pp. 175–178).** Ordinary indeterminate expectations
keep fear in place because "it is to me" that something will happen
(p. 176). Where it is conceptually undecidable whether I am involved, fear
"seems neither appropriate, nor inappropriate, nor appropriately
equivocal" (pp. 176–177). A conventional decision, "appropriate to
lawyers", "as a line to deal with a person's fears or expectations about
his own future … seems to have no sense at all" (p. 178).

**The reversal and the resolution (pp. 179–180).** The mentalistic
conclusion came from a third-person argument and the bodily one from the
first person (p. 179). The neat exchange is "basically artificial", the
one situation of a range "we should be most disposed to call a change of
bodies". So A, choosing selfishly, should pass the pain to the
B-body-person: "It would be risky".

**What could have come out otherwise.** Some step in the series could have
been one at which fear plainly stops being apt. On the paper's analysis,
none is, and the last step concerns only B.

## What this does not say

- **What does make a future pain mine.** The paper argues that psychological
  change does not stop it being mine. It does not say what does, beyond the
  body that "has had the leg on it for some time". It offers no criterion of
  identity.
- **That the body is the person.** The first-person argument leans to the
  body; the conclusion is a choice under risk. Williams is "not in the
  least clear which option it would be wise to take" (p. 179).
- **That the principle about pain is established.** It is defended by
  cases and by pain's independence from character, not by an account of
  ownership. Parfit's reply targets exactly this.
- **Anything about fission.** There is never more than one candidate at a
  time on A's side, so Lewis's tensed identity has nothing to do here.
- **That the series survives a gradually replaced body.** The body is held
  fixed throughout.
