# THEORY-056: Emotion regulation is not a process separate from emotion generation: most of it is one motive state checking another, with no distinct regulating system above them

## Source

- Frijda, Ridderinkhof & Rietveld (2014), [LIT-540](../literature.d/LIT-540.md), read in [NOTE-433](../notes.d/NOTE-433.md):
  "Multiple emotions can exert control", "Emotion regulation and impulse
  control", and the Conclusion's third and fourth points (C5).
- Barrett (2017), [LIT-542](../literature.d/LIT-542.md), read in [NOTE-434](../notes.d/NOTE-434.md): the list of claims not
  made, Figure 4C, and the Conclusions (C8).

## The question

Gross's founding review ([LIT-554](../literature.d/LIT-554.md), [NOTE-429](../notes.d/NOTE-429.md)) defines regulation as "the
processes by which individuals influence which emotions they have, when
they have them, and how they experience and express these emotions"
(p. 275), and sorts it by the stage of generation it acts on. His first
open challenge is whether regulation can be told apart from generation
at all. "Some theorists argue that emotion regulation is best seen as part
and parcel of emotion (Frijda, 1986)." He keeps the distinction, and asks
for "high" thresholds before regulation is inferred, because inferring it
needs an unregulated course to compare against (p. 286; C6 in [NOTE-429](../notes.d/NOTE-429.md)).

## What was actually shown

**Frijda's argument.** Events draw several appraisals at once, and each
appraisal elicits its own readiness. Appraising what acting on an impulse
would lead to is one such appraisal, and the readiness it elicits can
check the first: "Anger is often held back by fear of retaliation or
loss of affection". Hence: "There is no
clear distinction between processes of emotion and processes of emotion
regulation. In large measure, emotion regulation also proceeds
impulsively: effortless, by the interaction of concurrent impulsive,
emotional processes." Emotions "are moderated or controlled to the extent
that one emotionally cares about the affective consequences of their being
uncontrolled". The paper presents this as "an alternative" to
dual-process models (Strack & Deutsch). They "should not, in general, be
seen as the outcomes of reason controlling emotion, or the rider
controlling the horse … our perspective accords with Hume (1739), in
viewing reason as the slave of the passions". Reflection is not removed.
It "seek[s] to help solving impulsive dilemmas that impulsive processes
alone are unable to solve". The support is everyday examples, plus a
diary finding that 31% of reported emotions were multiple (Oatley & Duncan
1992, cited). Which state wins depends "upon their relative strengths",
which the paper does not specify further.

**Barrett's convergence.** Barrett says this from inside the rival theory
of emotion ([THEORY-057](THEORY-057.md) against [THEORY-055](THEORY-055.md)). "Automaticity and
control are different brain modes (each of which can be achieved with a
variety of network configurations), not two battling brain systems." She
offers as a hypothesis (C8) that "reappraisal and other regulation
processes … are accomplished with predictions that categorize sensory
inputs and control action with concepts", which is the mechanism she
gives for constructing an emotion. Her Figure 4C shows overlap between the
regions active in emotional experience and in regulation (anterior insula,
vlPFC, MCC, SMA, posterior STS), with reappraisal drawing more on the
frontoparietal network. She reads that difference as the modification of
predictions, not as a second system.

So two accounts that disagree about what an emotion is agree that
regulation is not a different kind of process. Frijda supports the
whole claim. Barrett supports the clause about there being no distinct
regulating system, and does not say that regulation is one emotion
checking another.

## The philosophy-of-mind holdings

- **Minsky ([LIT-432](../literature.d/LIT-432.md), [NOTE-348](../notes.d/NOTE-348.md))** reaches the same architecture by design.
  Emotional states and Ways to Think are one kind of thing, a selection of
  resources. One emotion can be used to switch off another, as when
  anger is used against sleep (§3-8, as [NOTE-348](../notes.d/NOTE-348.md) reports it). Control is done by Critics, among
  them Suppressors and Self-Controllers, which belong to the same
  machinery. "Free will" is "a process we use to stop other processes".
  This is a design proposal argued by example, so it is a neighbour and
  not a source.
- **Brooks ([LIT-436](../literature.d/LIT-436.md), [NOTE-351](../notes.d/NOTE-351.md))** builds it. A newer behaviour layer steers
  an older one only by overriding its signals for a while, and the robot
  "is a collection of competing behaviors". The layers are ordered,
  but the overriding layer is another behaviour and not a reasoner. That
  is control without a controller of a different kind, which is what this
  account claims for emotion.
- **Haidt ([LIT-511](../literature.d/LIT-511.md), [NOTE-403](../notes.d/NOTE-403.md))** has the same shape for moral judgement.
  Reasoning rarely overrides intuition (link 5). Its private influence
  runs mostly through role-taking, which triggers a competing intuition
  (link 6). Frijda's reflection likewise works by raising a competing
  readiness. Both cite Hume.
- **Self-determination theory argues the contrary ([LIT-558](../literature.d/LIT-558.md), [NOTE-439](../notes.d/NOTE-439.md)).**
  Deci and Ryan (2000) hold that needs, not emotions, are the higher-order
  regulators of behaviour, "because emotions themselves must be
  self-regulated for effective functioning, and the basic psychological
  needs are centrally involved in the processes by which this
  self-regulation occurs" (p. 254). That posits a regulating level above
  emotion, which this account denies. The claim is argued, not tested, and
  no record THEORY states it, so it is not declared a rival. It may be
  closer than it looks: Frijda's concerns, which give events their
  pertinence, include the person's needs ([NOTE-433](../notes.d/NOTE-433.md)).
- **[THEORY-029](THEORY-029.md) (higher-order attitudes and ownership).** Hierarchical
  accounts of agency place the agent's authority in an attitude of a
  higher order than the motive it governs. If this account is right,
  there is no such level in the psychology of control. A motive prevails
  by its relative strength and by the concerns it recruits, and all of
  these are first-order. This does not decide [THEORY-029](THEORY-029.md)'s question,
  which is about whose a motive is and not about which one wins. It
  does mean that a hierarchical account cannot borrow its structure from
  how self-control works. Frijda's lever, recruiting "non-obvious but
  relevant concerns, such as moral concerns and self-esteem", puts what
  one cares about in the place Watson's values occupy, and [THEORY-029](THEORY-029.md)
  records that Velleman's dilemma reaches values too. The bearing is the
  record's. Neither Frijda nor Barrett discusses autonomy.
- **[THEORY-040](THEORY-040.md) (manipulation and history)** is not touched. This account
  is about present structure only.

**Three accounts filed since.** Two support the clause that there is no
regulator above the competing states, from outside emotion research.
Ainslie's bargaining ([THEORY-tmpgx68d](THEORY-tmpgx68d.md)) makes self-control the interaction of
interests that dominate at different delays, with no faculty of will over
them; it also offers an answer to which state wins, though what does the
checking there is an expectation about one's own future choices rather than
a concurrent emotion. Arbitration by reliability ([THEORY-tmp2wlfb](THEORY-tmp2wlfb.md)) lets
model-based and model-free controllers compete as peers, the more reliable
estimate winning, not the one a higher system prefers. The expected value of
control ([THEORY-tmpkujus](THEORY-tmpkujus.md)) is the challenge, and a conditional one: it posits a
dedicated system that decides how much control to apply, which would be the
distinct system this account says refutes it only if that specification
were shown to govern emotion regulation generally, effortless regulation
included. The test is the one `promote_when` already names, whether
effortless regulation by a competing emotion works without recruiting the
control networks that deliberate regulation recruits.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say that regulation never happens, or that deliberate
  strategies do nothing.** Gross's cited contrast, in which reappraisal
  lowers negative experience and suppression does not reliably do so and
  raises sympathetic activation (C3 in [NOTE-429](../notes.d/NOTE-429.md)), stands. Strategies
  differ in their consequences whether or not a separate system runs
  them.
- **"Most of it" is the claim.** Frijda says "in large measure", and he
  keeps a role for reflection. A rare case of reflection overriding an
  impulse directly would not refute the account. A distinct system that
  does the work generally would.
- **It does not say which state wins.** Neither source predicts that, and
  it is the account's largest gap.
