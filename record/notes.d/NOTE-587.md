# NOTE-587: Pervasive negative effects of rewards on intrinsic motivation: The myth continues

## Contribution

A reanalysis meant "to resolve differences in previous meta-analytic
findings and to provide a meta-analysis of rewards and intrinsic motivation
that permits tests of competing theoretical explanations" (p. 1). It
accepts the frame Deci, Koestner and Ryan ([LIT-557](../literature.d/LIT-557.md)) insisted on. Tasks are
split by initial interest first, dissertations are included, missing effect
sizes are imputed as zero, and studies without a proper control are
dropped. It then changes one thing: reward contingencies are coded from the
procedures the original studies used, in seven categories (Table 1, p. 12),
instead of cognitive evaluation theory's four. With that change, the
theory's "completion-contingent" and "performance-contingent" categories
each turn out to pool procedures with opposite effects (Figure 2, p. 18).

## Key insight

A meta-analysis inherits the categories of whoever codes it. "The problem
with organizing studies according to a particular theoretical stance is that
each theory could be used to organize the literature and, using categories
appropriate to the theory, each theory could gain support" (p. 10). Coding
by procedure is offered as the way out: it assesses "the effects of the
actual contingencies rather than those presumed to be an effect by any
theoretical perspective" (p. 10). Under that coding, what predicts harm is
how loosely the reward is tied to performance, and whether the reward
amounts to failure feedback (pp. 22–23).

## Assumptions

- **Procedural categories are theory-neutral.** Assumed, and only partly
  true. The seven categories are themselves choices: "doing well" with no
  stated standard is kept apart from "surpassing a score", for instance.
  Coding reliability on 32 expected-tangible studies was 97% (p. 12).
- **Both control types count** for performance-tied rewards: a control told
  the performance objective and given feedback ("complete"), or not
  ("partial"). Eisenberger, Pierce and Cameron (1999, [LIT-754](../literature.d/LIT-754.md)) found one small
  difference between them, in the same direction, so both were pooled
  (p. 13).
- **Imputed zeros** for missing effect sizes, as Deci et al.; each analysis
  was also run without them, and both are reported where they differ
  (p. 13).
- **Free choice combines time and performance measures**, as Deci et al.;
  time alone gave "no significant differences" (p. 10).
- **Low-interest tasks belong in the analysis**, because practitioners and
  critics of rewards do not distinguish them (pp. 8–9).

## Key results

- **Sample (p. 11).** 145 independent studies, 21 of them dissertations; 115
  with a free-choice measure, 100 with self-report. Boal and Cummings (1981)
  is dropped because "all participants (including the control group)
  received monetary payments", and Boggiano and Hertel (1983) because its
  measure preceded the task.
- **All rewards (Table 2, p. 14).** Free choice −0.08, not significant;
  self-report 0.12. Both heterogeneous.
- **Low-interest tasks (p. 15).** Free choice 0.28 (K = 12, homogeneous);
  self-report 0.12, not significant. Expected tangible rewards on
  low-interest tasks: free choice 0.26; offered for doing the task, 0.26
  (K = 9).
- **High-interest tasks, overall (p. 15).** Free choice −0.09; self-report
  0.12. Both heterogeneous.
- **Verbal rewards (pp. 15–16).** Free choice 0.31, homogeneous; children
  0.22 (K = 10), adults 0.36 (K = 15), both significant. Self-report 0.32
  after the same three outliers Deci et al. removed.
- **Tangible rewards (p. 16).** Free choice −0.17; self-report 0.08.
  Unexpected: no effect. Expected: −0.18 and 0.08, both heterogeneous.
- **Expected tangible rewards by contingency, free choice (pp. 16–17,
  Figure 1).**
  - No effect: task-noncontingent rewards, rewards for finishing, and
    rewards for surpassing a score.
  - Negative: rewards for doing the task, for doing well, and per unit
    solved. For doing the task, K = 57, −0.35 (CI −0.43 to −0.27) before
    outliers. Seven outliers were removed to reach homogeneity, with no
    common factor found, and the effect stayed significantly negative. Its
    post-removal value is in the figure only.
  - Positive: rewards for meeting or exceeding others' performance.
- **Expected tangible rewards by contingency, self-report (p. 18,
  Figure 3).**
  - Doing the task: small negative, −0.13; −0.22 without the 14 imputed
    zeros (K = 24).
  - Task-noncontingent: no effect.
  - Every other contingency: positive. Per unit, 0.19 with all studies
    (K = 22), before two outliers.
- **Per unit, maximum versus less than maximum reward (pp. 19–20).**
  - Maximum reward: −0.03, not significant (K = 6).
  - Less than the maximum: −0.22 (K = 14).
  - The authors read this as time pressure or failure feedback, "not
    reward" (p. 23).
- **Durability (pp. 23–24).**
  - Of seven between-groups studies of rewards for doing the task with a
    second free-choice measure weeks later, two showed undermining at the
    second (Morgan 1983, Exps. 1–2).
  - Five single-subject operant studies (no control groups) found no
    decline after the reward ended, against baseline.
  - The authors read this as "transitory effects".
- **Magnitude (pp. 24–25).** Rewards for doing or doing well average about
  −0.30. With Pretty and Seligman's pooled SD of 2.6 min, that is "about 47
  s less" in an 8-min free period. The authors flag that this rests on one
  study's SD and on extrapolating beyond 8 minutes.
- **Theory (p. 26).** Social cognitive theory (Bandura 1986) and a
  behavioural account fit the pattern. A strict reading of cognitive
  evaluation and overjustification theories does not, because rewards "tied
  to level of performance (surpassing a score, exceeding others) do not
  undermine". The authors grant that CET could fit if competence
  information "override[s] the controlling aspect of reward". They cite
  Eisenberger, Pierce and Cameron ([LIT-754](../literature.d/LIT-754.md)) for reward contingencies raising
  perceived self-determination.

## Concepts

- **procedural reward contingency**: one of seven categories defined by what
  participants were told the reward was for (Table 1, p. 12):
  - task noncontingent;
  - doing the task;
  - doing well;
  - finishing;
  - per unit solved;
  - surpassing a score;
  - exceeding a norm.
- **maximum versus less than maximum reward**: whether rewarded participants
  met every requirement and received the full reward. Less than maximum
  typically arises from a time limit (p. 13).
- **complete versus partial control**: a control group that was, or was not,
  told the performance objective and given feedback (p. 13).

## Connections

**Cameron & Pierce (1994), [LIT-556](../literature.d/LIT-556.md).** This is the authors' own earlier
analysis, restated on pp. 6–7. It keeps that paper's conclusion while
adopting corrections it was criticised for. Its database is merged with
Deci et al.'s (p. 11).

**Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999), [LIT-557](../literature.d/LIT-557.md).** This is the paper it compares
itself against. The agreements are larger than either side's rhetoric:
- unexpected and task-noncontingent rewards do nothing;
- verbal rewards enhance;
- expected tangible rewards for merely doing an interesting task undermine
  free choice: −0.35 here before outliers, −0.40 there ([NOTE-438](NOTE-438.md));
- rewards earned at less than the maximum undermine.

The disagreements are about rewards tied to standards or to others' scores,
about self-report, about scope (all tasks or only interesting ones), and
about durability.

**Self-determination theory's summaries.** [LIT-559](../literature.d/LIT-559.md), [LIT-558](../literature.d/LIT-558.md) and [LIT-560](../literature.d/LIT-560.md)
state the 1999 conclusion as settled. This paper shows it is contested on
coding, though not on the core effect.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-046](../theory.d/THEORY-046.md)** names this reading as its promotion condition: "a
  first-hand reading of the reply ... finds the same direction for expected
  tangible rewards given for engaging in an interesting task". It does,
  −0.35 on 57 studies, significant after outliers. The durability result
  (C6) is weaker evidence than [THEORY-046](../theory.d/THEORY-046.md)'s refutation clause asks for,
  since it counts significant results instead of pooling them.
- **[THEORY-049](../theory.d/THEORY-049.md)** (cognitive evaluation theory) named this reading as
  something that "could move this either way". Rewards for exceeding others
  raise free choice (C4). That is the case the account's refutation clause
  describes, but only if the control group received the same performance
  feedback. Here the two control types are pooled, and the
  complete-control result is reported only from Eisenberger, Pierce and
  Cameron (1999, [LIT-754](../literature.d/LIT-754.md)), filed but unread. So it does not refute.
- **[THEORY-154](../theory.d/THEORY-154.md)** states this paper's own account, that the tightness of
  the stated contingency decides the effect, as the rival to [THEORY-049](../theory.d/THEORY-049.md).

  anthology.

## Limitations

- Its key numbers are images. The per-contingency effect sizes in Figures 1
  and 3 after outlier removal are not in the text, so the record has the
  direction and significance of each contingency, but sizes only where the
  prose gives them.
- It measures no mediators. Self-efficacy, perceived competence and felt
  control are inferred from the pattern.
- The two control types are pooled. The most diagnostic contrast between it
  and CET, performance-tied reward against a control given the same
  feedback, is therefore not isolated here.
- Small categories: "few studies" for finishing and for doing well on
  self-report, by the authors' own account (pp. 22–23).
- It is an adversarial reanalysis by the authors of the result under
  dispute, as Deci et al.'s was.

## Open questions

- Does a performance-tied tangible reward raise free choice against a
  control given identical performance feedback? A preregistered experiment
  with both controls would close the question between this account and
  CET.
- Is "less than maximum" undermining an effect of failure feedback, of time
  pressure, or of reward? A design crossing time limits with reward would
  separate them.
- Does the undermining by rewards for doing the task persist? Pooled delayed
  measures with controls would decide what C6 only counts.
