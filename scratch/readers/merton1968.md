**Summary.** A sociological essay, based on Harriet Zuckerman's interviews
with US Nobel laureates and on scientists' letters and biographies. It names
"the Matthew effect": recognition for a given contribution accrues
disproportionately to scientists already eminent, chiefly in collaborations
and in independent multiple discoveries. It then turns from the reward system
to the communication system, where the same effect raises the visibility of
contributions by eminent scientists and so may speed their diffusion; traces
the effect's psychosocial bases in the laureates' socialization, taste for
important problems and self-assurance, and as a self-fulfilling prophecy;
names its dysfunction (an "idol of authority" that violates universalism);
and ends with an "institutional version", the principle of cumulative
advantage in the allocation of resources and talent among universities.

**Contribution.** A name and a functional analysis, not a model. The paper
coins "Matthew effect" (and the "41st chair", "Ecclesiasticus component");
separates its effect on individual careers (dysfunctional, an inequity) from
its effect on the communication system (possibly functional); and states
testable hypotheses (citation indexes for unequal-rank multiple discoveries;
readership by rank).

**Key results, with places and quotes.**
1. The pattern (p. 58): laureates "repeatedly observe that eminent scientists
   get disproportionately great credit for their contributions to science
   while relatively unknown scientists tend to get disproportionately little
   credit for comparable contributions."
2. The definition (p. 58): "the Matthew effect consists in the accruing of
   greater increments of recognition for particular scientific contributions
   to scientists of considerable repute and the withholding of such
   recognition from scientists who have not yet made their mark." Evidence is
   "presumptive": laureates testify "not as victims ... but as unwitting
   beneficiaries."
3. Fixed numbers at the summit (pp. 56-57): the "41st chair", "an artifact of
   having a fixed number of places available at the summit of recognition";
   and a "ratchet effect" (p. 57): "once having achieved a particular degree
   of eminence, they do not later fall much below that level." Recognition
   "can be converted into an instrumental asset as enlarged facilities are
   made available", giving "differential access to the means of scientific
   production" (p. 57).
4. The communication hypothesis (p. 59): "a scientific contribution will have
   greater visibility in the community of scientists when it is introduced by
   a scientist of high rank than when it is introduced by one who has not yet
   made his mark." Dysfunctional for careers, but for communication it "may
   operate to heighten the visibility of new scientific communications."
   Evidence: the Coles' visibility scores, 85 (laureates), 72 (NAS), 38, 17
   (no awards) (p. 59), called "tangential rather than central".
5. Not merely halo (p. 60): "This greater visibility is not merely the result
   of a halo effect"; socialization, values and "ego strength" account in part.
6. Self-fulfilling prophecy (p. 61): the greater "stimulus value" of an
   eminent scientist's work is "a special application of the self-fulfilling
   prophecy (42)", "a self-confirming process".
7. Dysfunction (pp. 61-62): "When the Matthew effect is thus transformed into
   an idol of authority, it violates the norm of universalism"; frequency
   among editors and referees "remains largely a matter of anecdote".
8. Cumulative advantage (p. 62): "One institutional version of the Matthew
   effect ... is expressed in the principle of cumulative advantage that
   operates in many systems of social stratification to produce the same
   result: the rich get richer at a rate that makes the poor become relatively
   poorer (46)." Note 46: "Derek Price perceived this implication of the
   Matthew principle" (Nature 206, 233, 1965). Data: six universities with 22
   percent of doctorates produced 69 percent of PhDs who became laureates.

**Assumptions.** Laureates' testimony as evidence of a pattern ("presumptive");
recognition as the coin of the reward system (Parsons); comparable
contributions can be identified across authors; a functionalist frame
(functions and dysfunctions for a social system).

**Concepts.** Matthew effect; 41st chair; ratchet effect; Ecclesiasticus
component (status-enhancement); visibility; functional redundancy of multiple
discoveries; self-fulfilling prophecy; universalism; cumulative advantage;
"Nobelesse oblige" (note 18).

## Verdict on how the exchange characterized it

A1 (as CLAIM-817 quotes it): the feedback is "sometimes called the *Matthew
effect*, after sociologist Robert K. Merton", and CLAIM-817's summary has
"Rich-get-richer, cumulative advantage, the Matthew effect and preferential
attachment name one feedback". **Right on the name and source, loose on the
identity.** Merton's Matthew effect is a misallocation of *credit for a given
contribution* by the contributor's existing rank (an attribution and
visibility effect), not growth in proportion to size. Cumulative advantage
appears only in the last section, as "one institutional version" of it,
where Merton does write "the rich get richer". The paper has no model, no
distribution, no power law, no rate in proportion to current holdings; the
paper credits the cumulative-advantage implication to Price (note 46: Nature
206, 233, 1965, not read here), and whatever proportional rule the record
attaches to the name is not stated by Merton. LIT-3002's summary ("eminent scientists get disproportionate credit
for comparable contributions") is accurate.

## Bearing on the named codes

- **CLAIM-817: background, and qualifies its naming clause.** Supports that
  "Matthew effect" is Merton's name and that he links it to cumulative
  advantage and "the rich get richer" (p. 62). Qualifies "name one feedback":
  the Matthew effect proper (p. 58) is credit by rank, and the paper's own
  mechanisms include a fixed number of places at the summit (p. 56) and a
  ratchet (p. 57), neither of which is linear attachment. Leaves open
  everything the claim asserts formally (kernel form, entry, exponents). It
  should stay in `grounds` only for the naming; it grounds nothing in the
  title.

## Candidate THEORY

Merton names the Matthew effect: credit for a given scientific contribution
accrues disproportionately to scientists who are already eminent and is
withheld from those not yet known, an inequity for careers that may also
raise the visibility of eminent scientists' work in science's communication
system; he treats cumulative advantage, in which "the rich get richer at a
rate that makes the poor become relatively poorer", as one institutional
version of it, and gives no model of either.

promote_when: a study holding contribution fixed (for example, matched
multiple discoveries or papers whose authorship is varied or concealed) finds
that more eminent authors receive more citations or acceptance for the same
work.
