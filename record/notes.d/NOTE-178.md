# NOTE-178: De Haro & Butterfield — Philosophy and Physics of Duality

## Contribution

A book-length account of dualities in physics, meaning equivalences between often very different theories. It covers what dualities are, examples from quantum mechanics, electrodynamics, statistical mechanics, QFT and string theory, and the philosophical questions they raise. Part I sets out a "Schema" for duality and relates it to symmetry and interpretation, with simple examples (position–momentum, wave–particle, electric–magnetic, Kramers–Wannier). Part II works through advanced cases up to gauge–gravity duality. Part III takes up theoretical equivalence, the geometric view of theories, emergence, fundamentality, and understanding and explanation.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1.1 (pp. 3–4): duality is an equivalence between disparate theories, and the book aims to be the first book-length philosophical account. Its readerships are philosophers and physicists, with Part II as the technical learning curve.
- §3.6 (pp. 94–95): duality is an isomorphism of models with respect to the structure of a common core. Once the models are interpreted, the duality and interpretation maps form a square (non-commuting) or triangle (commuting) diagram, and only in the commuting case do the models describe the same sector of reality. "Equivalence is not automatic."
- Contents (ch. 11, pp. 357–394): defends isomorphism against logically weaker criteria of theoretical equivalence, including a reply to Barrett's signature argument and a "No deus ex machina" argument.
- §16.1 (pp. 527–528): a scientific theory is a bare theory (states, quantities, dynamics) plus an interpretation. The authors find no evidence that dualities favour structural realism or undermine realism. The usual underdetermination argument is "too strong" and should be restated in terms of compatibility rather than theoretical equivalence.
- §16.2 (pp. 528–529): quasi-dualities motivate a "geometric view of theories" in which models are charts on a successor theory's moduli space. Dualities by themselves do not indicate emergence or fundamentality, AdS/CFT excepted. Reduction (formal) and emergence (partly interpretative) are compatible.
- §16.3 (pp. 530–531): open questions, such as isolating the common core of string dualities, and a stance that physics and philosophy should be interlaced rather than integrated.
