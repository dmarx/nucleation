# NOTE-1121: Edwards et al. — Skills homework and phone coaching in a DBT intensive outpatient programme

## Contribution

DBT says skills are learned in group and carried into daily life by
homework and by phone coaching, but, by the authors' account, no published
study had related DBT homework to outcomes (p. 506), and the phone-coaching
literature was mostly clinical anecdote (p. 507). This study relates both,
measured over the first three months, to change in urges and behaviours by
the fourth month, in a programme run in a day-hospital.

## Key insight

The authors read homework and coaching as the components of DBT built for
generalization, so their association with outcome is evidence for
generalization as a mechanism of change. In their abstract's words, "These
generalization methods appear to be important ingredients of DBT
effectiveness" (p. 504). What the study can show is narrower: patients who
did more homework, or called more, also reported larger falls in some
urges.

## Assumptions

- **The programme (pp. 508–509).** DBT modified for intensive outpatient
  care: six one-hour groups on two days a week for 16 weeks, about 96
  hours; one skills module a month; a primary clinician, a trainee, for
  each patient; individual therapy outside the programme and not
  necessarily DBT. Handouts and worksheets followed Linehan's manual
  ([LIT-1492](../literature.d/LIT-1492.md)). Phone coaching was available to all, with DBT's rule of
  no coaching within 24 hours after self-harm or a suicidal act.
- **The sample (p. 509).** 88 charts, 2017–2019; 4 excluded for not
  finishing the cycle and 28 for fewer than 7 diary-card days in month 1
  or month 4, leaving 56. 79% female, 89% White; 32 with a BPD diagnosis,
  35 with major depression. No diagnostic criteria for entry.
- **Measures (pp. 510–512).** Homework: the share of assignments
  complete in attended review groups, weeks 1–12, from progress notes, with
  partial completion counted incomplete. Coaching: the number of
  skills-focused calls in weeks 1–12. Outcomes from diary cards: change in
  mean daily highest urge (month 4 minus month 1) and in the share of
  reported days with the behaviour, and whether any target behaviour
  occurred in month 4. Attendance as a covariate. No reliability check of
  the chart coding (p. 518).
- **Temporal order (p. 510).** Predictors from weeks 1–12 and the outcome
  window weeks 13–16, which the authors call cross-lagged. Month-1 urges
  enter the change score, and they overlap the predictor window.

## Key results

- **Descriptives (p. 512).** Attendance 88%; homework completion 86%
  (median 91%; 24 of 56 completed all); coaching calls a mean 5.39
  (median 3, range 0–31), with 5 patients making none. Diary data on 68% of
  possible days. Urges were "relatively stable" from month 1 to month 4,
  and behaviour frequency fell slightly.
- **Urges (p. 513; Table 4).** Homework correlated with change in suicide
  urges (r = .35) and substance-use urges (r = .36 in the text, .37 in the
  table), and at r = .25 (p = .06) with alcohol urges. Coaching correlated
  with change in self-harm urges (r = .31). The other five pairings were not
  significant. Attendance correlated with nothing.
- **Behaviour frequency (p. 513).** "Regarding frequency of engagement in
  target behaviours, results reflected no significant associations."
- **Any behaviour in month 4 (p. 513).** 27 patients reported a target
  behaviour in month 4 and 27 did not. Controlling for attendance, the
  omnibus test differed (Wilks' λ = 0.86, p = .03). Those with none had
  completed more homework (91.3% against 79.8%, p = .02) and made more
  calls (6.00 against 3.85), the latter not significant (p = .096).
- **Coaching's contingency (p. 512).** Not measured, but reported:
  "patients also noted that knowing they would lose access to phone
  coaching was helpful in motivating them away from acting on urges for
  crisis behaviours".
- **Coaching outside DBT (pp. 507, 517).** The authors set the result
  against trials in which unstructured telephone access did not help, or
  went with 85% more self-harm among patients with repeated prior
  self-harm (Evans et al. 1999), and suggest that DBT's orientation,
  brevity and rules make the difference. Suggested, not tested.

## Concepts

- **Generalization**: carrying behaviours learned in treatment into the
  patient's natural environment, proposed as a mechanism of change in
  behaviour therapies and in DBT (p. 505).
- **Skills homework**: practising the week's skill with a worksheet; in
  DBT "delivered and reviewed in a group context following a curriculum and
  is not individualized" (p. 506), unlike CBT homework.
- **Phone coaching**: "Phone coaching is intended to provide patients
  in-the-moment instruction and reinforcement surrounding application of
  skills learned in treatment" (p. 507), with crisis reduction and repair of
  the therapeutic relationship as its other functions.
- **The 24-hour rule**: no coaching for 24 hours after self-harm or a
  suicidal act, so that contact does not reinforce the crisis behaviour
  (pp. 507, 510).

## Connections

It draws on Linehan (1993; the skills manual, 2015, [LIT-1492](../literature.d/LIT-1492.md)) for the
model; on the CBT homework meta-analyses of Kazantzis and colleagues for
the expected effect; on Neacsiu, Rizvi & Linehan (2010) for skill use as a
mediator; and on Chalker et al. (2015) and Oliveira & Rizvi (2018) for
phone contact in standard DBT.

## Bearing on the record

- **The exchange's description.** The exchange cited this study at U10,
  unnamed, as one in which homework and phone coaching went with better
  behavioural outcomes, practice carried into daily contexts being where
  the skill is generalized; the LIT's summary repeats the abstract. Two
  corrections.
  - **Urges, not behaviour frequency.** The associations are with change
    in urges; with change in how often the behaviours occurred there were
    none. The one behavioural result is whether any target behaviour
    occurred in month 4, and only homework reached significance there.
  - **Not all completers.** The 56 are those of 88 who finished the cycle
    and also gave at least a week of diary data in months 1 and 4; 28 were
    excluded for missing diary data. The authors say this may bias the
    result, in either direction (pp. 518–519).
- **[CLAIM-443](../claims.d/CLAIM-443.md) (a learned prior over which processes govern).** The
  claim uses DBT as its worked example of practice changing which process
  is recruited. This study is about practice and outcome, and it fits the
  claim's premise that practice matters, weakly. It cannot bear on the
  claim's two limits. Nothing measures what changed: no skill-use measure
  (the authors' fourth limitation, p. 518), no measure of the cost of
  regulating, and no situation in which the practised response stopped
  being right. Homework completion may also index motivation, which the
  design cannot separate ("analyses could not exclude the possibility of
  reverse-order effects", p. 518).
- **[CLAIM-349](../claims.d/CLAIM-349.md) (access, authority, action; its note on DBT skills as
  acquired governance operations).** The note distinguishes knowing how to
  respond from being able to deploy the response under pressure and having
  practised it until deployment costs little. Phone coaching is the case
  where deployment is supported from outside at the moment of pressure:
  another person recruited "in-the-moment". The association with falling
  self-harm urges is consistent with that reading and too thin to support
  it. The 24-hour rule is a contingency on access to the external
  regulator, which patients reported using to hold off acting on urges.
- **[CLAIM-358](../claims.d/CLAIM-358.md) and [QUESTION-095](../questions.d/QUESTION-095.md) (amortized cost; caching).** Nothing here
  measures cost. The study does not show that practised skills become
  cheaper to deploy.
- Filed: [THEORY-922](../theory.d/THEORY-922.md), the associations as measured.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

The authors list most of these (pp. 517–519).

- **Correlational, from charts, with no reliability check**; reverse
  causation and confounds such as motivation, personality and
  pre-treatment functioning cannot be excluded.
- **A selected sample**: completers with adequate diary data, 64% of the
  charts.
- **"statistical adjustments for multiple comparisons were not feasible,
  increasing the likelihood of type I error"** (p. 519). Of eight
  urge-change correlations with the two predictors, three were
  significant at p < .05.
- **Low base rates.** Two patients reported any suicidal behaviour in
  month 1 and none in month 4 (Table 2); behaviour frequencies were low
  and likely under-reported.
- **One modified programme**, mostly White and female; individual therapy
  outside the programme was not controlled.
- **The abstract overstates.** It says homework and coaching were
  significantly associated with reduced urges for alcohol use and with a
  lower likelihood of any target behaviour in month 4. The alcohol
  correlation was p = .06, and the coaching difference in month 4 was not
  significant (p. 513).
- **Reporting slips.** r = .36 in the text against .37 in Table 4 for
  substance-use urges; univariate tests reported as F(1, 54) on 54
  patients with attendance as a covariate, where 51 error degrees of
  freedom would be expected; the
  homework score's example divides by "skills-coaching groups" where
  homework review groups are meant (p. 510). The sign convention of the
  change scores (month 4 minus month 1) would make reductions negative,
  yet positive correlations are read as reductions; the text's reading is
  taken as intended.

## Open questions

- Does assigning more homework or coaching, rather than observing who
  does more, change urges or behaviour? A dismantling or randomized
  component trial would tell.
- Does skill use carry the association, as the generalization account
  says? Measuring use of skills between sessions as a mediator, as the
  authors propose, would.
- Does practice make a skill cheaper to deploy under pressure, the
  question [CLAIM-349](../claims.d/CLAIM-349.md)'s note and [CLAIM-358](../claims.d/CLAIM-358.md) ask? That needs a measure of
  effort or latency, not only of outcome.
