# LIT-tmpds717: Testing Judicious Use of Safety Behaviors During Exposure: A Randomized Controlled Trial Examining When and For Whom Safety Behaviors Improve Fear Reduction

Meckes, Cole, Gee & Lancaster (2025), Cognitive Therapy and Research
50(2), 341–359. DOI 10.1007/s10608-025-10667-1. Registered at
ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT04470882.

## Key takeaways

- Three arms, one 30-minute exposure each, differing only in a safety
  behaviour: rubber boots faded after 20 minutes, worn throughout, or not
  worn. "Repeated measures ANOVAs yielded no significant time × SB
  condition interactions across phobia-related outcomes (ps > .05)" at
  post-treatment and one-month follow-up.
- The boots raised approach in the first exposure trial, and the no-boots
  arm rated the treatment less acceptable at post-treatment.
- The boots did not lower threat expectancy on average: "there were no
  between-group differences in the level of change in threat expectancy"
  when they were introduced.
- Where they did lower it for a person, fading them did worse than
  keeping them on disgust at follow-up, the opposite of the hypothesis.

## Standing in the record

Filed by LEAD-032 for QUESTION-045's experiment. It holds exposure fixed
and varies only the safety behaviour, with a follow-up, and finds no
lasting difference. It does not cut the person's own maintaining link:
the safety behaviour is new to the participant and supplied by the
experimenter, which the authors list as their first limitation.
