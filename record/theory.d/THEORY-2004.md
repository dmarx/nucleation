# THEORY-2004: Carnap (1950) holds that logical probability is degree of confirmation, a relation between evidence and hypothesis whose statements are analytic, measured as partial entailment between ranges of state-descriptions relative to a language; that what holds for every admissible measure leaves every non-trivial value open, so one must be chosen by adequacy, provisionally; and that relevance under any such measure is symmetric but not transitive, with evidence positive singly able to be negative together

## The account

Probability in the logical sense, Carnap's probability-one, is degree of
confirmation: a relation between a hypothesis and evidence, not a
property of the hypothesis, whose elementary statements are analytic. A
regular measure weights the state-descriptions of a language, and
c(h,e) is the share of the evidence's range that lies in the
hypothesis's, "so to speak, partial L-implication". The value depends on
the language as well as the sentences, and in application on the total
evidence available.

Every theorem common to the axiom systems of Keynes, Jeffreys and the
rest, Bayes's theorem among them, holds for all regular measures, and
these leave any value strictly between 0 and 1 open. So an explicatum
has to be chosen, by adequacy requirements (symmetry among individuals
is one) and by comparing consequences with rivals; c* is offered "not
... a perfectly adequate explicatum", only the better of the two
simplest.

Under every regular measure, relevance is symmetric; high confirmation
is not transitive; two items each positive to a hypothesis can be
negative in conjunction; and that an item is positive to a hypothesis
fixes nothing about its relevance to what the hypothesis entails or what
entails it.

## Evidence

Definitions and theorems in chapters IV to VI and VIII, and the appendix,
with worked examples (the chess tournament, §69). The theorems are
proved for Carnap's simple languages; the choice of c* is argued, not
proved, and its values are promised for a second volume.
