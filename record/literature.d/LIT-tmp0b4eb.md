# LIT-tmp0b4eb: The Biology of Moral Systems

Richard D. Alexander (1987), *Aldine de Gruyter, Hawthorne, N.Y. (Evolutionary Foundations of Human Behavior series, per Routledge; xx + 301 pp.). Reissued by Routledge, 2017* — DOI-10.4324/9780203700976

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, as one of the evolutionary
sources of morality-as-cooperation that the record lacked ([ADR-018](../decisions.d/ADR-018.md)).

`Deferred` because no lawful full text was reachable, so it is not read
and no NOTE is filed.

**Contents** (chapter titles and pages from Crossref's records for the
2017 reissue):

- 1 Biology and the Background of Moral Systems (1–76)
- 2 A Biological View of Morality (77–144)
- 3 Morality as Seen by Philosophers and Biologists (145–196)
- 4 Applying the Biological View of Morality (197–252)
- Conclusions (253–258); Epilogue (259–264)

**What it is said to argue**, attributed:

- *Per the publisher's description* (Routledge): moral questions "arise out
  of conflicts of interest", and "moral systems are ways of using
  confluences of interest at lower levels of social organization to deal
  with conflicts of interest at higher levels". Moral systems "are
  described as systems of indirect reciprocity": people gain and lose "not
  only by direct transactions, but also by the reputations they gain".
  The ultimate interests of humans are reproductive; "the concept of
  morality has arisen within groups because of its contribution to unity
  in the context, ultimately, of success in intergroup competition".
  Morality "is not easily relatable to universals", and the last part
  discusses the nuclear arms race as "the greatest of all moral
  problems".
- *Per Nowak 2006* ([LIT-tmpbmh7b](LIT-tmpbmh7b.md), ref. 30): indirect reciprocity "leads to
  the evolution of morality".

**Bearing on morality-as-cooperation**, as far as these sources show. It
is the earliest of the record's sources to make morality itself the
object of an evolutionary account. On the description it would ground
MAC's reciprocity domain in its indirect, reputational form, and its group
domain through within-group unity for between-group competition. Nothing
reachable suggests it treats contests, division or possession. Its claim
that morality is "not easily relatable to universals" sits awkwardly with
MAC's prediction of seven universal morals ([LIT-047](LIT-047.md); [NOTE-034](../notes.d/NOTE-034.md)), and a
reading should check what exactly it denies. Curry 2016 ([LIT-tmpcsgn6](LIT-tmpcsgn6.md))
does not cite it; [NOTE-034](../notes.d/NOTE-034.md)'s lineage paragraph does not name it.

**Neighbours.** Trivers ([LIT-tmppstp9](LIT-tmppstp9.md)) supplies the reciprocity it builds
on, and his multiparty section anticipates indirect reciprocity.
Boehm ([LIT-tmpwfq9u](LIT-tmpwfq9u.md)) also explains morality by reputation and social
selection within groups. Tomasello ([LIT-tmpdvlhz](LIT-tmpdvlhz.md)) explains it by
collaboration rather than by reciprocity.

**Topic.** `moral-psychology` first: the book's subject is morality as a
biological phenomenon. `natural-sciences` for the evolutionary biology,
`social-science` for its anthropology. `ethics` is not added on what is
reachable; chapters 3–4 engage moral philosophers and apply the view to
a moral problem, and a reading should decide whether they argue what is
owed.

**Anthology.** No instruction for machine-learning practice; not held
there.

**What a reading should check.** How indirect reciprocity is modelled
(verbally, or with the reputational dynamics Nowak later formalized);
whether intergroup competition is a group-selection argument; and what
"not easily relatable to universals" means for the cross-cultural claims
of [LIT-047](LIT-047.md).
