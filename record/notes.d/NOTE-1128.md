# NOTE-1128: Goodson et al. — The standalone effect of safety behavior manipulations

## Contribution

Earlier reviews looked at safety behaviours inside exposure therapy:
whether instructions to drop or keep them during exposure change its
outcome (Meulders et al. 2016 found small effects either way). This is the
first meta-analysis of studies that manipulate safety behaviours *on their
own*, with no exposure: treatments that fade them, and experiments that
tell people to cut them down or step them up, sometimes with no rationale
given. Its inclusion criteria require studies that "directly target SB
outside of the context of exposure" (Methods, full-text review). Both
directions moved symptoms. Cutting safety behaviours down lowered
symptoms, stepping them up raised them, and the bigger the drop in safety
behaviour, the bigger the drop in symptoms.

## Key insight

Safety behaviours are treated here as a manipulable cause, not only a
symptom. If dropping them helps and adding them harms, in experiments
where participants are not told why, they do more than accompany
pathology. The literature that shows this, though, is young, small and
concentrated on appearance and social anxiety in students.

## Assumptions

- **Safety behaviour.** "overt actions and mental strategies used to
  prevent or reduce anxiety, avoid a potential threat, or prevent a
  negative outcome" (Introduction, after Salkovskis 1991). Mirror checking,
  body comparison, avoiding eye contact, searching symptoms online,
  checking and trauma-cue avoidance all count.
- **Between-group effects only.** Within-group effects are reported but
  not meta-analysed, after Cuijpers et al. 2017.
- **Cohen's d from standardized mean differences**, random effects,
  REML meta-regression, outlier screening (Viechtbauer & Cheung 2010),
  funnel plots, trim-and-fill and Egger's test (Methods).
- **Search.** PsycINFO, Academic Search Complete and PubMed, June 2025,
  updated September 2025; adults only; English; PROSPERO
  CRD420251063442 (Methods).

## Key results

- **The studies (Characteristics of Studies).** 26 studies, 2,048
  participants, mean age 24.90, 26.63% male, 67.30% white; 20 from the
  United States. Fifteen were non-treatment experiments and 11 were
  treatment trials. Nineteen were RCTs. By focus: eight social anxiety,
  seven appearance concerns, three eating disorders, three health
  anxiety, two broad anxiety, two obsessive-compulsive, one PTSD. Twelve
  recruited from universities. In nine experiments "deception" was used,
  and participants were not told why safety behaviours were being changed.
  Thirteen manipulations were daily digital reminders. "Interestingly,
  only five studies involved a control condition that included contact
  with a clinician."
- **Within-group (not meta-analysed).** Reduction: d = 1.08 on safety
  behaviours and d = 0.99 on symptoms. Increase: d = 2.81 on safety
  behaviours and only d = 0.35 on symptoms.
- **Reduction → safety behaviours.** d = 0.75 (k = 16, I² = 86.7%), and
  d = 0.52 (95% CI 0.36–0.68, I² = 38.1%) with one outlier (Wilver et al.
  2020) removed. Study type moderated it: 0.76 for non-treatment
  experiments, 0.31 for treatments. Trim-and-fill gives 0.46, and Egger's
  test is not significant.
- **Reduction → symptoms.** d = 0.43 (95% CI 0.27–0.59, k = 16,
  I² = 45.8%). "In observing moderators, no variable moderated the pooled
  effect." The size of the safety-behaviour effect did moderate it
  (B = 0.23, R² analogue 0.85), "suggesting that for every 1-point
  increase in SB effect size, the effect size for clinical symptoms
  increased by 0.23". Egger's test is significant (p = .012), and
  trim-and-fill imputes four missing studies and gives d = 0.31 (95% CI
  0.12–0.51).
- **Increase → safety behaviours.** d = 2.96 (five effects, N = 368),
  heterogeneous, with no bias tests possible.
- **Increase → symptoms.** d = 0.65 (95% CI 0.46–0.83; five studies,
  seven effects, I² = 0%), with no bias tests possible.
- **The authors' cautions (Discussion).** The increase studies "were
  examined in healthy college samples rather than symptom-elevated or
  clinical samples that were included in the SB decrease studies". On
  expectancy: "It is possible that the effects seen in these studies may
  be in part due to expectancy effects", since all 11 treatment trials
  gave a rationale. Demand effects and weak no-instruction controls may
  inflate the experimental effects. The verdict: "Taken together, the
  current findings allow for cautious optimism in the state of the
  standalone SB treatment literature."

## Concepts

- **Standalone manipulation**: a change to safety behaviours made outside
  exposure, either as a treatment with a rationale or as an experiment,
  sometimes deceptive (Introduction; Methods).
- **Safety-behaviour fading**: systematically reducing and eliminating
  safety behaviours, here often by daily text or app reminders
  (Introduction; Cougle et al. 2020).

## Connections

The cognitive-behavioural tradition makes safety behaviours a
maintaining factor (Salkovskis 1991; Ehlers & Clark 2000; Wells et al.
1995). The review sets itself beside Meulders et al. 2016, which
meta-analysed safety-behaviour instructions within exposure, and Blakey
and Abramowitz 2016, which reviewed safety behaviours from the
inhibitory-learning view of Craske et al. ([LIT-1500](../literature.d/LIT-1500.md)). Most of the
included studies come from the senior author's group (Cougle; Patel &
Cougle; Stentz & Cougle; Summers & Cougle; Wilver et al.; Zech et al.).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-446](../claims.d/CLAIM-446.md) (governance-maintained anxiety).** The exchange cited
  it, unnamed, for the effect of reducing safety behaviours on its own,
  and gave the counts and d = 0.43 correctly. Three corrections. The
  bias-adjusted estimate is d = 0.31. Bias is shown for the symptom
  outcome specifically (Egger p = .012), not for the review as a whole.
  And none of the planned moderators moderated the symptom effect; only
  the size of the safety-behaviour change did. The reading bears on the
  claim as follows.
  - **For the claim, at its base.** The claim needs protective behaviour
    to do causal work in keeping symptoms, not only to accompany them.
    The increase experiments are the best evidence the record holds that
    it does: in non-clinical people told to check, compare or seek
    reassurance more, symptoms rose (d = 0.65). The dose-response
    moderator points the same way for reductions.
  - **Silent on what is distinctive.** The claim says the maintaining
    link is defensive *authority* over action and evidence, not belief.
    The review cannot tell those apart. No study measured threat
    estimates, exploration, or what evidence the participants gathered
    once the safety behaviour stopped. Symptoms could fall because the
    reduction acts as unprotected exposure that corrects belief, and that
    is the belief-maintained reading. It could also be because the
    defence loses its grip, and that is the claim's. The claim's
    four-intervention test is not approached.
  - **A narrowing of what "safety behaviour" covers.** Most of the
    evidence is appearance-related checking and comparison and social
    safety behaviours in students. Avoidance-maintained anxiety
    disorders, the claim's case, are thinly represented: one PTSD study,
    two checking studies and no panic or agoraphobia.
- **[THEORY-053](../theory.d/THEORY-053.md) (efficacy is not evidence for a theory of change).** The
  moderator is the kind of evidence [THEORY-053](../theory.d/THEORY-053.md) asks for, that moving
  the process moves the outcome, but it is between studies. No included
  study is reported as testing whether the safety-behaviour change
  precedes and mediates the symptom change. The authors flag expectancy
  effects as an alternative.
- **[THEORY-058](../theory.d/THEORY-058.md) (self-reinforcing loops).** The bidirectional result
  (adding raises symptoms, removing lowers them) fits a loop through
  behaviour. It does not show the loop closes, which would need symptoms
  to raise safety behaviour in turn.
- Filed from this reading: [THEORY-903](../theory.d/THEORY-903.md).
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Small, early literature.** The meta-analyses hold 16 and 5 studies.
  The authors call them "preliminary".
- **Publication bias and small-study effects.** "Taken together,
  publication bias and small-study effects cannot be ruled out in the
  present findings" (both reduction analyses). For the increase analyses
  bias could not be tested.
- **Samples.** "most of the participants included in this review and
  meta-analysis were young women". Increase studies used healthy college
  samples. Clinical diagnoses were rare.
- **Heterogeneous outcomes.** "primary symptoms" range from social anxiety
  to loneliness, distress tolerance, authenticity, disgust propensity and
  body satisfaction (Table 1), all pooled as one outcome.
- **Risk of bias.** 13 of 19 RCTs had "some concerns", and all seven
  non-randomized studies were at moderate risk.
- **One group's work.** Many included studies are from the authors' own
  laboratory. The review does not discuss this.
- **Reporting slips in the manuscript.** The within-group symptom effect
  of reduction is d = 0.99 in Results and d = 1.03 in the Discussion. The
  increase-on-symptoms effect is printed "d = 65". Table 1 labels van
  Uijen & Toffolo 2015 "decrease checking SB" but describes a checking
  increase. "Eger's" appears for Egger's. The version of record may
  correct these.

## Open questions

- Do standalone reductions change threat estimates, and do they bring
  people into the situations they had avoided? Measuring both would show
  whether reductions work as exposure (belief correction) or by another
  route.
- Does the effect hold in diagnosed panic, agoraphobia and
  obsessive-compulsive disorder, with active controls and masked
  rationale?
- Within a study, does the safety-behaviour change precede and mediate
  the symptom change?
