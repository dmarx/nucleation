# NOTE-1262: Group-level self-definition and self-investment: A hierarchical (multicomponent) model of in-group identification

## Contribution

It gives identification a structure. Earlier multicomponent measures
disagreed on how many components there are and mixed items across them
(Table 1). The authors specify five narrow components, each with two to
four items, and group them under two higher-order dimensions taken from
classic sociology: Durkheim's mechanical solidarity and Tönnies's
Gemeinschaft (likeness) for self-definition, organic solidarity and
Gesellschaft (chosen commitment) for self-investment. They show by
confirmatory factor analysis that the structure holds across groups and
cohorts, and that the components predict different intergroup reactions.

## Key insight

Defining oneself as a group member and being invested in the group are
different, and under threat they pull apart. "When confronted with an
in-group's misdeeds, it is the individuals' self-definition at the group
level that seems necessary to the self-critical feeling of group-based
guilt" (p. 163), while "those who most invested themselves in their
in-group identity most strongly defended this identity by legitimizing
the in-group's misdeed" (p. 163). The investment dimension is not plain
affect: centrality, which has no evaluative content, loads on it as
highly as satisfaction does (p. 162).

## Assumptions

- **Identification as the individual's relation to the group as a
  whole**, not interpersonal ties within it (p. 148).
- **Self-stereotyping as depersonalization**, following
  self-categorization theory: seeing oneself as similar to the average
  member (p. 146).
- **Short scales**: 14 items, 7-point agreement, two to four per
  component; six candidate items were dropped as too general ("I see
  myself as [In-group]"), double-barrelled or vague (note 1, Appendix).
- **Bivariate prospective correlations** carry Studies 5–7; regressions
  are used for discriminant validity.

## Key results

- **The components and items** (Appendix).
  - Solidarity: "I feel a bond with", "I feel solidarity with", "I feel
    committed to".
  - Satisfaction: glad, proud, pleasant, good feeling.
  - Centrality: "I often think about the fact that I am", "an important
    part of my identity", "an important part of how I see myself".
  - Individual self-stereotyping: "I have a lot in common with the
    average [In-group] person", "I am similar to the average ...".
  - In-group homogeneity: members "have a lot in common with each other",
    "are very similar to each other".
- **Studies 1–2: structure** (pp. 149–154). First-year students at the
  University of Amsterdam, native-born Dutch only: N = 464 (Dutch,
  European, university identity), then a new cohort of 363 (Dutch,
  European).
  - The five-component, two-dimensional model fit all five measurements
    (CFI .966 to .983, SRMR .034 to .051). All item loadings exceeded
    .60.
  - The two dimensions correlated .61 to .77 (Study 1) and .69 to .71
    (Study 2).
  - Alternatives: five components on one factor fit marginally with AIC
    about twice; centrality on self-definition (Model 1f) fit
    satisfactorily with AIC about twice; two components only, one
    factor, or cognitive versus affective fit poorly (CFI .57 to .83).
  - A multigroup model constraining all parameters equal across the five
    measurements fit (CFI .94, RMSEA .03).
  - Satisfaction was the most endorsed component, centrality the least.
- **Studies 3–4: concurrent validity** (pp. 154–157). Partial
  correlations control satisfaction.
  - Self-stereotyping had the highest partial correlation with Jackson's
    depersonalization items (Dutch pr = .33).
  - Homogeneity alone correlated with meta-contrast items (Dutch only).
  - Centrality had the highest partial correlation with identity
    collective self-esteem, solidarity with membership self-esteem.
  - **The self–in-group overlap circles** (Schubert and Otten 2002).
    Dutch: r = .20 with self-stereotyping, .10 homogeneity, .31
    satisfaction, .29 solidarity, .17 centrality. European: .22, .13,
    .23, .25, .29. With satisfaction controlled, self-stereotyping's
    correlation was marginal (Dutch pr = .10, European .09).
- **Study 5** (N = 440, weeks after Study 1; Muslims and the West). In-group
  homogeneity alone predicted perceived differences between Dutch and
  Muslims (r = .13); centrality best predicted perceived threat to the
  in-group (.15), solidarity less (.10). Both held with established
  identification scales controlled.
- **Study 6** (N = 36, months after Study 1, days after the Madrid bombing
  of 11 March 2004).
  - Centrality predicted support for banning headscarves (.34) and, at
    trend, threat measures (.29, .33).
  - Holding the Netherlands responsible for provoking terrorism: in-group
    homogeneity .35, satisfaction −.50. In regression, satisfaction
    b = −.507, p = .003; homogeneity b = .225, p = .07.
  - Solidarity (.37) and satisfaction (.37) predicted wanting Dutch
    Muslims to "integrate better".
  - Self-stereotyping predicted none of these.
- **Study 7** (N = 49, one month after Study 2). A fabricated Human
  Rights Watch report described Europe's mistreatment of Rwandan asylum
  seekers.
  - Guilt was moderate (M = 2.92 on 0–5).
  - Self-stereotyping as European was the only predictor of guilt (.33).
    It held with the four collective self-esteem subscales controlled
    (b = .244) and with Jackson's subscales controlled (b = .271,
    p = .041). No component predicted shame, sympathy or being appalled.
  - Satisfaction (.39) and centrality (.29) predicted legitimizing
    European asylum policy; self-stereotyping did not (−.20, ns).
    Satisfaction held with the subscales controlled (b = .373, p = .046).

## Concepts

- **group-level self-definition**: defining oneself in terms of the
  group, by likeness: self-stereotyping and in-group homogeneity.
- **group-level self-investment**: positive feeling about, bond with, and
  importance of the membership: satisfaction, solidarity, centrality.
- **individual self-stereotyping**: seeing oneself as similar to the
  average or prototypical member.
- **in-group homogeneity**: seeing the members as alike, which makes the
  group a coherent entity distinct from out-groups.
- **centrality**: the chronic salience and subjective importance of a
  membership.

## Connections

- **Ashmore et al. 2004 ([LIT-1795](../literature.d/LIT-1795.md))**, unread. Cited as the review
  showing unitary identification measures inadequate, and for
  satisfaction and centrality. Leach et al. do not map their components
  onto its elements.
- **Self-categorization theory ([THEORY-1114](../theory.d/THEORY-1114.md)).** Self-stereotyping is
  its depersonalization, measured as a trait-like perception rather than
  a salience effect. The paper puts centrality, which the theory treats
  as part of identification, on the investment side.
- **Brewer and Gardner 1996 ([LIT-1757](../literature.d/LIT-1757.md)).** Their relational self is
  said to resemble solidarity (p. 147).
- **Smith 1993 ([LIT-1722](../literature.d/LIT-1722.md)) and Smith and Mackie 2016** (read in this
  batch, NOTE-1262). Group-based emotion presumes identification;
  Leach et al. say it is self-definition that guilt needs, and
  investment that undercuts it.
- **The group entity versus a category ([TERM-096](../terms.d/TERM-096.md)).** The authors
  suggest identification with categorical "common identity" groups rests
  more on self-definition, and with "common bond" groups more on
  self-investment (p. 163). Their homogeneity component makes a group a
  "coherent social entity" by likeness, which is the category side of
  the term.

## Bearing on the record

- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md)).** This paper answers part
  of the question the objection asks. These are inferences; the authors
  do not discuss egoism.
  - Identification does split into a self-extent dimension and a caring
    dimension. Self-stereotyping (I am like the average member) is a
    criterion of the self's extent fixed by perceived likeness, not by
    what one values. Satisfaction, solidarity and centrality are caring.
  - They come apart, though not far. The dimensions correlate .61 to .77,
    but they predict opposite reactions to the group's misdeeds:
    self-definition predicts guilt, investment predicts defence.
  - Two of the record's candidate criteria fall on the caring side. The
    overlap circles, the measure fusion descends from, correlated more
    with satisfaction and solidarity than with self-stereotyping for the
    Dutch, and with centrality most for Europe; self-stereotyping's
    partial link was marginal. And "an important part of how I see
    myself", the plainest self-extent wording, is centrality, which loads
    with caring. Situational salience by fit, the objection's other
    example, is not measured.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).**
  - If the larger self is fixed by self-definition, its signature is
    sharing the group's guilt, not defending its image. An extended self
    in this sense takes the group's misdeeds as partly one's own. That is
    self-concern in the thesis's sense, but self-critical.
  - If it is fixed by investment, it defends the group's image. Then
    self-interest on a larger self looks like protecting a valued
    identity, which fits the reading the fusion note gave (a self-concept
    to be kept, [CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)'s note on fusion).
  - No study measures helping or sacrifice, so which dimension carries
    prosocial action is open. Solidarity predicted wanting Muslims to
    integrate, an in-group-directed wish.
- **A dated note should be added to [CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md).** Proposed text:
  "Leach et al. (NOTE-1262) split identification into group-level
  self-definition (seeing oneself as like the average member) and
  self-investment (satisfaction, solidarity, centrality). The two
  correlate .61 to .77 but act differently: faced with their group's
  misdeeds, self-definition predicted guilt and investment predicted
  defending the group. Self-stereotyping is a criterion of the self's
  extent that comes apart from caring. The overlap circles and the item
  'an important part of how I see myself' go with investment, so they are
  weaker candidates than the claim supposed."

## Limitations

- **One population**: Dutch university students, mostly women, ethnic
  Dutch only.
- **Small samples for the key tests**: N = 36 in Study 6, 49 in Study 7.
  Studies 5–7 report bivariate correlations, many marginal.
- **Self-report throughout**; no behaviour, and no helping.
- **Two items per self-definition component.**
- **Deception in Study 7**: the Human Rights Watch report was fabricated,
  adapted from a real one.
- **Not the 2004 review.** Ashmore et al.'s elements and measures are not
  laid out.
- **Typos and inconsistencies.**
  - "participatns" (p. 150); "illiustrate" (p. 156); "its affect on
    individual feelings" for effect (p. 155); "Cindy Picket" for Pickett
    (p. 144).
  - Study 7 reports t(49) and F(1, 48) with N = 49 (p. 161), and F(1, 48)
    for adding four subscales; the degrees of freedom cannot be right.
    Its t(49) = 2.14 is given p = .051.
  - Study 5 gives F(2, 438) and F(2, 439) for parallel models with
    N = 440 (p. 158).

## Open questions

- Which dimension predicts helping other members, or sacrifice for the
  group? A study pairing these scales with a costly helping measure would
  tell which kind of extended self the owner's thesis needs.
- Does situational salience (self-categorization by fit) behave like
  self-stereotyping or like centrality?
