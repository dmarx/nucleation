# THEORY-449: If sentience comes in degrees, plants may be faintly sentient, and moral status is graded by degree of sentience, then plants have a partial moral status so low that animal interests outweigh theirs in most cases; all three premises are assumed, and the second rests on contested, second-hand evidence

## Source

Terrill & Veit (2024), "Better to be a Pig Dissatisfied than a Plant
Satisfied", [LIT-193](../literature.d/LIT-193.md), read in full in [NOTE-120](../notes.d/NOTE-120.md).

## What was actually shown

A conditional argument, which the paper calls "our conditional argument"
(p. 3) and whose goal it calls "modest" (p. 2).

- **The premises (pp. 8–10).** A1: sentience is "a spectrum that comes in
  degrees". A2: plants may be "quasi-sentient", with "such faint feelings
  that we could not even imagine them". A3: moral status is a matter of
  degree, "determined by the strength and complexity of the degree of
  sentience" (p. 9). Behind them sits the assumption that "without sentience
  an entity would have no interests or welfare" (p. 9, after Timmer 2023).
- **The conclusion.** Plants may have partial moral status, "on a smaller
  magnitude relative to animals" (p. 3).
- **Table 1 (p. 11).** No sentience gives no status. Quasi-sentience gives
  partial status, with obligations that are "Some, but outweighed by animal
  interests in most cases". Sophisticated sentience gives full status.
- **Non-zero-sum (p. 12).** Admitting plants does not lower animals' status
  (Douglas 2013), so the view avoids treating eating plants and eating pigs
  alike. Ethical veganism stays "the best moral option" (p. 11).
- **Weight (p. 13).** Plants get "a very low value" that loses to animals
  "even when we compare a lot of plants against a few animals", though "an
  entire forest" might outweigh one pig.

The inference from A1–A3 is valid. Nothing in the paper could have come out
otherwise, since every premise is assumed.

## What this does not say

- **That plants are sentient.** The paper says "Nothing we … have said
  settles the final question" (p. 13), and that the burden shifts to plant
  neurobiologists. The evidence for A2 is surveyed at second hand, mainly
  from Calvo & Lawrence 2023, and its auxin–neurotransmitter analogy is
  chemical similarity only, which does not bear on intensity.
- **That graded sentience gives graded status.** That is A3, assumed with
  citations to DeGrazia, Timmer and McMahan. Footnote 13 notes it is
  contested.
- **How much plants weigh.** The account is "coarse-grained" (p. 10). There
  is no scale, no aggregation rule and no mapping from degree to weight, so
  the forest-against-pig concession sits uneasily with "a very low value".
- **Which plant states are missing.** Footnote 21 names them: the degree of
  plant-specific valenced states, and whether plants have high harm
  thresholds or harm-numbing mechanisms.
- **That it is the second author's current view.** Veit's later work
  ([LIT-105](../literature.d/LIT-105.md)) declines to count *Paramecium* or *Chlamydomonas* as conscious,
  calls plant "experiences" predictive control, and cites his forthcoming
  case against plant consciousness ([NOTE-120](../notes.d/NOTE-120.md)).

## Connections

- **Rival.** Bruckner ([THEORY-699](THEORY-699.md)) gives plants welfare without
  sentience, by adaptive self-maintenance. This account assumes the
  opposite: no sentience, no interests. Footnote 2 sets such non-sentience
  grounds aside.
- **The precaution it borrows.** The step from a non-negligible chance to a
  pro tanto obligation (Sebo & Schukraft 2021) is the precaution that
  [THEORY-677](THEORY-677.md) says has no direction until an error preference is
  stated.
- **Over-generation.** Schwitzgebel ([LIT-159](../literature.d/LIT-159.md)) argues that criteria liberal
  enough to admit plants on behavioural and information-processing grounds
  over-generate. The paper does not address it.
- The map of grounds and weighing it relies on is the SEP entry ([LIT-146](../literature.d/LIT-146.md)),
  which is not neutral on weighing.
