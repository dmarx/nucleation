# THEORY-tmp4c2lf: Whether a machine thinks is best replaced by whether a digital computer can play the imitation game as well as a man, because the reasons for withholding thought from a machine that does would equally withhold it from other people, whose thinking we also infer from what they say; the objections from consciousness, mathematical limits and informality of behaviour fail on that test or rest on unproved premises

## Source

Turing (1950), *Computing Machinery and Intelligence*, [LIT-394](../literature.d/LIT-394.md) (*Mind*
59:433–460), read in full in [NOTE-355](../notes.d/NOTE-355.md).

## What was actually shown

**The replacement (§1).** "Can machines think?" is "too meaningless to
deserve discussion". In its place: a man (A), a woman (B) and an
interrogator (C) communicate by teleprinter; "What will happen when a
machine takes the part of A in this game?" Turing never calls this a test
of thinking, and never says "Turing test" ([NOTE-355](../notes.d/NOTE-355.md), Corrections).

**The reduction (§5).** A digital computer with adequate storage and speed
mimics any discrete-state machine, "considerations of speed apart". So the
question becomes whether one computer, suitably programmed, can "play
satisfactorily the part of A in the imitation game, the part of B being
taken by a man".

**The prediction (§6).** In about fifty years, at storage about 10^9, "an
average interrogator will not have more than 70 per cent. chance of making
the right identification after five minutes of questioning".

**The objections (§6).** The ones that bear on the account:

- *Consciousness* (Jefferson). Pressed fully, it is solipsism, and most
  holders would accept the game, as a viva voce, rather than that.
  Turing adds that he does not think there is "no mystery about
  consciousness"; the mystery need not be solved first.
- *Mathematical.* Any one machine has questions it cannot answer, but "it
  has only been stated, without any sort of proof, that no such
  limitations apply to the human intellect".
- *Informality of behaviour.* It confuses rules of conduct with laws of
  behaviour, and a 1000-unit Manchester programme already defeats
  prediction from its replies.
- *Lady Lovelace.* "Machines take me by surprise with great frequency."

**What Turing concedes (§7).** "I have no very convincing arguments of a
positive nature to support my views." The onion and atomic-pile passages
are "recitations tending to produce belief".

**What could have come out otherwise.** The prediction could, and it is
the paper's only empirical claim. The account itself is an argument about
what we would be entitled to say.

## What this does not say

- **That passing the game is a sufficient condition for thinking.** Turing
  replaces the question; he does not define thought. Block (n. 13) reads
  him as settling for passing as sufficient, and that is the reading
  [THEORY-092](THEORY-092.md) denies. The parity argument is what would make it sufficient,
  and only for an entitlement to say "thinks".
- **That the machine is conscious.** The reply to Jefferson is about what
  objectors would accept, not about what is there. Nagel's claim that a
  functional analysis is compatible with absent experience ([LIT-096](../literature.d/LIT-096.md)) is
  not engaged.
- **That parity holds for an imitator.** §2 assumes "the best strategy is
  to try to provide answers that would naturally be given by a man", and
  calls the alternative unlikely to matter. The parity argument assumes the
  machine's answers come about the way a man's do. A machine trained on
  human output, whose objective is imitation, is the case the assumption
  does not cover ([NOTE-355](../notes.d/NOTE-355.md), Open questions).
- **That the game has been passed.** A claim that it has should be measured
  against §6: an average interrogator, five minutes, at most 70 per cent
  right, with a human competitor in B's place.

## Connections

- **Rival: psychologism ([THEORY-092](THEORY-092.md)).** Block's lookup machine matches
  every actual and counterfactual response and "has the intelligence of a
  toaster", so there is a reason to withhold intelligence that does not
  apply to people: how the responses are produced. The two cannot both be
  right. [THEORY-092](THEORY-092.md) does not declare `corrects`, since both are Proposed.
- **Searle ([THEORY-tmpted1p](THEORY-tmpted1p.md))** answers the other-minds reply in one line:
  the question is what is attributed, not how it is known. He also grants
  that we would rationally ascribe intentionality to a humanlike robot
  until we learned it ran a formal program.
- **Dennett ([THEORY-tmphcjj0](THEORY-tmphcjj0.md))** gives a criterion of the same family for
  belief: anything whose behaviour the intentional strategy predicts
  reliably and voluminously is a believer.
- **Mimicry.** [THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md) holds that mimicry undercuts behavioural evidence
  for AI consciousness. It is aimed at the assumption of §2, which this
  account states and does not defend.
