# NOTE-tmp9kpa9: "Exit, Voice, and Loyalty": Further Reflections and a Survey of Recent Contributions

## Contribution

It extended the exit–voice framework in three directions the book had
neglected: when voice is a benefit rather than a cost, when exit is costly
or uninformative, and how organizations, including states, manage the two
responses for their own purposes. It also surveys early uses of the
framework in political science and urban studies.

## Key insight

Exit and voice are not only members' options; they are feedback channels,
and an organization's survival depends on keeping each between a floor,
below which it learns nothing about its decline, and a ceiling, above which
it falls apart. Those who run the organization have their own reasons to
set the levels.

## Assumptions

- The book's framework: members or customers of a deteriorating
  organization respond by leaving or by complaining, and both responses
  can prompt recovery.
- Exit is market-like and voice political; the paper aims to show
  economists that voice matters for economic phenomena.

## Key results

- **Voice as benefit** (pp. 432–434). Participation can become "a highly
  desired end in itself". Problems with a public-interest side, such as a
  health hazard or car safety, call forth voice where matters of taste
  call forth exit. Shareholders who long sold their stock instead began to
  use their influence when public-interest issues arose.
- **Information** (pp. 437–438). Voice carries detailed information; exit
  may not even show that customers are discontented, when they churn
  between equally bad suppliers. Voice suits situations of "ignorance and
  uncertainty, shared by consumers and producers", and dominates in
  periods of general loss of confidence in the traditional system.
- **Exit's costs** (p. 439). The book took exit as costless except where
  loyalty was present; switching costs between firms make it costly
  anyway, and vertical integration and marriage institutionalize voice.
- **Floors and ceilings** (p. 441). Every organization "navigates between
  the Scylla of disintegration-disruption and the Charybdis of
  deterioration due to lack of feedback". Managers may encourage the
  response "least dangerous to its perpetuation in power" (p. 442).
- **A hedged theorem** (p. 444). A state can control only two of exit,
  voice and repression; Hirschman does "not wish to make too much of" it.
- **Parties and cities** (pp. 444–451). Voice is used with special energy
  by those with nowhere to exit to. Where loyalty is stronger, as in
  two-party systems, dissatisfied members voice rather than exit. In
  cities, exit to the suburbs often wins narrowly, so small improvements
  in voice could change much. Defenders of the National Health Service
  want potential exiters kept inside as critics.

## Concepts

- **exit** — leaving: ceasing to buy, resigning, emigrating, moving away.
- **voice** — trying to change the organization from within by protest or
  participation.
- **loyalty** — not redefined here; it appears as what makes exit costly
  and voice more likely.
- **exit-fatigue** — after one exit, the inclination to use voice in the
  new setting (after Orbell and Uno, p. 449).

## Bearing on the record

For the owner's essay: §6.3's capture from below and extraction from above
have here the members' side, what they can do when the organization serves
them worse, and management's interest in steering them to the least
threatening response. §6.4's platform case fits the exit that signals
nothing, though platforms are not mentioned. §7.2 (acting through one's own
organization) is what voice is. §8.4 has the observation that floor levels
of exit and voice are a condition of the organization learning about its
own decline. The record's application to platforms is its own.

## Limitations

Informal and partly a survey of others' work. The theorem is hedged and
the party model admitted to be "primitive". Loyalty is presupposed from the
book, not treated. No systematic evidence is offered; examples carry the
argument.

## Open questions

What determines the floor and ceiling levels for a given organization, and
whether members who are locked in, with exit made costly, voice more or
simply comply.
