# NOTE-tmpgrtgu: Carnap — Logical Foundations of Probability

## Contribution

Two things. The classic statement of explication as a method, with its
requirements (chapter I), and its use throughout the book on probability,
confirmation, relevance, estimate, reliability and reasonable decision.
And a logical theory of probability: degree of confirmation as partial
logical implication, relative to evidence and to a language, with a
general theory of what holds for every admissible measure and a
provisional choice of one.

## Key results

### The method (chapters I, VII, IX and the appendix)

1. **Explication (§2, p. 3).** "The task of explication consists in
   transforming a given more or less inexact concept into an exact one
   or, rather, in replacing the first by the second." It may deviate
   "deliberately from the explicandum but still takes its place in some
   way".
2. **Not right or wrong (§2, p. 4).** "Strictly speaking, the question
   whether the solution is right or wrong makes no good sense because
   there is no clear-cut answer." The question is whether it is "more
   satisfactory than another one".
3. **Clarify the explicandum first (§2, p. 4).** A term is "at least
   prac- tically clear" to Y "if Y is able to predict correctly X’s
   interpretation for most of the simple, ordinary cases". Examples of
   uses intended and not intended "belong still to the formulation of the
   problem, not yet to the construction of an answer."
4. **Four requirements (§3, pp. 5–7).** "(1) similarity to the
   explicandum, (2) exactness, (3) fruitfulness, (4) simplicity", each "to
   a sufficient degree". Similarity holds "in most cases"; "close
   similarity is not required, and considerable differences are
   permitted". Fruitful: "useful for the formulation of many universal
   statements". Simple: "as simple as the more important requirements (1),
   (2), and (3) permit".
5. **Fish and Piscis (§3, p. 6).** The zoologists' change "was not a
   correction in the field of factual knowledge but a change in the rules
   of the language", made because Piscis "promised to be much more
   fruitful than any concept more similar to Fish". "It is important to
   recognize both the conventional and the factual components".
6. **The explicatum may overrule (§5, pp. 12–13).** "we have become
   accustomed to let the scientific concept overrule the prescientific",
   so "the term ‘warmer’ has undergone a change of meaning."
7. **Interpretation is essential (§6).** "for a genuine explication,
   however, an interpretation is essential."
8. **Rivals may answer different questions (§9).** The theories of
   probability "are not answers to the same problem"; Carnap is
   "distinguishing between two explicanda and not between the various
   explicata". Of Mises against Jeffreys: "This whole controversy seems
   futile and un- necessary." The vis viva dispute had "two correct
   answers to two different prob- lems." In §42A the frequentists "do not
   seem to recognize that the explicandum itself has been changed".
9. **Necessary conditions, not sufficient (§53, p. 285).** "a concept
   fulfilling these conditions may still be inadequate; but no concept
   which violates one of these conditions can be regarded as adequate."
   Symmetry is such a condition (§91, p. 489): "no concept can be regarded
   as an adequate explicatum for probability, unless it possesses the
   charac- teristic of symmetry."
10. **Testing by cases (§47F, §86).** Values are compared with intuitive
    values on simple cases, and "an approximate agreement will be regarded
    as sufficient". An explicatum is "clearly too wide if we find cases in
    which K holds but the explicandum clearly (i.e., with practically
    general agreement) does not hold" (p. 466), and too narrow
    symmetrically. A too narrow explicatum "constitutes a much less
    serious objection than the result that it is too wide" (§88, p. 479).
11. **Many explicata, none sharply best (§54).** For "Warmer" "there is an
    infinite number of quantitative ex- plicata", meaning "every one of
    these concepts is not entirely inadequate as explicatum, although
    there is, of course, no sharp boundary line."
12. **Judged by consequences, provisionally (§110A, pp. 563–564).** "It
    will not be claimed that c* is a perfectly adequate explicatum for
    probability,, let alone that it is the only adequate one." Simplicity
    "cannot, of course, be regarded as a sufficient reason for this
    choice"; the choice is justified "only by studying the consequences to
    which the definition leads and especially by comparing them with the
    consequences of other definitions." §40 (p. 149): "the plausibility of
    a definition is judged by the plausibility of the theorems derived
    from it".
13. **No self-certification (§103, p. 539).** Reliability computed within
    one confirmation function "is, so to speak, an internal affair within
    one system of inductive logic based upon a chosen function ¢; it
    cannot be used as a method for obtaining an external, objective judg-
    ment on the goodness of a system of inductive logic."
14. **A rational, corrected reconstruction (§110J, p. 576).** The theory
    is meant "not merely as an uncritical representation of customary ways
    of thinking with all their defects and inconsistencies, but rather as
    a rational, critically corrected reconstruction."

### Logical probability (chapters II to IX)

15. **Two explicanda (ch. II, p. 19).** Of probability-one: "if it is
    true, it is L-true (analytic)." Of probability-two: "A sentence about
    this concept is factu- al, empirical." Confirmation is "a relation
    between two sentences, not a property of one of them" (§8).
16. **Partial entailment between ranges (§10A, §55).** L-implication
    "states a complete logical implica- tion, the second states only, so
    to speak, a partial logical implication". "Inductive logic deals with
    the relation of partial inclusion between ranges, so to speak, partial
    L-implication." Range "will, in our construction, be the cornerstone
    both for deductive and for inductive logic" (§18D).
17. **Relative to a language (§14, §52).** The degree of confirmation
    depends "not only upon the two sentences but also upon the particular
    features of the language to which the sentences belong" (§14). "The
    relativity with respect to language has generally been overlooked"
    (§52). Choosing between language forms: "Preference will then be given
    to that language system for which a more adequate or more convenient
    inductive method can be developed" (§15B).
18. **Total evidence (§45B, p. 211).** "the total evidence available must
    be taken as basis for determining the degree of confirmation." It is a
    rule of methodology, and "There is no analogue to this requirement in
    deductive logic."
19. **Too many functions (§47).** "There is not a lack of suitable
    functions but an overabundance, in- deed an infinity of them."
20. **What all regular functions share leaves every value open (§§59,
    62).** For 1,000 observed instances "there are regular c-functions
    which have in this case any value however small". The axiom systems of
    Keynes, Jeffreys and others hold for all regular functions, which
    "makes these systems unnecessarily weak" (p. 344).
21. **Relevance behaves badly (§§59, 68–71).** "the relation of a high
    degree of confirmation is in certain respects similar to L-implication,
    it is not transitive." "the superficial appearance of plausibility in
    inductive logic is very often entirely misleading" (p. 368): two items
    each positive to h can be negative together. Knowing that i is
    positive to h, "it is not possible for us to infer whether 7 is
    positive, negative, or irrelevant to a sentence L-im- plied by / or to
    a sentence L-implying /" (p. 397).
22. **Hempel's conditions (§87).** Of the consequence condition and its
    converse: "Which of them is valid? The answer is, neither." The
    plausible conditions come from an "inadvertent transition" between
    increase of confirmation and confirmation above a threshold.
23. **Estimate and reasonable decision (§§51, 99–104).** Maximizing the
    estimated utility is proposed as the explicatum of a reasonable
    decision; "The rule is not a moral rule but a rule of applied logic."
    The estimate is the confirmation-weighted mean; the estimate of the
    relative frequency of truth among sentences "is equal to the
    arithmetic mean of the degree of confirmation of these sentences".
24. **c* (§110).** Choosing c† "would be tantamount to the principle
    never to let our past experi- ences influence our expectations for the
    future"; of the two
    simplest measure functions, "m* is the only one which is not entirely
    inadequate." For the infinite system a factual universal law gets c* =
    0, and a "reliable" law is re-read as a prediction about instances.

## Assumptions

- A sharp line between analytic and synthetic. Probability-one statements
  are analytic; empirical knowledge enters only as the evidence.
- Simple languages: individual constants, finitely many primitive
  predicates, monadic for most results; the infinite system by limits.
  Predicates logically independent and complete for the universe (§18B),
  completeness required for inductive and not deductive logic and
  "not yet quite clear" in its full strength.
- Explication is judged by fit to a purpose: laws or theorems for
  fruitfulness, agreement with careful inductive thinking for adequacy.
- Utility measurable on one scale (§51), presupposed and not examined.

## Concepts

Explicandum, explicatum, practical clarity (§2); the four requirements
(§3); classificatory, comparative and quantitative concepts (§4);
probability-one and probability-two (§9); state-description, range,
L-concepts (§§18–20); Q-predicate, logical width (§§31–32); requirement
of total evidence (§45B); regular and symmetrical m- and c-functions
(§§55, 90–91); null confirmation (§57); relevance and the relevance
measure (§§65–67); comparative confirmation MC (§81); clearly too wide,
clearly too narrow (§86); estimate as c-mean (§100); m*, c*, c†,
instance confirmation (§110).

## Bearing on the record

- **The explication form (ADR-tmptwlsg).** The book supports its
  critical questions and adds three of Carnap's own practices: asking
  whether rivals share the explicandum (§§9, 42A, 87), testing for clearly
  too wide and clearly too narrow (§§86, 88), and screening by necessary
  conditions (§§53, 91). It also warns that evidence of adequacy should
  not be internal to the explicatum (§103).
- **ARG-tmp23khr.** By §9's test, the performance definition it weighs as
  a rival may explicate another explicandum, successful learning, which
  the term already keeps apart.
- **CLAIM-472.** Supports the first half throughout, and the second half
  through the Fish case and §45A ("For any single fact in the world, a
  language sys- tem can be constructed which is capable of representing
  that fact while others are not covered").
- **CLAIM-293 and CLAIM-294.** Relevance theory: structure does not fix
  support, and separate supports do not fix their joint effect.
- **CLAIM-401.** Completeness is adequacy relative to a purpose.
- **THEORY-1239.** A logic in which later evidence lowers an earlier
  assessment, by total evidence, without non-monotonic inference.
- **THEORY-1797.** The primary text behind the SEP entry's account.

## Limitations

The OCR, as above. Several results are promised for a second volume
(c*'s values, the variety of instances, the case against maximum
likelihood). The languages are far poorer than science's, as Carnap says.
Carnap's readings of Keynes, Jeffreys, Mises, Reichenbach, Koopman and
Hempel are his; those authors were not read here.
