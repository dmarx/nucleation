# NOTE-362: Kammerer — How a materialist can deny that the US is probably conscious

## Contribution

Schwitzgebel ([LIT-159](../literature.d/LIT-159.md), §2) argued that anti-nesting principles are both
unmotivated and refuted by cases: the antheads, whose insect parts may be
conscious, and Block's tiny conscious organisms taking over neuron roles.
Kammerer replies with a principle that keeps both cases conscious and
still excludes the US. He does this by moving the criterion from *whether
parts are conscious* to *what the parts are conscious of and whether the
whole needs that*. It is built specifically to survive Schwitzgebel's
counterexamples, and is the only principle in the record that is.

## Key insight

A whole should not be credited with a conscious state when the
organisation that would ground the state is supplied by its parts
thinking about the whole. Parts that are conscious but represent nothing
about the whole (insects in an anthead, tiny people playing neurons) do
not block anything. Parts whose representations of the whole are
indispensable (citizens who know what the US is and should do) do. On
this view the US's behaviour is fully explained by the conscious states of
other subjects, so ascribing it a further stream of consciousness
multiplies subjects needlessly.

## Assumptions

- **Supervenience on functional organisation (§2, p. 3).** Phenomenally
  conscious states supervene on functional organisation. Kammerer notes
  this is implied by most materialisms and is also compatible with
  Chalmers's naturalistic dualism. Type materialisms (McLaughlin, Polger)
  are not strictly committed to it, but must accept that some functional
  complexity is required (n. 1). He then works with functionalist
  materialism "for reasons of simplicity".
- **A theory of consciousness supplies, for each conscious state S, a
  functional property P sufficient for S (p. 4, nn. 5–6).** Ideally an
  exhaustive disjunction Z of such properties, necessary and sufficient.
  The SAP is an exception clause added to any such theory: "if an entity
  has P, then it has S, except if it falls under the cases described by"
  the SAP.
- **Parts are functional parts.** A part of W's functional organisation
  must make "a certain lasting (even if merely dispositional)
  contribution" to W's causal properties. A one-off intervention is not a
  part, as brain surgery is not part of the organisation it changes
  (n. 8).
- **Humans are conscious, and so (possibly) are the anthead insects** (p. 3).
- **No particular theory of consciousness is assumed** (p. 3). Kammerer
  says the SAP "may in the end imply that certain kinds of functional
  organization cannot be the basis of a genuine stream of consciousness".

## Key results

All are informal. There are no formal results and no data.

- **The SAP (§3, p. 4).** Given W with P, where P is normally sufficient
  for S, W does not have S if W has at least one part playing a role in its
  functional organisation such that:
  - **(A)** performing the role requires the part to have conscious mental
    states (beliefs, emotions, hopes, experiences, desires) *representing
    W*: what it is, what it does, what it should do. That is, the part has
    a functional property Q sufficient for a conscious state R about W.
  - **(B)** if no part of W performed a role of that kind, W would no longer
    have P, nor any other property sufficient for S. "In other words: if
    no subpart of W had R, then W would no longer have S."
- **The cases (§4, pp. 5–7).**
  - *Antheads: conscious.* The insects do not represent the whole creature
    (A fails), and neurons could do their job (B fails).
  - *Block's tiny people in place of neurons: conscious.* The role can be
    played by a non-conscious neuron, so B fails "even if condition A is
    fulfilled".
  - *The US: not conscious.* Citizens have conscious states representing
    the US. A country of neurons or simple computers, or one whose
    inhabitants "had no idea of their existence as a group", would not
    show the US's behavioural and cognitive complexity. So A and B hold.
  - *Unwitting group consciousness: allowed.* Humans could be part of a
    collective organisation that is conscious without their knowing. If
    some come to know of it and that knowledge becomes necessary for new
    organisation, the new aspects cannot ground new conscious states of
    the whole. Its "old" states may persist (p. 5).
  - *Fine-grained verdicts.* Since the SAP works on states, not on
    consciousness wholesale, the tiny people could make me *talk* about a
    theorem M by consciously steering my speech. I would then have no
    conscious thoughts about M, while keeping my ordinary states and the
    auditory experience of my own speech (pp. 5–6).
  - *Reviewer's variant (n. 8).* If the organisms insert a thought about M
    once, so that I can go on unaided, I do consciously think about M:
    their act is not part of my organisation. If their continuing
    intervention is needed, the SAP says my "thoughts" about M are not
    conscious. Kammerer bites this bullet and says it is not "such a
    counter-intuitive claim after all".
  - *Spatial scatter and animals.* The SAP is consistent with
    Schwitzgebel's supersquids and with animal and alien consciousness, so
    it entails neither "contiguism" nor "neurochauvinism" (p. 7).
- **Chalmers's objection kept, Schwitzgebel's reply escaped (pp. 6–7).**
  Chalmers suggested that the US's capacities arise largely from its
  members' capacities rather than their relations. Schwitzgebel replied
  that global-workspace theories say the same of human minds. Kammerer
  answers that workspace modules are not conscious and do not represent
  the person, so the SAP excludes the US but not humans.
- **Defence against arbitrariness (§5, pp. 7–8).**
  - *Intuitive.* Putnam's principle has an "intuitive pull"; his mistake
    was to read "subpart" spatially, not functionally.
  - *Not ad hoc.* It follows from "a specialized version of Occam's
    Razor": "one should only ascribe consciousness to an entity when one
    cannot explain the behavior and the organization that seems to justify
    this ascription as the consequence of mental states of other, distinct
    subjects" (p. 8). This is also why we deny consciousness to books,
    computers and puppets. Kammerer grounds it in the explanatory role of
    consciousness ascriptions, adding that "a satisfying justification
    would require further reasoning".
  - *Residual arbitrariness is everyone's.* "Why is a whole conscious in
    this case but not that?" can be asked of any theory of consciousness
    (the hard problem, Levine's explanatory gap), so it is no special
    objection to the SAP (p. 8).

## Concepts

- **Anti-nesting principle** — any principle that a conscious entity cannot
  be constituted by other conscious entities (p. 2). Putnam's is the
  standard example: no pain-capable organism decomposes into parts
  separately capable of pain.
- **Sophisticated Anti-Nesting Principle (SAP)** — the A-and-B exception
  clause above, added to any functional-supervenience theory.
- **Representing W** — having conscious states about "what it is, what it
  does, what it should do" (p. 4).
- **Functional subpart** — a part that makes a lasting, possibly
  dispositional, contribution to the whole's causal properties (n. 8).
- **Genuine nested consciousness** — the case the SAP rules out: a whole
  whose would-be conscious state exists only through parts consciously
  representing the whole (p. 4).

## Bearing on the record

- **For the bridge THEORY.** This is the source for the claim that the
  anti-nesting question can be split: nested consciousness as such
  (allowed), and consciousness grounded in parts' representations of the
  whole (denied). Cite it for the SAP's statement and its verdicts on the
  standard cases. Do **not** cite it as showing that the US fails
  condition B; that is asserted.
- **Correction to [NOTE-188](NOTE-188.md)'s gloss.** [NOTE-188](NOTE-188.md) reports the principle as "a
  whole is not conscious if its conscious parts represent the whole". The
  text has two further restrictions: indispensability (condition B), and a
  scope of single conscious states, not whole consciousness. Without B,
  the SAP would make Block's tiny-people brain unconscious too, which is
  exactly the case it was built to keep. [NOTE-188](NOTE-188.md) is not edited here.
- **No ML instruction.** Nothing belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- **The US verdict rests on condition B, asserted.** No US-level capacity
  is analysed to show that it needs members' representations of the US,
  rather than of their offices, neighbours, markets or rules. Schwitzgebel's
  reply ([LIT-tmplt3pf](../literature.d/LIT-tmplt3pf.md)) does not press this: its first concern is about
  condition A, whether citizens consciously represent the US at all, and
  condition B is not discussed ([NOTE-tmpqu2kh](NOTE-tmpqu2kh.md)). The gap here is still open.
- **Representation is left undefined.** Which states count as
  "representing W" (does a tax clerk's belief about a form?) is not
  settled.
- **The justification is promissory.** The Occam rule is stated, and the
  author says it needs "further reasoning".
- **It keeps old states, denies new ones.** The state-by-state scope means a
  whole can be partly conscious under the SAP. The paper does not say how
  a phenomenal field is split this way.
- **Short.** A nine-page reply. It addresses §2 and Chalmers only.

## Open questions

- Which of the US's capacities would survive replacing its members with
  agents that represent only local matters (their job, their neighbours)?
  The answer decides condition B, and with it the SAP's verdict.
- Does a self-model in a Minsky-style mind (an agent that represents the
  whole person) trigger the SAP for the states that depend on it? If so,
  the principle has consequences for human self-consciousness that the
  paper does not draw.
- How does the SAP fare against Schwitzgebel's three concerns ([LIT-tmplt3pf](../literature.d/LIT-tmplt3pf.md),
  read in [NOTE-tmpqu2kh](NOTE-tmpqu2kh.md)) and the Planck-sized-person case ([LIT-216](../literature.d/LIT-216.md))?
