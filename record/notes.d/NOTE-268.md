# NOTE-268: Identification and Identity

## Contribution

Velleman disputes one of Frankfurt's themes, "the importance of which things we care about". On Frankfurt's view, cares matter because "our cares and concerns define our individual essences as persons: what we care about determines who we are". Velleman denies "that we have motivational essences of this sort, though I agree that we sometimes seem to have them", and sets out "to look for the source of this misleading appearance" (p. 331). The essay has four parts:

1. It reconstructs Frankfurt's "new conception of the self" and shows that it yokes autonomy to personal identity in a neo-Lockean, Parfitian way (pp. 331–338).
2. It criticizes that conception and diagnoses its appeal as wishful and defensive, using Winnicott, Freud's Rat Man and Sartre (pp. 338–347, 352–354).
3. It distinguishes the ordinary, imaginative sense of "identification" from Frankfurt's stipulated sense (pp. 347–353).
4. It sketches an "aspectual" conception of self that keeps Frankfurt's "guiding insight" about autonomy without the metaphysics of an essential self (pp. 354–360).

## Key insight

**Identification is not identity.** Frankfurt's recent work assumes that the sense of "self" in an account of autonomy (the self whose governance is self-governance) is the same as the sense in accounts of personal identity and self-regard. On that assumption the self that makes action autonomous is also the kernel "whose existence is necessary and sufficient for his existing" (p. 353). Velleman separates the two:

- "Self" is a word for reflexivity. It "has various uses in various contexts" and there is "no single entity to which it refers in all of them" (p. 354).
- What we "cannot help identifying" with, the understanding, is essentially *self* only in an aspectual sense: it always presents a first-personal aspect, as the point between the eyes is always visually "here". It is not essential to our *identity* (pp. 357–359).

## Assumptions

- **Velleman's own account of personal identity, from "Self to Self", ch. 8 of the collection.** One's past and future selves are the persons one can pick out with notionally reflexive, first-personal thoughts. These thoughts are mediated by experiential memory and anticipation, not by retained motives or traits (pp. 339, 355, nn. 29, 63). The critique of motivational essences leans on this view. It is stated here, not argued.
- **Psychoanalytic case material is admissible.** Freud's "Notes Upon a Case of Obsessional Neurosis" (the Rat Man), Winnicott, Anna Freud, Rubin and Eagle are used as evidence about the psychology of ambivalence and defence. Velleman claims the moral holds "without relying on any distinctively Freudian hypotheses" (p. 345).
- **A conception of the self should not be tailored to wishes.** "We are justified in wishing to embody an ideal implicit in our self-conception; but our self-conception should not be tailored to suit our antecedent wishes" (p. 341). This premise drives the genealogical part of the critique.
- **Frankfurt's "identification" is a stipulated technical term**, introduced for "the psychological process by which a person empowers some of his motives to implicate him in causing behavior, so that whatever they motivate will be attributable to him, as his doing" (p. 353). Velleman explicitly does not accuse Frankfurt of using the ordinary sense (pp. 347, 353).

## Key results

Pages are *Self to Self* (2006). All claims about Frankfurt are **as Velleman reports them**, with the page references he gives. IWWCA = *The Importance of What We Care About* (CUP; "1987" per this chapter's note 1). NVL = *Necessity, Volition, and Love* (CUP 1999).

### Frankfurt, as Velleman reports him

- **The early theory (p. 331).**
  - "Ever since 'Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person'", Frankfurt has sought to distinguish motives internal and external to the self. The distinction was prompted by alienation cases in which motives "seem to assail the agent from without and to compete with him for control of his behavior".
  - His "initial answer relied on the concept of identification": motives are internal "when the subject identifies with them, by reflectively endorsing them as determinants of his behavior". An agent is autonomous "when he is actuated in ways that he reflectively endorses".
  - This "elicited a number of objections … leading to various revisions" (not discussed; n. 2).
  - The recent revision is "not obviously prompted by objections: it appears to express a further intuition about the boundaries of the self".
- **The new criterion (p. 332).** "A person acts autonomously only when his volitions derive from the essential character of his will" ("Autonomy, Necessity, and Love", NVL 132, n. 3). Frankfurt first made the claim less explicitly in "Rationality and the Unthinkable": "With respect to a person whose will has no fixed determinate character, it seems that the notion of autonomy or self-direction cannot find a grip" (IWWCA 178).
  - Inessential characteristics of the will are "separable", and in that sense "external", so being governed by them is heteronomy.
  - *Internality is now essentiality to one's volitional nature.*
- **Anti-Kantian (pp. 332–333).** Frankfurt rejects locating autonomy in Kant's "pure will". Velleman quotes: "The pure will has no individuality whatsoever … the pure will is thoroughly *impersonal*. The commands that it issues are issued by no one in particular" (ANL 132).
  - The self of self-governance must be "a thoroughly *personal* self", made of "contingent personal features that make people distinctive".
  - "Even though a person's interests are contingent, they can belong to the essential nature of his will" (ANL 135).
- **Volitional necessity, in stages (pp. 333–335).**
  1. The essence of an agent is what is volitionally necessary for him, by analogy with a triangle's conceptual necessities. But "Volitional necessity constrains the person himself, by limiting the choices he can make" ("On the Necessity of Ideals", NVL 113; ANL 138).
  2. Compulsion and overwhelming aversion also constrain, from without. So the necessity must be a *willing* inability, as when an act is "unthinkable". It becomes voluntary when due to a motive the subject identifies with by reflective endorsement, so that "the constraint is itself imposed by his will" (NI 111–12; ANL 136–38; R&U 182–83).
  3. If endorsement could be withheld, the constraint would not be essential. So the agent must be *unable to help endorsing* it. This is a second-order inability, which must itself be willing, "and so on", resounding "through higher and higher levels of the motivational hierarchy". Velleman notes the image is Frankfurt's own (FWCP, IWWCA 21; "Identification and Wholeheartedness", IWWCA 168; n. 9). Quote: "it is not only unthinkable for him to perform the action in question; it is also unthinkable for him to form an effective intention to become willing to perform it" (NI 112; R&U 187). Velleman's n. 10: "Presumably then, the person must endorse his inability to will any change in his inability to will. Here the threat of a regress reappears."
  4. These are "contingent volitional necessities by which the will of the person is as a matter of fact constrained" (ANL 138). They define the individual essence, so he "could not alter them while remaining the same person".
- **Agamemnon and the dissolution of self (p. 335).**
  - Agamemnon at Aulis is "destroyed by an inescapable conflict between two equally defining elements in his own nature … the person he had been no longer exists. Hence, there can be no continuation of *his* story" (ANL 139 n. 8).
  - Whoever is free of all volitional limits has "no essential nature or identity" (IWWCA preface, ix; R&U 188; NI), and suffers "a diminution, or even a dissolution, of the reality of the self" (R&U 179).
  - Boredom "threatens the extinction of the active self". Our aversion to it expresses self-preservation "in the sense of sustaining not the *life* of the organism but the *persistence of the self*" ("On the Usefulness of Final Ends", NVL 89).
  - Ambivalence is a third threat (ANL 139 n. 9). Hence the self must be *wholehearted*, "unequivocal in its essential concerns".
- **Authority (p. 336).** Essential concerns "possess not simply power but authority" (ANL 138), derived from self-preservation: opposing them risks "drastic psychic injuries" (ANL 139).
- **Identity and essence (n. 19).** "The idea that the identity of a thing is to be understood in terms of conditions that are essential for its existence is one of the oldest and most compelling of the philosophical principles …" (NI 113).
- **Wholeheartedness (pp. 341–342).** It is not the absence of conflict. It is decisive identification with one desire and dissociation from its rival, a "radical *separation* of the competing desires, one of which is not merely assigned a relatively less favored position but extruded entirely as an outlaw" (IWh, IWWCA 170).
  - "The conflict between the *desires* is in this way transformed into a conflict between *one* of them and the *person* who has identified himself with its rival" (IWh 172).
  - "Wholeheartedness does not require that a person be altogether untroubled by inner opposition to his will. It just requires that … he himself be fully resolved … resolutely on the side of one of the forces struggling within him and not on the side of any other" ("The Faintest Passion", NVL 100).
  - Healing analogy: the body *eliminates* conflicts, whereas a person who makes up his mind may integrate himself "without actually eliminating the desires that conflict … as long as he dissociates himself from them" (IWh 173–174).
  - In TFP (100), Frankfurt quotes Augustine on ambivalence as "a disease of the mind" and himself calls it "a disease of the will" (n. 33).
- **Another sense of identification (n. 51).** Frankfurt once uses "identification" in a different sense: "A person who cares about something is, as it were, invested in it … he *identifies* himself with what he cares about" (NI 111). The target here is an external object, not one of his own motives.
- **Blurring (n. 67).** Frankfurt "doesn't adequately distinguish the person's grasp of his identity from the identity so grasped". He slides from "extensive proliferation of [a person's] options may weaken his grasp of his own identity" (R&U 177; cf. NI 109) to "an excess of freedom gives rise to a diminution, or even a dissolution, of the reality of the self" (R&U 179; cf. NI 110: "With total freedom, there can be no individual identity").

### Frankfurt and Parfit (pp. 336–338)

- Frankfurt's late view "yoked" autonomy to a conception of personal identity that agrees with Parfit's. It agrees in the respects in which Parfit departs from Locke.
- Locke, even extended recursively, makes survival a matter of memory alone. The prince and the cobbler could swap "beliefs, desires, ideals, loves, projects".
- Parfit counts persistence of attitudes and traits as part of survival (*Reasons and Persons* 205–206, 301–302). Differentiating traits count more (300–301, 515 n. 6), and so do valued ones (299). Parfit's "Nineteenth Century Russian" (327–328) confirms the Frankfurtian consequence.

### Velleman's critique

1. **Essence would give authenticity, not autonomy (p. 338).** Winnicott's "False Self" conforms to others' expectations. He is motivated by neither the motives he simulates nor the essential ones. Yet "his problem is one of excess: he is overly self-controlled … his failure to be motivated from within his true self makes him inauthentic, but it seems to result from his being all too autonomous". Note 27: his pseudo-autonomy is a misdirected *real* self-control.
2. **There is no motivational essence (pp. 338–341).** Rubin's critique of the True Self as "a quixotic enterprise" (p. 339) is applied to Frankfurt. Velleman does not deny that some concerns are authoritative "because they are somehow central to our personalities", only the literal self-preservation reading, which "can easily lead to absurdity". Two tests:
   - Robert Paul Wolff's "I am *essentially* such a man": forty years old, a father of small boys, American-Jewish (p. 340).
   - The Agamemnon legend: "estrangements and betrayals are precisely what set the stage for a sequel". If Aulis ended its protagonist, "we wouldn't have the concept of a sequel at all" (pp. 340–341).
3. **Frankfurt gives no argument (p. 341).** "Frankfurt never offers us a convincing example of someone who ceases to be the same person because of abandoning a project … nor does he offer any argument … He simply asserts". We accept it because we want to say it. The worry is "wishful thinking".
4. **Wholeheartedness is defensive (pp. 341–347).**
   - The Rat Man's illness came not from ambivalence (loving and hating his father) but from *repressing* the hatred: self-misinterpretation motivated by the desire to dissociate from it. Freud's words: the hostile thoughts were "entirely foreign and repugnant to him" (SE X 167). The repression "brought his whole subsequent career under the dominion of the neurosis" (238). Freud's view that a wish becomes compulsive when the ego "regard[s] it as something foreign to itself" (162–163) is "close to what Frankfurt believes as well".
   - So "the Rat Man suffered, not from the disease of ambivalence, but from something like Frankfurt's cure". The "radical separation" of desires led to the "splitting of the personality" (p. 344).
   - Frankfurt would say the Rat Man expelled the hatred from the wrong place, from self-awareness rather than from the self. Velleman replies that he should have accepted "*himself* as ambivalent toward his father"; "the mistake was indeed his attempt to separate competing desires by expelling one of them, not the specific form of expulsion" (p. 345).
   - Common-sense version: mixed emotions must "mingle" to be digested (p. 346). Our wish for wholeheartedness manifests fear of our own emotions, so "our affinity for Frankfurt's ideal may not indicate that he's right about the constitution of the self; it may indicate no more than our own defensiveness" (p. 346).
   - Motivational essences and the "inner sanctuary of the self" are likewise comforting, defensible territories (p. 347).
5. **Identification in the ordinary sense (pp. 347–352).**
   - Ordinary identification is primarily with *other people*, fictional characters and role models. It can be evaluatively neutral or negative: Anna Freud's "identification with the aggressor".
   - It is "above all, an exercise of the imagination" (after Wollheim). It is a spontaneous imagining that one is the other, held in the background, sometimes unconsciously, while it colours one's realistic attitudes. Usually one imagines the world as experienced by him.
   - Its consequences are empathy, insight into the other, and a tendency to behave like him, as in make-believe.
   - Frankfurt's usage is not this; he treats "identify with" and "identify oneself with" interchangeably (n. 53).
6. **Identifying with a motive is bad faith (pp. 352–353).** Read in the ordinary sense, identifying with a desire means imagining *being* a proper part of oneself. That is Sartre's café waiter, "playing at being" a waiting machine, Sartre's "prime specimen of bad faith". One can likewise imagine being "parent-lovers and nothing more" to keep hatred at bay. "We do indeed identify with some of our motives, but we thereby engage not in self-definition but self-deception" (p. 353). Velleman stresses that this is how *readers* may hear Frankfurt, not what Frankfurt meant.
7. **The real target (pp. 353–354).** Frankfurt's term "has greatly advanced the philosophy of action over the past twenty years". His initial assumption, that identification incorporates motives into "the self" so that governance by them is autonomy, is "harmless … so long as it leaves open the sense in which the term 'self' is being used". What his recent work adds is the assumption that the same "self" underlies autonomy, personal identity and self-regard (self-betrayal, self-worth). That is the assumption Velleman rejects.

### Velleman's alternative (pp. 354–360)

- "I don't believe in the self, so conceived", and "believing in the self is optional". The view is stated "briefly" and "developed more fully" in "What Happens When Someone Acts?" (n. 62).
- **"Self" expresses reflexivity**, the coincidence of subject and object, especially notional reflexivity. It is used to report "I"-thoughts "just as we use 'present' to report a thought or utterance containing 'now'". A theory of the self "should have as little substance as a philosophical theory of the present" (pp. 354–355).
- **In identity contexts**, past and future selves are the persons one can refer to first-personally, via experiential memory (Locke was right) and experiential anticipation (p. 355).
- **In self-regard contexts**, "self" is the self-image or self-conception. Here Velleman is "happy to say that particular cares and concerns can be definitive of a person's identity or essential to the self", but only to the self-*conception*. Losing them means temporarily not knowing who one is, not ceasing to be (p. 356). In the example of fundamentalist students threatened by discussion of same-sex marriage, Frankfurt's view "seems to imply that these students are justified to resist any change of mind … on grounds of self-preservation". Velleman allows some resistance, "but not as much as would be justified for the sake of literal self-preservation" (p. 357).
- **In autonomy contexts**, a part of a person he cannot regard non-reflexively, from a detached third-personal perspective, "will be essentially 'self' to him … Its being his essential self won't mean that it is essential to his identity" (pp. 357–358). The analogy is the visual standpoint between the eyes, still "back here" when seen "over there" in a mirror (after Dennett's "Where Am I?"). Aristotle's "each person seems to be his understanding" (*NE* 1178a) is explained the same way (p. 358).
- **Autonomy.** "Roughly speaking, my view is that autonomous action is behavior motivated in part by the understanding"; how it motivates is "beyond the scope of this essay" (p. 359). Personhood is not aspectual; only selfhood is (n. 73).
- **Reconciliation (pp. 359–360).** "This conception of autonomy remains deeply indebted to Frankfurt". His guiding insight is that autonomy rests on "his capacity to reflect on aspects of his personality and to feel a special relation to some of them". What Frankfurt now says is that autonomous behaviour is motivated by parts "with which he cannot help identifying" (n. 74, pointing to the quotations at n. 10). If "identification" means first-personal thinking, that becomes the aspectual thesis. Velleman's conclusion: "I agree with Frankfurt that autonomous action is guided by a part of us with which we cannot help identifying; I disagree mainly with his claim that our identifying with a part of ourselves incorporates it into something called the self" (p. 360).

## Method

A textual reconstruction of Frankfurt's late essays followed by critique. The critique uses counterexamples (the False Self, Wolff, Agamemnon), debunking genealogy (wishful and defensive thinking), psychoanalytic case interpretation (the Rat Man) and ordinary-language analysis of "identification", backed by the psychology of imagination. The positive part is a sketch that leans on arguments made in other essays of the collection ("Self to Self", ch. 8; "What Happens When Someone Acts?"; the PPR introduction).

## Concepts

- **Internal vs. external motives**: Frankfurt's organizing distinction since 1971, as Velleman reports it (p. 331).
- **Identification as reflective endorsement**: Frankfurt's "initial answer" (p. 331).
- **Essential character of the will / volitional essence**: Frankfurt's late criterion of internality (p. 332).
- **Volitional necessity**: a willing, higher-order-endorsed inability ("unthinkable"), constraining the person himself (pp. 333–334).
- **Wholeheartedness**: being resolutely on one side of every inner conflict, the rival desire "extruded entirely as an outlaw" (pp. 341–342).
- **Ambivalence as disease of the self/will**: Frankfurt, following Augustine (n. 33).
- **Authenticity vs. autonomy**: Velleman's distinction, illustrated by Winnicott's False Self (p. 338).
- **Ordinary identification**: spontaneous, background imagining that one is another, with empathetic and behavioural consequences (pp. 348–352).
- **Bad faith**: identifying with a proper part of oneself, as Sartre's waiter does (p. 352).
- **Aspectual self**: "self" as a marker of reflexivity. The *essential self* is what one cannot regard non-reflexively, distinct from what is essential to one's *identity* (pp. 354–359).
- **Self-conception / sense of identity**: what an "identity crisis" threatens (p. 356).

## Limitations

- **Scope is the late Frankfurt only.** The early hierarchical theory and its standard objections are compressed to one paragraph and outsourced (n. 2). A reader wanting "Velleman on Frankfurt 1971–1987" needs the 1992 paper.
- **The positive view is announced, not defended.** Velleman says "Expounding my own views is not the purpose of this essay" (p. 354). The aspectual self and the understanding-based account of autonomy rest on other essays. "How the understanding motivates" is "beyond the scope" (p. 359).
- **The debunking argument (C6) shows only that the appeal of the view *could* be wishful.** It does not show the view false. Velleman frames it as a "worry" (p. 341).
- **The Rat Man argument depends on Freud's case interpretation** and on an unargued folk-psychological premise that emotions must "mingle" to be digested (p. 346).
- **C4, that Frankfurt "simply asserts", is a claim about Frankfurt's whole corpus** that cannot be checked from here. Frankfurt's Agamemnon passage (ANL 139 n. 8) is at least an attempted example, which Velleman addresses on p. 340.
- **Bibliographic slips in the reprint.** The note dates *Contours of Agency* 2001 and IWWCA 1987. Other sources, including Velleman's own PPR, give 2002 and 1988 respectively.
- **The body text was not compared with the MIT Press printing.**

## Open questions

- How does Frankfurt answer in "Reply to J. David Velleman" (*Contours of Agency*, pp. 124–128)? In particular, does he accept the identity/self-conception distinction or deny the slide that Velleman locates in n. 67?
- Does the "cannot help identifying" formula (n. 10, n. 74) re-open the regress that Velleman flags in n. 10? And does his own understanding-based self escape it in the way the 1992 paper claims the desire to act for reasons does?
- Is the Winnicott counterexample (C3) decisive against autonomy-as-essence, or does it only show that autonomy and authenticity can diverge in pathological cases?
- The SEP and IEP autonomy entries in this batch should be checked on whether later hierarchical theorists kept Frankfurt's late link between autonomy and identity, or took Velleman's separation.
