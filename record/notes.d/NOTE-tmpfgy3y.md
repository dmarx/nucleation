# NOTE-tmpfgy3y: McKenzie — Emergence from physics to computer science

## Contribution

McKenzie defines an emergent property by novelty: a many-part system has properties its parts lack. He reviews other putative marks — universality, order, complexity, unpredictability, irreducibility, diversity, self-organisation, discontinuities and singularities — noting it is not established whether any is necessary or sufficient for novelty. Through examples spanning physics, biology, the social sciences and computing, he frames emergence via scales and a stratified reality, stresses the mesoscale and modularity, and presents effective theories and toy models (the Ising model above all) as the core methods.

- §§4–20 (pp. 29–132): long case catalogue — Ising models, classical and quantum condensed matter, nuclear/particle physics, quantum gravity, causal-emergence measures (§14), chemistry, protein folding, biology, economics, sociology.
- §21 Computer science (pp. 132–139): neural networks and LLMs, recounting Wei et al.'s (2022) "emergent abilities" as a phase-transition-like onset with scale.
- §22 Philosophical issues (pp. 139–143): questions of explanation and fundamentality from a physicist's side; favours condensed-matter cases over consciousness as test beds; notes Laughlin's claim that some emergent properties are exact and hence more fundamental than micro-theories.
- §§23–24 (pp. 143–145+): history (Anderson first used "emergence" in print in 1981; Polanyi's Tacit Dimension); conclusions on scientific strategy.
