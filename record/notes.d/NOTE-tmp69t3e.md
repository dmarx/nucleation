# NOTE-tmp69t3e: Apps and Tsakiris — The free-energy self: a predictive coding account of self-recognition

## Contribution

Before this paper, accounts of the bodily self came in two kinds.
Comparator accounts tie self-specificity to matching efference copy with
reafference (Legrand and Ruby) or interoceptive prediction with its
outcome (Seth et al.). Others posit self-specific cortical networks
(Northoff et al.). The multisensory illusions were explained piecemeal,
by visual capture and remapped reference frames. The paper places
self-recognition inside the free-energy account of cortical function. It
claims that one principle covers the rubber-hand, enfacement and
rubber-voice illusions, the effects of context and culture on self-face
recognition, and the spread of self-related activity across non-specific
regions. Its distinctive move is to let any sensory channel, not only
motor efference, explain away surprise in any other: "information from
each and every sensory system potentially able to modulate
self-recognition" (§4).

## Key insight

"The notion that there is a 'self' is the most parsimonious and accurate
explanation for sensory inputs. In mathematical terms, this parsimonious
accuracy is exactly the quantity that is optimised when minimising free
energy or prediction error" (§4). The self is a hypothesis the brain
holds about the causes of its multisensory input. A hypothesis can lose
ground to a rival, and the illusions are what that looks like.

## Assumptions

- **The free-energy principle and hierarchical predictive coding as
  given.** "The aim of this paper is not to discuss the validity or
  evidence supporting the free-energy principle" (§3). Representational
  and error units, predictions descending, errors ascending.
- **The self-label sits at the top of the hierarchy.** "Recognised as
  'self' when different streams of multisensory information are
  integrated" (§4), in multimodal areas that issue parallel predictions
  to several unimodal systems.
- **Ownership is graded and probabilistic.** Assumed, and conceded to be
  untested: "there has yet not been a direct test of whether the
  likelihood of stimuli being accepted as a part of one's body is
  probabilistic in nature" (§7).
- **Self and other are competing generative models**, selected by
  relative fit with mutual suppression, as in Hohwy et al.'s account of
  binocular rivalry (§5.2).
- **Action as active inference.** "Only actions that have predictable
  sensory outcomes are performed" (§4); motor cortex sends proprioceptive
  predictions, so "corollary discharge is just like any other descending
  prediction", citing Friston and Ao 2012 and Friston, Mattout and Kilner
  2011. The illusions are then treated without action.
- **Illusions are Bayes-optimal.** "Illusions can be explained as an
  unusual set of sensory circumstances being interpreted under prior
  beliefs about their causes in a Bayes optimal fashion. In our examples,
  these prior beliefs reflect the fact that most of our sensations are
  caused by ourselves" (§5.1).

## Key results

There are no formal or empirical results. The paper's claims, by section:

- **§2, the free-energy principle.** Surprise is minimised two ways:
  "agents can act upon the environment to alter the incoming sensory
  events, sampling the environment in a manner that minimises prediction
  errors. That is agents will perform actions with predictable
  consequences that are confirmatory of expectations", or update their
  estimates of causes. Perceptual inference (fast, activity) is
  distinguished from perceptual learning (slow, synaptic).
- **§4, the mirror.** An infant meets an object that moves in time with
  its corollary discharge, in an allocentric frame, with a specific
  visual form. Multisensory areas explain these surprises away, and
  perceptual learning makes one's face the expected input at a
  reflective surface. The rouge task is the behavioural marker.
- **§5.1, the illusions.** The rubber-hand illusion ownership is cited to
  Tsakiris and Haggard 2005, and proprioceptive drift to Tsakiris et al.
  2006. Full-body illusions are cited as Ehrsson 2007 (perspective),
  Lenggenhager et al. 2007 (self-location; the name is misspelled
  "Leggenhanger" in the text) and Petkova and Ehrsson 2008
  (self-identification), mapped onto Blanke and Metzinger's three
  conditions of selfhood. Enfacement shifts self-other face judgements
  and self-report (Tsakiris 2008; Sforza et al. 2010; Tajadura-Jiménez
  et al. 2012). One study reports a rubber-voice illusion (Zheng et al.
  2011). Longo et al. 2009 is cited for those who felt the illusion
  judging the rubber hand more similar to their own.
- **§5.2, the account.** Seen and felt synchronous touch is surprising,
  because touch on a rubber hand does not predict felt touch. Multimodal
  areas explain it away by raising the probability that the rubber hand
  is "me". Figure 1's caption states this in three panels. The real
  body's probability of being "me" falls. Cooling of the real hand
  (Moseley et al. 2008; Hohwy and Paton 2010) and raised histamine
  reactivity (Barnsley et al. 2011) are read as "the real limb is being
  partially rejected".
- **§5.3, context as prior.** Masked self-primes speed self-recognition
  across domains. Western participants show more self-face bias than
  East Asian participants, and Chinese participants reverse it in a
  supervisor's presence (Liew et al. 2011). Religious participants show
  less self-bias than atheists. The rubber-hand illusion weakens for a
  non-corporeal object, a different skin tone or an incongruent
  position. All of it is read as priors set before stimulation.
- **§6, three predictions.** (1) No unimodal area is self-specific.
  Face, body and voice areas respond to all faces, bodies and voices, and
  greater activity for self-stimuli is read as more surprise passed up.
  (2) Self-recognition engages multimodal areas, none exclusively. TPJ,
  IPS, anterior insula and IFG are active during the rubber-hand illusion
  (Ehrsson et al. 2004, 2005; Tsakiris et al. 2007, 2008, 2010b) and in
  full-body illusions. Their connections are reviewed, and they are
  proposed as "a core circuit which is engaged when recognising one's
  self and creates a sense of ownership". (3) A predicted self-stimulus
  suppresses activity: self-produced tickle attenuates SI (Blakemore et
  al.), and seeing one's own face touched lowers SI and IFG activity
  (Cardini et al. 2011).
- **§7, caveats.** Prediction and error units sit in different cortical
  layers, but 3T BOLD sums both, so "a highly surprising event and a
  highly predicted event may ... both evoke large responses". The
  remedies proposed are orthogonal designs (Egner et al. 2010), MVPA,
  high-field layer-resolved fMRI, computational models fitted to
  behaviour, and DCM.

## Concepts

- **High-level empirical prior** — a belief at an intermediate or high
  level of the hierarchy, constrained from above and below; here, that a
  given body, face or voice is "me".
- **Explaining away** — top-down predictions from a multimodal node
  cancelling ascending prediction error in a unimodal system.
- **Corollary discharge versus efference copy** — under active inference
  the descending signal is a prediction of proprioceptive consequences,
  not a copy of a motor command. The paper uses this to treat motor
  signals as one channel among others.
- **Self plasticity** — the malleability of what is recognised as "me"
  under multisensory stimulation (§5.1).
- **Enfacement; rubber-voice illusion** — the face and voice analogues of
  the rubber-hand illusion.

## Limitations

- **No model.** No generative model is written down, no quantity is
  computed, and nothing is fitted. "Probability that the rubber hand is
  me" is never given a variable, a likelihood or a prior.
- **What would refute it is not stated.** The account explains both the
  illusion (synchronous stroking raises the prior) and its failure
  (context lowers it) by priors set after the fact. The authors concede
  the theory "is hard to falsify". Their §7 remedies are methods, not
  predictions.
- **Evidence is the comparator literature's.** The suppression evidence
  for prediction 3 (self-tickle, seeing one's own face touched) is what
  efference–reafference accounts already explain. Nothing cited
  separates the free-energy account from those it sets itself against.
- **The "rejected" limb.** C3 rests on the cooling and histamine reports
  alone. Outside this reading and not checked here: a later study
  (de Haan et al. 2017) reported no consistent cooling of the real hand.
- **Slips.** Four regions are named for the "core circuit" (TPJ, IPS,
  anterior insula, IFG), and the text twice speaks of "three regions"
  (§6.2), so which three is unclear. "Leggenhanger" for
  Lenggenhager. "Friston et al. 2010" is cited for Kilner's
  mirror-neuron account, which is a different paper's subject.
- **Author manuscript.** The text read is the accepted manuscript, not
  the publisher's version.

## Open questions

- Written as a model, does the account predict anything a
  causal-inference model of visuotactile integration does not? The
  natural test is the illusion's dependence on asynchrony and on
  hand position, fitted trial by trial.
- If the real hand is "partially rejected", does the self-model's
  probability for the real hand fall measurably by any non-questionnaire
  measure that survives replication?
- With action available, does the illusion weaken? Active inference
  predicts that movement, whose consequences the rubber hand does not
  share, would generate errors that favour the real hand. The paper
  treats action and the illusions separately and does not say.
