# LIT-tmpl6if3: Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness

Keith Frankish (2016), "Illusionism as a theory of consciousness",
*Journal of Consciousness Studies* 23(11–12), 11–39, Imprint Academic;
reprinted as the opening essay of K. Frankish (ed.), *Illusionism as a
Theory of Consciousness* (Imprint Academic, 2017). Read from the author's
eprint at
https://keithfrankish.github.io/articles/Frankish_Illusionism%20as%20a%20theory%20of%20consciousness_eprint.pdf.

## Key takeaways

- **Three options for any apparent anomaly** (§1.1): radical realism
  (real, needs new science), conservative realism (real, explicable with
  existing science), illusionism (not real; explain the appearance).
  Illusionism shares radical realism's view that phenomenal properties
  are anomalous and conservative realism's refusal of radical innovation.
- **Strong versus weak illusionism** (§1.2). A *quasi-phenomenal*
  property is a physical property introspection typically misrepresents
  as phenomenal. Strong illusionism says experiences have only these.
  Weak illusionism keeps real but deflated phenomenal properties. Unless
  weak illusionists can name a "phenomenal residue" stronger than
  quasi-phenomenality yet still conservatively explicable, which Frankish
  doubts, only radical realism and strong illusionism remain.
- **What illusionism does not deny** (§§1.6–1.7). Conscious experiences
  exist in an inclusive sense; what is denied is that they have
  phenomenal properties. Experiences are "like something" in a
  functional sense (introspective representation), and in that sense a
  zombie is like us. Everyday phenomenal talk can be read as talk within
  a fiction, like talk of Lear's anguish.
- **Arguments for it** (§2): a presumption of conservatism, especially
  against pressure from introspection; epiphenomenalism threatens
  radical realism; conservative realism tends to explain the
  *representation* of phenomenality and so is covertly illusionist; the
  debunking argument (our phenomenal claims would be the same without
  phenomenal properties), which Frankish takes from Chalmers (1996, pp.
  186–7) without Chalmers's endorsement; and an abductive inference from
  anomalousness plus the uncheckability of introspection.
- **Replies to objections** (§3): denying the data (acquaintance makes
  the datum psychologically inert); no appearance–reality gap (true only
  in a weak sense compatible with illusionism); who is the audience (no
  inner observer, appreciation distributed over unintelligent
  subsystems); how non-existent properties are represented
  (functional-role or hybrid concepts).

**The record's illusionist thread.**

- **Frankish's later paper.** "Closing the Cartesian Bureau de Change"
  ([LIT-103](LIT-103.md), read in [NOTE-143](../notes.d/NOTE-143.md)) applies this article's position to Veit's
  account of valence and leans on it: its "quasi-phenomenal properties"
  and its post-Cartesian paradigm are this paper's terms, cited there as
  Frankish 2016/2017. This article is the argument [LIT-103](LIT-103.md) assumes.
- **Dennett's reply.** "Illusionism as the Obvious Default Theory of
  Consciousness" ([LIT-434](LIT-434.md)) appeared in the same JCS issue as a commentary
  on this article. It is filed unread. Frankish calls Dennett illusionism's
  pre-eminent defender (p. 1) and draws on him throughout: the user
  illusion, the multiple drafts of sensation, heterophenomenology,
  and the storyteller's authority over fiction. "Quining Qualia"
  ([LIT-433](LIT-433.md), read in [NOTE-357](../notes.d/NOTE-357.md)) is the source of the list of marks
  (ineffable, intrinsic, private, immediately apprehended) that Frankish
  says weak illusionists deflate.
- **Chalmers's meta-problem.** "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness"
  ([LIT-425](LIT-425.md), read in [NOTE-354](../notes.d/NOTE-354.md)) takes this article's illusionism as its
  target in §§6–7, and [NOTE-354](../notes.d/NOTE-354.md) records that Chalmers says he suggested
  the name. Two points of contact are checkable here. Frankish's §2.3
  debunking argument has the shape of the one [LIT-425](LIT-425.md) §5 calls Argument A
  and answers: there is a consciousness-independent explanation of our
  phenomenal beliefs, so they are unjustified. [LIT-425](LIT-425.md)'s replies
  (acquaintance, and the constitutive role of consciousness in phenomenal
  belief) are the positions this article's §3.1 attacks as making the
  datum "psychologically inert". And [LIT-425](LIT-425.md)'s claim that
  only strong illusionism dissolves the hard problem agrees with this
  article's argument that weak illusionism is unstable, for different
  reasons: Chalmers argues that only the strong form removes the problem,
  while Frankish argues that the weak form has no stable content. Where
  they part is [LIT-425](LIT-425.md)'s Moorean rejection of strong illusionism, which
  is the "denying the data" objection this article answers in §3.1.
- **Taylor's interpretation of Dennett** ([LIT-tmpoxm1b](LIT-tmpoxm1b.md), read in
  [NOTE-tmpj7u7y](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpj7u7y.md)) reads Dennett's view as realism about a real pattern
  and treats qualia eliminativism as peripheral. That is in tension with
  this article, which counts Dennett as the leading strong illusionist.
  [NOTE-tmpj7u7y](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpj7u7y.md) sets out the difference.
- **Dennett's 1980 eliminativism about pain** ([LIT-tmpjt24r](LIT-tmpjt24r.md), read in
  [NOTE-tmp2q5sj](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp2q5sj.md)) is an early form of the view this article renames.
  Frankish's §1.6 says "eliminativism" wrongly suggests a wider rejection
  of folk psychology; Dennett in 1980 holds exactly that wider view of
  pains, beliefs and desires, and rescues the latter two by the
  intentional stance.

**On the society-of-mind bridge ([THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)).** The article never
discusses groups. It bears on [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) in one place. Its §3.3 answer to
"who is the audience?" spreads the work of being taken in by the illusion
over "numerous unintelligent subsystems", with no unified audience
smaller than the organism. That is Dennett's discharge of homunculi
applied to the illusion itself. On this view the THEORY's narrowed
question about groups becomes whether a group has the introspective
representations that generate the illusion. That framing is mine, not
Frankish's, as it was in [LIT-434](LIT-434.md)'s entry.

**On AI consciousness ([THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md)).** Not discussed by Frankish. His
§1.7 has a consequence he does not draw: if what makes experience "like
something" is introspective representation, then the question for a
machine is whether it has the introspective mechanisms, not whether it
has a further property. That sharpens the mimicry worry in [THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md)
rather than settling it, since a system can produce phenomenal reports
without such mechanisms; the meta-problem paper raises the same caution
about puzzled phenomenal reports as evidence ([LIT-425](LIT-425.md), fn 26).

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice, and it does not
belong in the anthology.
