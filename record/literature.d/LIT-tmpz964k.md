# LIT-tmpz964k: Long-Term Cognitive and Neuropsychiatric Consequences of Repetitive Concussion and Head-Impact Exposure

Thomas McAllister, Michael McCrea (2017), *Journal of Athletic Training (vol. 52, no. 3, pp. 309–317)* — DOI-10.4085/1062-6050-52.1.14

## Key takeaways

- This review concludes that most athletes' symptoms recover within about 1–2 weeks of a concussion. Evidence that repetitive concussion or sub-concussive head impacts cause long-term cognitive decline, neuropsychiatric change or neurodegeneration (including CTE) is mixed and preliminary, with signals of vulnerability in subgroups (e.g., verbal memory). Settling it needs large prospective longitudinal studies with baselines and controls.

*Seeded from the abstract alone, not a reading. What follows is what the work says about itself.*

Sport-related concussion was first studied as a window into mild traumatic brain injury. Over the last decade, concern has moved to whether repeated concussions raise long-term risk of cognitive decline, behavioural change and neurodegenerative disease, and concussion is increasingly seen as possibly triggering lifelong neurobiological processes. Helmet sensors now measure how often and how hard players' heads are hit. They show that many large impacts cause no diagnosed concussion while many concussions follow modest impacts, so total head-impact exposure matters as well as diagnosed concussions. The article reviews the state of the evidence on long-term neurocognitive and neurobehavioural effects in contact sports.
