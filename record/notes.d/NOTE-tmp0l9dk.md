  existential-risk intervention changes the chance that civilization exists

  "exogenous nullifying event" arrives at a constant rate r, which acts as
  an exponential discount. Against a global-health benchmark of 10,000
  QALYs, it wins if r is below about 0.000135 per year with interstellar
  settlement, or 0.000000012 per year on Earth alone. Small credences in
  settlement, low r, fast travel or star-sized value make it win by
  orders of magnitude, but then the win can rest on probabilities as small
  as 10^-18. So the case for longtermism depends on non-obvious empirical
  claims or on Pascalian fanaticism, and its horizon may be thousands or
  millions of years, not billions.

# NOTE-tmp0l9dk: The epistemic challenge to longtermism

## Contribution

It turns a common worry, that we cannot predict the far future well enough
for it to guide us, into a model with numbers. Tarsney grants the
longtermist every normative premise (precise probabilities, total welfarist
consequentialism, expected value maximization, which he calls
"expectational utilitarianism"), errs against longtermism on every
empirical input, and asks whether a longtermist intervention still beats a
good neartermist one. His answer is a qualified yes: by the rules of
expected value, but on some views only through minuscule probabilities.

## Key insight

The chance that an intervention's effect is undone or made irrelevant acts
like a discount rate. If such "nullifying events" arrive at a constant rate,
value that grows only polynomially (cubically, as settlement spreads) is
eventually overwhelmed by the exponential discount. So the size of the
future does not automatically win. What decides the comparison is the
long-run rate of those events, and EV(L) is "nearly (inverse) quartic in
some ranges of r, so that an order-of-magnitude decrease in r corresponds
to a four-order-of-magnitude increase in EV(L)" (p. 25).

## Assumptions

- **Expectational utilitarianism** (p. 4): precise probabilism, total
  welfarist consequentialism, expected value maximization. The point is to
  test the epistemic challenge where the normative frame favours
  longtermism.
- **The ought is evidence-relative** (p. 5): what one should do given the
  probabilities one's evidence and reasoning capacities support.
- **Persistent-difference strategies only** (pp. 6–7): put the world in a
  better state S and hope it stays there. Speed-up strategies are set aside.
  Their value turns on four factors: importance, tractability,
  persistence and complement persistence. The paper models the last two.
- **Exogenous nullifying events (ENEs)** (pp. 13–14). Negative ENEs put the
  world in not-S (an existential catastrophe); positive ENEs put it in S
  (another civilization arises). After the first ENE, the earlier choice no
  longer matters. Their rate r is constant, which Tarsney defends as
  conservative against the "time of perils" view (p. 18).
- **Value tracks resources.** In the cubic growth model, value per year
  grows with the number of stars settled, at constant speed in all
  directions. In the steady state model, humanity stays in the Solar
  System at constant value.
- **The far future begins 1000 years from now** (p. 13), the year 3022.

## Key results

- **The working example** (pp. 11–12). A philanthropist chooses how to
  spend $1 million.
  - N, the neartermist benchmark: vitamin A supplementation at about $3000
    per life saved, 333⅓ lives at 30 QALYs each, so 10,000 QALYs. This
    defines the unit: one "valon" is 10,000 QALYs, and EV(N) = 1 V.
  - L, the longtermist option: research on pandemic risk from novel
    pathogens, to raise the probability that "the accessible universe
    contains an intelligent civilization" in 3022.
- **Parameter values** (pp. 18–23).
  - p = 2 × 10^-14 (p. 19, fn. 23): a "very safe" 1% change in survival to
    3022 if all of humanity worked on it for 1000 years, times the
    fraction of humanity's labour $1 million buys (2 × 10^-12). Other
    sources would give at least 6.4 × 10^-14 and up to 8 × 10^-10
    (Millett and Snyder-Beattie) or about 10^-7 (Todd) (fn. 24).
  - v_s, value per settled star per year. "Space Opera", 300 million
    people per star at 1 QALY a year: 3 × 10^4 V. "Dyson Spheres",
    Bostrom's figure of 10^25 human-equivalent lives per star: 10^20 V.
    The model uses the first.
  - v_e, value on Earth: today's 6 billion QALYs a year, so 6 × 10^5 V a
    year.
  - Star densities 2.2 × 10^-5 per cubic light year in the Milky Way and
    2.9 × 10^-9 in the Virgo Supercluster; settlement speed s = 0.1c;
    settlement starts at t_l = 0; the bound t_f = 10^14 years.
  - r is left free. Tarsney thinks r below 10^-2 per year is safe (the
    most pessimistic estimate of near-term risk in the academic literature,
    Rees's 0.5 for this century, implies about 6.9 × 10^-3 a year) and sees no clear reason to rule out 10^-20 (p. 23).
- **Cubic growth model, Table 1** (pp. 23–25).
  - EV(L) > EV(N) if and only if r < about 0.000135 per year.
  - EV(L) in valons: r = 10^-3, 3.44 × 10^-4; r = 10^-4, 3.32;
    r = 10^-5, 3.32 × 10^4; r = 10^-6, 4.74 × 10^7; r = 10^-8,
    4.39 × 10^12; r = 10^-10, 4.37 × 10^20. With r = 0, about 10^57.
  - At r = 10^-5, 99% of EV(L) is realized by about 1 million years.
  - Starting settlement 500 years later moves the threshold only from
    0.000135 to 0.000133 (fn. 28).
- **Steady state model, Table 2** (pp. 26–27). With t_f = 5 × 10^8 years
  and no settlement, L wins only if r < about 0.000000012 (1.2 × 10^-8) per
  year. Even r = 0 gives EV(L) = 6 V. Raising p to 10^-10 and Earth's value
  a hundredfold lifts the threshold to r < about 0.006.
- **Adding uncertainty, Table 3** (pp. 27–30). Lower bounds on credence:
  10^-3 in the cubic growth model; 10^-3 in r ≤ 10^-6 (given it); 10^-2 in
  s ≥ 0.8c; 10^-6 in Dyson-sphere values of v_s. The rest goes to the
  steady state model with r = 10^-3. Minimum EV(L):
  - cubic growth alone, about 1.23 × 10^-5 V; with r, 47.4 V; with s,
    1.38 × 10^-5 V; with v_s, 1.11 × 10^3 V;
  - with r and s, 48.2 V; r and v_s, 1.58 × 10^11 V; s and v_s,
    6.76 × 10^3 V; all three, 1.62 × 10^11 V.

  The cubic model plus any two of these bounds puts L above N by at least
  an order of magnitude. Uncertainty "will tend to strengthen rather than
  weaken the case for longtermism" (p. 30), because a little credence in
  enormous upside goes a long way.
- **Fanaticism** (pp. 31–32). Tarsney measures how Pascalian a choice is by
  how much of each prospect's tails must be cut away before the best
  option changes. On some views, the component of EV(L) that beats N
  "depends on a conjunction of improbable assumptions with joint
  probability on the order of (say) 10^-18 or less" (p. 32). On more
  optimistic views it might be 10^-8 or more.
- **Conclusions** (pp. 33–35).
  - The case for longtermism survives this challenge under expectational
    utilitarianism.
  - On some plausible worldviews, it does so only through Pascalian
    probabilities.
  - It leans on interstellar settlement.
  - Persistence favours existential-risk work over reforming institutions
    or values, whose effects are easier to undo or would come about later
    anyway.
  - "We should be 'longtermists' on the scale of thousands or millions or
    years, rather than billions or trillions of years" (p. 34).
  - Overall the challenge is "serious" and probably has "significant
    practical implications", "but is not fatal" (p. 34).

## Concepts

- **longtermism** (p. 5): in most of the most important choices, what one
  ought to do is mainly determined by possible effects on the far future.
  Close to Greaves and MacAskill's deontic strong longtermism.
- **persistent-difference strategy**: bringing about a better state in the
  hope that it lasts where it otherwise would not.
- **control challenge versus epistemic challenge** (pp. 7–8): we cannot
  improve the far future even with perfect knowledge of chances, versus we
  cannot tell which actions would.
- **exogenous nullifying event (ENE)**: a far-future event after which the
  world's state no longer depends on the intervention.
- **valon**: 10,000 QALYs, the expected value of the benchmark.
- **expectational utilitarianism**: precise probabilism plus total
  welfarism plus expected value maximization.
- **Pascalian-ness**: 1 − 2μ*, where μ* is the smallest share of each tail
  that must be ignored to change the best option.

## Connections

- **Greaves and MacAskill ([LIT-tmp1we1q](../literature.d/LIT-tmp1we1q.md)).** Tarsney's target is their
  thesis: his statement of longtermism "closely resembles" their deontic
  strong longtermism (fn. 8), cited from the 2021 working paper. He grants
  them the normative frame and contests only the empirical inputs.
- **Bostrom ([LIT-tmph7mkx](../literature.d/LIT-tmph7mkx.md), [LIT-tmpr74qy](../literature.d/LIT-tmpr74qy.md)).** Tarsney names Bostrom's
  "naive back-of-the-envelope estimates" (p. 8) as what persistence
  worries cut down. Bostrom 2003 also supplies the Dyson-sphere value of
  10^25 lives per star. One of the four credence floors that rescue
  longtermism is credence in exactly that scenario.
- **Thorstad ([LIT-tmp37kk5](../literature.d/LIT-tmp37kk5.md)).** Tarsney assumes a constant rate of
  nullifying events and cites Thorstad's 2022 working paper as "a case
  against" the time of perils (fn. 22). Thorstad's 2023 paper works in
  per-century risk from the present, where Tarsney starts the clock 1000
  years out. Inference: Tarsney's p, the change in the chance of
  surviving to 3022, is where Thorstad's problem would land. If risk stays
  high for the next ten centuries, a cut in this century's risk changes
  survival to 3022 very little.
- **Ord ([LIT-tmp0ocqn](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0ocqn.md))** is cited on existential risk and the time of
  perils, and for a model in the same spirit (Appendix E).
- **Discounting.** Uncertainty about r makes the effective discount
  hyperbolic in the long run (fn. 37). That is a discount for risk, not for
  time as such, and the record's [QUESTION-tmpmx9ha](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.md) cites Ramsey's refusal
  to discount for time.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-tmpmx9ha](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.md).** The paper bears on its first ground, the impersonal
  good, and on the "can longtermism guide action" line the question already
  names. It does not refute longtermism; Tarsney concludes the challenge is
  "not fatal". What it shows is what longtermism's action-guidance rests
  on: an unknown long-run hazard rate, interstellar settlement, and, on
  some views, credences of order 10^-18.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** The paper says nothing about
  motivation. Its bearing is indirect, and these are inferences.
  - The question calls concern for far-future strangers the thesis's
    hardest case. That case is pressing only if such concern is owed and
    acted on. If the impersonal case for it rests on Pascalian
    probabilities, the normative pressure behind the hardest case is
    weaker, and more of what the living actually owe falls in the nearer
    future. Concern for that future (one's descendants, one's community,
    humanity going on) is the kind a self extended in time or across
    people can explain.
  - But Tarsney's "nearer" is still thousands to millions of years. Even
    where his model favours a short horizon, it lies far past any self the
    thesis could reasonably name. So the paper narrows the hardest case; it
    does not remove it.
  - It offers a rival explanation of why concern fades with time. Rational
    concern declines with the hazard that an effect is undone, with no
    reference to the self. A test of whether concern for the future tracks
    felt continuity with it (Hershfield, [LIT-tmp8yuyb](../literature.d/LIT-tmp8yuyb.md); the test
    [QUESTION-tmpmx9ha](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.md) and [QUESTION-tmpruhu4](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md) propose) must separate that
    from expected-hazard discounting.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** Not addressed directly.
  The confound above is a point about how to run its test across
  generations.
- **No dated note on [CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md).** The paper does not touch motivation.
  Its bearing goes into [QUESTION-tmpmx9ha](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.md) (section 3 below).
- **No THEORY filed.** The model is a contribution to a normative debate
  the record holds as a question, not an account of what is true.

## Limitations

- **One intervention, one kind of strategy.** Existential-risk reduction
  is chosen because it is easy to quantify. Speed-ups and other
  non-persistence strategies are not modelled.
- **The inputs are guesses**, chosen to be conservative, and Tarsney
  admits s = 0.1c is not (fn. 27). The credence floors in Table 3 are
  "unavoidably subjective" (p. 29).
- **Only persistence is tested.** Tractability (p) is a lower bound with
  "only a minimal argument", and importance is set aside. Tarsney thinks
  the stronger worry may be that a civilization's goodness is not
  persistent even if its existence is (p. 35).
- **Only precise probabilism.** Imprecise probabilities, unawareness and
  bounded rationality are left open, and Tarsney suspects longtermism
  fares worse in them (pp. 35–36).
- **Not compared with the Synthese version.**
- **Typos and slips.**
  - "suitbable" (p. 9); "integrand of of EV(L)" (p. 25); "these bounds
    quite conservative" (p. 29); "we will chose" (fn. 35, p. 29);
    "peristent" and "millions or years" (p. 34); "rather than those
    objectives" for "rather that" (p. 35).
  - Table 2 gives the 99th percentile at r = 10^-5 as "460,517 million
    years"; the series (4605, 46,052, then 4.6 million years) shows it
    should be about 460,517 years (p. 27).
  - "p = 2 × 10^14" for 2 × 10^-14 (p. 35).
  - Footnote 41 calls the sure payoff "3000 QALYS (1 valon)" and converts
    with it, though a valon was defined as 10,000 QALYs (pp. 12, 33).

## Open questions

- How does the case change if r falls over time, as the time of perils
  hypothesis says? Tarsney's appendix lists time-dependence as the second
  most important simplification, and expects it to favour longtermism.
  Thorstad's paper ([LIT-tmp37kk5](../literature.d/LIT-tmp37kk5.md)) asks how fast and how far it would have
  to fall.
- Does lay concern for the far future fall off with time the way an
  expected-hazard discount would, or the way felt self-continuity does?
  The two predict different curves, and no work the record holds compares
  them.
