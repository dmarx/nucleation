# NOTE-tmpzaeih: Block — Mental Paint

## Contribution

It recasts the qualia debate as a debate about representation: not whether
phenomenal character goes beyond function, but whether it goes beyond what
experience represents. It gives the debate a vocabulary (mental paint,
mental oil, phenomenism, quasi-representationism), turns Inverted Earth
against representationism, and adds an argument from colour science, not
thought experiment, that representationism makes normal colour vision
non-veridical for large groups of people.

## Key insight

For appearance properties like colour there is nothing left over for the
representationist to point to. When a Twin Earth emigrant's water content
changes, sameness of experience is explained by shared represented
appearances (colour, sheen, flow). When an Inverted Earth emigrant's
colour content changes, there is no further represented appearance that
stayed the same. What stays the same must then be a feature of the
experience itself: paint, not what is painted.

## Assumptions

- **Definitions.** A quale is "a phenomenal property of an experience that
  eludes the intentional, the functional, and the purely cognitive"; Block
  prefers to define it as not conceptually reducible to these (n. 1).
  Representationists hold phenomenal character "is or at least is determined
  by" representational content; supervenience is too weak a statement of
  their view (n. 3).
- **Supervenience of phenomenal character on the brain**, defended partly by
  the arguments that follow (pp. 170–171).
- **Externalist shift of content** after long residence, now with a
  deliberate change of allegiance (p. 185); recognitional capacities can be
  "swamped" by a new causal source, as with Block's dog (pp. 184–185).
- **Objective colour**: Munsell chips, Benjamin Moore paints and Crayola
  names show minimal shades are objective (pp. 193–194).

## Key results

- **Erisa (pp. 166–168).** Raised among surfaces that change colour every few
  minutes, with no colour words or standing colour beliefs. Her red and blue
  experiences differ vividly; nothing functional could constitute the
  difference. Stimulus differences cause, not constitute, it; innate
  colour preferences are speculation. Given the necessity of identity,
  internal functional identity rules out even possible creatures with
  different phenomenal characters and no functional difference (p. 168).
- **Water emigrant (pp. 168–170).** Representational change with phenomenal
  sameness is explained by two kinds of represented content, one of which
  is appearance properties. The representationist wins this round.
- **Swampman (pp. 170–171).** Same phenomenal character, null content, so
  phenomenal character is not content; two swampmen with different
  phenomenal characters and the same null content show it does not even
  supervene on content. Representationists who deny brain supervenience
  (Dretske, Lycan, McDowell) take "a desperate maneuver".
- **What is the issue (pp. 171–174).** Attention is not awareness; Harman's
  transparency is right about the first only. Closing your eyes in daylight
  or focusing past a finger shows attention to experience. Shoemaker's view
  makes mental paint "a theoretical entity", which is paradoxical. Lycan lets
  us be aware that an experience represents, but a child without the
  concept of representation can be aware of what two experiences share.
- **Bodily sensations, phosphenes, Bach-y-Rita (pp. 175–179).** Tye's and
  Lycan's representational contents for orgasm leave "Everything that
  matters (phenomenally speaking)" over; "The representationists should put
  up or shut up." Pain twinges recognised only as "that one again" suggest
  inward-directed recognition is "a capitulation to phenomenism".
- **Quasi-representationism (pp. 179–180).** Plugging modality differences
  with functional role invites the question why not functionalism outright;
  a peripheral-vision and an auditory experience of the same location differ
  phenomenally.
- **Marvin (pp. 180–183).** Lycan's answer, that Marvin's two recognitional
  concepts represent objective red and blue, "eases the way" for thought
  experiments; Rey's syntactic answer collapses into eliminativism. Block
  accepts Loar's reply to Jackson's Mary: the case works better against
  representationism than against physicalism.
- **Inverted Earth and memory (pp. 183–188).** Lycan's gradual-shift reply
  ignores long-term memory ("I remember the stars from my childhood!");
  externalism about phenomenal memory is "an error theory" with "nothing to
  recommend it" beyond saving representationism. If the subject's content
  never shifts, he is inverted relative to the natives, which also defeats
  representationism (p. 188).
- **Shifted spectra (pp. 188–194).** Cone peak sensitivities vary (standard
  deviation 1–2 nm; long-wave cones split 51.5% / 48.5% in men, differing by
  5–7 nm); unique green across 50 normal subjects runs from 490 to 517 nm.
  For a minimal shade (co-opted "aquamarine") learned from a standard chip,
  representational content is the same and phenomenal character probably
  differs. Relativising colour to groups fails because minimal shades are
  objective. Representationism is "empirically false" (p. 194).
- **Burge's suggestion (p. 195).** If phenomenal character individuates the
  sense of colour concepts, representationism wins only a "Pickwickian
  victory", because the representationists in question construe phenomenal
  character referentially.

## Concepts

- **Mental paint** — mental properties of experience that represent, e.g.
  the phenomenal character that represents the tomato as red (p. 173).
- **Mental oil** — mental properties of experience that represent nothing
  (p. 174); the property of having an intentional content is excluded (n. 2).
- **Phenomenism / representationism / quasi-representationism** — phenomenal
  character outruns representational content / is exhausted by it / is
  exhausted by it within a modality (pp. 165, 179).
- **Shifted spectrum** — a small systematic difference, e.g. orange to you
  looks as reddish orange looks to me (p. 188).

## Limitations

- Several sections are, by Block's own account, appeals to introspection
  meant to "have an impact on the noncommitted", not proofs (pp. 172, 177).
- The shifted-spectrum argument depends on how fine representational
  colour categories are; Block defers that to Block (1999), not in the
  record.
- Mental oil is claimed for bodily sensations without an argument that
  representationists cannot meet once they specify contents.

## Open questions

- Could a representationist accept minimal-shade content that differs
  between perceivers without making colour relative?
- Is the long-term-memory reply to Lycan decisive, or does it need the
  amnesia device of Block (1990) after all?
