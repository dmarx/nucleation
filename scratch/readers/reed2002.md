### Summary

If a process that grows exponentially "in a loose sense" (in expectation)
is killed, or observed once, at a random time with a constant hazard, the
killed state has power-law behaviour in one or both tails. The paper shows
this for four processes: geometric Brownian motion (GBM), a discrete
multiplicative process, a homogeneous birth-and-death process and a
Galton-Watson branching process. It offers the mechanism as the explanation
for power-law tails in incomes, settlement sizes, web-site and file sizes,
genus sizes, extinct taxa, gene and protein families, and surnames, with
log-log rank-size plots of US data.

### Contribution

One mechanism, random killing or observation of exponential growth,
unifies a set of earlier models (Reed's own on incomes and cities, Huberman
and Adamic on web sites, Mitzenmacher on file sizes, Yule on genera). It
predicts a *lower*-tail power law as well where the process can decrease,
"facts previously unrecognized" for incomes and settlement sizes.

### Key results (page, verbatim)

1. **Deterministic case** (p. 1): "Consider the simple case of
   deterministic exponential growth X(t)=e^{μt} killed at a random time T
   which is exponentially distributed with parameter ν. The killed state
   X̄=e^{μT} has the probability density function f(x)=(ν/μ)x^{−ν/μ−1}
   for x>1, giving power-law behavior over its full range." Survival
   x^{−ν/μ}.
2. **Killed GBM, the double Pareto** (p. 2, Eqs. 8 and 9): log X̄ has an
   asymmetric Laplace law; "the distribution of X̄ is a two-sided Pareto or
   double Pareto distribution". Its density is A X0^{−β} x^{β−1} below X0
   and A X0^{α} x^{−α−1} above, where "α and −β (α, β>0) are the roots of
   the quadratic equation ½σ²s² + (μ − ½σ²)s − ν = 0", with A = αβ/(α+β).
   Checked (python3 -I, 4 million draws, μ = ½, σ = 1, ν = 1, so zero log
   drift and α = β = √2): P(X̄ > 5) 0.0513 against 0.0513, P(X̄ > 20)
   0.00724 against 0.00723, and the lower tail the same by symmetry.
3. **Why ages are exponential** (p. 2): "the time that an individual has
   been earning and the time a settlement has been in existence can
   reasonably be modeled as having exponential distributions, based on the
   assumption that the workforce and the population of settlements are
   growing at a fixed rate."
4. **Killed multiplicative process** (p. 2): with geometric killing, there
   is an upper tail if Z > 1 always and a lower tail if Z < 1 always. If Z
   can go either way, there is "power-law behavior in both tails", from the
   poles of pM_U(s)[1 − qM_U(s)]^{−1} at the roots of M_U(s) = 1/q.
5. **Killed birth-and-death** (p. 3, Eq. 10): "P(X̄=n) ~ k1 n^{−[1+ν/(λ−δ)]}
   for λ>δ"; "'stretched exponential' behavior in the case λ=δ", and a
   geometric factor (λ/δ)^n times a power for λ<δ. Checked for the pure
   birth case (λ = 1, δ = 0, ν = ½, 2 million draws): n^{ν/λ}·P(X̄ ≥ n) is
   0.90, 0.89 and 0.88 at n = 10, 100 and 1000. It is steady, so the
   survival exponent is ν/λ.
6. **Yule** (p. 3): "Yule developed the eponymous Yule distribution to fit
   such data, using essentially the above model with δ=0. He assumed that
   the size of a genus was that of a killed pure birth (or Yule) process."
   Genera "are assumed to be created in a similar fashion to species, so
   that the time since origination of a live genus is exponentially
   distributed".
7. **Total progeny** (p. 3, Eq. 11): the number that ever existed, Ȳ, also
   has an upper power tail with the same exponent, "in the case λ>δ+ν".
8. **Killed Galton-Watson** (pp. 3 and 4): G(s) = pg(s) + (1−p)G(g(s));
   "the dominant behavior P(X̄=m)~R(m)m^{−1−κ} ... where κ=ln[(1−p)^{−1}]/ln
   μ and R(m) has log-periodic oscillations". The oscillations are proved
   for a geometric offspring law and are "of very small amplitude". The
   result is said to be argued "elsewhere" [19].

### Assumptions

- A constant killing hazard (an exponential or geometric observation time).
- Growth exponential in expectation.
- Killing independent of the state, and excluded at generation zero.
- For the applications, a population of units entering at a fixed
  exponential rate, so that ages are exponential.
- Empirical support is visual linearity of log-log rank-size and CCDF
  plots, binned where needed; there is no fit, no test and no alternative.

### Concepts

- killed (or once-observed) process
- constant hazard
- double Pareto (two-sided Pareto)
- asymmetric Laplace law of the log
- poles of the MGF or PGF setting the tail exponents
- stretched exponential at the balanced birth-death point
- log-periodic oscillation from a branching hierarchy
- Yule process
- rank-size plot

### Verdict on the exchange's characterization

Accurate in substance. A5 §6 (as filed in CLAIM-795 item 6) says "a common
growth rate g and exponential ages with rate λ give P(S > s) =
(s/S_0)^(−λ/g)". That is the paper's deterministic "simple case", with
ν = λ and μ = g. Two qualifications:

- It is the paper's opening illustration, not its result. The paper's
  mechanism is stochastic growth "in expectation", and its headline is the
  double Pareto with two tails.
- "Even identical growth rates can coexist with highly unequal sizes" is
  A's gloss. It is true of the deterministic case; the paper does not say
  it.

LIT-3007's summary ("A quantity growing exponentially, observed after an
exponentially distributed time, has a power-law distribution; the mechanism
covers gene families, incomes and file sizes") is correct. It should add
"in one or both tails" and that the growth may be stochastic. Newman's
restatement (NOTE-tmpa7yd2) and Mitzenmacher's zero-drift double Pareto
(CLAIM-795 note of 2026-11-21) both agree with Eq. 9: at μ = ½σ² the
quadratic gives α = √(2ν)/σ.

### Bearing on the named codes

- **CLAIM-795, item 6: supports, and qualifies the framing.** Result 1 is
  item 6 exactly. Result 2 extends it: with noise, the upper exponent is
  the positive root α of ½σ²s² + (μ−½σ²)s − ν = 0, not ν/μ, and a lower
  power tail appears. Item 6's "common growth rate" is a special case
  (σ → 0 gives α → ν/μ).
- **CLAIM-795, item 1: parallel.** Result 8 is a second branching route: a
  *supercritical* (ln μ > 0) Galton-Watson process killed geometrically,
  with exponent 1+κ, κ = ln(1/(1−p))/ln μ. It is unrelated to the critical
  s^{−3/2}. Result 5's λ = δ case gives a stretched exponential, not a
  power law, so balance is where this mechanism *fails*.
- **CLAIM-830: supports the age half and the role of entry; leaves the
  identification open.** Result 3 grounds exponential ages in entry into a
  population "growing at a fixed rate". Result 6 builds Yule's power law
  from new genera entering as species do, which is CLAIM-830's thesis that
  entry turns proportional growth into a Pareto tail. The paper never
  mentions preferential attachment, Barabási-Albert or logarithmic time.
  The route to exponential ages differs: here the population grows
  exponentially in real time, while in CLAIM-830 births are uniform in
  real time and the log-ages are exponential. Both give the same law.
  LIT-3007 can stay in `grounds` for Reed's formula; it does not carry the
  identification.
- **NOTE-tmpa7yd2 (Newman): consistent.** Newman's α = 1 − a/b, with
  a = −ν and b = μ, is result 1's density exponent 1 + ν/μ. Newman's
  reading of LIT-3007 as a "combination of exponentials" covers the
  deterministic case only. The stochastic cases and the lower tail are not
  in his two sentences.

### Limitations

- A four-page brief report. The birth-death, total-progeny and branching
  results are stated, with proofs elsewhere.
- The empirical figures are visual, on log-log axes. There are no fitted
  exponents, no test against the lognormal and no estimate of ν or μ from
  independent data. The tie between mechanism and data is plausibility.
- Exponential observation times are assumed, justified only by fixed-rate
  population growth.
- The lower-tail claim for incomes rests on binned data ("unbinned income
  data are not available").
