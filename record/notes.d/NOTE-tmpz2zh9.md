# NOTE-tmpz2zh9: The Major Transitions in Evolution—A Philosophy-of-Science Perspective

## Contribution

A conceptual map of the major-transitions research programme from the
philosophy of science: what counts as a transition, whether a general
theory of them is possible, how genic and hierarchical explanations
relate, what the transitions do to the levels of selection debate, and
whether the resulting hierarchy has a privileged level.

## Key insight

The levels at which selection acts are not a fixed background. Every
higher-level unit was once a collective of free-living units, and it holds
together only if selection among collectives comes to dominate selection
among its members. So the hierarchy itself is something evolution
explains.

## Assumptions

- A transition is best defined by Maynard Smith and Szathmáry's second
  characterisation, the formation of a higher-level unit (p. 2).
- Explanations of evolutionary advantage are one kind among several;
  mechanism and the sequence of stages matter too (p. 4).
- Organisms are entities that exhibit adaptations, functional
  organization and a certain "autonomy" or "agency" (p. 7).

## Key results

- **Definition** (p. 2) and the pared-down list of transitions it gives.
- **Contingency** (p. 3): transitions may be "rare singularities", and an
  MET may be "qualitatively different" from the evolution of altruism.
- **General principles** (pp. 3–4): short-term advantage to form a
  collective; instability "owing to defectors pursuing their own
  interests"; kinship, policing and division of labour as aligning
  mechanisms; higher-level selection that must "trump" lower-level
  selection. Relevant, Okasha says, but possibly too coarse to explain any
  particular transition; "The jury is still out".
- **A caution** (p. 4): after Rainey et al. (2014), "cheating" and
  "public good" have been misappropriated in studies of bacterial
  sociality, from an a priori commitment to a collective action problem
  in every transition.
- **Genic vs hierarchical** (pp. 4–5): compatible; but formal equivalence
  of multilevel and inclusive-fitness models rests on approximations and
  "is not the same as causal-explanatory equivalence".
- **Levels of selection** (pp. 5–6): a transition creates the potential
  for selection at two levels (citing Okasha 2005); success needs
  mechanisms "such as policing, to regulate the selfish tendencies of the
  lower-level units"; hierarchical organization is "endogenized".
- **Privileged level** (pp. 7–9): the highest occupied level is where
  organisms are; the part-whole question is left open between three
  defensible options.

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | A transition is best defined as the evolution of a higher-level unit from formerly free-living units | moderate | conceptual argument |
| C2 | For a transition to succeed, higher-level selection must trump lower-level selection, typically via policing or aligned interests | moderate | attributed to Buss and Michod and endorsed; no model here |
| C3 | General principles may explain transitions only coarsely | moderate | argued from the proto-cell example |
| C4 | Genic and hierarchical approaches are compatible, but formally equivalent models need not be causally equivalent | moderate | argued, citing model-equivalence results and their approximations |
| C5 | The transitions make the hierarchy an explanandum | strong | conceptual; follows from the definition |
| C6 | The highest occupied level is privileged as the level of organisms | weak | argued, with the alternatives left open |

## Concepts

- **major evolutionary transition (MET)** — "the evolution of a
  higher-level biological unit out of formerly-free living units" (p. 2).
- **fraternal and egalitarian transitions** — after Queller: related
  lower-level units versus unrelated, possibly of different species
  (p. 3).
- **endogenizing the hierarchy** — making hierarchical organization part
  of what evolutionary theory explains, not part of its background (p. 6).
- **organism** — Queller and Strassmann's "largest unit of near-unanimous
  design" (p. 8).

Aktipis et al. ([LIT-tmpo461k](../literature.d/LIT-tmpo461k.md)) is a worked case of the general principle at
one transition, multicellularity: cell-level fitness suppressed for
organism-level fitness, with cancer as the failure. Okasha's caution
applies to it. Aktipis et al. define cheating functionally rather than as
a strategy, and that answers part of the caution. They do not establish a
fitness conflict for each growth they report.

## Bearing on the record

Source of [THEORY-tmpevx7w](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpevx7w.md). For the owner's essay:

- **§6.3** (capture from below). A collective "would not necessarily be
  stable owing to defectors pursuing their own interests", and succeeds
  only if higher-level selection comes to trump lower-level selection.
  That is the biological form of the essay's first mode of cross-scale
  conflict, with policing as one remedy.
- **§6.2** (constitutive dependency). The record reads the endogenized
  hierarchy as a parallel: the level an organization's members depend on
  is itself produced, not given. Okasha does not discuss organizations.
- **A caution for the essay.** Calling a member's conduct "capture" or
  "cheating" presupposes a collective action problem; Okasha's warning
  against assuming one applies to organizations too.

## Limitations

A review with no model or data, and "inevitably provisional" by its own
account. It is not the 2005 argument, and the record cannot say how far
the 2005 paper differs.

## Open questions

Whether a general theory of transitions can be specific enough to explain
any one of them, which Okasha leaves open; how to treat organisms within
organisms.
