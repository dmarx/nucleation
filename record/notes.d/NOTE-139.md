# NOTE-139: Wenmackers — Philosophy of probability (PhD thesis)

## Contribution

The dissertation is a contribution to formal and computational philosophy. Its first part exploits parallels between very large finite lotteries and countably infinite ones to gain insight into both the foundations of probability and epistemology, solving their respective "adding problems" with non-standard analysis. Its second part uses computer simulation to study communities of agents who update beliefs by averaging, and computes the probability that an agent ends in an inconsistent belief state.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- Ch. 1 (pp. 1–38): motivates formal and computational philosophy; reviews interpretations of probability, an epistemic approach to objective chance, and problems of infinite sample spaces.
- Ch. 2, §2.7 (p. 61): constructs a uniform, infinitely additive probability on the full power set of ℕ valued in the non-standard [0,1]; not a general endorsement of Regularity, but Regularity follows here from independent desiderata.
- Ch. 3 (pp. 63–89): threshold (Lockean) models of belief are faulted for putting a sharp border on a vague notion; relative analysis yields "Stratified Belief", satisfying a weakened Conjunction Principle.
- Ch. 5 (pp. 99–128): for averaging agents, the probability of ending in an inconsistent belief state is under 2% and falls with more atomic sentences or a larger community.
- Ch. 6 (pp. 129+): the solution is summarised as "normalized numerosities" (equivalent to a free-ultrafilter construction); the outlook previews Non-Archimedean Probability (NAP) theory.

## Open questions

- The origin of Wenmackers' NAP programme (later with Benci, Horsten), central to debates on regularity, infinitesimal probabilities and zero-probability events.
- Check how the ultrafilter-dependence (non-uniqueness) of the construction is handled — the main objection pressed in the later literature (e.g. Williamson-style arguments against infinitesimal regularity).
- Chapter 5's agent-averaging model links to opinion dynamics in social epistemology.
