# LIT-575: Identification and Wholeheartedness

Harry G. Frankfurt, "Identification and Wholeheartedness", in Ferdinand
Schoeman (ed.), *Responsibility, Character, and the Emotions: New Essays in
Moral Psychology*, Cambridge University Press, pp. 27–45 (chapter 2) —
DOI-10.1017/CBO9780511625411.002. Reprinted as chapter 12 of Frankfurt, *The
Importance of What We Care About: Philosophical Essays*, Cambridge University
Press, 1988, pp. 159–176 (DOI-10.1017/CBO9780511818172.013), and in John
Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza (eds.), *Perspectives on Moral
Responsibility*, Cornell University Press, 1993, pp. 170–187 (Crossref
DOI 10.7591/9781501721564-007).

**The date.** The brief, and most of the literature, dates the essay 1987.
Cambridge's own chapter page gives the Schoeman volume a "Print publication
year: 1988", and Crossref gives it a full date of 29 January 1988; the 1988
collection follows on 27 May 1988. I did not see the volume's copyright
page, which is where a 1987 date would come from. `published:` carries the
earliest full date a source gives, 1988-01-29, as the record's rule asks;
"1987" in other readings is the same first printing, not a different
text. Both publications are confirmed; the Schoeman volume is the first.

## Key takeaways

*Registered from the record's second-hand readings, not a reading of the
essay. Each point says who reports it.*

- **The reply to the regress.** Dryden's IEP entry ([LIT-297](LIT-297.md), [NOTE-266](../notes.d/NOTE-266.md))
  reports, with page numbers in the 1988 reprint, that this essay ends the
  series of higher-order attitudes with a decisive commitment made "without
  reservation", where the agent feels no reason to go on deliberating
  (pp. 168–169); that stopping there is "hardly arbitrary", since possible
  error is a feature of all deliberation; that in deciding a person seeks
  "to overcome or to supersede a condition of inner division and to make
  himself into an integrated whole" (p. 174); and that the decision sets "a
  constraint by which other preferences and decisions are to be guided"
  (p. 175).
- **Wholeheartedness is separation, not peace.** Velleman's "Identification
  and Identity" ([LIT-292](LIT-292.md), [NOTE-268](../notes.d/NOTE-268.md)) quotes the essay's image of a decisive
  identification as a "radical *separation* of the competing desires", the
  loser "extruded entirely as an outlaw" (p. 170), and cites p. 168 for the
  image of a commitment that resounds "through higher and higher levels" of
  the hierarchy.
- **Who reads it, and how little.** Velleman's "What Happens When Someone
  Acts?" ([LIT-295](LIT-295.md), [NOTE-265](../notes.d/NOTE-265.md)) lists the essay but takes Frankfurt's
  decisive-commitment view from a 1976 footnote (IWWCA p. 68 n. 3), not from
  here. McKenna and Coates ([LIT-291](LIT-291.md), [NOTE-270](../notes.d/NOTE-270.md)) date wholeheartedness to
  1994, where the IEP dates it to this essay. Buss and Westlund ([LIT-296](LIT-296.md),
  [NOTE-267](../notes.d/NOTE-267.md)) list it and never cite it.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, in the batch on authenticity and
self-governance. It is philosophy of agency with no instruction for
machine-learning practice, so no anthology topic holds it. The Anthology of
the SOTA does not hold it: a grep of its literature.d for "Wholeheartedness"
found nothing.

`Deferred` because it was not read. Five readings in the record cite it, and
none of them reads it closely, so this entry gathers what they say rather
than reconstructing the essay from them.

**How it bears on [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md).** That account holds that no higher-order
attitude makes a motive one's own by its order alone: any stopping point
specified without the agent's participation can be disowned, and any
specified to include it begs the question. Its middle case is Frankfurt's
decisive commitment, which Velleman ([LIT-295](LIT-295.md), pp. 113–114) defeats with
an unwitting decision to end a friendship. This essay is the text that
stopping point comes from, and the record has never checked the case
against it:

- Velleman argued against the 1976 footnote, where a decision is said to be
  immune to externality because decisions "do not seem to be susceptible
  both to internality and to externality". This essay, as the IEP reports
  it, adds conditions the footnote lacks: the commitment is made "without
  reservation", and the agent sees no reason to deliberate further. An
  unwitting decision plausibly fails both conditions, so the counterexample
  may not touch the 1987 view.
- If it does not, the 1987 view sits on [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)'s other horn. Velleman
  already says (n. 35) that restricting decisive commitment to commitments
  the agent actively makes would make the answer "uninformative". "Without
  reservation" and "no reason to continue deliberating" are conditions on
  the agent's own stance, so they look like that restriction. Whether they
  can be stated without presupposing the agent's participation is exactly
  what [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md) turns on, and only the essay can settle it.
- The essay is also where, by the IEP's report, Frankfurt answers the
  regress itself ("hardly arbitrary"). [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md) says the 2004 lectures
  ([LIT-293](LIT-293.md)) never address the regress; this essay is the one that does.
  [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)'s `promote_when` lists the 1992 and 1994 replies and Bratman's
  policies but not this essay, though it is the earliest of Frankfurt's
  replies and the one the middle case is about. That omission is worth
  repairing in [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md).

**How it bears on the SDT theories.** The integration Frankfurt seeks here,
a decision that makes the person "an integrated whole" (p. 174, as
reported), is all-or-nothing and reached by extruding the rival desire.
[THEORY-054](../theory.d/THEORY-054.md) holds that autonomy is integration *graded* by congruence with
one's other values and needs (Ryan & Deci, [LIT-559](LIT-559.md)). An extruded desire
that still moves the person is close to what SDT calls an introject, "within
the person, but still relatively external to the self" (Deci & Ryan,
[LIT-558](LIT-558.md), p. 235, as [THEORY-054](../theory.d/THEORY-054.md) quotes it). Velleman ([LIT-292](LIT-292.md))
argues from the Rat Man case that extrusion is a defence, not health. So
the two accounts give opposite verdicts on the same agent: Frankfurt counts
the decisively divided agent as wholehearted, and SDT counts the extruded
desire as a failure of integration. Reading the essay would show whether
Frankfurt's wholeheartedness requires the rival desire to stop operating.

**Its successors in the record.** Frankfurt's Tanner Lectures ([LIT-293](LIT-293.md),
[NOTE-264](../notes.d/NOTE-264.md)) are his late statement of wholeheartedness, now as love whose
lovers are stipulated never to be ambivalent. Bratman's "Three Theories of
Self-Governance" ([LIT-596](LIT-596.md)), filed with this, revisits the
Frankfurt–Watson exchange, and is filed Deferred too.

**Priority for a reading: high.** It is the cheapest first-hand reading
that would move [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md), and it settles two of the record's open
disputes about Frankfurt's dates: whether wholeheartedness is 1987 or 1994,
and whether the "unopposed volition plus settled judgment" formulation that
[NOTE-270](../notes.d/NOTE-270.md) asks about is Frankfurt's wording.
