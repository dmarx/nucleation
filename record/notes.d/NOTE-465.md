# NOTE-465: The Sources of Normativity

## Contribution

The lectures recast the foundations of ethics as a first-person question, the
normative question, and give a historical sequence of answers: voluntarism
(Hobbes, Pufendorf), realism (Clarke, Price, Prichard, Moore, Nagel),
reflective endorsement (Hutcheson, Hume, Williams) and autonomy (Kant).
Their own contribution is the concept of practical identity, which turns
Kant's autonomy argument into an account of where all reasons and
obligations, moral or not, come from. Their conclusion is that all four
answers are "true", once each is placed as an aspect of the relation of
the reflective self to itself.

## Key insight

A being that can step back from its impulses cannot act on them as such. It
must decide whether they are reasons, and it can do that only by reference
to some law it takes as its own. Taking a law as one's own is having a
conception of who one is. So identity, in a practical sense, is not a
metaphysical fact about a person that ethics must respect. It is a
precondition of acting at all, and obligation is what that identity forbids.
On this view the threat of losing oneself (in "I couldn't live with myself if
I did that") is the form obligation takes.

## Assumptions

- **Reflective structure, not luminosity.** "The human mind is
  self-conscious in the sense that it is essentially reflective". It is not
  internally transparent: "our knowledge of our own mental states ... is no
  more certain than anything else."
- **Acting is acting for a reason, and reasons come from principles.** Kant's
  premise: a free will "must have its own law or principle", and "since
  reasons are derived from principles, the free will must have a principle".
- **The categorical imperative is the law of a free will**, as Kant argues in
  Groundwork III. This argument is summarised from her paper "Morality as
  Freedom", not given in full.
- **A distinction Kant does not make.** The categorical imperative (act on a
  maxim you can will as law) is not yet the moral law (the law of the
  Kingdom of Ends). The step between them is made by practical identity.
- **Wittgenstein's private-language argument**, read normatively: meaning is
  a relation between a legislator and an obeyer.
- **Aristotle's account of living things** as self-maintaining forms, used
  for the argument about pain and animals.

## Key results

Lecture by lecture:

- **Lecture I.** Voluntarism makes obligation depend on a legislator's
  authority, and authority cannot be reduced to power or to prior
  normativity without a regress or circle. Realism stops the regress "by
  fiat", like the cosmological argument. Procedural realism (there are
  correct procedures for moral questions) is separated from substantive
  realism (there are moral facts the procedures track). Only the second is
  rejected.
- **Lecture II.** Hume's "normativity as reflexivity": a faculty's verdicts
  are normative if the faculty, reflecting on itself, approves itself. The
  understanding fails the test and the moral sense passes. Williams
  justifies values by their contribution to flourishing. But reflection
  applied to particular motives, as in the case of the knavish lawyer,
  drives the reflective endorsement method to Kant: "The test of reflective
  endorsement ... is morality itself."
- **Lecture III.** Autonomy is the source of obligation. Practical identity
  is introduced, and there is "a jumble of such conceptions". Obligation is
  unconditional when violating an identity would be worse than death. Good
  maxims are "intrinsically normative entities" in virtue of their form, so
  "realism is true after all", in a procedural sense. Relativism has limits:
  valuing any identity requires valuing humanity, so "Enlightenment
  morality is true". Reasons are public, so others can obligate us. Pain is
  "the perception of a reason", so "we have obligations to animals".

## Concepts

- **Normative question**: "what justifies the claims that morality makes on
  us", asked from the first person when confidence is shaken.
- **Practical identity**: "a description under which you value yourself, a
  description under which you find your life to be worth living and your
  actions to be worth undertaking". It is not "a theoretical one, a view
  about what as a matter of inescapable scientific fact you are".
- **Reflective endorsement / reflective rejection**: reasons arise from the
  first, and obligation from the second.
- **Procedural vs substantive realism**: whether there are correct answers
  because of correct procedures, or correct procedures because of
  independent facts.
- **The thinking self and the acting self**: the two sides of reflective
  consciousness between which there is "a relation not of mere power but
  rather of authority".

## Connections

The lectures engage Frankfurt (the wanton, "Freedom of the Will and the
Concept of a Person"), Nagel (The Possibility of Altruism and The View from
Nowhere), Williams (Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy), Hume and Kant. None
of those works is read in the record, except Frankfurt's later Tanner
Lectures ([LIT-293](../literature.d/LIT-293.md)), which answer the normative question through caring and
love rather than autonomy. The later "Normative Constitution of Agency"
([LIT-587](../literature.d/LIT-587.md)) turns practical identity into self-constitution: we "constitute
our own practical identities" by choosing under the categorical and
hypothetical imperatives.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)** (the regress of higher-order attitudes). The lectures offer
  a stopping point the theory has not yet weighed. The agent's standpoint is
  not a higher-order attitude but the practical identity she must have in
  order to act at all. The wanton note (C5) makes every reflective agent's
  action express some law. Against [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md), this is specified by the
  structure of reflection, independently of any particular attitude. For
  [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md), which identity counts as one's own is still "how she thinks of
  herself". Identities form "a jumble", and the lectures give no criterion
  that discriminates a self-governed choice from a wanton one, except that
  the latter treats the desire of the moment as law. So the lectures may
  supply what [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)'s refutation condition calls "a stopping point that
  is specified independently of agency", but only if practical identity can
  be specified without presupposing the agent's participation. Korsgaard
  says practical identity is chosen or constituted. That looks like the
  second horn of the dilemma. Worth a sentence in [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)'s connections
  when it is next revised. The decision is the owner's.
- **[THEORY-047](../theory.d/THEORY-047.md) and SDT.** Korsgaard's relativism about identity ("human
  identity has been differently constituted in different social worlds") is
  the philosophical counterpart of SDT's claim that how a value was taken in
  fixes how it is held. The lectures do not discuss how identities are
  acquired.
- **A THEORY, filed as [THEORY-075](../theory.d/THEORY-075.md) (Proposed).** *Obligation is constituted by
  reflective rejection of what would violate an agent's practical identity,

  is Proposed until the book's reply to its commentators

## Limitations

- By Korsgaard's own account the lectures are "necessarily ... sketchy, and
  sketchily argued", and they leave the content of obligations, and how to
  resolve conflicts between identities, "for another day".
- The step from "the will must have a law" to "the agent must have a
  conception of her identity" is made by appeal to how deliberation seems
  from inside ("it is as if there were something over and above all of
  your desires"), not proved.
- The publicity argument depends on one reading of Wittgenstein.
- The commentators' objections in the book version (Cohen on obligation to
  bad identities, Williams, Nagel, Geuss) are not here, and nor is the
  reply.

## Open questions

- Can practical identity be specified without the agent's participation? If
  not, does the account answer the regress, or relocate it?
- What happens when one's identities conflict? The lectures leave it open
  (note 16), and the book's reply is the next place to look.
