be represented. Nothing on it was held before this entry. A scoping pass
found that the core texts are all out of legitimate reach:

- Frankfurt's 1969–1994 papers, including "Freedom of the Will and the
  Concept of a Person" (1971);
- Watson's "Free Agency" (1975);
- Bratman's papers.

## The account, and where each piece comes from

Each claim below is marked **first-hand** (read in Frankfurt here) or
**second-hand**, with the source named.

1. **The 1971 hierarchy (second-hand, SEP *Compatibilism* §4.2.1).**
   - First-order desires are for actions; second-order desires are
     desires about one's desires.
   - The *will* is the "effective first-order desire", the one that moves
     one "all the way to action".
   - A mere second-order desire (the therapist who wants to know what
     craving is like) differs from a *second-order volition*, a desire that
     a given desire be one's will.
   - The unwilling addict has a second-order volition against the craving
     that moves him. The willing addict endorses it.
   - The formula for free will: "One acts of her own free will if and only
     if her action issues from the will she wants". I checked the wording
     against the entry when filing.
   - Page references are to the Watson (ed.) 1982 reprint.
2. **Wantons and persons (second-hand, and only in older revisions).**
   - Only persons have second-order volitions. Wantons are "passive
     bystanders to their wills". "The wanton is not a person, and so is not
     a candidate for freely willed action."
   - This is in the SEP *Compatibilism* revisions of 2004–2015 only (e.g.
     the Fall 2019 archive edition). The 26 Nov 2019 revision dropped it.
   - None of the other six texts states it, and the Tanner Lectures
     mention only "wantonly unreflective" animals and small children
     (p. 172).
3. **Freedom of action vs freedom of the will (first-hand, Tanner p. 177).**
   Frankfurt restates the distinction and his compatibilism (p. 178). No
   secondary source in the batch does.
4. **The mature view (first-hand, Tanner Lectures).** I checked the
   quotations against the lecture PDF.
   - *Identification* is largely a default "willing acceptance", "whether
     with a welcoming approval, or in weary resignation".
   - *Free will* is "a certain volitional unanimity": the governing desire
     agrees "with a higher-order volition" and conflicts with no desire "at
     any higher order". That is wholeheartedness.
   - *Caring* is wanting to go on wanting.
   - *Love* is volitional. Its necessities "may be irresistible, but they
     are not coercive". It is "the only legitimate authority" for our
     normative attitudes.
   - The lectures do not use the terms "second-order desire" or
     "second-order volition", do not discuss the regress, and never name
     Watson. The case for love's authority rests on the stipulation that
     lovers are never ambivalent.
5. **The regress and Watson (second-hand, and the sources disagree).**
   - The IEP and SEP *Compatibilism* credit Watson (1975) with the
     authority/regress objection. SEP *Personal Autonomy* states the regress
     as its own argument, credits it to nobody, and files Watson 1975 as a
     *rival* (valuational) account.
   - Velleman (1992) reports the regress as Watson's objection, which
     Frankfurt conceded.
   - None of the sources reports Watson's valuing-versus-desiring
     distinction in any detail.
6. **Frankfurt's replies (second-hand, dated inconsistently).**
   - *Decisive commitment:* the IEP and Velleman place its germ in 1971
     itself; SEP *Compatibilism* says 1971 "rested solely" on higher-order
     volition.
   - *Wholeheartedness:* the IEP dates it to 1987, SEP *Compatibilism* to
     1994.
   - *Satisfaction (1992):* only SEP *Personal Autonomy*, n. 1, mentions it.
   - *Manipulation:* SEP *Compatibilism* quotes Frankfurt (2002, p. 27)
     accepting that a character given by a manipulator still leaves a person
     responsible.
   - *Resignation:* the resigned willing addict is excluded by the 1994
     amendment according to SEP *Compatibilism*, but Frankfurt's own 2004
     text counts acceptance "in weary resignation" as identification.
7. **Critiques.**
   - *Velleman 1992:* on the "standard story" of action the agent drops
     out. Any state defined without the agent's participation (second-order
     desires, Watson's values, decisive commitments) can be disowned, and
     any state defined to include it presupposes what it was meant to
     explain. His alternative: the agent is whatever plays the agent's
     functional role, the desire to act in accordance with reasons.
   - *Velleman 2002:* this targets the late view. An essence made of
     volitional necessities would yield authenticity, not autonomy.
     Wholeheartedness is a defensive ideal (the Rat Man). Frankfurt's
     "Reply to J. David Velleman" in the same volume was not obtained.
   - *Manipulation* and *mesh* objections: per SEP *Compatibilism* and
     *Personal Autonomy*.
   - *Bratman's self-governing policies* (2007): per SEP *Compatibilism*
     only. Bratman 1996 and 2004 are reported by no source.
8. **Frankfurt cases (1969; second-hand, Robb).** These cases are the
   strongest attack on the principle that responsibility requires being
   able to do otherwise, and there is "not much consensus". The 1969 case
   appears only in Fischer's retelling. The bridge to the hierarchy (§5.1)
   is that identification or endorsement is one of two compatibilist
   accounts of responsibility after Frankfurt cases.

## Gaps, and what would fill them

- **Frankfurt 1971, 1976, 1987, 1992 and 1994, and Watson 1975**, are all
  paywalled. Watson is the largest loss: his objection is reported, but
  with contradictory attributions.
- **Stump 1988** has a legitimate author-linked copy on PhilArchive, which
  blocks this environment. The owner could fetch it in a browser.
- **The mid-2019 SEP *Compatibilism* revision** holds the wanton/person
  text. It was read only for that section.
