summary: >-
  Pettigrew & Tropp (2006). A meta-analysis of 515 studies (713 independent samples, 250,089 participants, 38 countries, through 2000) finds that intergroup contact typically reduces intergroup prejudice (random-effects r ≈ −.21; 94% of samples negative). Samples without choice of contact show larger effects, and the more rigorous studies larger ones (experiments −.34), though for racial and ethnic targets experiments show no advantage. Five of six publication-bias checks find none; Vevea and Hedges's model, adjusted, leaves no significant overall effect but a significant one for well-controlled designs. Effects generalize to the whole outgroup and, in few tests, beyond. Allport's optimal conditions enhance the effect (structured programs −.29 against −.20) but work as a bundle and are not essential to it.

# LIT-1413: A Meta-Analytic Test of Intergroup Contact Theory

Pettigrew, T. F. and Tropp, L. R. (2006). A meta-analytic test of intergroup contact theory. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 90(5), 751–783. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.90.5.751.

Read in full on 2026-10-29 (NOTE-tmplxoe7).

## Why this is here

The exchange widened the Franklin effect to groups: contact between members of different groups works like the favour, an encounter that comes before the revised view of the other. It cited this meta-analysis for 515 studies and 713 samples, contact typically reducing prejudice and generalizing to the group. The abstract agrees on all three. The exchange did not mention what the abstract also says, that Allport's conditions are facilitating, not essential, which bears on how much the encounter itself does. Bears on [CLAIM-275](../claims.d/CLAIM-275.md) and, as a coupling of interpretation and conduct between groups, on [CLAIM-297](../claims.d/CLAIM-297.md) and [CLAIM-330](../claims.d/CLAIM-330.md).
