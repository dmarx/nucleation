# THEORY-tmpn0uzd: Siemroth shows that when an agent privately knows its organization's spending needs and gains from spending above them, a principal who commits in advance does weakly better by letting the agent roll over some share of unused funds than none, effective only when the share exceeds the agent's marginal gain from misuse and sometimes best below full roll-over, while auditing only spending above a threshold under a punishment large enough that it is never imposed

## Source

Siemroth (2024), LIT-tmpuad7h; read in NOTE-tmpm0esa, on the Essex
discussion paper of February 2022.

## What was actually shown

- **Some roll-over** (Proposition 1): "Consequently, allowing at least some
  roll-over of unused funds is weakly better." Without roll-over the agent
  spends the whole budget.
- **The effectiveness condition** (p. 18): the share "must exceed α for the
  agent to want to roll-over any funds at all and stop misusing funds in
  year 1".
- **Partial roll-over** (Proposition 1): Δ* < 1 is optimal when α and b are
  small and λ large; "for most parameter values allowing a full fund
  roll-over is optimal" (p. 2). The result could have been that full
  roll-over always dominates; it is not.
- **Threshold audits** (Lemma 1, Proposition 2): any audit rule is
  outcome-equivalent to a threshold rule; in year 1, when roll-over induces
  saving, an interval rule can be optimal instead (Lemma 2, Proposition 4).
- **Punishment** (Lemma 3): maximal, "so that punishment never occurs in
  equilibrium".

## What this does not say

- That organizations in fact misuse funds at year-end: there is no
  empirical test, and what counts as undesirable spending is left to the
  reader (p. 33).
- That roll-over helps because of precaution: the agent is risk neutral
  (p. 19). The precautionary account is Liebman and Mahoney's
  (LIT-tmp4kmcg).
- That a cap on roll-over is optimal: Δ is a share of savings, and caps are
  not analysed.
- Anything beyond two periods, where savings could accumulate (p. 33).
