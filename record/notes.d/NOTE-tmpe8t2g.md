# NOTE-tmpe8t2g: Gaboriaud et al. (2024): Wrongness and Blame Judgments and Their Dynamics

## Contribution

Proposes a "three-input processing model" combining Cushman's (2008) two processes and Malle's (2021) hierarchy of judgments. It is tested on wrongness and blame with many scenarios, mixed models and mouse-tracking.

## Key results

1. Abstract: "This preregistered experiment (N = 145) re-uses an adapted mouse-tracking paradigm to analyze these influences over time."
2. Abstract: "Results on final judgments replicate the effects of intent, outcome, and causality, as well as partial evidence for their interaction effects."
3. Abstract: "However, this study does not reveal clear differential weight for intent and outcome inputs in blame versus wrongness judgments."
4. Results, step 2: harsher judgments "when the agent directly caused the outcome (M = 3.72, SE = 0.07) compared to when the outcome was caused by another source (M = 2.82, SE = 0.07)".
5. Results, step 2: "the causality effect was stronger (+ 0.16) in the case of blame judgments (b = 0.98) compared to wrongness judgments (b = 0.82)", d = 0.05.
6. Conclusion: "the distinction between types of judgment (particularly wrongness versus blame) must not be overestimated".

## Assumptions

Intent as belief only. Causality as whether the agent or "another independent source" caused the harm. A fixed order of information (intent first). A within-participant design, which the authors concede may merge the two questions.

## Concepts

Wrongness of the act versus blame of the agent: "Wrongness focuses more on the moral wrongness of the act itself (independently of who perpetrated it), whereas blame targets more the agent who performed the action". Cushman's mental-state versus causal information; moral luck; process tracing by mouse trajectories.

## Bearing on the record

- **The exchange** (A5 §5) reports the result accurately ("only partly supported"). It then rests its "Four kinds of responsibility worth keeping separate" on "the emerging science of moral responsibility". This paper studies none of those kinds. Its finding is that lay wrongness and blame judgments differ little, so it gives A's distinction no empirical footing.
- **CLAIM-tmpnw7ez**: the LIT linked the work to this claim. The paper does not bear on it: no party structures another's circumstances in its scenarios.

## Limitations

145 French psychology students, 131 of them women; vignettes, mostly care/harm; type effects "remain low in size"; the information order is fixed.
