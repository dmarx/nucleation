# NOTE-tmplcbcz: Powers, Clark & McFarland — General Feedback Theory, Part II

## Contribution

Part II applies Part I's model to people. It names six orders of
perceptual variable and gives a tabletop "Portable Demonstrator" for the
first five, read off reaction times. It works out the N-system
(reorganization) as the source of insight, defines consciousness by it,
and gives a theory of conflict between control systems from which it
derives regression, resistance and the psychoanalytic defences. It closes
with a statement of values. The one experiment is "still in process" (p.
36).

## Key insight

The hierarchy fixes what a person can perceive, and only reorganization
changes that. "it is perception which gives form to behavior" (p. 26).
The N-system "never adds new information to the system in the sense of
providing a specific answer to a problem. It merely alters the properties
of a system, thus changing the transformations applied to existing
information" (p. 37). Learning, in this paper, is a change of perceptual
transformation, experienced as insight, driven by intrinsic error and
filtered by imagined conflict as well as by behaviour.

## Assumptions

- **All people are organized in the six orders,** "for the time being"
  (p. 26). The authors cannot say why there are six: perhaps reality, the
  brain, the culture, "or even ... the authors' microcosm!" (p. 26).
- **The observer is a system like the observed.** "One must never forget
  that the person observing human behavior is a system like the one he is
  observing" (pp. 25–26).
- **Order is not complexity.** "Complexity is not a factor in determining
  relative order" (p. 27).
- **Part I's model,** with its recording functions, imagination connection
  and N-system, is taken as given.

## Key results

- **Rules of order (p. 27).** "In order to perceive and control nth-order
  variables, one must simultaneously perceive and control lower-order
  variables (except for n = 1), but the reverse is not true. Eliminating
  higher-order perceptions leaves the lower; blocking the lower partially
  or totally eliminates the higher." Each system is "controlled to the
  extent that it does not choose which of its past experiences are to
  serve as goal-perceptions" (p. 27). "The highest order of
  reference-signal is set by noise or by random action of the N-system"
  (p. 27).
- **The orders and demonstrations (pp. 28–35).**
  - First: spinal loops; an arm pushed down rebounds "as if on a spring",
    an active correction by electromyograph (p. 28).
  - Second: elementary sensations, "identity signals" (p. 29); a pushed arm
    first returns, then swings down as instructed (p. 29).
  - Third: configurations; a finger-pointing task shows a slower third
    phase of correction (p. 30).
  - Fourth: sequences; tracking a circling finger, S "continues to 'track'
    for nearly half a second" after it stops, and "His reaction time does
    not shorten significantly with practice" (p. 31).
  - Fifth: relationships, shown by a rule over sequences
    ("increasing the number of L sequences by one after every U
    sequence", p. 32), with a reaction time "considerably slower" than
    fourth order (p. 33). "it is the act of changing interpretations that
    reveals the presence of a fifth-order system" (p. 33).
  - Sixth: "rather vague"; system concepts, such as personality, a
    government, "a self-concept" (pp. 34–35). No sixth-order reaction time
    was found (p. 35).
- **Facts and sixth-order error (p. 35).** "A 'fact' may be partially
  defined as a perception which does not cause an error in a sixth-order
  system". A perception that does causes the person to look "for added
  information which will give a different fifth-order or lower
  perception, thus correcting the sixth-order error ... Sometimes the
  required information is found, sometimes not. The nature of one's
  sixth-order systems and the activity of one's N-system will then
  determine whether the required shift in sixth-order perception and
  reference-level will occur."
- **The N-system (pp. 35–37).** Its intrinsic variables are "probably the
  signals associated with drives, while others may represent more subtle
  conditions, such as average stimulus input rate, or mean error signal in
  the hierarchy" (p. 35). Its specificity is unknown: random, "localized in
  regions where error-signals exist", or by some better rule (pp. 35–36).
  During reorganization skilled behaviour deteriorates; in the authors'
  learning experiment, reaction time "begins to vary and becomes longer"
  just before each drop to a new plateau, which they "take to be evidence
  of N-system activity" (p. 36).
- **Insight, and its veto by imagining (pp. 36–37).** The N-system is
  responsible for "insight", "the 'aha' reaction when one suddenly
  perceives a pattern in lower-order information which he has never seen
  before"; "it is merely a new organization of perception." "insights
  which are not useful are quickly discarded because they create conflict
  or tend to increase intrinsic errors". The golfer who thinks of using
  his putter like a billiard cue: "a moment spent imagining doing so would
  reveal the fifth-order and sixth-order ('That's not golf!') conflicts
  which he could expect. Therefore this behavior pattern is not selected
  as a reference-level on the golf course." A new transformation may "lie
  dormant" until useful (p. 37).
- **Consciousness (p. 37).** "Consciousness, we propose, is the state of
  the feedback-function in a subsystem in the hierarchy which is being
  affected by the output of the N-system. Thus the same subsystem can
  perceive and control a variable either consciously or unconsciously,
  depending on whether the N-system is actively connected to it."
  Stated "merely" as a definition, with reasons withheld.
- **Reduction of order (pp. 38–39).** Subjects "attempt to use the lowest
  order of information available that will suffice": asked to tell "left,
  left" from "right, left", nearly all showed third-order reaction times
  ("0.3 sec., approximately") instead of fourth ("0.4-0.45 sec.,
  approximately"), having seen the sequence as an arrangement (p. 38).
  Symbols are "order-reduced representations of higher-order
  perceptions"; "most language is an order-reduced representation of
  experience" (p. 39).
- **Conflict (pp. 39–42).** When two order-n systems demand contradictory
  settings of a shared lower system, it holds a "virtual reference-level"
  and the two higher systems are "removed from the organism's set of
  environment-controlling systems" (p. 40). Driven to their limits they
  oscillate ("vacillation") or hand control to a cruder older system
  ("regression", pp. 40–41). Remedies: altering a controlling system,
  altering a reference, "switching one system entirely to the
  imagination-connection [the mechanism (See Part I) of fantasy,
  wish-fulfillment, and closure]", or suppression (p. 41). Anything other
  than a higher-order change of reference "or a realistic change in
  properties via N-system action" is "the 'defense of the ego'" (p. 41).
  The rigidly maintained behaviour is one order below the conflict (pp.
  41–42). "putting both or all the conflicted systems into the state of
  consciousness is the only way to start the N-system to work changing
  them" (p. 42).
- **Values (pp. 43–44).** The optimum hierarchy has no significant
  conflict and a self-concept that includes "knowledge of the properties of
  the N-system and the signs of its action" (p. 43). A person "would even
  have to learn, if he could, just what constitutes his own intrinsic
  state, because this information is not built into his learned systems"
  (p. 44). "A person must beware of setting up any ideal in his mind as
  sacred and untouchable, for by so doing, he sets up an automatic
  mechanism to counter the effect of his N-system, and guarantees a halt in
  his development" (p. 44).

## Concepts

- **FBCS.** "externally fed-back Feedback Control Systems" (p. 25).
- **Multiordinality.** The same behaviour describable at many levels,
  "Both" an artefact of observation and a property of the system (p. 25).
- **Order n.** A class of perception self-evidently different from the
  class it is built from (p. 27).
- **Identity signals.** Second-order elementary sensations (p. 29).
- **Fact.** "a perception which does not cause an error in a sixth-order
  system" (p. 35).
- **Insight.** A new organization of perception produced by the N-system
  (p. 36).
- **Consciousness.** As above (p. 37).
- **Reduction of order.** Using lower-order information, or symbols, in
  place of higher (pp. 38–39).
- **Virtual reference-level.** The compromise value a conflicted shared
  subsystem holds (p. 40).

## Connections

reinterpreted, not cited. Learning-machine experiments are mentioned
without citation (p. 36). The six orders became nine in Powers's 1973 book
(LIT-tmp44sbd, not read) and later eleven (my knowledge, not this
paper's).

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpq9do8 (thesis).**
  - *This is the closest the 1960 theory comes to the claim.* Two passages
    have the claim's loop, a judgement that changes what is perceived next,
    without action.
    - The golfer: an insight, a new organization of perception, is
      rejected because "a moment spent imagining doing so would reveal the
      fifth-order and sixth-order ... conflicts" (pp. 36–37). The
      imagination connection here tests a change of the observer and
      selects against it, with no action on the world.
    - The sixth-order "fact": a perception that conflicts with a system
      concept sends the person looking for information that "will give a
      different fifth-order or lower perception"; if none is found, the
      N-system decides "whether the required shift in sixth-order
      perception and reference-level will occur" (p. 35). That is a
      judgement at the top reshaping perception below.
  - *The engine is still not judgement.* What changes perception is the
    N-system, driven by intrinsic error. If its intrinsic variables include
    "mean error signal in the hierarchy" (p. 35), then persistent error in
    learned systems, which is what a failing judgement is in this model,
    would itself drive reorganization. That would close the claim's loop
    inside PCT: judgement → error → reorganized perception → new
    observations. The paper offers the variable as a guess and does not
    draw the consequence (my observation).
  - *Consciousness ties to the reshaped observer.* Defining consciousness as
    "the state of the feedback-function ... being affected by the output of
    the N-system" (p. 37) makes conscious perception just the perception
    whose transformation is being changed. That fits the claim's "lucid
    agent" better than anything in the 1973 paper, though it is offered as
    a definition, without reasons.
- **CLAIM-tmpki5gp (counter).** Part II supplies the counter's failure mode
  by name. Switching a system "entirely to the imagination-connection" is
  "the mechanism ... of fantasy, wish-fulfillment, and closure" and one of
  the ego's defences (p. 41), and an ideal held "sacred and untouchable"
  "sets up an automatic mechanism to counter the effect of his N-system"
  (p. 44). The paper's cure is not action but consciousness: putting the
  conflicted systems into consciousness "is the only way to start the
  N-system to work changing them" (p. 42).
- **QUESTION-tmpr5tck.**
  - *Candidate 4, processing signals.* "mean error signal in the
    hierarchy" (p. 35) is a signal about how the interpretation is going,
    not what it says, fed to a system the learned hierarchy does not set.
    That is candidate 4 placed inside candidate 2's channel.
  - *Candidate 1, unselected input.* "average stimulus input rate" (p. 35)
    makes the amount of input, not its content, an intrinsic variable. In
    sensory deprivation it would be in error and drive reorganization (my
    observation). In locked-in syndrome, with input spared, it would not.
  - *What is lost with action.* "Eliminating higher-order perceptions
    leaves the lower; blocking the lower partially or totally eliminates
    the higher" (p. 27) is about perception. Nothing in it says that loss
    of first-order output removes higher perceptions. So on this model a
    locked-in person keeps all orders of perception, and loses only the
    control of them through movement (my observation).
- **CASE-tmpxgcq2.** Nothing directly; the paper does not discuss
  paralysis.
- **For the anthology.** No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- The six orders are introspective proposals; the authors say they may be
  peculiar "to the authors' microcosm" (p. 26).
- The demonstrations are informal, with reaction times given only for
  orders 3 and 4, without sample sizes or variance. The complex-learning
  experiment is "still in process" (p. 36).
- The definition of consciousness is stated without its reasons.
- The N-system's specificity and the intrinsic variables are guesses (pp.
  35–36).
- Read from an OCR extraction of a reset reprint.

## Open questions

- Whether "mean error signal in the hierarchy" is an intrinsic variable.
  If it is, error in learned judgement reshapes perception through
  reorganization, which would make CLAIM-tmpq9do8's loop a part of PCT.
- Whether the golfer's veto, imagined conflict selecting among new
  perceptual organizations, can keep a model in touch with the world, or
  only with the person's existing system concepts. In the example it
  protects "That's not golf!", a sixth-order reference, which is the
  self-confirming case.
