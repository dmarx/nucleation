# LIT-tmpw2wwz: Attention to Action: Willed and Automatic Control of Behavior

Donald A. Norman & Tim Shallice (1986), "Attention to Action: Willed and
Automatic Control of Behavior", in R. J. Davidson, G. E. Schwartz & D. Shapiro
(eds.), *Consciousness and Self-Regulation: Advances in Research and Theory*,
vol. 4, New York: Plenum, pp. 1–18 — DOI-10.1007/978-1-4757-0629-1_1.
Crossref gives only the year, so `published:` carries it as the first of
January. Crossref's chapter title is "Attention to Action" alone. The
subtitle is the 1980 report's, and is used here because the standard
citation of the chapter carries it. Whether the printed chapter carries it
was not checked.

An earlier version was issued in December 1980 as CHIP Report 99, Center for
Human Information Processing, University of California, San Diego. ERIC
catalogues it as ED205562, "Technical Report No. 8006", 43 pages, sponsored
by the Sloan Foundation, the Office of Naval Research and NIMH. This entry is
for the 1986 chapter, the version with a DOI. The report is the same
argument in an earlier form, and it is not filed separately.

## Key takeaways

*Registered from the ERIC abstract of the 1980 report, not a reading of
either version.* The abstract, in part:

- "The major theme of the paper is that the primary role of attention is in
  the control of action." Action sequences "can run themselves off,
  efficiently, smoothly, without any need for deliberate attention". When a
  plan must be modified, a novel sequence followed, or "some habitual act"
  prevented, "it is necessary to insert deliberate attentional
  intervention".
- "Most attentional conflicts occur with the initiation rather than the
  execution of actions."
- "Two levels of control are suggested: a contention scheduling mechanism
  that selects from among competing schemas; and a supervisory attentional
  mechanism that biases the selection process." The supervisory system is
  required "where the action sequences are ill-learned or novel, where the
  action is highly critical or dangerous, or where planning is required".
- "The result is three modes of the control of performance: automatic,
  contention scheduling without deliberate direction, and deliberate
  conscious control. Will becomes the application of attentional resources
  to the control of action."

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, for the record's coverage of
willed and automatic control. It is the classic statement of a
*supervisory* system set over a field of competing action schemas.

That makes it the older counterpart to the dual-process accounts that
Frijda ([LIT-540](LIT-540.md)) and [THEORY-056](../theory.d/THEORY-056.md) argue against, and the ancestor of the
controlled/automatic distinction that Shenhav, Botvinick and Cohen
([LIT-tmpncuk4](LIT-tmpncuk4.md)) formalize. Its lower level, contention scheduling among
schemas, is control without a controller, the architecture Brooks ([LIT-436](LIT-436.md))
built. The open question for [THEORY-056](../theory.d/THEORY-056.md) is whether the supervisory level is
a regulator of a different kind, or one more competitor that biases the
contest. The abstract's word "biases" leaves that open. Only a reading can
answer it.

**Priority for a reading: high** for [THEORY-056](../theory.d/THEORY-056.md) and for the record's
account of will.
