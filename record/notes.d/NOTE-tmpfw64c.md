# NOTE-tmpfw64c: Sewell — A theory of structure

## Contribution

Sewell keeps Giddens's thesis that structures are both the medium and the
outcome of practice, and repairs the concept so that it can carry agency
and change. He replaces "rules" with "schemas", argues that Giddens's
"virtual" structures cannot include resources, which are actual, and
defines structure as schemas and resources that "mutually imply and sustain
each other over time" (p. 13). Against Bourdieu's habitus, which he finds
reproduces itself with no room for change, he gives five axioms under which
the ordinary working of structures generates their own transformation.

## Key insight

Knowing a schema is being able to apply it to a case it was not learned
on, and that ability is agency. "Knowledge of a rule or a schema by
definition means the ability to transpose or extend it-that is, to apply
it creatively. If this is so, then agency, which I would define as
entailing the capacity to transpose and extend schemas to new contexts, is
inherent in the knowledge of cultural schemas that characterizes all
minimally competent members of society" (p. 18). Since the results of a
transposition are not predictable, and schemas live only if their results
validate them, reproduction is never automatic.

## Assumptions

- **Schemas are virtual.** "they cannot be reduced to their existence in
  any particular practice or any particular location in space and time"
  (p. 8). Codified rules (statutes, contracts) are resources, not schemas.
- **Resources are actual,** human and nonhuman, and "media of power"
  unevenly distributed, though "some measure of both human and nonhuman
  resources are controlled by all members of society" (pp. 9–10).
- **Mutual dependence.** "Schemas not empowered or regenerated by
  resources would eventually be abandoned and forgotten, just as resources
  without cultural schemas to direct their use would eventually dissipate
  and decay" (p. 13).
- **Society is plural.** The change axioms require "a far more multiple,
  contingent, and fractured conception of society-and of structure"
  (p. 16).

## Key results

No data. By section:

- **Three problems with "structure" (pp. 2–3):** it implies rigid
  determinism, makes change awkward, and means opposite things in
  sociology (hard, material) and anthropology (culture).
- **Rules to schemas (pp. 6–8).** Giddens's rules, read as "generalizable
  procedures", become "the informal and not always conscious schemas,
  metaphors, or assumptions presupposed by such formal statements" (p. 8),
  at depths from Lévi-Strauss's oppositions to etiquette.
- **Resources (pp. 9–12).** Giddens's definition rendered as "resources are
  anything that can serve as a source of power in social interactions"
  (p. 9). Resources take their value from schemas: Hudson Bay blankets are
  warmth, or, in a potlatch, the chief's power (pp. 11–12).
- **Duality kept (p. 13).** "Structure, then, should be defined as composed
  simultaneously of schemas, which are virtual, and of resources, which are
  actual." A factory's gate and punch clock "teach and validate the rules of
  the capitalist labor contract"; "Resources, we might say, are read like
  texts".
- **Against habitus (pp. 14–16).** Bourdieu's habitus "retains precisely
  the agent-proof quality that the concept of the duality of structure is
  supposed to overcome" (p. 15); Kabylia "produced a momentous anticolonial
  revolution shortly after Bourdieu returned to France" (p. 15); what
  misleads is his "unrealistically unified and totalized concept of
  habitus" (p. 16).
- **Five axioms of change (pp. 16–19).** The multiplicity of structures
  (never all homologous); the transposability of schemas; the
  unpredictability of resource accumulation (schemas "will in fact be
  differentially validated when they are put into action", p. 18); the
  polysemy of resources ("Any array of resources is capable of being
  interpreted in varying ways and, therefore, of empowering different
  actors and teaching different schemas", p. 19); and the intersection of
  structures. "Structures are at risk, at least to some extent, in all of
  the social encounters they shape" (p. 19).
- **Agency (pp. 20–21).** "Agency is implied by the existence of
  structures" (p. 20). It differs "in both kind and extent" with position
  (p. 20). It is "collective as well as individual", but Sewell rejects
  applying "agent" to corporate units in the same sense as to persons
  (p. 21).
- **Depth and power (pp. 22–26).** Structures vary in depth (schemas: how
  pervasive and taken for granted) and power (resources). Language: very
  deep, little power, very durable. States: usually high power and low
  depth, so "natural targets for open struggles" (p. 24); the United States
  "has had a single constitution since 1789" yet "at least five sharply
  distinct party systems" (p. 24). "Durability, then, would appear to be
  determined more by a structure's depth than by its power" (p. 24).
  Capitalism combines both: the commodity form is "exceptionally
  transposable", and its surface instability "actually reinforces its
  deeper structures" (pp. 25–26).

## Concepts

- **Schema**: a virtual, transposable procedure or assumption, "not
  formally stated prescriptions" (p. 8).
- **Resource**: anything that can serve as a source of power, human or
  nonhuman, always actual; its value is set by schemas.
- **Structure**: "sets of mutually sustaining schemas and resources that
  empower and constrain social action and that tend to be reproduced by
  that social action" (p. 19).
- **Transposition**: applying a schema to a new case, "but in such a way
  that the rule will have subtly different forms in each of its
  applications" (n. 9, p. 17).
- **Depth and power**: the schema and the resource dimensions along which
  structures vary (p. 22).

## Connections

It works from Giddens (1976–1984) and Bourdieu's Outline (1977), with
Sahlins on how categories are "burdened with the world" (n. 11) and
Goffman for everyday agency. Swidler ([LIT-1367](../literature.d/LIT-1367.md)) is thanked and her
"tool kit" is close to his schemas, but she is not cited. Wacquant
([LIT-1377](../literature.d/LIT-1377.md), 2016) answers the later version of this critique (Sewell
2005, ch. 4) by saying habitus needs no supplement, without engaging the
argument. Sewell is right about Outline's sentence ("all the actions …
and no others"); Wacquant's habitus, layered and able to misfire, is a
later reading the 1992 essay does not meet.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-417](../claims.d/CLAIM-417.md) (selves as organizational perspectives).** The LIT says
  the exchange cited this as an account of how structures are reproduced
  and transformed through activity. That is accurate. Three points bear on
  the claim. First, Sewell's agency is the claim's carrier with a
  capacity, not a perspective: agents are persons, and structures are
  multiple and intersecting in each, "a wide range of different and even
  incompatible schemas" (p. 17), so one carrier enacting several structures
  is his normal case. Second, he says what the claim also says, that
  collective agency is real without collectivities being agents in the
  same sense as persons (p. 21); that is the claim's "Not that
  perspectives are agents", reached from structure. Third, the defeater
  that established accounts "explain the same transitions as well with
  fewer assumptions" bites hardest here: transposition and remobilization
  are a theory of how one organization changes into another, which is
  [CLAIM-417](../claims.d/CLAIM-417.md)'s "transformation of organizational perspectives". What
  Sewell lacks is the claim's distinction between a lineage and a
  character; "transformations of the old" (p. 27) are recognized, he says,
  but he gives no criterion for when a transformed structure is the same
  one.
- **[CLAIM-416](../claims.d/CLAIM-416.md) (competition over tests).** The polysemy of resources is
  the claim's reflexive part stated in general. Sewell's example: the gains
  of a successful cavalry charge "may be attributed to the superior
  discipline and elan of the cavalry officers and thereby enhance the
  power of an aristocratic officer corps, or it may be attributed to the
  commanding general and thereby result in the increasing subordination of
  officers to a charismatic leader" (p. 19). What a success shows is
  itself contested, and the reading that wins redistributes power. And
  unpredictable accumulation says success validates the schema that
  produced it ("A brilliantly successful cavalry attack on a new terrain
  may change the battle plans of subsequent campaigns or even theories of
  military tactics", p. 18). The claim could cite this as the general
  mechanism of which competition over tests is a case.
- **[CLAIM-423](../claims.d/CLAIM-423.md) (timescale and authority in a political collective).**
  Sewell offers a rival account of the same durabilities. Where the claim
  puts timescale, Sewell puts depth: state structures change because they
  are powerful and visible, and deep political structures (the American
  constitutional system, the French bureaucracy, English community law)
  last because they are taken for granted as power-neutral (p. 24). His
  "single constitution" under "five sharply distinct party systems" is the
  claim's "a state can stay continuous as an actor while the organization
  producing its positions changes under it", stated in 1992. The claim's
  defeater asks whether existing accounts already state it; Sewell states
  the continuity, not the link between speed and authority.
- **[CASE-133](../cases.d/CASE-133.md) (party realignment).** The same sentence: party systems
  change sharply under a continuous constitution (p. 24, citing Burnham
  1967). It supports the case's carrier-and-character distinction for the
  state, not for the parties.
- **[TERM-072](../terms.d/TERM-072.md) (perspective).** A Sewell structure is not a perspective in
  any of the term's senses: it is a schema-resource set, virtual on one side
  and material on the other, and he denies that schemas alone are
  structure. The nearest object in the term's vocabulary note is a kind
  (the schema, transposable across carriers) coupled to a realization (the
  resources in one place). His transposition is how a kind acquires a new
  realization.
- **THEORY warranted?** None filed. C1–C5 are a conceptual framework and
  C6 is a conjecture with three illustrations; neither is a finding the
  record can hold as a claim about the world.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- Conceptual throughout. The examples (factories, the Mass, potlatch,
  cavalry charges) show the concepts can describe cases; nothing shows they
  predict any.
- "Depth" is defined by pervasiveness and taken-for-grantedness, but no
  measure is offered, and C6's comparisons (Britain and France, Costa Rica
  and its neighbours) are listed, not analysed.
- The critique of Bourdieu rests on Outline (1977); later Bourdieu and
  Wacquant's restatement ([LIT-1377](../literature.d/LIT-1377.md)) answer some of it.
- Transposition makes every competent person an agent; the essay does not
  say what would count as a schema that failed to transpose because of the
  schema rather than the situation.

## Open questions

- When is a transformed structure the same structure transformed, and
  when a new one? Sewell says transformations are "recognizable as
  transformations of the old" (p. 27) and does not say by what.
- Can depth be measured independently of durability, so that C6 is not
  circular?
- In the polysemy of resources, what decides which interpretation of a
  success prevails? The cavalry example names two readings and no
  mechanism for choosing between them.
