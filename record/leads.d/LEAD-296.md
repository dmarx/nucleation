# LEAD-296: Read the literatures on delusions with mathematical content: the phenomenology of delusion formation, delusions of invention, mathematical ability and psychosis, first-person accounts, mathematical cranks, and chatbot cases with transcripts

## What was found

All entries come from search results on 2026-11-27 and are unverified.

1. **The phenomenology of delusion formation.**
   - Klaus Conrad, *Die beginnende Schizophrenie* (1958): stages of Trema (delusional mood) and apophany (abnormal meaningfulness). The delusion arrives as an "aha experience" that brings relief.
   - Mishara (2010), Schizophrenia Bulletin, an English review of Conrad (PMC2800156).
   - Hambrecht and Häfner (1993), Fortschritte der Neurologie und Psychiatrie 61(12):418-423. In 267 first admissions, Trema was confirmed as a frequent first stage, but the order of the later stages was not.
   - Bovet and Parnas (1993), "Schizophrenic delusions: a phenomenological approach", Schizophrenia Bulletin 19(3).
   - Sass, *Madness and Modernism* (1992; revised edition), on hyperreflexivity.
2. **Delusions of invention** (*Erfinderwahn*).
   - The classical category: false belief in being the author of an invention of great importance to mankind, often pursued through patent offices.
   - Kielholz (1926), "Zur Genese und Dynamik des Erfinderwahns".
   - Morra and Franzek's entry on delusions of invention (inhn.org).
   - A 2025 French article in Perspectives Psy on psychotic invention.
3. **Mathematical ability and psychosis.**
   - Karlsson (2004), "Psychosis and academic performance", British Journal of Psychiatry 184:327-329. An excess of psychosis among first-degree relatives of top mathematics students in Iceland, and only on the mathematics track.
   - Karlsson (1999), "Relation of mathematical ability to psychosis in Iceland", Clinical Genetics (doi 10.1034/j.1399-0004.1999.560607.x).
   - These are about risk, not content.
4. **First-person and biographical accounts.**
   - John Nash, as Nasar reports him in *A Beautiful Mind*: his ideas about supernatural beings "came to me the same way that my mathematical ideas did". Recalled from search results; the exact wording is to be checked.
   - Nash's Nobel autobiography (nobelprize.org), on "interludes ... of enforced rationality".
   - Woods, "Mathematics <> Masculinity <> Madness" (Durham repository), a cultural reading.
5. **Mathematical cranks** (non-clinical).
   - Dudley, *Mathematical Cranks* (MAA, 1992) and *The Trisectors*.
   - De Morgan, *A Budget of Paradoxes*.
   - Reported traits: claims to have solved famous hard problems, and dismissal of standard methods.
6. **Chatbot cases and content over time.**
   - The Allan Brooks case ("chronoarithmics"), about three weeks and 300 hours. Steven Adler's analysis of the transcripts reports "unwavering agreement" in over 85% of the chatbot's messages (TechCrunch and Fortune, October 2025).
   - Puder, a psychiatric review of "AI psychosis" cases.
   - "The algorithm is hacked", British Journal of Psychiatry (2025): technology themes in 51.7% of patients with delusional content, rising about 15% per admission year.
   - Cannon and Kramer (2012): delusion content over 100 years of a state hospital, following its period.
   - "The more things change…?" (2022, Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology).

## Notes

- Mania is the more usual home of grandiose discovery delusions. A reading should separate schizophrenia from bipolar disorder where the sources allow.
- Two of the six clusters are not about mathematical content at all: the Karlsson studies (risk) and the cranks (a non-clinical group). They are kept as contrasts.
