# NOTE-1664: Andrews, Pritchett & Woolcock — Escaping capability traps through problem-driven iterative adaptation

## Contribution

The paper names a mechanism by which external support degrades, rather
than merely stands in for, a recipient's own capability. Support is
conditioned on reforms that can be seen and scored in a short time, so it
selects for the adoption of forms and crowds out local search for what
works. It then sets out PDIA, four principles each set against mainstream
practice, as the way capability is built rather than transferred, and
draws a boundary: donor-led projects work for logistical tasks and for
reducing government, and fail at building implementation capability.

## Key insight

Capability is a property of agents, organizations and their ecosystem
found by local search, and evaluation keyed to form rewards form. When
support flows to "the tendency to introduce reforms that enhance an
entity's external legitimacy and support, even when they do not
demonstrably improve performance" (p. 2), the organization's legitimacy no
longer depends on what it does, and the incentive to build function goes.

## Assumptions

- Capability belongs to the human system (agents, organizations and the
  ecosystem around them; Figure 1), not to physical stock.
- Agents respond to the legitimacy and funding incentives they face.
- The right "technology" for an implementation-intensive task is unknown
  ex ante and must be found by search ("crawling the design space", p. 14
  note 10), so it cannot be transplanted.
- Diffusion needs understanding shared by the distributed implementers,
  which edict does not produce.

## Key results

- **The trap (p. 2).** "'capability traps': a dynamic in which
  governments constantly adopt “reforms” to ensure ongoing flows of
  external financing and legitimacy yet never actually improve."
- **External flow (abstract).** "the flow of development resources and
  legitimacy without demonstrated improvements in performance undermines
  the impetus for effective action to build state capability"; capability
  "stagnates, or even deteriorates, over long periods of time even though
  governments remain engaged in developmental rhetoric and continue to
  receive development resources."
- **Repeated projects (p. 1).** The sixth in a string of education
  projects; "all of the five previous projects over a span of twenty years
  had also sought to build both schools and ministry capability, and had
  succeeded at only one of those objectives."
- **Afghanistan (p. 1).** The United States is "almost certainly leaving
  behind a state less capable than what Afghanistan had in the 1970s." An
  assertion, not a measurement.
- **Four conditions (p. 2).** Traps arise from interventions that
  "reproduce particular external solutions considered 'best practice'",
  "through pre-determined linear processes", with tight monitoring of
  inputs and compliance, "driven from the top down".
- **An equilibrium (p. 5).** "once the “capability trap” is sprung there
  is no incentive—and often no possibility—for any one organization or
  leader or front-line agent to break out."
- **Conditionality as driver (pp. 5–6).** Reform initiatives "are now,
  ironically, among the drivers of capability traps"; since the 1980s
  funds have been tied to ex ante commitments to reform "open to visible
  evaluation in relatively short time periods"; scripts such as the WGI,
  Doing Business and PEFA "have essentially closed the space for novelty".
- **Crowding out (p. 7).** In procurement reform in Liberia and
  Afghanistan, external role players "crowd out potential contributions
  local agents might make to change", and local agents "are rewarded so
  handsomely for complying with externally mandated 'forms' (appearances)".
- **Why transplants fail (p. 13).** Imposed answers "are unlikely to
  address all of the problem dimensions needing attention", and new to a
  context they lack political acceptance and everyday capacity.
- **Small steps (pp. 13–14).** Small steps "are relatively cheap and have
  the prospect of early success, or quick wins" (p. 13); "Stepwise reforms
  contribute to building capacity and loosening these constraints over
  time" (p. 14). The solutions are hybrid, bricolage.
- **Learning loops (pp. 15–16).** Lessons "make the biggest difference
  when immediately incorporated into the design discussions about change",
  against evaluation that yields "'lessons' only at the end of a project".
  The worked case is hypothetical: a one-month, district-bounded
  textbook-delivery database with daily feedback.
- **Diffusion (p. 18).** Citing Jin, Kim and Srivastava (1998), "higher
  levels of decision centrality in institutional change processes yield
  lower rates of intra-organizational diffusion".
- **The boundary (pp. 19–20).** For a "logistical" task, scaling a known
  technology of low implementation intensity, "donor projects nearly
  always succeed" (p. 19); the approach founders where tasks need "(a)
  enormous numbers of discretionary decisions and (b) extensive and
  intensive face-to-face transactions" (p. 20) by agents resisting
  temptation with technology unknown ex ante.
- **Results-based aid (p. 21).** Cash on Delivery aid "would disburse
  against outcomes, however achieved". Mentioned as kindred, not
  evaluated.
- **Conclusion (p. 21).** The standard approach yields "unimplemented
  laws, unfunded agencies, and unused processes"; PDIA is "an ongoing
  process" (p. 22) whose accounts the authors are still gathering.

## Concepts

- **Isomorphic mimicry** (after DiMaggio and Powell 1983): adopting the
  forms of successful organizations, without their function, for external
  legitimacy and support.
- **Capability trap**: a low-level equilibrium of mimicry across agents,
  organizations and the ecosystem, kept up by external flows.
- **Form and function**: what organizations "look like" against what they
  "do" (p. 7).
- **PDIA**: problem-driven; an authorizing environment that permits
  positive deviance; tight iterative feedback; broad engagement.
- **Authorizing environment** (Moore 1995): the domain over which managers
  have formal decision authority.
- **Positive deviance** (Marsh et al. 2004): locally found practice that
  outperforms the norm.
- **Bricolage**: hybrid solutions made of what is at hand.
- **Logistical against implementation-intensive tasks** (after Pritchett
  and Woolcock 2004).

## Connections

It rests on the companion, Pritchett, Woolcock & Andrews, "Looking Like a

for mimicry, and on Lindblom, Kingdon, Heifetz, Moore and Ostrom for
incremental, authorized search. PDIA cites Ostrom (2005, 2008) on rules
found by experimentation and small adjustments, the account the record

Pettersson & van de Walle (2006) on aid dependency and state building,
Moore (2004) on revenue and state formation, and Ostrom, Gibson,
Shivakumar & Andersson (2002) on aid incentives and sustainability.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md) (supplied maintenance).** Qualifies it, and in one respect
  goes beyond it. The claim's supply is a neutral repair term. Here supply
  is conditioned on form, so it feeds appearance while the organization's
  own repair weakens, which is worse than a share of supply in the total.
  The claim covers only systems that make none of their own parts, and
  states are not that case. The paper has no evidence on withdrawal, so
  the decay after supply stops is left open; the Afghanistan remark is an
  assertion, not a measured decay.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md) (independence from the founding intervention).** A
  partial answer: independence comes as the organization learns to solve
  its own problems under local authorization with broad engagement, rather
  than complying with external forms. No threshold and no timing.
- **[QUESTION-399](../questions.d/QUESTION-399.md) (self-maintaining nucleus).** A mechanism, asserted:
  problem-solving with tight feedback builds capability that loosens
  constraints over time, and small wins build political support. Nothing
  is measured.
- **[QUESTION-386](../questions.d/QUESTION-386.md) (funding by observed activity).** Strongly relevant.
  Funding keyed to observable, short-horizon inputs and compliance selects
  for form and loses function (pp. 6–7). Results-based aid is offered as
  the alternative, unevaluated.
- **[CLAIM-566](../claims.d/CLAIM-566.md) (assemble, persist, regenerate).** Supports the distinction:
  mimicry assembles forms (laws, agencies) that do not persist as
  function, "unimplemented laws, unfunded agencies, and unused processes"
  (p. 21).
- **[CLAIM-516](../claims.d/CLAIM-516.md) (production by others needs a cycle).** Consistent:
  capability supplied by external agents alone does not persist, and the
  diffusion argument puts distributed local agents inside the producing
  loop. No formal model.
- **[CLAIM-273](../claims.d/CLAIM-273.md), [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md), [QUESTION-085](../questions.d/QUESTION-085.md) (supplied against self-maintained
  order).** Analogy only. Mimicry is order imported from an outside design
  and held by an outside flow; the paper adds that such order can look
  complete while lacking function.
- **[CLAIM-558](../claims.d/CLAIM-558.md) (capabilities decay with disuse).** Weakly consistent:
  "unused processes" is the image, with no decay data.
- **[CLAIM-598](../claims.d/CLAIM-598.md) (uniform abundance).** A loose analogy: more aid under
  form-keyed evaluation rewards the wrong step. The paper does not frame it
  so.
- **[TERM-117](../terms.d/TERM-117.md) (productive nucleus).** Suggestive: cheap quick wins and a
  one-month pilot act as seeds that change later opportunities. No
  outcomes are measured.
- **[THEORY-859](../theory.d/THEORY-859.md) (capture through the evaluative framework).** A parallel:
  the indicator scripts close the space for novelty by defining what
  counts as reform.
- **[THEORY-429](../theory.d/THEORY-429.md) (institutions as constraints).** Frames the form and
  function distinction the paper uses.
- Filed: [THEORY-1481](../theory.d/THEORY-1481.md). No instruction for machine-learning practice;
  nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- No original data, no identification and no evaluation of PDIA, which the
  authors call "an ongoing process" (p. 22).
- The examples are anecdotal or hypothetical; the textbook-procurement
  case is invented and the Afghanistan claim is unmeasured.
- Silent on own revenue and taxation, aid volume, exit and tapering and
  fiscal dependence. It is about aid's conditionality and evaluation
  regime, not aid dependence as such.
- The time a trap lasts comes from the companion paper, not this one.
- Read in the working-paper version; the journal text may differ.
