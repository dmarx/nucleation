# CASE-128: The voluntary delegation: a person gives a friend the password to a distracting application during a calm period and is refused it later under temptation, while another person lives under the same restriction imposed without consent

## The case

A18: "A person knows that impulsive late-night decisions often undermine
an important commitment. During a calm period, they arrange for a trusted
friend to hold the password to a distracting application. Later, they
feel a strong urge to use it. The friend refuses to disclose the password.
A second person is subject to the same arrangement, but an external
authority imposed it without their consent. Both experience the same
immediate restriction and may behave identically."

The harder variant: "suppose the first person arranged for the
restriction to be deliberately difficult to revoke during a predictable
temptation. Immediate revocability cannot simply be required in every
context, or precommitment becomes impossible."

It varies [CASE-013](CASE-013.md), the reluctant writer who blocks distracting websites,
by adding the second person and making the device someone else's hand.

## What it shows

A18: "This is an essential counterexample to a simplistic non-domination
theory. An agent does not cease governing itself whenever it delegates or
constrains a constituent process." The questions are "who authorized the
arrangement, under what conditions it can be reconsidered, and whether the
delegation remains answerable to its justifying purposes."

[CLAIM-344](../claims.d/CLAIM-344.md) already holds that resolve keeps a temporally extended policy
effective while keeping the capacity to revise it. This case shows the
capacity to revise can be deliberately placed outside the moment of
temptation and still be the person's. [CLAIM-362](../claims.d/CLAIM-362.md) already says that
non-domination is "Not a ban on override", and admits override that "is
bounded, accountable, and capable of giving way to stable cooperation".
The case is not set against it. What it adds is a source of warrant
that claim does not name: the agent's own earlier authorization, which
may be made deliberately hard to revoke in the moment, so "capable of
giving way" cannot mean immediately revocable. Set beside the same
restriction imposed without consent, it also shows that the two cannot be
told apart by present behaviour, only by who authorized the arrangement
and whether it stays answerable. That is a history, which is where
[CLAIM-364](../claims.d/CLAIM-364.md) says the difference must be read.

## What it does not show

Where legitimate precommitment ends. A18: "A good theory must distinguish
legitimate precommitment from a restriction that has become unaccountable
to the purposes for which it was adopted." The case poses that line and
does not draw it. Bénabou and Tirole's personal rules ([LIT-1389](../literature.d/LIT-1389.md)) and
Ainslie's bargaining interests ([THEORY-067](../theory.d/THEORY-067.md)) are the existing accounts it
would be tested against.

## Note of 2026-10-31: the collective mirror, which separates authorizing an arrangement from attributing its result

Bencherki's daycare (2017; [LIT-1901](../literature.d/LIT-1901.md), pp. 791–792; [THEORY-1285](../theory.d/THEORY-1285.md)) is
a collective case that varies this one along an axis it does not have.
The spouses consent to the arrangement: "If I volunteered, my daughter
could go to daycare for free". Nothing is imposed on them in the way
this case's second person's restriction is. The wrong Bencherki finds is
in what follows. The army presents the result as "one of the services we
offer" and the spouses' work "goes unacknowledged, unrecorded, and
unpaid". So an arrangement can be authorized and its result still
misattributed. This case asks "who authorized the arrangement". The
daycare adds a second question: to whom its result is credited. The
checklist in the same paper (pp. 789–790), presented as the army's
service with the community named as its maker, is the contrast with
attribution intact.

It is a second-hand reading of a documentary (p. 785), and it shows the
distinction, not where either line falls. No status, title or warrant
changes.

## Note of 2026-11-12: spending limits, stimulators and advance consent, from "Medical Ethics and Media Enshitification"

The exchange (2026-11-11) applies this case three times. A25 §6 applies it
to a player's self-set spending limit, "A person who voluntarily establishes
a spending constraint may be exercising autonomy, not relinquishing it",
where the question is whether the software supports the authorized
constraint. A21 §1 applies it to adaptive deep brain stimulation, an
external regulator the patient authorized ([LIT-tmp6oofp](../literature.d/LIT-tmp6oofp.md), unread). A9 §5
applies it to advance directives. In each the case's contrast between the
delegated and the imposed restriction carries over. No change.

## Note of 2026-11-12: Muñoz et al. (2020), read

From the reading in [NOTE-tmpc3zs5](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpc3zs5.md) ([LIT-tmplfdsz](../literature.d/LIT-tmplfdsz.md)), the reader's assessment as
checked by the filer: result 4 (an always-available patient override) and
result 5 (too much patient control can undermine autonomy) are the clinical
form of the case's harder variant, that "Immediate revocability cannot
simply be required in every context". Supports the case's point that the
authorization and revisability of the arrangement, not present control,
carry the difference.
