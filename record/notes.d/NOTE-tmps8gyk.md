  A secondary analysis pooling six open-access survey datasets (found
  through structured literature searches before 28 March 2020) with
  self-reported monthly loot-box spend and Problem Gambling Severity Index
  (PGSI) scores, three of them also with self-reported earnings. After
  removing outliers (> $1000/month spend, > $250,000 earnings), 7,771
  loot-box purchasers remain (5,933 with earnings). Spend correlates with
  problem-gambling score (Spearman ρ = .33), not with earnings (ρ = .02, p =
  .10); the top ~5% of spenders (> $100/month) account for over half of
  reported loot-box spending and about a third of high spenders are classed
  as problem gamblers. The authors conclude developers are
  "disproportionately profiting" from at-risk players rather than wealthy
  ones, and draw policy implications (legislation, spending limits).

# NOTE-tmps8gyk: Close and others 2021 — Secondary analysis of loot box data: Are high-spending “whales” wealthy gamers or problem gamblers?

## Contribution

The first pooling of loot-box survey data to set spending concentration against both problem-gambling scores and earnings, testing the industry framing of "whales" as affluent players.

## Key results

- Abstract/Results: "the top 5 % of spenders (> $100/month) represent over half of loot box revenue." (Discussion: "represent half of loot box revenue"; Results: "nearly a third of revenue (in our dataset) is derived from the top 2% of purchasers.")
- Results: "The combined data, comprising 7,771 loot box purchasers, establishes a significant aggregate (Spearman’s rho) correlation between problem gambling score and loot box expenditure, ρ = .33, p < .001." Robust to age/gender control (ρ = .32, n = 6950) and to keeping outliers (ρ = .34).
- Results: Kruskal-Wallis effect of spend category on PGSI, H(4) = 273.24, p < .001; high spenders' PGSI "considerably higher", "almost one third falling into the ‘problem gambler’ category".
- Results: "there is no evidence in our dataset (n = 5,933 with self-report earnings) that higher loot box spend is correlated with higher earnings, ρ = .02, p = .10".
- Discussion: "High-spending ‘whales’ do not have higher earnings."
- Discussion, citing Zendle's CS:GO transaction data: "over half of loot box spending is attributable to around 10% of spenders".
- Preprint Figure 1 caption (not in the accepted manuscript's text layer): "around 53% of revenue ... is derived from approximately the top 5% spenders".

## Assumptions

- Self-reported spend and earnings; categorical answers converted at bin midpoints; outliers trimmed.
- Samples treated as independent though "complete independence of each sample cannot be guaranteed" (two from Amazon Mechanical Turk).
- "Revenue" is the sample's summed self-reported spending, a proxy for industry revenue, not company data.
- Bivariate Spearman correlations; no causal identification (spending could lead to, follow, or share causes with problem gambling).

## Concepts

Whales; spending concentration (cumulative revenue share); PGSI; loot boxes as gambling-like mechanisms; gaming–gambling convergence; harm minimisation.

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** A's account is right in substance: concentration of spending in the top ~5% (about half) and spending tracking problem-gambling severity, not earnings. Two precisions: the count 7,767 is the preprint's (the published manuscript has 7,771); and "half of reported spending" is of self-reported spend summed across pooled survey respondents, not of any company's revenue.

- **[CLAIM-tmpmz271](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpmz271.md)** (activity- or revenue-weighted demand diverges from person-weighted preference; a responsive minority can set design): **supports the premise, empirically**: revenue really is concentrated in a small minority (5% of purchasers ≈ half of spend), so revenue-weighted demand is dominated by them. It does **not** test the second half (that the minority's marginal response moves design); the paper has no design variable.
- **[CASE-tmpmw3hc](../cases.d/CASE-tmpmw3hc.md)** (proposed loot-box study): **supports its motivating premise** (the high-spender group exists and carries economic weight) and characterises that group (problem-gambling severity, not wealth), which bears on H3 (endogenous high-spender demand) only as association. It gives no evidence on H1/H2 (design drift tracking high spenders). Its methods note (self-report, pooled, no company data) is a direct argument for the case's player-level transaction data.
- **[CLAIM-tmpcv1pg](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpcv1pg.md)**: leaves open; the analogue for loot boxes of "heaviest users" is confirmed to exist, not that they set the product.

## Limitations

Self-report surveys, pooled heterogeneous samples, cross-sectional correlations; revenue concentration is in-sample; the figure (spend bins vs PGSI and earnings) was seen only in the preprint version.
