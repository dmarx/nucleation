# LIT-tmptn3yr: Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA)

Andrews, M., Pritchett, L. & Woolcock, M. (2013). Escaping Capability Traps Through Problem Driven Iterative Adaptation (PDIA). *World Development* 51: 234–244. doi:10.1016/j.worlddev.2013.05.011. First issued as CGD Working Paper 299 (June 2012) and HKS Faculty Research Working Paper RWP12-036 (August 2012).

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmpfpey1](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpfpey1.md). Page numbers are the working paper's, not the journal's.*

- **The trap.** "a dynamic in which governments constantly adopt
  “reforms” to ensure ongoing flows of external financing and legitimacy
  yet never actually improve" (p. 2). Isomorphic mimicry is "the tendency
  to introduce reforms that enhance an entity's external legitimacy and
  support, even when they do not demonstrably improve performance" (p. 2).
- **External flow undermines own capability.** "the flow of development
  resources and legitimacy without demonstrated improvements in
  performance undermines the impetus for effective action to build state
  capability" (abstract). Conditionality tied to reforms "open to visible
  evaluation in relatively short time periods" (p. 6) has "essentially
  closed the space for novelty" (p. 6); external players "crowd out
  potential contributions local agents might make to change" (p. 7).
- **No one can leave alone.** Once the trap is sprung "there is no
  incentive—and often no possibility—for any one organization or leader or
  front-line agent to break out" (p. 5).
- **Capability is built, not transferred.** By stepwise, locally nominated
  problem-solving under authorization, with lessons fed back at once and
  broad engagement for diffusion: "Stepwise reforms contribute to building
  capacity and loosening these constraints over time" (p. 14).
- **A boundary.** For a "logistical" task "donor projects nearly always
  succeed" (p. 19); they founder on tasks needing "(a) enormous numbers of
  discretionary decisions and (b) extensive and intensive face-to-face
  transactions" (p. 20).
