# NOTE-tmpzh69c: Perzanowski and Schultz — The End of Ownership

## Contribution

It brings together in one book the authors' law-review work on "digital
exhaustion" and the "Buy Now" survey (Acknowledgments). It tells one story
across media, software, devices and patents: rights that buyers held by
default as owners of copies are moving to rights holders through licences,
DRM and the courts. Its question is put on p. 20: "do we own our digital
goods?" It answers that, for the most part, we do not, and that we should.
It adds a legal test for when a buyer is an owner (pp. 76–77), a test for
the scope of digital exhaustion (pp. 179–183), and survey evidence that
buyers are misled (ch. 5).

## Key insight

Copyright protects buyers through rules about copies. Exhaustion gives the
owner of a lawful copy the rights to resell, lend, display and, for
software, run and back it up (pp. 25–26, 63–64). It rests on a sharp line
between the work and the copy (pp. 36–38). Digital distribution breaks the
line in two ways. A transfer makes a new copy, so the first sale doctrine
does not cover it (*ReDigi*, pp. 40–41). A cloud or streamed purchase may
leave no copy the buyer holds (pp. 43–48). Retailers then fill the gap with
licences that deny a sale and with DRM that enforces the licence. The
authors' fix is to stop counting copies: "Copies today are cheap,
disposable things." (p. 46). What should matter is "who has the right to
use and transfer it" (p. 188).

## Assumptions

- **US law, early 2016.** The cases and statutes are American: the
  Copyright Act §§ 109, 117 and 1201, the Lanham and FTC Acts, and the
  patent cases to *Lexmark v. Impression Products* (Fed. Cir., February
  2016). The EU appears only as a foil (pp. 175–176, 184–185).
- **The RAM-copy rule as live risk, not settled law.** "Some courts" treat
  a copy in memory as a copy (p. 46, p. 63). The authors rely on the risk
  rather than the rule, and cite Perzanowski's own article against it
  (ch. 4 n. 26). *Cablevision* (1.2 seconds is not a copy) is set against
  *MAI v. Peak* (p. 47).
- **Property is made by law.** Property rights are "a product of law"
  (p. 192), defined by who wins "a contest for control over the behavior of
  others" (p. 23). The authors favour the bundle-of-rights view but want a
  short, standard menu of transactions for consumer goods (pp. 21–22).
- **Information costs justify standard forms.** The case against bespoke
  licences rests on the *numerus clausus* argument (Merrill and Smith) that
  idiosyncratic rights impose costs on third parties (pp. 8–9, 17). It is
  argued, not measured.
- **Consumer expectations are evidence, not a ground of right.** The
  survey shows what buyers believe. The authors say plainly that this does
  not settle what rights they should have: "Rights—property,
  constitutional, or human—need a firmer foundation." (p. 98).

## Key results

The results are arguments from cases, statutes and one survey. In the
book's order:

- **The stakes (ch. 1).** Amazon's remote deletion of *1984*, Linn
  Nygaard's wiped Kindle and Scholastic's Storia show purchases revoked
  (pp. 1–3). Licensing costs buyers resale, lending, permanence and privacy
  (pp. 6–7). It also imposes costs on others: information costs, lost
  preservation, less user innovation and higher switching costs (pp. 8–10).
  The authors write that "the most fundamental value at stake in the choice
  between ownership and licensing is autonomy" (p. 10). They argue that
  ownership is also the carrot that makes lawful copies worth buying
  (p. 12).
- **Property and exhaustion (ch. 2).** Personal property admits no
  servitudes. Restrictions do not "run with the tuxedo" (pp. 16–17).
  Rowling's goblins, for whom the maker remains the owner, are the
  imagined alternative (p. 17). Exhaustion runs from nineteenth-century
  cases (*Clemens v. Estes* on *Huckleberry Finn*, bound fire-damaged
  pages, Kipling's loose sheets) through *Bobbs-Merrill v. Straus* (1908)
  to the 1909 Act and *Kirtsaeng* (2013) (pp. 26–29). Rights holders have
  resisted it in every medium. GameStop says 70 percent of trade-in value
  goes to new games (p. 31). The authors favour exhaustion "not because
  property is a label with talismanic properties, but because it is smart
  policy" (p. 33).
- **Copies, clouds and streams (ch. 3).** The work/copy distinction runs
  from *Pope v. Curl* (1741) to § 202 of the 1976 Act (p. 37). *ReDigi*
  held that a forward-and-delete transfer still reproduces the work
  (p. 41). Section 117 extended exhaustion to software copies, but no
  equivalent exists for other media (p. 42). A cloud copy might be like a
  cinema's film reel, a library book or a safe-deposit heirloom, and only
  the first two fit copyright's vocabulary (p. 44). *White-Smith v. Apollo*
  (1908), where piano rolls were not copies, shows the law failing to find
  a copy before (p. 47). On ownership and identity, buying an album
  "identifies you, both to others and to yourself" as a fan, and the
  endowment effect holds for digital goods (p. 53).
- **The fine print (ch. 4).** Licences are "privately made law" (p. 58).
  The iTunes terms run past 19,000 words (p. 59). Perhaps one software
  shopper in a thousand looks at them (p. 60), so licences are a market for
  lemons. Record labels call iTunes downloads sales when royalties are due
  (p. 62). Since *ProCD*, courts enforce terms buyers see only after paying
  (pp. 67–71). On the permission view the GPL takes, a licence cannot take
  back what the Copyright Act gives owners (pp. 71–74). *UMG v. Augusto*
  looked at the facts of distribution and *Vernor v. Autodesk* at the
  licence's words, on near-identical terms (pp. 75–76). The authors'
  test for a sale is duration, one-time payment and the public
  characterization of the deal (pp. 76–77). Price discrimination, which
  licences enable, aims to take the whole consumer surplus (p. 80).
- **The "Buy Now" survey (ch. 5).** It sampled nearly 1,300 likely buyers
  on a mock store, MediaShop (p. 90). Only 14 of the 956 shown a digital
  product opened the terms (p. 92). Nearly nine in ten thought they could
  keep the purchase as long as they liked. More than 40 percent thought
  they could lend or give it away, and nearly 30 percent that they could
  leave it in a will (pp. 93–94). On resale the figures were 12 percent for
  books and 17 for music and movies (p. 95). This was the only right not
  well above the legal threshold, though the authors argue it still
  suffices. Preferences for these rights were stable across digital and
  physical formats. Respondents would pay on average $3.82 more to lend
  and $3.24 to resell (pp. 96–97). On a seven-point accuracy score, the
  short notice averaged 3.0, "Buy Now" 2.45, "License Now" 2.27 and
  physical goods 4.7 (pp. 100–101).
- **Libraries (ch. 6).** Publishers impose "artificial friction":
  HarperCollins's 26-loan cap, delays, caps and per-loan pricing (pp.
  106–109). OverDrive serves over 90 percent of the library ebook market
  (p. 108). A Dudamel recording sold only on iTunes could not be lent at
  all (p. 109). Ownership underwrote preservation, patron privacy and the
  HathiTrust fair-use win (pp. 111–118). It also underwrote Friends of the
  Library book sales (pp. 118–119).
- **DRM (ch. 7).** DRM enforces terms no legislature wrote: "We've
  replaced courts and due process with code and license terms." (p. 123).
  It fails by Godwin's "smart cow" problem (p. 125). After *Sony*, *Vault
  v. Quaid* and *Sega v. Accolade* (pp. 126–129), the DMCA's § 1201 banned
  circumvention and circumvention tools (pp. 130–131). In *Corley*,
  "Breaking DRM was unlawful, regardless of the reason." (p. 132). DRM
  locked labels into iTunes until they dropped it (pp. 133–134). The
  Sony BMG rootkit compromised about two million computers (pp. 135–137).
  *Chamberlain* (Federal Circuit) and *Lexmark* (Sixth Circuit) refused to extend § 1201 to
  garage door openers and printer cartridges (pp. 137–138).
- **The Internet of Things (ch. 8).** Nest bricked the Revolv hub despite
  a "lifetime subscription" (pp. 140–141). Apple told the Copyright Office
  that iPhone users are licensees of iOS (p. 143). John Deere said farmers
  have "an implied license for the life of the vehicle to operate the
  vehicle" (p. 145). Further cases are GM, Mercedes, Ford's speed limiter,
  Ferrari's resale option, Keurig 2.0 and Hello Barbie, whose recordings
  ToyTalk may use (pp. 146–151). Pacemaker and insulin-pump owners may not
  read their own devices (pp. 152–154).
- **Patents (ch. 9).** Patent exhaustion runs from *Bloomer v. McQuewan*
  (1852, "the ordinary pursuits of life") through *Adams v. Burke*,
  *Keeler*, *Straus v. Victor* and *Motion Picture Patents* (1917) (pp.
  156–159). Edison's agents built the first electric chairs from
  second-hand Westinghouse dynamos because the patents were exhausted
  (pp. 157–158). The Federal Circuit's *Mallinckrodt* (1992) allowed
  conditional sales, and *Jazz Photo* (2001) national exhaustion. *Quanta*
  (2008) and *Kirtsaeng* undercut both, but the en banc *Lexmark v.
  Impression Products* (2016) reaffirmed them (pp. 160–168).
- **Reforms (ch. 10).** The "sharing economy" is mostly for-profit
  temporary access, and real sharing presupposes ownership: "You usually
  can't lend something that you don't own." (p. 170). The proposals are
  (pp. 174–185):
  1. An FTC inquiry into "Buy Now" and short notices.
  2. Unconscionability, the EU Unfair Terms Directive and copyright
     preemption against form contracts.
  3. Owners exempted from § 1201, which could still enforce rentals and
     subscriptions.
  4. A return to patent exhaustion.
  5. A digital exhaustion test. A one-time payment for open-ended access
     makes the buyer an owner. The use is then weighed by whether the owner
     gave up access, whether it denies the rights holder a fair return, and
     whether the owner altered the expression. A perfectly efficient
     lending pool shows why the second factor is needed (p. 182).
  *UsedSoft* (CJEU) and the Dutch Tom Kabinet rulings show resale working
  in Europe (pp. 184–185). Mulligan's proposal of "taking the copy out of
  copyright altogether" is called promising but too broad (p. 185).
  Forward-and-delete schemes and the Amazon and Apple resale patents are
  rejected as private, platform-bound and DRM-dependent (pp. 185–188). A
  blockchain ledger is offered as a title registry for "bitproperty"
  (pp. 188–191).

## Concepts

- **Exhaustion.** The rule that a rights holder gives up control of an
  item once it is sold or given away by its authority. In copyright the
  main form is the first sale doctrine (§ 109); others are display
  (§ 109(c)) and software copies (§ 117).
- **Copy/work distinction.** The work is the author's intangible
  expression; the copy is the material object that fixes it. Copyright's
  exhaustion rules give buyers the copy and leave the work with the
  author (pp. 36–38).
- **Licence as contract / licence as permission.** On the contract view, a
  licence binds by assent, which courts now find in clicks and inaction.
  On the permission view, a licence is a property owner's grant, needs no
  assent, and works only within the licensor's existing rights (pp. 64–74).
- **Essential step copy.** A copy needed to run software, allowed to the
  owner of a copy by § 117(a)(1) (p. 63).
- **Artificial friction.** Limits imposed on ebook lending to mimic the
  costs of physical books (pp. 106–107).
- **Smart cow problem.** Mike Godwin's name for the fact that one person
  who breaks a lock frees everyone else (p. 125).
- **Short notice.** A plain, iconographic statement of the rights a buyer
  gets and does not get (pp. 99–100).
- **Temporary-access model.** Rentals, subscriptions and most of the
  "sharing economy". The authors accept these when they are clearly
  labelled (pp. 169–173).
- **Bitproperty.** Fairfield's term for property interests decoupled from
  any tangible object (p. 191).

## Connections

It draws on the authors' law-review articles ("Digital Exhaustion",
"Copyright Exhaustion and the Personal Use Dilemma", "Legislating Digital
Exhaustion", "Reconciling Personal and Intellectual Property") and on
Perzanowski and Hoofnagle's "What We Buy When We Buy Now"
(Acknowledgments). For property theory it uses Merrill and Smith on the
*numerus clausus*, Van Houweling and Mulligan on servitudes, and Coase and
Pigou on externalities. For contracts it uses Radin's *Boilerplate*, Kim's
*Wrap Contracts* and Lemley. For DRM and lock-in it quotes Cory Doctorow's
*Information Doesn't Want to Be Free* three times (ch. 7 nn. 4, 19, 27).
It uses von Hippel's *Democratizing Innovation* for user innovation and
Fairfield for bitproperty. It does not cite Lessig, Benkler or Jenkins.
Its "sharing economy" (ch. 10) is Uber and Airbnb, not Benkler's or
Lessig's.

## Bearing on the record

- **Lessig's copy trigger (LIT-tmpreyek, NOTE-tmptp3k0).** This is the
  closest contact, and the two books agree on the mechanism. Lessig: the
  1909 Act made copyright regulate "copies", every digital use makes one,
  so the law now reaches ordinary use (pp. 97–103 of *Remix*). Perzanowski
  and Schultz show the same mechanism from the buyer's side. The rules
  that protect owners are rules about copies. A digital transfer is a new
  copy (*ReDigi*), and use may be one (the RAM-copy cases). So the buyer's
  property shrinks just as the rights holder's reach grows. The remedies
  converge too. Lessig's fourth reform is to regulate uses, not the copy.
  These authors want courts to track "who has the right to use and
  transfer" a work rather than count copies (p. 188), and they note
  Mulligan's proposal to drop the reproduction right (p. 185). They
  differ on what is at stake. Lessig's concern is read/write culture,
  making new work from old. Theirs is what Lessig would call read-only
  use: keeping, lending, reselling and repairing. They exclude remix from
  exhaustion in so many words. Exhaustion "isn't the right framework to
  analyze the legality of remixing" (p. 183). Read together, the two books
  show the copy trigger reaching both ends of the reader's relation to a
  work: the right to remake it and the right to keep it. The record has
  no claim yet that states the copy trigger as its own. If one is
  written, this book supplies the case law Lessig's sketch lacks.
- **Doctorow on platforms (LIT-731, LIT-033; THEORY-150, THEORY-470).**
  The connection is real but partial. THEORY-150 lists lock-in through
  DRM among what sustains platform decay, and THEORY-470 names DMCA
  § 1201 as what disabled the interoperability constraint. This book
  supports both steps with cases. DRM raised the cost of leaving iTunes
  until the labels abandoned it, quoting Doctorow's "roach motel"
  (pp. 133–134). § 1201 makes circumvention unlawful whether or not a use
  infringes (*Corley*, p. 132). Keurig and Lexmark used locks to tie
  consumables, as in Doctorow's HP ink example (NOTE-041). The book also
  qualifies Doctorow. Courts have sometimes required a nexus to
  infringement before § 1201 bites (*Chamberlain*, Federal Circuit;
  *Lexmark*, Sixth Circuit; pp. 137–138), so the law is less uniformly hostile to
  interoperability than NOTE-041's talk implies. The book says nothing
  about the three-stage cycle of THEORY-150, or about competition,
  regulation and worker power as THEORY-470's other constraints. It
  supports the lock-in mechanism, not the decay schema.
- **CLAIM-332 and QUESTION-119 (distributed uptake).** The book has
  incidental cases of a loose population changing a firm's conduct.
  Microsoft dropped its Xbox One resale limits after "persistent and
  overwhelmingly negative feedback from customers" (p. 32). Keurig partly
  retreated after Facebook posts, Amazon reviews and a 10 percent stock
  drop (p. 150). A petition of over 100,000 led to the unlocking law
  (p. 143). They fit CLAIM-332's pattern of separately motivated acts
  without a collective judgment. But they are chosen anecdotes, and the
  book itself says market pressure "is often only partly effective in
  protecting consumer interests" (p. 150). That is QUESTION-119's caution.
  They are not evidence for the claim.
- **CLAIM-002 (artifact identity).** The book's copy/work distinction is
  the law's version of CLAIM-002's first relation, identity of the
  artifact. The book's point is that in digital media the artifact no
  longer has a stable identity: copies "flit into and out of existence"
  (p. 46). Even "the definition of the work" becomes hard for copyright
  (p. 181). That bears on how the record uses artifact identity for
  digital texts. It says nothing about retelling or translation.
- **No bearing** on CASE-100 or CLAIM-034. The book is about consumption
  and transfer, not making new work, and excludes remix. It has no bearing
  on QUESTION-002, QUESTION-023, CLAIM-210 or CLAIM-220 either. Its
  remarks that owned things "help define who we are" (p. 53) and on Hegel
  (p. 22) are passing, and bear on nothing in the personhood line.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice, and not held by the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- **Advocacy by participants.** The authors argue one side. One of
  them represented one of the groups that found the Sony rootkit
  (p. 136), and one represented the bnetd developers (ch. 7 n. 24). One author consulted for the
  plaintiffs in the iPod antitrust case (ch. 7 n. 18). Rights holders'
  arguments are reported, but mostly to be answered.
- **The survey is the authors' own and limited.** It used a mock store and
  stated preferences and willingness to pay. Women were overrepresented
  (ch. 5 n. 49). Its full report was forthcoming at the time (ch. 5
  n. 34). That stated beliefs show deception is a legal argument no court
  has tested. The book concedes that no consumer is likely to bring the
  case (p. 174).
- **The economic claims are argued, not measured.** The externality of
  information costs, the benefit of resale to rights holders, and the harm
  of price discrimination rest on theory and industry figures. The
  "undue harm" factor of the proposed exhaustion test is left open
  (p. 182).
- **Dated by events.** These are from general knowledge and not checked in
  this session. The Supreme Court reversed the Federal Circuit in
  *Impression Products v. Lexmark* (2017), applying exhaustion to post-sale
  restrictions and to foreign sales, as ch. 9 hoped. The Second Circuit
  affirmed *ReDigi* (2018). The CJEU held in *Tom Kabinet* (2019) that
  ebook resale is not covered by exhaustion. That cuts against the book's
  European example (pp. 184–185). The blockchain chapter (pp. 188–191)
  predates the NFT episode.
- **Small errors.** "Capital Records" for Capitol (p. 41). "digital copies
  are more than occasionally more expensive than their digital
  counterparts" (p. 134) should read analog. Smaller slips occur in the
  notes.
- **The digital bundle is left uncalibrated.** The last page of the
  Conclusion (p. 193) grants that the ideal digital property bundle
  will differ from the analog one and that calibrating it will not be
  easy, and asks only for meaningful choice among rentals,
  subscriptions and ownership. Which rights the bundle holds is not
  specified.

## Open questions

- Does resale or lending of digital goods reduce rights holders' revenue
  enough to matter, and under what lending rules? The book's second
  exhaustion factor needs an answer it does not give.
- Would short notices change behaviour at purchase, or only answers in a
  survey?
- If copyright moved from counting copies to tracking rights of use, as
  both this book and Lessig propose, what would count as a use? Would
  private reading or running then fall inside copyright, as Mulligan's
  display-and-performance proposal suggests (p. 185)?
- For the record: does the loss of a stable artifact in digital media
  change what CLAIM-002's first relation can do for digital texts?
