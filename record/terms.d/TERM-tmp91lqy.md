# TERM-tmp91lqy: independent execution gate, of a process: a step past which the process cannot continue unless a condition holds that its executor does not control (a person's approval, an elapsed notice period, a check another party runs or a limit another party set), whether or not the step delays it; distinct from execution impedance (added time or cost) and from an intervention opportunity (a point at which another party can bring an objection that changes the process), and not settled as a sense of procedural friction

## The definition

A step in a process is an **independent execution gate** when the process
cannot go past it unless a condition holds that the executor of the process
does not control. The condition can be a person's act (an approval, a
signature, a hearing's outcome), the passage of time the executor cannot
shorten (a notice period), or a test another party runs or a limit another
party set (an automatic suspension on a threshold the executor cannot
change).

Three properties of a step, which vary independently:

| property | question |
|---|---|
| **execution impedance** | Does the step add time or resource cost to execution? |
| **independent constraint** (this term) | Can the executor proceed alone, or is continuation conditional on something outside its control? |
| **intervention opportunity** | Can another party bring an objection capable of changing what the process does? |

| | impedance | gate | opportunity |
|---|---|---|---|
| a review at which an approver can reject and has time | yes | yes | yes |
| a standing limit enforced by another's mechanism | no | yes | no, for what was not foreseen |
| a rubber-stamp approval (the HKMA's perfunctory approval) | yes | yes | no |
| a slow step the executor could skip | yes | no | no |
| a channel for challenging the rules that does not pause execution | no | no | yes, on the rules |

## What is left open

Whether every gate is **procedural friction** in the sense of the owner's
claim (CLAIM-tmpuiaop). If it is, A's objection (CLAIM-tmpwpvbz) removes
impedance and not friction, but "friction characterizes opportunity" then
becomes close to a definition and is false of gates that open no
opportunity. If friction is impedance, the objection stands against
"characterizes". The disagreement is kept open rather than settled by
choosing a word; CLAIM-tmpkx7ct says what holds either way.

## Neighbouring senses it must not be confused with

- **Delay.** The ordinary sense, and the one A's objection
  ([CLAIM-tmpwpvbz](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpwpvbz.md)) relies on. A delay the executor controls opens no
  opportunity to intervene.
- **A21's "extractive friction"**, time and effort imposed on those subject
  to a process without influence over it. That is a burden on others, not
  a gate on the executor, and is a gate only where the process waits on
  it.
- **Administrative burden** (Herd and Moynihan, [LIT-1609](../literature.d/LIT-1609.md) and
  [LIT-tmp1o7dq](../literature.d/LIT-tmp1o7dq.md)): costs of access borne by claimants. Again a burden, not a
  gate.
- **Mechanical friction**: the metaphor's source, energy lost to motion.
  Version 1 of this term kept only the resistance and dropped the loss;
  the review of 2026-11-16 showed that this merged resistance to the
  executor with openness to others.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Cuéllar, read

Cuéllar writes that governors and civil society "can also create friction
and slow down White House edicts" (p. 648), so he speaks of friction as
delay. His instances are dependence, though: states using "their legitimate discretion to decide not" to
cooperate, and litigation that can enjoin. Those gate the
executive and are friction in this term's sense. Mild support for defining
friction by dependence. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Hong Kong Monetary Authority, read

The HKMA's agent harness is this term's "friction without delay". The
agent cannot pass a deterministic check, and it may not modify its own
boundary controls unless the institution has explicitly approved an
arrangement for that (§7.1, pp. 35–36). The limits are held by the
institution's change control and an independent control owner (§5.3).
The paper also supplies a case the term's table does not separate out:
an approval step on which the process waits, and so friction by
dependence, that has become perfunctory. "A formal approval step is not,
by itself, meaningful human oversight." (§7.2, p. 37). Approval counts
only where the approver sees the substance, can reject, "regards rejection
as a legitimate outcome", and has time. So dependence on another's
condition is necessary on this term's reading for an opportunity to
intervene, and not sufficient: a gate can be friction and expose nothing.
This qualifies the reading of [CLAIM-tmpkx7ct](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpkx7ct.md), where "an opportunity to
intervene is a piece of friction" is close to definitional. The converse,
that a piece of friction is an opportunity, fails. Relation: none (a THEORY
cannot bound a TERM); a note.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Natali, read

The poster defines friction by inefficiency: "deliberate inefﬁciencies designed to foster engagement, critical reason[ing], and user agency", "speed bumps", "intentionally slower algorithms". That is the delay sense the term sets aside; dependence on another party is not mentioned. Two of its mechanisms are nonetheless friction in the term's sense: a pause before AI output, or a required justification before acceptance, is a condition the user cannot pass alone because the designer set it. A neighbouring sense to add: **designed (interaction) friction**, effort or delay a designer imposes on a user's own decision to make the user engage. It is friction in this term's sense only where the user cannot skip it, and then it gates the user's decision for the user's own judgement, not for another party's.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Alfrink, read

The second-system safeguard is a clean case of the term's "friction without delay": every decision's passage depends on a check another party runs, and the process waits on a human only when the check flags. The paper never calls it friction, which supports the term's point that dependence, not delay, is what such safeguards have in common with reviews and approvals.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Herd and Moynihan, read

The term classes administrative burden as "a burden, not a gate" on the executor and cites [LIT-tmp1o7dq](../literature.d/LIT-tmp1o7dq.md).
The 2023 review supports that: its burdens are conditions on the claimant's access, imposed by the agency,
and the agency's own action does not wait on them. Where a burden becomes a correction channel (EITC
audits answered by mail, "If the IRS does not receive a response, the default judgment" follows, p. 109) it
gates the claimant's remedy, not the agency. Unchanged. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Citron, read

Eligibility workers who finalize a computer's recommendation, and hearing officers who review it, are
procedural friction in this term's sense (the process waits on their act). Citron shows that such a step
can be passed in form while deciding nothing (p. 1272), which separates friction from the opportunity it
might offer. The term is unchanged. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Bovens and Zouridis, read

§2.2's superior's signature and ex ante legal review are clean instances of friction in this term's sense:
steps the executor cannot pass alone. Their disappearance in system-level bureaucracy is the term's case of
friction removed. The term is unchanged. Relation: none.
