# NOTE-tmp858eg: Personal Identity

## Contribution

The entry is the field's standard map of personal identity, and its value
is in the distinctions. It splits "personal identity" into seven questions
and shows that the persistence question is regularly confused with
characterization ("the same person" in the sense of character) and with a
narrower question that assumes we are persons essentially. It then gives
the persistence debate in its current form: the psychological view as the
majority view, fission as its central problem, and the too-many-thinkers
objection as the animalist's main argument.

## Key insight

"What makes you the same person?" is three different questions, and most
of the debate's confusion comes from answering one while asking another.
The numerical question (whether a past being is you at all) is about what
kind of thing you are, and so may not be answerable from the concept of a
person alone.

## Assumptions

- **Persistence is numerical identity**, which is transitive and one–one.
  The arguments from fission (§5) and from memory chains (§4) turn on
  this.
- **Human organisms think** if anything does. The too-many-thinkers
  objection (§6) depends on it, and the entry says the denial is "hard to
  defend".
- **Organisms have brute-physical persistence conditions**: a transplanted
  brain does not take its organism with it. "New animalism" (§6), which
  denies this, is noted.
- **Thought experiments are evidence** about our persistence conditions.
  Brain transplants, fission and duplicating machines carry the argument.

## Key results

There are no formal results. The entry's structure, in order:

- **§1, seven questions.** Characterization ("Who am I?", a "small subset"
  of one's properties, contingent and changeable; "the characterization
  question (Schechtman 1996: 1)"), personhood (Locke's "thinking
  intelligent being" definition as the commonest answer), persistence,
  evidence (memory versus physical continuity; Williams's Guy Fawkes case),
  population (split brains, multiple personality, partial twinning),
  personal ontology (seven candidate answers from animalism to "we don't
  really exist", with "no consensus or even a dominant view") and what
  matters in survival.
- **§2, the persistence question.** Not a question about meaning, which
  would make the answer a priori and the same for all possible people. Not
  "remaining the same person", which "presupposes" numerical identity.
  Framing it as "a person at one time and a person at another" assumes
  person essentialism, which with a Lockean definition of person implies
  you were never an embryo and could not be in a vegetative state. The
  memory criterion, so framed, is "compatible with your surviving with no
  memory continuity at all, as long as this happens when you are not a
  person".
- **§3, three answers.** Psychological continuity, brute-physical
  continuity, anticriterialism.
- **§4, psychological continuity.** The memory criterion fails
  transitivity (the student, the lawyer and the old woman) and is
  circular (genuine memory presupposes identity). Quasi-memory answers the
  circularity. Causal dependence (Shoemaker) answers dreamless sleep:
  beliefs "have caused themselves to continue existing". Whether
  brain-state transfer counts is left open (Shoemaker yes, Unger no).
- **§5, fission.** Lefty and Righty cannot both be you. Multiple occupancy
  (two people coincide before the operation, usually with temporal parts)
  or non-branching (fission is death). Non-branching makes survival depend
  on whether a second hemisphere is destroyed, which the entry calls
  "mysterious", and it is why Parfit and others say identity is not what
  matters.
- **§6, too many thinkers.** If you are not your organism, two beings think
  your thoughts and you cannot know which you are, as after a duplicating
  machine. Reply one: organisms cannot think (threatens to make them
  zombies). Reply two: only persons, defined to persist psychologically,
  are referred to by "I". Reply three: new animalism.
- **§7, animalism.** That we are organisms. Not an account of personhood,
  and consistent with "wholly inorganic people such as gods or intelligent
  robots". It fits every actual case of survival and our having been
  foetuses. The transplant intuition is the main cost, weakened by
  Snowdon's tumour case.
- **§8, wider themes.** Temporal-parts ontology yields many candidate
  referents for "I" (a continuity-aggregate, a connectedness-aggregate,
  an organism, a stage), so reference and therefore what we are may be
  indeterminate (Sider).

## Concepts

- **persistence question** — what is necessary and sufficient for a past
  or future being to be someone existing now (numerical identity over
  time).
- **characterization question** — what makes someone the person they are,
  in the "identity crisis" sense; the term is Schechtman's.
- **person essentialism** — nothing that is in fact a person could exist
  without being a person.
- **psychological connectedness / continuity** — causal dependence of
  later psychological states on earlier ones / an overlapping chain of
  such connections.
- **brute-physical view** — persistence consists in a physical relation
  not involving psychology; with animalism, in being the same organism.
- **anticriterialism** — there are no informative necessary and
  sufficient conditions for persistence.
- **multiple occupancy / non-branching** — the two responses to fission.
- **too-many-thinkers objection** — if you are not your organism, both
  think your thoughts.

## Connections

- **Dufner, "Personal Identity and Ethics"** ([LIT-130](../literature.d/LIT-130.md), [NOTE-166](NOTE-166.md)). The
  sister SEP entry. Dufner surveys the ethical uses of the positions this
  entry sets out, and finds "the relation that fixes persistence and the
  relation that matters ethically keep coming apart". This entry's §5 is
  where that split starts: non-branching makes identity not what matters.
  Read the two together.
- **Olson, *The Human Animal*** ([LIT-tmp6ryn8](../literature.d/LIT-tmp6ryn8.md), Deferred). §§6–7 compress
  its argument: the "fetus problem" and the transplant cases are its
  chapter titles. The entry cites "Olson 1997: 22–26" for the claim that
  the memory criterion, framed person-to-person, says nothing about
  non-persons.
- **Schechtman, *The Constitution of Selves*** ([LIT-tmpxa6sf](../literature.d/LIT-tmpxa6sf.md), Deferred).
  The entry takes the term "characterization question" from her and
  treats that question as not the persistence question. Her publisher says
  she "takes issue with analytic philosophy's emphasis on the first sort
  of question [the amnesiac's] to the exclusion of the second [the
  adolescent's]". So the entry sets aside the question her book says
  analytic philosophy wrongly sets aside. Olson and Witt's "Narrative and Persistence" (2019),
  in the bibliography, is where the author takes narrative views on
  directly.
- **Floridi** ([LIT-136](../literature.d/LIT-136.md), [NOTE-130](NOTE-130.md)). Floridi's deflation of the diachronic
  question to purpose-relative levels of abstraction is a version of the
  view, noted in §2, that the persistence question is answerable by
  conceptual means. The entry rejects that view.
- **Dennett, "Where Am I?"** ([LIT-405](../literature.d/LIT-405.md), [NOTE-338](NOTE-338.md)). The duplicated narrator
  is the entry's brain-state-transfer and duplicating-machine cases in
  story form. Dennett answers by point of view; the entry would ask which
  being the point of view belongs to.
- **Dennett, "The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity"** ([LIT-442](../literature.d/LIT-442.md),
  [NOTE-364](NOTE-364.md)). It answers a question the entry declines to ask, what a self
  is, and its "two selves in one body" bears on the population question.
- **Meincke's process view of pregnancy** ([LIT-142](../literature.d/LIT-142.md), [NOTE-115](NOTE-115.md)) is a
  counting problem of exactly the population kind, and the entry's §1
  cites partial twinning as one. Meincke's answer (something between one
  and two) is the kind of indeterminacy §8 reaches by another route.
- **Parallel filings.** The classical sources the entry reports (Locke's
  definition and memory criterion, Hume's bundle, Parfit's fission and
  "what matters", Williams's Guy Fawkes and body-swap cases, Lewis on
  survival and multiple occupancy) are being filed by another reader at
  the same time. This entry is the frame they fit into.

## Bearing on the record

- **The record's self cluster.** Most of what the record holds on the self
  (Dennett, Minsky's self-models in [LIT-432](../literature.d/LIT-432.md), Levin's scale-free selves in
  [LIT-439](../literature.d/LIT-439.md), the extended self of [LIT-097](../literature.d/LIT-097.md)) is about what a self is, which
  this entry explicitly puts aside. So the entry does not adjudicate
  them. It shows which question each is answering: Levin and Clark and
  Chalmers answer the personal-ontology question about boundaries, Dennett
  the characterization and population questions, none the persistence
  question.
- **[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) (the bridge between a society of mind and a conscious
  United States).** The too-many-thinkers objection is a counting argument
  of the kind [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) meets: whether a part (an organism, a
  hemisphere) and a whole that shares its thinking can both be thinkers.
  The pressure the entry relies on, that there should be one thinker per
  chair, is an anti-duplication intuition like the anti-nesting
  principles that document lists, applied to persons and organisms rather
  than to groups. The pairing is mine; the entry does not discuss groups.
- **Machines.** Personhood is asked of computers and animalism allows
  robot persons. The entry does not discuss AI, uploading or model
  copies, but its §4 (brain-state transfer) and §5 (fission) are the
  standard frame for them, and [LIT-206](../literature.d/LIT-206.md)'s per-conversation instance is a
  population-question answer.
- **No ML instruction.** Nothing here belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- **A survey with a side.** §6 presents the objections to the
  psychological view at length and their replies as hard to defend. §7
  presents the main objection to animalism and then argues it is "less
  compelling than it first appears". The author is the leading animalist.
- **No self.** The narrative self, the minimal self and self-models are
  outside it by decision, so it is not a map of the self literature this
  batch files.
- **Citation slip.** §2 cites "Olson and Witt 2020" for the claim that
  person essentialism is controversial. The bibliography's only Olson and
  Witt entry is 2019, "Narrative and Persistence", *Canadian Journal of
  Philosophy* 49: 419–434. Either the in-text year or the bibliography is
  wrong, or an entry is missing. I did not check which.

## Open questions

- Does the characterization question have a metaphysics of its own, as
  Schechtman claims, or is it psychology? The entry treats it as the
  latter by placing it outside persistence.
- What does the population question say about a system that runs many
  simultaneous copies of one set of weights? The entry's tools apply; the
  entry does not apply them.
