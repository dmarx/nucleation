# LIT-tmp18qgb: The power of goal-directed processes in the causation of emotional and other actions

Agnes Moors, Yannick Boddez & Jan De Houwer (2017), *Emotion Review*
9(4):310–318 — DOI-10.1177/1754073916669595

## Key takeaways

- **The goal-directed process** (pp. 4–5). It weighs action options by
  outcome value and action–outcome expectancy. "The action option with
  the highest utility activates its corresponding action tendency (i.e.,
  goal to act), and this action tendency may or may not translate in an
  overt action" (p. 4). It sits in a cybernetic cycle (after Carver and
  Scheier 1990, LIT-tmp63om1): "Cybernetic models further assume that
  people must balance multiple goals, which implies that several cycles
  may run in parallel" (p. 5). The cycles are nested, and keeping
  lower-order goals in balance is itself a higher-order goal (pp. 5–6).
- **The reversal** (pp. 8–11). Standard models give stimulus-driven
  processes the default, either in a default-interventionist architecture
  or in a "parallel-competitive architecture in which both types of
  processes constantly operate in parallel, but compete to determine
  behavior" (p. 8; Daw, Niv and Dayan, LIT-580). On Moors's account
  goal-directed processes should win instead, so "most of the competition
  does not occur between stimulus-driven and goal-directed processes, but
  instead between different goal-directed processes. Which process wins
  the competition is not only determined by complexity proportional to
  operating conditions, but also by the values and expectancies of
  outcomes" (p. 11).
- **Appraisal theories are stimulus-driven** (p. 13). "none of the current
  appraisal theories include an appraisal of the utility of specific
  action options afforded by a stimulus (i.e., affordances), and they do
  not hypothesize that the action tendency with the highest utility is
  always activated." Their links from appraisal patterns to action
  tendencies "are better conceived of as sophisticated types of
  stimulus-driven processes" (p. 13).
- **Control precedence without irrationality** (p. 15). If emotional
  actions ensue "from action tendencies with control precedence (i.e.,
  that take priority over other action tendencies) because these action
  tendencies, in turn, ensue from stimuli that are relevant for highly
  valued goals (Frijda, 2007), then there is no a priori reason to deny
  the role of goal-directed processes in the causation of emotional
  actions." Emotionality is graded: "Episodes can be called more or less
  emotional" (p. 15). Emotions "can themselves be regarded as decision
  processes, ones that rely on the very same mechanisms as non-emotional
  decision processes, but in which the stakes are higher" (pp. 15–16).
- **Evidence** (pp. 16–20). Frequency and felt automaticity are weak
  criteria. Outcome revaluation and contingency degradation are stronger,
  but a null result leaves room for hidden goals. Four complexities are
  named: multiple outcomes of one action, multiple actions to one
  outcome, old action–outcome links kept beside new ones, and
  meta-expectations.
- **Behaviour change** (pp. 20–21). Change values and expectancies rather
  than install habits, for example by making long-term outcomes vivid:
  "This provides an opening for long-term goals to gain dominance over
  short-term ones" (p. 21).

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-23 as the novelty check for the record's proposal that
an organization's appraisal is the negotiation among its perspectives
(CLAIM-tmpz8lli, TERM-tmp8gbrt). Moors already holds the competitive half
of it: action selection, emotional action included, is a competition
among goal-directed processes, and control precedence is what wins it
when the stakes are high. What she does not hold is the other half. Her
competition is settled on one scale, expected utility, which the
proposal treats as something a negotiation may fail to produce. It also
gives no account of valence. The bearing is set out in
NOTE-tmpyam8d. Her book-length statement of the theory,
LIT-tmp0s27v, is unread.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing here belongs in the
anthology.
