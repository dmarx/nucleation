# THEORY-471: Psychological states are not the machine-table states of an organism's unique best description as a probabilistic automaton, because some are never occurrent, several hold at once, there are indefinitely many and they contain one another, none of which a machine-table state can be; this leaves open that they are computational states of such a system

## Source

Block & Fodor (1972), "What Psychological States are Not", [LIT-641](../literature.d/LIT-641.md), read in
[NOTE-491](../notes.d/NOTE-491.md).

## What was actually shown

**The target (§II, p. 165).** The functional state identity theory (FSIT):
every organism that satisfies psychological predicates has a *unique best
description* as a probabilistic automaton, and each psychological state
type is one of its machine-table state types. The uniqueness clause keeps
FSIT from being trivial, since every organism realises some Turing machine
(pp. 165–166).

**Five arguments against the machine-table clause.**

- *Dispositions (pp. 168–170).* Every machine-table state is a possible
  occurrent state; "believes that P" has no occurrent "is believing that
  P".
- *Simultaneity (pp. 170–172).* An automaton is in one state at a time, so
  FSIT models sequential but not simultaneous interaction among states.
  Organisms need "a set of intercommunicating automata operating in
  parallel"; an equivalent single automaton exists, but it and the parallel
  set cannot both be the best description.
- *Fineness (pp. 174–175).* Saying "damn" where I say "darn" makes your pain
  type-distinct from mine, and with paths between all states, "any two
  automata which have less than all their states in common will have none
  of their states in common".
- *Productivity (pp. 175–177).* Machine-table states form a list; a
  person's possible psychological states are productive, specifiable "at
  best ... by finite axiomatization".
- *Structure (pp. 177–179).* Believing P is "somehow" a constituent of
  believing P & Q; lists have no notion of constituency. Productivity and
  structure are "two aspects of the same phenomenon".

**The escape each leaves.** Psychological states may correspond to "some
abstract property of the organization of machine tables", or to
computational states, "any state of the machine which is characterizable
in terms of its inputs, outputs, and/or machine table states" (p. 177).
[NOTE-491](../notes.d/NOTE-491.md) rates the case against FSIT's stated form strong (C3), and the
computational-state alternative moderate (C5).

**What could have come out otherwise.** Psychological states could have
been one at a time, finitely many, unstructured and all occurrent. Each
argument points to a feature of ordinary psychological description that
rules this out.

## What this does not say

- **That functionalism is false.** Each argument comes with an escape that
  keeps it. They show that FSIT's machine-table clause fails ([NOTE-491](../notes.d/NOTE-491.md),
  Limitations).
- **That qualia refute functionalism.** Inverted or absent qualia are
  "only a prima-facie objection" (p. 172), with two replies left open. That
  is the one argument here aimed at functionalism broadly, and it is not
  part of this account. Block develops it later ([LIT-407](../literature.d/LIT-407.md)).
- **That psychological states are computational states.** The paper leaves
  it open; argument 6 has the constituency relation only "somehow" (n. 8:
  "Very much 'somehow'").
- **That behaviourism and type physicalism are refuted a priori.** §I says
  the decisive arguments against them are empirical (C1, moderate, with no
  data presented).
- **That argument 1 is beyond dispute.** It rests on English grammar ("is
  believing" is not said), backed by a conservatism principle. A theory
  might posit occurrent belief states.
