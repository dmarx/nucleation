# LIT-tmp7mx1r: Intuitions about personal identity: An empirical study

Nichols, S. & Bruno, M. (2010). Intuitions about personal identity: An empirical study. *Philosophical Psychology* 23(3), 293–312. DOI 10.1080/09515089.2010.490939.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmpd3ur0](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpd3ur0.md).*

- **Williams's divergence is real for these participants.** A
  transplant case makes memory look necessary: the patient whose
  memories were erased is judged not to be "still Jim" (t(28) = 3.17).
  A simplified Pain case makes it look unnecessary: 68% (all N = 50)
  and 75% of those who understood the case (N = 28) say that "you will
  feel the pain" after your distinctive mental states are eliminated.
- **First against third person is not what drives it.** Both cases come
  out the same in either telling (Pain: 72% of N = 54 for "Jerry";
  χ²(1, N = 64) = 0.062 against the first-person version). Williams's
  suggestion that the mentalistic verdict comes from the third person and
  the bodily one from the first is not borne out.
- **Abstract questions favour psychology.** Asked openly what makes a
  future person you, over 70% name memory, personality or the like; over
  80% deny that someone with none of your memories could be you.
- **Under reflection the psychological answer is preferred, narrowly.**
  Shown both and told they are inconsistent, 64% of 45 side with memory
  (χ² = 3.76, p = 0.053).
- **The claim is conditional and robustness-based.** The authors propose
  that the Pain frame carries a "thought experimenter demand" ("if I am not
  going to feel it then who is?") and that the more robust intuitions
  should get more weight, "assuming that intuitions should inform our
  theories of personal identity". Only the necessity of psychology was
  tested; sufficiency was abandoned because pilot participants could not
  follow Williams's full case.

What it does bear on, set out in the NOTE:

- **[THEORY-531](../theory.d/THEORY-531.md)** (Williams's two tellings). Its promote_when already says
  that reports of what people would fear "cannot settle" a claim about
  what fear can rationally follow, and this study does not ask about fear
  at all. It does test two empirical asides in Williams (pp. 179–180): that
  grammatical person goes with the verdict, and that the swap verdict is
  an artefact of the experimenter stopping where the description is
  neatest. Among these students neither holds.
- **[THEORY-417](../theory.d/THEORY-417.md) and [NOTE-374](../notes.d/NOTE-374.md)** (Olson's map). The study runs on the persistence
  question, but its Pain item ("you will feel the pain") may be read as a
  question about a subject of experience rather than about the person, and
  in the vignette the procedure cures the brain rather than destroying it. [NOTE-374](../notes.d/NOTE-374.md) lists "thought
  experiments are evidence" as an assumption of the debate, and Olson holds
  the persistence question is not about the meaning of words. On that
  view, which this paper does not dispute, a survey of how people apply the
  word can inform a theory only through a premise that the concept tracks
  the fact.
- **[THEORY-546](../theory.d/THEORY-546.md)** (psychological continuity) and the bodily rival in
  Olson ([LIT-448](LIT-448.md)). The study finds that students' abstract and reflective
  judgements favour psychological necessity. That is evidence about the
  folk concept. It is not evidence for psychological continuity as the
  persistence condition, which is the claim [THEORY-546](../theory.d/THEORY-546.md) makes.
- **[LIT-451](LIT-451.md)** (Hume). The frame-dependence fits Hume's verdict that hard
  cases "can never possibly be decided" and are "grammatical rather than
  philosophical". The authors hold that the more robust intuition can
  break the tie. The pairing is mine.
