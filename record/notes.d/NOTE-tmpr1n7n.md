# NOTE-tmpr1n7n: Nagel: what is it like to be a bat

## Contribution

The paper gives the subjective character of experience the definition that is
still standard: "an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there
is something that it is like to be that organism". It then argues that this
feature is what makes the mind-body problem unlike every successful reduction
the reductionists of the day cited: water/H₂O, lightning/electrical discharge,
gene/DNA.

## Key insight

Reduction in science works by abandoning a point of view. Take sound: we stop
describing it by how it strikes the ear and describe it as a wave in a medium.
We still refer to the same thing, and the auditory viewpoint we leave behind
"remains unreduced". Experience is the viewpoint itself, not something seen
from it. So the move that makes other reductions succeed is exactly the one
that loses the thing to be reduced.

## Assumptions

- **Bats have experience.** This is taken as common ground ("I assume we all
  believe that bats have experience"). Bats are chosen because they are
  mammals, so the belief is secure, and because their sonar is unlike any human
  sense, so the problem is vivid.
- **Imagination is bounded by one's own experience.** Adding to, subtracting
  from or modifying one's own experience yields what it would be like for
  *me* to behave as a bat, not what it is like for a bat to be a bat.
- **The physical is objective by definition** (fn. 15). "Whatever else may be
  said of the physical, it has to be objective." Nagel leaves "physical"
  otherwise undefined.
- **The realist premise.** There can be facts beyond the reach of human
  concepts. Nagel's analogy: transfinite numbers would exist even if everyone
  had died before Cantor.

## Key results

This is an argument, not an empirical paper. Its conclusions, in the order the
paper reaches them:

1. The subjective character of experience is not captured by any available
   reductive analysis, because each is logically compatible with its absence.
2. Facts about what it is like to be an X embody a particular point of view.
   This is a *type* of viewpoint, not a private one. One person can know
   another's experience to the extent that they can adopt it.
3. Knowledge of bat neurophysiology faces no such obstacle. Intelligent bats
   or Martians might learn more about the human brain than we will.
4. A physical theory of mind must therefore explain how a single thing can
   have a subjective character. No present conception says how, and no
   analogy from other reductions supplies one.
5. Physicalism is not thereby false. We can even have evidence for it without
   understanding it: the caterpillar in the sealed safe that is found to be a
   butterfly. Nagel reads Davidson's anomalous monism this way.
6. The question that comes first, and that can be posed without mentioning
   the brain, is whether experiences can have an objective character at all.

## Method

Conceptual argument through a worked example. The bat stands for any
experiencer whose point of view is far from ours. The paper reasons from
contrast cases: lightning, rainbows, and sound as seen by a Martian scientist.
The fifteen footnotes do real work. Fn. 8 says the point is not
epistemological: it is not that we cannot *know* what it is like, but that
forming the conception at all requires taking up the viewpoint. Fn. 11 extends
Kripke on why a necessary mind-brain relation would still appear contingent.

## Concepts

- **Subjective character of experience**: what it is like for the subject.
  Fn. 6: "what it is like" means "how it is for the subject himself", not
  "what it resembles".
- **Point of view**: a type, shared by sufficiently similar beings, not an
  individual's privacy.
- **Objectivity as a direction**: understanding can travel toward the
  objective without an assumed end point.
- **Perceptual, sympathetic and symbolic imagination** (fn. 11): imagining
  a mental state sympathetically and its brain state perceptually makes their
  separation seem conceivable, even if it is necessarily impossible.
- **Objective phenomenology**: describing the structure of experience in terms
  comprehensible to those who cannot have it.

## Connections

- `LIT-056` (Butlin et al., 2023) defines its sole target, phenomenal

  functional indicators to that target by *assuming* computational

## Bearing on the record

For ML, this is the paper that says what a question about machine
consciousness is a question *about*, and why behavioural or functional
evidence cannot close it by itself. It gives the record the vocabulary for
keeping two claims apart:

- A system has the functional organisation a theory associates with
  consciousness.
- There is something it is like to be that system.

`LIT-056` is careful to present its indicators as raising a probability, not
settling the question. This paper is the reason that care is needed. The paper
supports no practice or theory in the anthology's sense, and none should be
drawn from it.

## Limitations

- The central criterion is stipulated, not argued. A reader who doubts that
  "what it is like" picks out anything, as eliminativists do, is addressed only
  by the Martian thought experiment.
- The objective-phenomenology proposal is one page with no worked instance
  beyond the dismissal of "red is like the sound of a trumpet".
- "Physical" is left undefined except as objective (fn. 15). So the claim that
  experience resists physical description comes close to following from the
  definitions.
- Its examples of reduction are 1960s–70s identity theory. It does not address
  later functionalist or representationalist accounts, because it predates
  them.

## Open questions

- Nagel concedes in fn. 8 that understanding across species varies by degree.
  Is objective phenomenology the same project as learning a structural,
  similarity-space description of perception, and what would it leave out?
- Fn. 2: is there anything that behaves like a person but has no experience?
  Nagel says concept analysis cannot answer this. The question is what could.

## Text read

The whole paper, including all fifteen footnotes, from a web transcription
([philosopher.eu](https://www.philosopher.eu/others-writings/nagel-what-is-it-like-to-be-a-bat/))
of the version reprinted in Nagel's *Mortal Questions* (Cambridge University
Press, 1979). Footnote 1 refers to "chapter 11 above", which marks it as the
reprint. The transcription drops the footnote markers and the original page
numbers. So claims above are located by footnote number, not by page, and the
text was not collated against the 1974 *Philosophical Review* printing. The
transcriber appends a quotation, attributed to Nagel's *The View from Nowhere*
(1986), that credits the phrase to Sprigge and to Farrell (1950). I did not
check it against that book.
