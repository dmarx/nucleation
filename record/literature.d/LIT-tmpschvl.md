# LIT-tmpschvl: Three Theories of Self-Governance

Michael E. Bratman, "Three Theories of Self-Governance", *Philosophical
Topics* 32(1/2), Spring/Fall 2004 (issue title "Agency"), pp. 21–46 —
DOI-10.5840/philtopics2004321/25. Reprinted as chapter 11 of Bratman's
essay collection *Structures of Agency* (Oxford University Press, 2007),
pp. 222–253, DOI 10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195187717.003.0011. Crossref and
the Philosophy Documentation Center give the article only the year, so
`published:` carries 2004 as the first of January.

The brief's citation is correct on both counts: the journal, volume and
year, and the 2007 reprint in *Structures of Agency*. The book is filed
separately in this batch by another reader; this entry is the essay.

## Key takeaways

*Registered from the publisher's abstract of the 2007 reprint, not a
reading.* The abstract says the chapter:

- "revisits the important exchange between Harry G. Frankfurt and Gary
  Watson concerning the psychological structures involved in significant
  forms of free agency", and treats that debate "as part of the debate about
  the psychological structures and forms of functioning that are central to
  human self-governance";
- aims "to deepen the account of how to be a wholehearted, pluralist,
  self-governing agent" using Joshua Cohen's reading of Rawls's "idea of
  reasonable pluralism";
- develops the "broadly Frankfurtian" claim that "agential authority is a
  matter of the non-Platonic psychological role in the Lockean organization
  of our temporally extended agency", even for evaluative attitudes that
  also track the good, and says that "the normal hierarchical structure of
  self-governing policies helps them play these Lockean organizing roles";
- "Finally, … proposes an intention-based theory."

The title's three theories are, on the abstract's evidence, Frankfurt's,
Watson's and Bratman's own. The abstract does not number them, so that is my
reading of it.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, as the record's statement of
Bratman's planning theory of self-governance. It is philosophy of agency
with no instruction for machine-learning practice, so no anthology topic
holds it. The Anthology of the SOTA does not hold it: a grep of its
literature.d for "Bratman" and the title found nothing.

`Deferred` because it was not read.

**No relation is declared to Frankfurt.** The brief suggested that
`compared_against` Frankfurt might be true, and on the abstract it is
likely: the essay sets its intention-based theory beside Frankfurt's and
Watson's. But the record's Frankfurt filings are "Identification and
Wholeheartedness" ([LIT-tmpcand6](LIT-tmpcand6.md)), filed with this, and the 2004 Tanner
Lectures ([LIT-293](LIT-293.md)), and I cannot say from an abstract which Frankfurt text
the essay measures itself against; it may well be a third that the record
does not hold. A reader should declare `compared_against` to whichever of
the two the essay actually engages.

What the record already says about it:

- **Buss and Westlund ([LIT-296](LIT-296.md), [NOTE-267](../notes.d/NOTE-267.md))** list this essay in their
  bibliography and never cite it in the text or notes.
- **Dryden ([LIT-297](LIT-297.md), [NOTE-266](../notes.d/NOTE-266.md))** gives Bratman one sentence: personal identity
  is partly constituted by the organizing and coordinating role of plans
  and policies.
- **[THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)** names "Bratman's self-governing policies" in its
  `promote_when` as a reply it holds only by report. This essay is the
  reply. The question [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md) puts to it is whether a policy's "Lockean
  organizing role" is specifiable without presupposing the agent's
  participation and is not disownable. A functional role in the
  organization of agency over time is a candidate that is specified without
  the agent's endorsement; whether an agent can be alienated from such a
  policy is what the reading would have to establish.

**Priority for a reading: high**, with "Identification and Wholeheartedness"
first, since Bratman's essay is in part a reply to it.
