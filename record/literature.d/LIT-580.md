# LIT-580: Uncertainty-based competition between prefrontal and dorsolateral striatal systems for behavioral control

Nathaniel D. Daw, Yael Niv & Peter Dayan (2005), *Nature Neuroscience*
8(12):1704–1711 — DOI-10.1038/nn1560

## Key takeaways

- **Two controllers, two reinforcement-learning methods.** The habitual
  system is model-free: it "caches" a scalar long-run value for each action
  in each state, learned by temporal-difference bootstrapping. The
  goal-directed system is model-based: it learns the task's transitions and
  outcomes and computes values "on the fly" by searching a tree. Only the
  tree knows which outcome an action leads to, so only it adjusts at once
  when that outcome is devalued.
- **Arbitration by uncertainty.** Each controller is given an approximate
  Bayesian posterior over its values. Behaviour uses the value from
  whichever controller has the smaller posterior variance. The tree is more
  data-efficient, but each step of search adds "computational noise". The
  cache is cheap but slow to propagate experience.
- **It reproduces the devaluation facts.** Moderately trained actions are
  devaluation-sensitive and over-trained ones are not. Over-trained actions
  stay sensitive when the task has two actions for two outcomes, or when the
  action is proximal to reward (magazine entry). Lesions of either system
  leave the other in control.
- **A normative reading of dual control.** "Both controllers are pursuing
  identical rational ends." This is set against economic accounts in which
  "irrational, impulsive or emotional limbic influences" interfere with "a
  more rational prefrontal controller".

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, for the record's coverage of how
an organism's control systems come to act as one. It is the record's first
reading of the model-based/model-free distinction. Dayan and Berridge
([LIT-584](LIT-584.md)) extend the distinction to Pavlovian prediction.

**The boundary.** The paper uses reinforcement-learning algorithms (Bayesian
Q-learning, Bayesian value iteration) to explain animal behaviour and its
neural substrate. It recommends nothing for machine-learning practice, and
its question is about brains, so it belongs here and not in the anthology.

It bears on the record's account of self-control. [THEORY-056](../theory.d/THEORY-056.md) holds that
regulation is one motive state checking another, with no distinct
regulating system. This paper's two controllers compete, and the choice
between them is made by comparing uncertainties, not by a supervisor that
knows better. That fits the theory's architecture, though the paper is about
instrumental action and not emotion. The paper does suggest that
infralimbic cortex or anterior cingulate might implement the arbitration,
which would be a distinct arbitrating structure, if not a regulating one.
Ainslie ([LIT-586](LIT-586.md)) and Shenhav et al. ([LIT-585](LIT-585.md)) give different
answers to the same question, and [NOTE-459](../notes.d/NOTE-459.md) sets them side by side.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing here belongs in the
anthology.
