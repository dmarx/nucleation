# NOTE-1122: Juhász — Temporary protection and technology adoption (December 2015 draft)

## Contribution

The infant-industry argument is old and its evidence weak, because
protection is chosen by the policy-maker for the whole country, so there is
no counterfactual and no way to separate the mechanism from the
implementation (p. 2). Here protection arrived without the policy-maker: the
blockade displaced British trade onto circuitous routes, raising the cost
of reaching northern France far more than southern France. Comparing
departments by the size of that shock, the paper estimates the short-run
effect of protection on adopting mechanized spinning and, using the shock as
an instrument, the long-run effect of having more spinning when protection
ended. It calls itself the first "well-identified, reduced-form evidence of
an infant industry mechanism" (p. 6).

## Key insight

A shock to trade costs that varies across regions within one country acts
like regionally differentiated tariffs. If protection only shelters an
industry, the advantage should vanish once trade reopens. If it lets the
industry reach scale where learning by doing or agglomeration lowers costs,
the protected locations should keep their lead after protection ends. They
did, for the industry, for most of the century.

## Assumptions

- **The trade-cost shock is exogenous.** The change in least-cost distance
  to London between pre-blockade routes and the blockade routes via
  Helgoland, Gothenburg, Gibraltar and Malta, with 1 sea km set equal to
  0.15 land km (p. 20). Direct smuggling is excluded, on historians'
  evidence that it was riskier (p. 21).
- **No contemporaneous shock correlated with it** (p. 26). Tested by
  pre-trends, placebo industries and controls for streams, coal, market
  potential, university access and literacy, each interacted with the
  treatment period (pp. 28–32).
- **Exclusion restriction for the long run.** The shock affects 1840–2000
  outcomes only through 1812 spinning capacity (p. 34). The reduced forms
  are reported so that a reader need only assume the shock is uncorrelated
  with other determinants (p. 35).
- **The sample.** 88 of 109 departments observed in 1803 and 1812 (p. 25),
  fewer in the long run because France lost its territorial gains: 67–75
  departments for persistence, 35 departments and 405 firms for
  productivity, 70–72 for value added (Tables 3–5).
- **Spindles per thousand inhabitants**, population held at 1811 (p. 25);
  1803 spindles partly imputed by predictive mean matching (p. 21).

## Key results

- **Short run (Table 1, p. 27).** Coefficient on log effective distance
  33.11 (s.e. 9.78), stable at 24–41 with controls. Moving from the 25th to
  the 75th percentile of the shock predicts an increase in spinning capacity
  about equal to mean capacity in 1812. Empire-wide spindles rose about
  370 per cent, from 380,000 to around 1.4 million (p. 22).
- **Falsification (Table 2, pp. 30–32).** Pre-trend 1794–1803 small and
  insignificant once market potential is controlled; no effect on
  capital-labour ratios, machine vintage, wool spinning or leather tanning.
  Most new capacity came from firm entry (p. 30).
- **Persistence (Table 3, p. 36).** One more spindle in 1812 from protection
  meant about 2–3 more in 1840 and 5–6 more in 1887 (2SLS). Southern
  departments shrank in absolute terms, which the author reads as the
  strongest evidence against slow diffusion from Britain (p. 36).
- **Productivity (Table 4, p. 37).** A one standard deviation increase in
  1812 capacity meant firm labour productivity 0.2–0.5 standard deviations
  higher in 1840.
- **Industrial value added (Table 5, p. 38).** Positive and significant in
  1860 (2SLS 0.00714); "The point estimate decreases in size over time and
  from 1896 onwards, the coefficient is longer distinguishable statistically
  from zero" (sic, p. 38). OLS is large and stable through 2000. No effect on
  agriculture or services.
- **Weak instrument, conceded (p. 35).** "The Kleibergen-Papp F-statistic
  for the first stage is generally below 10" KP F is 3.0–8.0 in Tables
  3–5. The author argues that just-identified IV is approximately median
  unbiased and that the standard errors are not uselessly wide.
- **Exports (p. 39).** France went from net importer to net exporter of
  cotton goods; by 1828 cottons were 7.5 per cent of French exports. As late
  as 1850 France and Belgium spun more than other Continental countries.

## Concepts

- **Trade-cost shock**: the log change in each department's shortest
  effective route to London between pre-blockade and blockade years
  (p. 20).
- **Temporary protection**: here, the period of the wars and blockade,
  1803–1815. It was not a tariff, and it was not lifted into free trade:
  after the wars "tariff and non-tariff trade barriers were put in place
  between the two countries" (p. 33).
- **Infant-industry mechanism**: protection renders a location profitable in
  the long run, after protection is removed, through agglomeration
  economies (p. 33).

## Connections

It belongs with the work on temporary shocks that permanently move economic
activity (Davis and Weinstein 2002; Redding and Sturm 2008; Kline and Moretti
2014, p. 7), and with the infant-industry simulations it says cannot address
the endogeneity of industry choice (Baldwin and Krugman; Head 1994; Irwin
2000). It sides with Crouzet against Heckscher, for whom the blockade's
industries were "hothouse" growth (p. 8).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-445](../claims.d/CLAIM-445.md) (protective regimes become self-maintaining).** The claim
  cites Juhász for its contrast between capability-building and
  dependence-building protection: protection "need not be self-defeating".
  The reading supports that use and corrects the frame around it.
  - **Supports.** Protection built a capability that outlasted it: the
    industry's location persisted and spinning firms were more productive.
    That is a temporary measure leaving a lasting structure, the claim's
    contrast case.
  - **Narrows.** What persisted was the industry, not the protection. The
    paper says nothing about whether protection maintained itself, which
    is the claim's mechanism. The aggregate gain faded: value added was
    higher in 1860 and not detectably so from 1896. The long-run estimates
    rest on a weak instrument.
  - **Corrects the frame.** This was not "A temporary tariff" (the claim's words). It was a
    war blockade, imposed against Britain and not to shelter cotton:
    "Napoleon remained highly ambivalent of developments in the cotton
    industry" (p. 24) and taxed raw cotton. Nor did protection simply end:
    post-war tariffs and non-tariff barriers followed (p. 33). So the
    within-France results show the protected regions' lead under continued
    national protection; only the export evidence speaks to competitiveness
    at world prices, and the author says the within-country results do not
    show it (p. 6).
  - **A detail for the claim's political-institutional feedback.** The
    constituency formed. Footnote 7 cites a petition "from large spinners
    across the Empire requesting a complete ban on English cloth" (p. 13).
    The paper does not follow it up, and it does not say whether post-war
    tariffs owed anything to the new industry. That would be the test of
    whether this capability-building protection also built its own
    constituency.
  - Filed as [THEORY-919](../theory.d/THEORY-919.md).
- **[CLAIM-446](../claims.d/CLAIM-446.md).** Not touched. Nothing here is about evidence or
  anxiety.
- **[THEORY-885](../theory.d/THEORY-885.md) (increasing returns).** A historical case of a temporary
  shock locked in by increasing returns, at the level of industry location.
  It fits the account; it was not designed to test it.

## Limitations

- **A draft, not the version of record.** The AER paper (2018) may differ.
  Its abstract, which the LIT summary follows, puts the gain in industrial
  value added up to the second half of the nineteenth century and not
  later, which matches Table 5 here.
- **Appendices not seen.** The robustness tables, the trade and price
  series, the export and Continental comparison figures are in Online
  Appendices 1–3.
- **Weak instrument** in every long-run first stage.
- **Small samples**: 35 departments for productivity.
- **Mechanism not identified.** The author separates learning-by-doing from
  pecuniary externalities only suggestively, and says this "does not imply a
  case for infant industry protection in similar settings" (p. 8); where the
  prerequisites are absent, "infant industry protection can turn out to be
  an extremely blunt tool, irrespective of whether the policy would be
  optimal or not" (p. 40).
- **Reporting slips.** "is longer distinguishable" for "is no longer"
  (p. 38); "technlogy" in a heading (p. 11); the conclusion's "the economy
  more generally" (p. 40) outruns Table 5, whose effect on value added is
  confined to 1860.

## Open questions

- Did the spinners' constituency shape the post-war tariffs? Archival work
  on the 1814–1816 tariff debates would show whether this protection
  maintained itself politically while it built capability.
- Does the version of record strengthen the first stage or change the value
  added results?
- Would a setting without post-war protection show the same persistence?
