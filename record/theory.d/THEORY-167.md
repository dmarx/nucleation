# THEORY-167: The Russian wondertale is built from a small fixed set of character functions in a fixed order, so that all wondertales share one composition and differ in who performs the functions and how

## Source

Vladimir Propp (1928), *Морфология сказки* [Morphology of the Folktale],
[LIT-828](../literature.d/LIT-828.md); read in [NOTE-615](../notes.d/NOTE-615.md) from the 1928 Russian text, chapters
II, III, VI and IX.

## The claim

Abstract each wondertale from its characters and their attributes, and
describe it by what the characters do, each act defined by what it leads
to in the story. Then the acts form a short list (31, after an initial
situation), they occur in one order, and every tale of the class is a
selection from that one sequence: it omits some functions, repeats or
trebles others, realises each in one of a range of forms, and assigns the
functions to characters. Two pairs, struggle and victory, and difficult
task and solution, almost never share a move, so the sequence has two
alternative branches; where a tale has both, the struggle comes in the
first move and the task in the second. The functions group into seven
roles: villain, donor, helper, princess and her father, dispatcher, hero
and false hero.

## What was actually shown

An analysis of a fixed corpus, Afanasyev's nos. 50–151, by one analyst.
Chapter III lists the functions with examples from the corpus; chapter IX
transcribes the tales as strings of function symbols and stacks them,
finding the common order. The analysis could have failed: tales might
have needed many more functions, or the strings might have resisted any
single order, which is what the earlier view (Veselovsky's "a certain
freedom", Shklovsky's "accidental sequence of motifs") expected. Counts
are given for the two exclusive pairs (41 and 33 of 100 tales, together
three times). The appendices give the schemes per tale; the record's
reading did not check them, because the OCR garbles them.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say all folktales share this structure.** The claim is
  about wondertales, and Propp ends by defining that class as the tales
  built on this sequence. Animal tales, anecdotes and novellas mostly fall
  outside, though Propp shows a few that fit (the wolf and the kids).
- **It does not say the order is exceptionless.** Propp records inverted
  sequences, swaps of adjacent functions, a mobile transfiguration, and
  larger departures in humorous tales that he calls decomposition. The
  invariance holds up to those.
- **It has not been tested for reliability.** Functions are assigned by
  consequence, which leaves the analyst latitude; the book reports no
  second coder.
- **It does not establish a single source tale or a mythic origin.**
  Propp offers both (all wondertales derivable from the dragon's abduction
  of a princess; the wondertale as morphologically a myth) as hypotheses,
  and says the evidence for them, the study of attributes and
  transformations, is not in the book.
- **It does not say tellers cannot innovate.** Propp's own account makes
  tellers free in omission, in the form a function takes, in the casting
  and naming of characters and in language.
