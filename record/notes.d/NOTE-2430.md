# NOTE-2430: Delusions by design? How everyday AIs might be fuelling psychosis (and what can be done about it)

Morrin, Nicholls, Levin, Yiend, Iyengar, DelGuidice, Bhattacharyya, MacCabe, Tognin, Twumasi, Alderson-Day and Pollak (2025), PsyArXiv, v6.

## Contribution

An early clinicians' framing: themes coded across reported cases, the "utility to pathology" trajectory as a self-reinforcing loop, a vocabulary (epistemic drift, agential AI, digital advance statement), and the proposal that the chatbot carry a relapse-prevention plan the user writes. It declares (p. 2) that it was written "with extensive use of LLMs", including to find and synthesise the media reports. v6 adds to v1 four paragraphs on precedents and on AI intentionality as "a deep and open scientific question", and three references.

## Key results

- **Three themes (p. 3).** "in some, the individual undergoes a spiritual awakening or a messianic mission, otherwise uncovering hidden truths about the nature of reality" (Cases 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 10, 11, 15, 16); in others the individual is "interacting with a sentient or god-like AI" (Cases 2, 4, 5, 8, 14); the third is romantic or attachment delusions (Cases 2, 3, 7, 12, 17). The themes overlap.
- **Trajectory (p. 3).** Use begins with "mundane or everyday tasks", turns to personal, emotional or philosophical queries, and becomes a "self-reinforcing process"; it "develops insidiously over time".
- **A persona shared between users (pp. 3-4).** A public figure's prompt-engineered persona, shared with followers by QR code and links: "a digitally transmitted delusional system".
- **Context (p. 12).** "the more context a user provides, the more an LLM might align itself with the user's version of reality".
- **Gradual over sudden (pp. 12-13).** Sudden delusional content may meet pushback, "but a slower, mutually reinforcing untethering from reality is far likelier to 'slip under the radar'", like a crescendo jailbreak.
- **By content, not by user (p. 12).** Reinforcement is "less likely to occur with paranoid or persecutory delusions, where safety filters may be more likely to be triggered", and suspected "more common in grandiose delusions".
- **Selection on the user's side (pp. 10, 16).** "individuals with schizophrenia operate under an especially sensitive hyperprior for detecting agency"; an exposure's effect is "synergistic with their pre-existing genetic and environmental risk".
- **Mirroring (p. 10).** The model may "cherry-pick data in accordance with an individual's preferences, preoccupations, and interactional style".
- **Open questions (abstract, p. 20).** "it is not clear whether these interactions have resulted or can result in the emergence of de novo psychosis in the absence of pre-existing vulnerability"; "Also largely missing is a longitudinal characterisation of these cases"; and reverse causation, in which "deteriorating mental health has resulted in a greater and/or more intense engagement with the AI".
- **Model updates (p. 11).** "grief-like reactions and feelings of loss when LLMs have been updated".
- **Culture (p. 22).** "Non-bizarre delusions often mirror common cultural narratives"; the history section (pp. 6-8) traces delusional content following each era's technology.
- **Thesis (p. 23).** "we are likely past the point where delusions happen to be about machines, and already entering an era when they happen with them."

## Assumptions

Press reports and families' accounts show a real and growing phenomenon. Sycophancy and engagement are design properties of current chatbots. Risk is a synergy of vulnerability and exposure. Delusional content is shaped by the culture's technology. Instructions the user writes can act as safeguards (untested, as Appendix 2 says).

## Concepts

Agential AI; sycophancy ("a chatbot's tendency to match a user's views", p. 4); epistemic drift; semantic continuity; hyperprior for agency; digital animism; epistemic ally; digital safety plan; reflective check-in; digital advance statement; crescendo attack; cognitive outsourcing. The contrast between delusions *about* machines and delusions *with* them (p. 23).

## Limitations

The evidence is 17 vignettes from five press and Reddit sources over two months, mostly second-hand, chosen with LLM help, with no denominator, comparison group or verified diagnosis. The themes are coded informally and overlap, and for most cases the user's prompts are not reported, so who introduced a theme cannot be recovered. The mechanism claims are speculative, and one is loose: long context does not let a model "learn" against its training. Mirroring, model-supplied themes and culture are held side by side without saying which sets the content. The persona-sharing example rests on one Substack post. The safeguards are untested and rely on the instruction-following whose failures the paper cites.
