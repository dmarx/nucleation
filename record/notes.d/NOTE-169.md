# NOTE-169: Seth — Conscious AI and biological naturalism

## Contribution

Seth asks whether advancing AI might become conscious as well as intelligent. He diagnoses the biases that make people expect it (anthropocentrism, human exceptionalism, anthropomorphism, and the resulting conflation of intelligence with consciousness). He questions whether computation suffices for consciousness and argues instead for biological naturalism, on which consciousness depends on our being living organisms. After surveying scenarios for artificial consciousness, he concludes that it is unlikely on current trajectories but grows more plausible as AI becomes more brain-like or life-like. He ends with the ethics of AI that is, or convincingly seems to be, conscious: "If we sell our minds too cheaply to our machine creations, we not only overestimate them – we underestimate ourselves."

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §2 (pp. 3–5): the three biases lead people to conflate intelligence and consciousness and to be overoptimistic about conscious AI. The examples run from the golem through HAL, Ava and Klara.
- §3 (pp. 5–15): computational functionalism is appealing, but substrate independence, neural-replacement arguments, emergence and the separation of scales, and "simulation vs implementation vs realisation" all put it under strain. The §3.9 summary: conscious AI needs computational functionalism *plus* substrate flexibility, and biological computation may not be separable from its material basis.
- §4 (pp. 15–22): the positive case comes from predictive processing (interoceptive, allostatic "beast machine" inference) and from the free energy principle and autopoiesis, with membranes, mitochondria and xenobots as examples. The §4.5 summary distinguishes an epistemic view (consciousness can only be understood in light of life) from a stronger ontological one. The paper explicitly rejects biopsychism and carbon chauvinism.
- §5 (pp. 22–27): scenarios run from "naïve along for the ride" through theory-based computational and substrate-dependent (weak and strong) to embodiment. The §5.8 summary favours the strong version: conscious AI would need to be "living AI", though not necessarily carbon-based. Seth admits "my case is not watertight".
- §6.2 (pp. 28–30): conscious-seeming AI may produce cognitively impenetrable illusions like the Müller-Lyer, and it can exploit users' psychological vulnerabilities.
- §7 (pp. 30–31): biological naturalism is "no more speculative than computational functionalism". Our biases favour false positives over false negatives, and LLMs are "more similar to us in ways that do not matter for consciousness, and less similar in ways that do".

## Open questions

- It is the leading statement of the biological-naturalist side that Birch's [LIT-111](../literature.d/LIT-111.md) sets against computational functionalism, and it connects to [LIT-120](../literature.d/LIT-120.md) (autopoiesis), [LIT-195](../literature.d/LIT-195.md) (life–mind continuity), [LIT-103](../literature.d/LIT-103.md) (Frankish's functionalism) and [LIT-185](../literature.d/LIT-185.md) (IIT).
- A deeper reading should check whether the free-energy/autopoiesis argument gives necessity or only an explanatory bridge. Seth's own epistemic/ontological split suggests the strong claim is not established.
- The BBS commentaries and Seth's response (2026 volume) are the natural next reading. They were not reached here.
