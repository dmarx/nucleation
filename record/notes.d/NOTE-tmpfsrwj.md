# NOTE-tmpfsrwj: Polski and Ostrom — An Institutional Framework for Policy Analysis and Design

## Contribution

The chapter turns the Workshop's IAD research framework into a stepwise policy-analysis and design procedure. It makes explicit:
- the focus on rules-in-use as opposed to rules-in-form;
- the analysis of nested levels of rules.

## Key results

- **Definition of an institution.** "We define an institution as a widely understood rule, norm, or strategy that creates incentives for behavior in repetitive situations"
- **Rules-in-use.** The analyst concentrates "on the operating rules that are commonly used by most participants and on the sources of these rules, rather than on rules that can be articulated but are not widely observed."
- **Seven rule types.** "(1) position, (2) boundary, (3) authority, (4) aggregation, (5) scope, (6) information, and (7) payoff."
- **Levels of rules.** "Rules are frequently nested in other sets of rules that define how lower-level rules function."
  - "Collective-choice rules determine who is eligible to participate in activity affecting the operating level, and how operating rules may be changed."
  - "constitutional rules determine who is eligible to participate in crafting collective-choice rules, and how these rules may be changed."
- **Banking example.** Banks could lobby but not branch across state lines, because collective-choice rules placed that decision with state and federal legislatures.

## Assumptions

- Institutions are largely invisible and must be inferred from how participants actually behave.
- Analysis should focus on the action arena.
- The three levels are analytically distinct, and each may contain several arenas.

## Concepts

- institution vs organization
- action arena, action situation and actors
- rules-in-use
- the seven rule types
- operational, collective-choice and constitutional levels
- the IAD framework
- the nesting of rules

## Bearing on the record

A's gloss is accurate for the chapter's "Analyzing Multiple Levels of Rules" section: the IAD framework's operational, collective-choice and constitutional levels of rules.

A's mapping of those levels onto luria is A's own, and the chapter does not support it:
- changes to entries = operational;
- changes to schemas and procedures = collective-choice;
- authority over those changes = constitutional.

Two caveats for A's mapping:
1. **Rules-in-use vs rules-in-form.** The chapter insists that analysis start from rules actually used, not rules that can be articulated. For a record, that would mean the conventions agents actually follow, not only what `luria.yaml` or an ADR says. That is a test the mapping must meet, not one it gets for free.
2. **The levels are attributed elsewhere.** The chapter credits the levels to Kiser and Ostrom (1982).

- **[CLAIM-086](../claims.d/CLAIM-086.md)** (a constraint may govern conduct without governing the conditions under which it remains authoritative). **Supports.** The levels of rules are a standing institutional-analysis formulation of this point. Operating rules govern conduct. The conditions under which they may be changed, and who may change them, are fixed at the collective-choice and constitutional levels.
- **[CLAIM-410](../claims.d/CLAIM-410.md)** (removing a constraint changes a profile of governing positions, not one scale). **Supports in part.** The seven rule types, especially position, boundary and authority, together with the three levels, give a richer profile than "more or less governance". This is consistent with [CLAIM-410](../claims.d/CLAIM-410.md)'s claim that authority can be eliminated, distributed or transferred. The chapter does not discuss deregulation as such.
- **[QUESTION-tmps5iou](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmps5iou.md)** (governed schema evolution as luria's distinctive feature). **Supplies the frame.** Schema change coupled to decision is a collective-choice arrangement; ADRs about how ADRs are made are constitutional. The chapter's insistence on rules-in-use suggests an empirical test: do agents' actual practices track the recorded decisions?
- **[LIT-2309](../literature.d/LIT-2309.md)** (Crawford, "A Grammar of Institutions", held but Deferred). The chapter cites Crawford and Ostrom (1995) for its definition of an institution. The two should be read together.

## Limitations

- This is a didactic chapter, not a research result.
- The examples are illustrative; the traffic example is described as "contrived (and quite exaggerated)".
- The reader did not read the policy-application examples in full.
