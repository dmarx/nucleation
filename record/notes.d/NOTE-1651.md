# NOTE-1651: Michaels et al. — Greenfield Sites and Services against slum upgrading in Tanzania, 35–40 years on

## Contribution

By the authors' account, the first long-run econometric evaluation of de
novo Sites and Services (p. 4). It tests whether one-off public
infrastructure, laid out before settlement and then left to residents,
still shows decades after the programme ended, and sets it against the same
kind of spending put into areas already built. The model adds a mechanism,
a two-way complementarity between public infrastructure and private
housing investment, with named failure conditions: too few owners able to
invest, and a legacy stock of low-quality housing.

## Key insight

Timing relative to settlement decides whether support takes. Put into empty
land where enough owners can build well, plots, roads and mains draw in
private investment, and that investment keeps the infrastructure up:
"infrastructure investment crowds in private investment in housing quality,
which in turn preserves infrastructure quality. This virtuous feedback,
however, does not occur in upgrading areas" (p. 3). Put into land already
covered with low-quality houses, the same spending meets a stock that owners
will not replace until it falls down, and the infrastructure decays.

## Assumptions

- **Identification (pp. 9–10).** Potential outcomes are continuous at the
  boundary of de novo and control areas, in bands of roughly 500 m. Control
  land was greenfield before the projects: 0.5% of de novo and 1.3% of
  control units were built before them, from noisy construction years in
  Mbeya and Mwanza (p. 10).
- **Controls were not untreated.** They received "significantly less
  infrastructure" but have some roads and water connections (p. 7). One
  later scheme in Dar es Salaam (Mbezi Beach) may overlap 10–15% of the
  control area there (App. p. 2).
- **Upgrading is not identified.** Upgrading areas were squatted before the
  projects, so present differences mix preexisting differences with the
  treatment; the authors learn about them "at least descriptively"
  (p. 7).
- **The model (§4, pp. 21–27).** Rent r = q^α I^(1−α) with convex building
  cost; infinitely lived owners, unconstrained (any number of plots, any
  investment) or constrained (one plot, low quality only). The authors
  "assume that high quality infrastructure deteriorates to medium quality
  unless the fraction of high quality housing is larger than a constant φ"
  (p. 22): φ is assumed, not estimated. No incremental improvement:
  rebuilding needs a fresh start, and "Relaxing this would reduce the
  benefit of early (de novo) investments" (fn. 52, p. 23). Houses are
  destroyed with probability d, which they cite as 3.2 percent a year from
  Kenya (fn. 53, p. 23).
- **Measurement.** The hedonic index is built from valuations in Arusha, a
  city without Sites and Services; owners are matched by full name.

## Key results

- **The support was one-off, and residents built (§2, App. p. 1).** "These
  projects provided basic infrastructure, leaving it to the residents to
  build their own houses" (p. 28); the bundle was "formal plots, roads, and
  water mains" (p. 7), built in 1975–76 and 1980–84.
- **Cost recovery largely failed.** "Collection rates were low and not
  timely" (App. p. 13); loans "were not fully repaid" (p. 7); the model was
  abandoned in the 1980s partly for the "difficulty of recouping Sites and
  Services costs" (p. 9).
- **Imagery, all seven cities (Table 1, p. 17).** Footprints "roughly 12
  percent larger" (log 0.114, s.e. 0.051), layout more regular, roof paint
  no different; z-index 0.168 (0.057). Doughnut RDs are larger (0.241),
  "suggesting that our baseline estimates may be a little attenuated due to
  spillovers" (p. 17).
- **Survey, three cities (Table 2, p. 17).** Footprints "about 50 percent
  (or 0.41 log points) larger" and buildings "about 23 percentage points (or
  48 percent) more likely to be connected to electricity"; z-index 0.342
  (0.091), log hedonic value 0.446 (0.081). Multi-storey and sanitation
  differences are "economically large but statistically imprecise".
- **Value against cost (p. 18).** "de novo may have increased local land
  values by at least $75-100 per square meter", against direct costs of no
  more than $8 and total costs of no more than $13. The step from house
  values to land values uses the model, the levels come from coarse Dar es
  Salaam data, and "the gains in other cities" may be lower.
- **Sorting (Table 3, p. 19).** "roughly a third of the quality advantage of
  de novo areas is accounted for by the different ownership"; with owner
  fixed effects the z-index is 0.447 against 0.642 without. Owner fixed
  effects cover only multi-unit owners. In 2012 "about 45 percent of the
  adults in de novo areas had no more than a primary school education"
  (p. 4).
- **Infrastructure persisted in de novo areas (Table 4, p. 20).** Road
  within 10 m +0.141 on a control mean of 0.202; road access +0.197 on
  0.477; water-mains connection +0.211 on 0.547: "showing that de novo
  investments translated into better infrastructure in the long run".
- **Upgrading: no gain, and decay (Tables A8–A11; p. 20–21).** Against
  nearby controls, road within 10 m −0.019 and water mains −0.078, neither
  significant; the survey shows "fewer multistory buildings, worse roofs,
  and possibly worse sanitation" (p. 20); against non-upgraded 1979 slums in
  Dar es Salaam, no different. The authors' explanation is "that those
  areas’ infrastructure deteriorated more than that of de novo areas"
  (p. 20), citing Kironde on "deterioration of roadside drainage due to lack
  of maintenance" (p. 21). First-round spending per square metre was $2.20
  in de novo and $2.37 in upgrading areas (p. 6).
- **The inaction zone (Prop. 1, p. 25).** With low-quality houses already
  built, an infrastructure upgrade into the "inaction zone" makes owners
  improve "only when it is exogenously destroyed"; only a larger upgrade
  triggers rebuilding at once.
- **The authors' caution (p. 27).** "if de novo investments were expanded
  widely, poor and credit constrained residents may be unable to make full
  use of them, since infrastructure may deteriorate without sufficient
  complementary private investment"; and "the importance of feedback from
  owner investments to infrastructure" is sometimes overlooked.
- **Created or displaced value.** Whether de novo areas created value or
  drew it from elsewhere: "it is difficult for us to answer this question
  definitively" (p. 15); they argue from regular layout and road access
  solving coordination failures.

## Concepts

- **De novo (greenfield) servicing against ex-post upgrading**: the same
  bundle of infrastructure before settlement or after; timing relative to
  settlement is the variable of interest.
- **Crowding in**: public plots and trunk infrastructure drawing private
  investment in housing.
- **Persistence by feedback**: infrastructure survives because the private
  investment it drew keeps it up, a neighbourhood externality.
- **Inaction zone**: an interval of infrastructure improvement within which
  owners of sunk low-quality stock do not rebuild until the house is
  destroyed.
- **Formal plots**: surveyed delineations meeting town-planning standards,
  giving tenure security, regular layout and protected rights of way.

## Connections

It sets itself against the literature on slum upgrading and serviced plots
(Laquian 1983; Mayo and Gross 1987; Buckley and Kalarickal 2006; Kironde on
Dar es Salaam), and borrows the no-renovation and destruction-rate
assumptions from Hornbeck and Keniston (2017) and Henderson et al. (2017).
In this batch it is the only work with a long-run spatial design on
infrastructure supplied once and then left to residents.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md) (the synthesis).** Supports its first and third
  conditions: one-off support (1975–84, with fees barely collected)
  started a loop that kept itself up, and residents did the building
  throughout. It adds two conditions the synthesis lacks: enough of the
  supported must be able to do the work (the share of unconstrained owners
  above φ), and the support must come before a competing configuration is
  in place (the built stock and the inaction zone). The second condition,
  the threat gone, maps only loosely onto formal plots resolving a
  coordination failure. Persistence is one snapshot; there is no time path
  and no withdrawal in the strict sense.
- **[CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md) (ignition, collapse, hysteresis).** Supports it by
  analogy. The model is a state-dependent upkeep: high-quality
  infrastructure holds only if the high-quality housing share is above φ,
  and otherwise falls to a lower level and stays there; the inaction zone is
  a hysteresis analogue. The empirics fit this but neither estimate φ nor
  show bistability.
- **[CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md) (support that does the work crowds out capacity).**
  Supports the ordering point loosely: the projects supplied only what
  residents could not do (plots, trunk roads, mains) and left the houses to
  them, and private investment was crowded in. The failure of upgrading is
  not a loss of learned capacity but sunk stock and credit constraints, so
  the mechanism differs from learning by doing.
- **[CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md) (supplied upkeep never becomes optional).** Supports it:
  where nothing local kept the infrastructure up, in upgrading areas, it
  deteriorated for lack of maintenance; where it persisted, the model
  credits owners' investment, not continued supply.
- **[CLAIM-603](../claims.d/CLAIM-603.md) (one-way against reciprocal supply).** Little bearing.
  The reciprocal element (ground rent and service charges) was mostly not
  collected, so the support was in effect one-way and one-off, and still did
  not create dependence; but nothing went on being supplied.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md) (independence from the founding intervention).** A
  positive case with conditions: planned neighbourhoods kept the
  intervention's structure for decades without it, given an empty starting
  state and enough capable participants; the same intervention on a
  built, poor state did not take. Intermediate states and the timing of
  withdrawal are not tested.
- **[THEORY-919](../theory.d/THEORY-919.md) (Juhász, temporary protection).** A parallel: a temporary,
  place-specific intervention leaving a persistent local advantage. Juhász's
  effect fades by 1896; this paper has no fade test. Its local
  identification is stronger, and its upgrading arm supplies a contrast
  Juhász lacks: the same spending on a different starting state.
- **Others.** [THEORY-1468](../theory.d/THEORY-1468.md) (Kadowaki et al.) is the closest held
  parallel to the upgrading result: a degraded state leaves a reinforcing
  legacy, so restoring inputs alone fails. [THEORY-1496](../theory.d/THEORY-1496.md) (Food for Peace,
  India) and [THEORY-1492](../theory.d/THEORY-1492.md) (Kenya) find continuation where the receiving
  side had working infrastructure and capacity before exit, the same
  receiving-capacity condition. [THEORY-1485](../theory.d/THEORY-1485.md) (Schröder et al.) is a
  reminder that the threshold here is modelled, not tested. With
  [THEORY-1480](../theory.d/THEORY-1480.md) and [THEORY-149](../theory.d/THEORY-149.md) (Ostrom), the persistence rests on the
  residents' own investment rather than on an outside supplier; [LIT-719](../literature.d/LIT-719.md) and
  [LIT-737](../literature.d/LIT-737.md) are the Ostrom works behind them. Unlike [THEORY-1483](../theory.d/THEORY-1483.md) (Azam et
  al.), there is no learning-by-doing mechanism.
- Filed: [THEORY-1472](../theory.d/THEORY-1472.md). No instruction for machine-learning practice;
  nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **One cross-section** (2012–2016 data), no baseline or intermediate
  outcomes: fading or growth cannot be seen.
- **Few units**: 12 de novo areas, 90 imagery clusters, 29 survey clusters
  in three cities; infrastructure in Tanga is uncertain (p. 7).
- **Upgrading is descriptive** (the authors' word); the comparison slums
  were about four times smaller (p. 10), and the poor were targeted.
- **φ is assumed in the model, not estimated**, and the no-incremental-
  improvement assumption is a modelling choice; the model is built to
  account for the findings, not tested against alternatives.
- **Value estimates are model-derived** from coarse Dar es Salaam data;
  displacement against creation is not identified (p. 15); spillovers to
  controls are possible (doughnut estimates are larger).
- **Sorting**: owner fixed effects cover only multi-unit owners; richer
  households moved in over time, and whether the loop needed that sorting
  is not separated.
- **Scale**: by the authors' caution, credit-constrained residents may not
  be able to sustain the loop if de novo projects were widened (p. 27).
- **The accepted manuscript, not the version of record**, was read.

## Open questions

- Does the de novo advantage grow, hold or fade over time? Repeated imagery
  between 1984 and 2016 would tell.
- Is there a threshold share of capable owners, or a smooth dose–response?
  Variation in owner composition across areas, with φ estimated, would
  tell.
- Would upgrading take if combined with formal plots or credit for
  rebuilding?
