# NOTE-2009: Lohmann et al. (2024): The Trauma and Mental Health Impacts of Coercive Control

## Contribution

The first pooled estimate of how exposure to coercive control co-varies with PTSD and depression symptoms, with a comparison against broader psychological IPV measured in the same studies. Preregistered (PROSPERO CRD42021252071); 68 studies reviewed, 45 with 107 effect sizes meta-analysed.

## Key results

1. Abstract: "These indicated moderate associations involving coercive control and PTSD (r = .32; 95% confidence interval [.28, .37]) and depression (r = .27; [.22, .31])".
2. Abstract: "These associations were comparable to those involving psychological IPV and PTSD (r = .34; [.25, .42]) and depression (r = .33; [.26, .40])".
3. Discussion: "It was unexpected that associations of coercive control with PTSD and depression would not be clearly stronger than associations involving other types of IPV"; among the reasons offered, "construct overlap remains likely and could explain similar effects".
4. Discussion: the comparison with physical IPV is with other meta-analyses, from which the authors infer that "these dimensions of coercive control are just as important and detrimental as physical IPV".
5. Results: the PTSD association was larger in DV services and shelters (r = .40) than "when compared to community settings (r = .26; [.16, .35])".
6. Limitations: "the findings included in the meta-analyses were cross-sectional and a direct causal link between coercive control and mental health could not be established".
7. Results: "Only one study reported on the relationship between coercive control and complex PTSD and meta-analyses could not be performed."

## Assumptions

That coercive control is captured by existing controlling-behaviour subscales (PMWI dominance/isolation, CBS-R, MMEA restrictive engulfment), which the Discussion itself doubts: "many measures may not fully capture whether respondents experience a threat that is embedded in a chronic pattern of power and control". Standardized betas were imputed as correlations where none was reported.

## Concepts

Coercive control after Stark (isolation, monitoring, deprivation of resources; terror and entrapment); Johnson's distinction between situational couple violence and intimate terrorism, with the same act able to fall in either: "Verbal threats can thus reflect situational couple violence or they can occur within the enduring pattern of domination that characterizes coercive control." Complex PTSD (ICD-11).

## Bearing on the record

- **The exchange** (A5 §1) reports the counts, the two correlations, the comparison with psychological abuse and with physical violence in other meta-analyses, and the observational caveat, all accurately. Its inference, "sustained control can be profoundly harmful even when physical assaults aren't the central feature", goes beyond the paper, which did not separate control from co-occurring physical violence and found moderate correlations. The LIT's earlier gloss, harm "beyond physical violence", is likewise more than was shown: the finding is comparability.
- **[CLAIM-709](../claims.d/CLAIM-709.md)** and **[CASE-279](../cases.d/CASE-279.md)**: no direct bearing. The review supports, from outside the exchange, the premise that one act can belong to a situational or a controlling pattern, and reports that measures struggle to tell which; it says nothing about responsibility.

## Limitations

Cross-sectional, self-report, high heterogeneity (I2 about 80-88%); 76% of studies recruited only women and 68% were in the US; overall risk of bias not rated; only one study of complex PTSD; the physical-IPV comparison is across reviews.
