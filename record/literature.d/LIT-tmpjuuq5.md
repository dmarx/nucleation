# LIT-tmpjuuq5: The lexicalist hypothesis: Both wrong and superfluous

Benjamin Bruening (2018), *Language (Linguistic Society of America) 94(1), 1–42* — DOI-10.1353/lan.2018.0000

## Key takeaways

- The lexicalist hypothesis, that a separate, encapsulated component builds words before phrasal syntax, is wrong and superfluous. Phrasal syntax feeds word formation, phrasal operations reach inside words, and the two obey the same principles. The facts it was meant to explain have independent explanations that never mention "word", so grammar needs only one combinatorial component.

*Seeded from the abstract and a skim, not a reading. What follows is what the work says about itself.*

The lexicalist hypothesis holds that the grammar component producing words is distinct and strictly separate from the one producing phrases. Bruening argues it is wrong for three reasons: phrasal syntax often feeds word formation, phrasal syntax can access subword parts, and claims that word formation and phrasal syntax follow different principles are false. It is also superfluous, because the facts it was supposed to explain have independent explanations. The most parsimonious model follows: one combinatorial component that builds both words and phrases.
