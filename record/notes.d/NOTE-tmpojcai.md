# NOTE-tmpojcai: Bruno et al. — self-assessed well-being in chronic locked-in syndrome

## Contribution

The largest survey of chronic locked-in patients' own ratings of their
lives at the time: a postal questionnaire to the membership of ALIS, the
French LIS association, measuring overall subjective well-being with a
biographically anchored scale, reintegration into normal life, and views
on resuscitation and euthanasia. It establishes that a majority of
surviving, association-member LIS patients rate their lives as good, and
names the correlates of the minority who do not.

## Key insight

Long survival in LIS is compatible with a life rated as good by the person
living it, and the rating improves with time in the condition, but the
sample that shows it is survivors who have, for the most part, recovered
some action: over half speak some words, most move some limb. The paper is
evidence about lucid evaluation over years in LIS, not about lucidity with
action reduced to an eyelid.

## Assumptions

- **Population.** Members of ALIS, invited by letter in January 2008;
  answers "aided by the patient's proxy". All included patients had chronic
  LIS (> 1 year) "following a brainstem vascular accident".
- **Definition.** LIS "is defined by quadriplegia (or quadriparesis) and
  aphonia (or severe hypophonia) with a primary mode of communication by
  eye movements or blinking", citing the 1995 American Congress of
  Rehabilitation Medicine nomenclature.
- **The forms.** "'classic' LIS is characterised by quadriplegia and
  aphonia with coded communication by vertical eye movement or blinking;
  'incomplete' LIS patients have remnants of voluntary motion other than
  vertical eye movement; and 'total' LIS is defined by complete
  immobility, including all eye movements", citing Bauer, Gerstenbrand and
  Rumpl (1979).
- **Measure.** The Anamnestic Comparative Self-Assessment (ACSA), whose +5
  and −5 anchors are "the patients' memories of the best period in their
  life before LIS and their worst period ever". Happy: ACSA 0 to +5;
  unhappy: −1 to −5. Reintegration by the RNLI on a 4-point scale.
- **Analysis.** Univariate tests, then backward stepwise logistic
  regression with casewise deletion; only questions answered by > 70%
  analysed.

## Key results

- **Response.** 168 invited; 77 (46%) did not reply; of 91 replies, 26
  excluded for missing RNLI or ACSA data (5 lacked RNLI, 21 lacked ACSA);
  65 analysed. Excluded respondents were less educated (p = 0.009) and had
  more pain (p = 0.009).
- **Sample.** Mean age 49 ± 11; median 8 years in LIS (range 1–28); 64%
  at home; 64% with a partner. Recovery of speech production: none 26/58
  (45%), words 11/58 (19%), sentences 21/58 (36%). Some limb mobility
  39/56 (70%).
- **Well-being.** 47 happy (72%, median ACSA +3), 18 unhappy (28%, median
  −4). RNLI: 51% severe and 49% mild-to-moderate restrictions in
  community reintegration; 82% comfortable with personal relationships;
  21% occupied most of the day in activities important to them.
- **Correlates of unhappiness (univariate).** Dissatisfaction with
  mobility in the community (p = 0.042), with recreational activities
  (p = 0.028) and with the capacity to deal with life events (p = 0.022);
  shorter time in LIS (median 7 vs 9 years, p = 0.005); non-recovery of
  speech (p = 0.049); anxiety (p = 0.015); depression (28% vs 7%,
  p = 0.040).
- **Multivariate (Table 3).** Duration in LIS OR 1.5 per year (95% CI
  1.1–2.0, p = 0.007); speech production OR 20.47 (1.89–221.45,
  p = 0.013); anxiety OR 0.19 (0.04–0.87, p = 0.032). The variables
  "explained only 38% of the variance of overall SWB".
- **End of life.** 58% (32/55) did not wish to be resuscitated in case of
  cardiac arrest, 86% of the unhappy and 49% of the happy; euthanasia
  envisaged by 53% (31/59); a current wish for euthanasia by 4 of 59 (7%).
  Suicidal thoughts often: 5/59 (8%).

## Concepts

- **Disability paradox.** People with severe persistent disability tend to
  report good subjective well-being.
- **ACSA.** A self-anchored scale whose ends are the respondent's own best
  and worst periods; chosen to reduce comparison with peers and
  "under-the-circumstances" responses.
- **Response shift / relativity bias.** Rating relative to peers or
  circumstances, which the ACSA is meant to limit; its own risk is a
  "nostalgia effect" that would depress current ratings.

## Connections

It extends a 15-patient quality-of-life study and Doble et al.'s
long-term cohort, and follows Cummins's homeostatic theory of subjective
well-being in interpreting the result. The forms of LIS come from Bauer et
al. (1979), and its cognitive claim from Schnakers et al. (2008). It points
to brain–computer interfaces (Kübler and Neumann 2005, in the same Progress
in Brain Research volume as Laureys et al. 2005, [LIT-tmpx97h7](../literature.d/LIT-tmpx97h7.md)) as expected
progress. Laureys is senior author here too.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-tmpxgcq2](../cases.d/CASE-tmpxgcq2.md).**
  - *The definition.* The CASE's quotation from the abstract is verbatim.
    The paper's own definition, in the Introduction, says LIS is
    quadriplegia "or quadriparesis" and aphonia "or severe hypophonia",
    with eye-coded communication "primary", and its three forms (classic,
    incomplete, total) are attributed to Bauer et al. (1979), not to
    Laureys et al. (2005). The CASE's "The literature distinguishes
    classic, incomplete and total forms (Laureys et al. 2005)" can be
    supported from this paper with the original source named.
  - *"Lucid over years".* Correct, with the qualifier the CASE does not
    state: this cohort is largely not on one eyelid. 55% had recovered
    some speech (36% sentences) and 70% some limb mobility, the
    Introduction notes that "many classic LIS patients may evolve to
    incomplete LIS", and answers were given with a proxy's help. So it
    shows lucid, evaluative life over years in people whose world-directed
    action was severely reduced but, in most, partly restored. The survey
    does not report how many were still in classic LIS.
  - *"Answered through the spared channels".* The paper says only that
    the questionnaire was filled in "aided by the patient's proxy" and
    adapted "Given the specific constraint of eye-coded communication";
    it does not report channels per patient.
- **[CLAIM-tmpq9do8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpq9do8.md).** The paper bears on the claim's "What it does not
  say" (the loop that keeps a built model in touch) only weakly. A
  biographically anchored self-rating is a judgement made against the
  person's own remembered past, maintained over a median of 8 years in a
  condition where action is reduced, and it moves with time (OR 1.5 per
  year). That is consistent with judgement keeping a model working with
  little action. It is not evidence for the thesis against active
  inference, because most respondents retained or recovered action, and
  speech recovery is the strongest correlate of happiness (OR 20.5): the
  data are equally read as action mattering to how well the life goes.
  Neither reading concerns whether the model stays in touch.
- **[CLAIM-tmpki5gp](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpki5gp.md), [QUESTION-tmpr5tck](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpr5tck.md).** No direct bearing. The authors'
  explanation of the minority, those "who cannot shed the needs and values
  from their previous life", is a judgement-side account of adaptation
  (recalibrating and reprioritising), not an action-side one, but it is
  offered as a hypothesis.
- **The LIT.** Its summary is right in its numbers. "Communication is
  classically by vertical eye movement or blinking" is the definition, not
  a description of this sample; the summary should say that most had
  recovered some speech or limb movement and that answers were given with
  a proxy. The published date can be the e-publication date, 23 February
  2011 (PMC).
- **For the anthology.** No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- 54% response, then 29% of responses excluded; association members,
  "indicating a stable condition and possibly a degree of social
  integration"; caregiver-assisted answers open to social desirability.
  The authors judge "most biases were likely to result in overestimations
  of SWB".
- Cross-sectional: the time-in-LIS association may be adaptation or
  "selection by attrition".
- Only vascular brainstem LIS; nothing on ALS or CLIS.
- Cognitive status was not measured; competence is asserted from the
  literature.

## Open questions

- Longitudinal: does well-being rise within patients over time, or do the
  unhappy die sooner? The authors call for this.
- For the record: how does well-being compare between those still in
  classic LIS and those who recovered speech or limb movement? The paper
  reports speech as a predictor but no split by LIS form.
