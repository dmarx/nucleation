summary: >-
  Rubin & Hewstone (1998) review over 40 studies of social identity theory's self-esteem hypothesis, in Hogg and Abrams's two corollaries: successful intergroup discrimination raises self-esteem (1), and low or threatened self-esteem motivates discrimination (2). They sort the measures by three distinctions, global or specific, personal or social, trait or state, and argue the theory implies specific social state self-esteem, while most studies used global personal trait measures such as Rosenberg's scale. Counting experiments, 18 support and 13 do not support corollary 1, but only 7 support and 28 do not support corollary 2; only one of eight full tests supports both. Nine studies find people with high personal self-esteem the most discriminatory, the reverse of corollary 2. So discrimination seems to raise self-esteem without being motivated by a need for it, which leaves the theory's motivational explanation without support. The authors attribute this to imprecision and propose a version in specific social state self-esteem, which the two studies that measured it support.

## Why this is here

Hornsey ([NOTE-tmp3dav9](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp3dav9.md)) calls the self-esteem hypothesis a dead end; Reimer et al. ([NOTE-tmp222eb](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp222eb.md)) call it well supported; both cite this review. Reading it settles which is right.
