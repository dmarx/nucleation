# LIT-735: The Epistemic Benefit of Transient Diversity

Kevin J. S. Zollman (2010), *Erkenntnis* 72(1):17–35 —
DOI-10.1007/s10670-009-9194-6

## Key takeaways

- **The case.** In the history of peptic ulcer disease as Zollman tells it,
  Palmer's widely read study turned research towards acid suppression and
  away from the bacterial hypothesis, until *H. pylori* was identified (§1).
- **The model** carries forward [LIT-724](LIT-724.md): a two-armed bandit with beta
  priors, simulated for 10,000 rounds, with success probabilities of 0.5
  and 0.499. Cycles beat wheels beat complete graphs, and over all
  six-agent networks denser networks are on average worse (Figures 3–4).
- **Dogmatism as a substitute.** As the upper bound on the prior parameters
  rises, the complete network becomes the best of the three and the cycle
  the worst (Figure 6). Sparse networks with dogmatic agents often fail to
  agree on anything within the run (§3).
- **Conclusion.** "At the heart of these models is one single virtue,
  transient diversity." Limiting information and extreme priors are two
  ways to get it, but "both together make the diversity too stable, and
  result in a worse situation than either individually."
- **Group versus individual.** The scientists look epistemically
  sub-optimal one by one and optimal as a community, which Zollman reads as
  confirming Hull's conjecture that the rational features of science
  belong to groups.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-05 at the owner's request, with [LIT-724](LIT-724.md). The account
is [THEORY-148](../theory.d/THEORY-148.md). For an essay on organizational will, the useful
result is the symmetry: the conditions that protect a minority view (low
connectivity, stubbornness) are the same ones that, combined, stop a group
from ever settling. That has the shape of the essay's "protective lock-in"
(§5.4), a protective regime that comes to sustain itself, in an epistemic
setting.
