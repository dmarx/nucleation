# CLAIM-1113: Mandated boards do not show lasting concern

## The objection

[CLAIM-1178](CLAIM-1178.md) reads the persistence of fewer violations after mandated
board changes as the redesign half of [CLAIM-432](CLAIM-432.md)'s part 5. Two things stand
against it.

1. **Who changed the board.** The changes were terms of the agreement. On
   [CLAIM-432](CLAIM-432.md)'s own sorting, a change the corporation was made to accept is
   the sanction's work. That it outlasts the agreement shows a sanction can
   leave a lasting structure, not that a corporation's own concerns were
   redesigned.
2. **What persists.** The outcome is penalised violations. A board that has
   learned what the enforcer punishes, and keeps the corporation clear of
   it, produces fewer penalties after the agreement for prudential reasons:
   it still pays, in avoided liability. That is the second row of
   [CLAIM-1169](CLAIM-1169.md)'s table, where both readings predict persistence.

So the result is consistent with [CLAIM-432](CLAIM-432.md) and with [CLAIM-431](CLAIM-431.md) alike.

## What it does not say

- That the changed boards do not weigh harms. The study cannot say either
  way.
- That board changes are worthless; only that their effect is not evidence
  for remorse over prudence.

## Why the run leans here

The coding rules (scratch/studies/lead-101/CODING.md) code a change as
redesign only when installed beyond what the sanction required, or kept
after it, with independent evidence; a mandated board change is settled by the
first test by construction (it fails it), and the study shows the
second only for fewer penalties, not for any harm-weighing the board
does.
