# THEORY-tmpai21e: Whether a person joins a fascist movement in a showdown is decided by inner disposition, whether she is secure and has a standard of her own that tells her what she likes and does not, and not by race, nationality, creed or class; humiliation, fear, spoilt entitlement and measuring worth by success make a person susceptible

## Source

Thompson (1941), [LIT-055](../literature.d/LIT-055.md), "Who Goes Nazi?" (*Harper's*, August 1941), read
in [NOTE-056](../notes.d/NOTE-056.md). Section labels are the essay's: an unnumbered opening, then II
and III.

## What was actually shown

Nothing is shown. The only evidence is the author's experience: "I have
gone through the experience many times—in Germany, in Austria, and in
France. I have come to know the types." The essay then sorts the guests at
an imagined party, calling itself a "macabre parlor game".

- **The thesis (§III).** "Kind, good, happy, gentlemanly, secure people
  never go Nazi." "Their race, color, creed, or social condition is not the
  criterion. It is something in them." What protects is having "anything in
  them to tell them what they like and what they don't".
- **Those who would.** Mr. B, the banker, early, once the movement looks
  likely to win. Mr. C, the humiliated scholarship-boy intellectual, "the
  product of a democracy hypocritically preaching social equality and
  practicing a carelessly brutal snobbery". Young D, the spoiled son. Mrs.
  E, humiliated and looking for a hero who proclaims women's subordination.
  L, the labour leader.
- **Those who would not.** Mr. A, an editor from an old family with his own
  code (set against B's code, which "is not his own; it is that of his
  class"). Mrs. F, a happy former actress. James the butler. Bill, an
  engineering student. H, a historian who "will grab a gun and fight". A
  German émigré, a former Reichsbanner member. Mr. K, a Southern Jewish
  writer.
- **Against race as criterion.** "I know lots of Jews who are born
  Nazis"; Mr. J, a Jewish financier, "agrees with Mr. C wholly".

**What could have come out otherwise.** Nothing could, in the essay. A
study of who joined could show class or status predicting support better
than disposition does.

## What this does not say

- **That any case is real.** The sketches are composites or inventions at
  a hypothetical party ([NOTE-056](../notes.d/NOTE-056.md), C2).
- **That the typology is coherent.** D is "the only born Nazi in the room"
  and L "the strongest natural-born Nazi". G "will never be a Nazi" and
  "would be a Nazi with purse-lipped qualifications".
- **That class is not the criterion, on her own evidence.** B, C, L, James
  and Bill are drawn by class position and class resentment (C7), and the
  portrait of L reads as an attack on union "compulsion".
- **That disposition is all of it.** She also calls Nazism "the disease of
  a generation" (C5) and floats "direct biological factors" of feeding and
  training (C6), neither integrated with the typology.
- **That later research confirms it.** [NOTE-056](../notes.d/NOTE-056.md) did not check the
  authoritarian-personality, status-anxiety or voting-data literatures.
  Its reading says any document using the essay as evidence about who
  supports authoritarian movements should cite that literature instead.
