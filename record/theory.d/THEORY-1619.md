# THEORY-1619: Otten reports that minimal-group categorization, with no personal stake in the allocations, reliably produces in-group favouritism alongside a strong pull towards fairness, that projecting the positive personal self onto the new group is a crucial route to it, and that people allowed to pay themselves take the most for themselves and favour the in-group with the rest

## The account

The paradigm was built to remove self-interest, so that "no
utilitarian self-interests can be directly served" (p. 85), and what is
left still favours the in-group. Social identity theory explains it by a
motive to "derive meaning and a positive self-concept" from membership
(p. 85). Self-anchoring gives another route: the new group is favoured
because the person's positive self-view is projected onto it, "rather
than a comparison with the respective outgroup" (p. 86). Which route
operates, projection or self-stereotyping, depends on how much is known
about the group. When participants could also allocate to themselves,
they kept the most and favoured the in-group with the rest.

## Evidence

Asserted from Tajfel et al. 1971, Brewer 1979 and Hewstone 2002, and
from two studies of self-allocation from one lab, all without figures
([NOTE-1301](../notes.d/NOTE-1301.md)).

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-300](../questions.d/QUESTION-300.md) asks for "a criterion of the self's extent that can
come apart from caring". Projection of one's own traits onto the group
is such a criterion, measured by self–group trait overlap, and Otten
reports it predicts identification and bias. The self-allocation result
shows an order within any extended self, with the personal self paid
first.
