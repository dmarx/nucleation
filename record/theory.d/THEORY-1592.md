# THEORY-1592: Smith and Mackie hold that identifying with a group makes it an aspect of the self through self-categorization, so that events are appraised for what they mean to the group and emotions are felt on its behalf, with the emotion shifting with the salient membership and identification moderating it

## The account

Appraisal theory required an object appraised as "affecting me
personally as an individual" (Arnold 1960, quoted p. 3). Intergroup
emotions theory puts the group there by way of the self: "when people
identify with a group, the group identity becomes an aspect of the self
through self-categorization. Like any aspect of the self, the group then
becomes imbued with affective significance" (p. 3). A group-based
emotion is "experienced by individuals on behalf of a group to which
they belong and with which they identify" (p. 3). Categorization "sets
the stage"; identification, "the importance and centrality of a group
membership to the individual", moderates (p. 4).

## Evidence

- More pride and less disgust thinking of oneself as a student of an
  admired university than as a citizen of a disliked country (p. 3).
- Guilt for in-group misdeeds one did not commit (Doosje et al. 1998)
  (pp. 3–4).
- The highly identified converge more on group norms but may reappraise
  to avoid guilt; they appraise in-group aggression as more justified
  (pp. 4, 7).
- Group-based emotions predict collective action better than cognitive
  perceptions; contempt more than anger predicts extreme violence (pp.
  5–6). Regulation studies are recent and few (pp. 8–10).
