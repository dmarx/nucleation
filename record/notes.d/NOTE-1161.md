# NOTE-1161: Chaloner and Verdinelli — Bayesian experimental design: a review

## Contribution

It puts Bayesian design on one footing, Lindley's (1972) decision-theoretic
formulation. Many criteria in use become special cases of maximizing
expected utility, and others are shown to have no utility behind them. It
surveys linear, analysis-of-variance and nonlinear designs within that
frame and says which approximations are being made. It also says where
practice lags: "Apart from Flournoy (1993) there are no true 'case
studies'".

## Key insight

A design is chosen before the data, so it is the one place in Bayesian
theory where averaging over the sample space is legitimate: "Experimental
design is the only situation where it is meaningful within the Bayesian
theory to average over the sample space." The right criterion is then
fixed by what the experiment is for. "A design that is optimal for
estimation is not necessarily also optimal for prediction." So choosing
among D, A, c and other criteria is choosing a utility, not a matter of
taste.

## Assumptions

- **Lindley's formulation (§1.3).** A design η is chosen from a set ℋ,
  data y are observed, and a terminal decision d is taken, with utility
  U(d, θ, η, y). The design maximizes
  U(η) = ∫ max_d ∫ U(d, θ, η, y) p(θ | y, η) p(y | η) dθ dy.
- **Approximate designs (§1.4).** A design is a probability measure η on a
  compact design region X, with the integrality of nη_i relaxed. The
  information matrix is nM(η) = n Σ η_i x_i x_iᵀ.
- **The linear model (§2.1).** y | θ, σ² ~ N(Xθ, σ²I) with σ² known, and
  prior θ | σ² ~ N(θ₀, σ²R⁻¹), R known. The posterior covariance is
  σ²D(η) = σ²(nM(η) + R)⁻¹.
- **Nonlinear models (§4.2).** Normal approximations to the posterior,
  using the expected Fisher information nI(θ, η), with or without R, and
  the prior used as the predictive distribution of θ̂.

## Key results

- **Bayes D-optimality (§2.2).** Expected Shannon information of the
  posterior, equivalently expected Kullback–Leibler divergence from prior
  to posterior, reduces in the linear model to maximizing
  φ₁(η) = det{nM(η) + R}. Spezzaferri's scoring-rule utility and Tiao and
  Afonja's two-valued utility lead to the same criterion. Re-derived in
  [THEORY-956](../theory.d/THEORY-956.md).
- **Bayes A- and c-optimality (§2.2).** Quadratic loss (θ − θ̂)ᵀA(θ − θ̂)
  gives expected utility −σ² tr{AD(η)}. With A = ccᵀ this is
  c-optimality, the criterion for one linear combination cᵀθ, or for
  prediction at a fixed point.
- **E- and G-optimality** "appear not to correspond to any utility
  function", so their Bayesian justification "is unclear".
- **The prior's weight (§2.2).** D(η) = n⁻¹(M(η) + n⁻¹R)⁻¹, so Bayesian
  and non-Bayesian linear designs differ little when n is large or R
  small. A proper prior keeps nM + R nonsingular even when nM is singular.
- **Unknown variance (§2.5).** With a conjugate normal–inverse-gamma prior
  the D and predictive criteria have no closed form. Bayes A-optimality
  does not depend on what is known about σ², which makes it robust in that
  sense.
- **ANOVA (§3).** The Bayes A-optimal allocation to a control and t − 1
  treatments tends to the square-root rule as the priors become vague,
  and can put no observations on the control when its prior is precise.
  Hierarchical (exchangeable) priors change the optimal designs: as the
  treatments are believed more alike, half the observations go to the
  control.
- **Nonlinear criteria (§4).** φ₁(η) = ∫ log det{nI(θ, η)} p(θ) dθ (Bayes
  D), and φ₂(η) = −∫ c(θ)ᵀ{nI(θ, η)}⁻¹c(θ) p(θ) dθ for a function g(θ)
  with gradient c(θ) (Bayes c). Versions with R added inside are also
  given. The authors prefer the versions without R: they correspond to
  designing with an informative prior and analysing with a
  noninformative one.
- **Local optimality (§4.4)** is the crude approximation that replaces the
  prior by a point mass at a best guess θ₀ (Chernoff).
- **The equivalence theorem (§5.1).** For a criterion that averages a
  concave local criterion over the prior, a design is optimal iff the
  directional derivative toward every one-point design is nonpositive.
  For Bayes D: d(η, x) = ∫ tr{I(θ, η_x) I(θ, η)⁻¹} p(θ) dθ − k ≤ 0. It
  holds under stated regularity conditions (a finite criterion,
  continuity on ℋ, continuous derivatives).
- **Support points (§5.2).** In linear problems Carathéodory bounds the
  number of support points, and D-optimal designs often have as many as
  there are parameters. For nonlinear Bayesian designs there is no such
  bound, and in the reported examples the number grows as the prior
  becomes more dispersed (Chaloner and Larntz).
- **Sequential design (§5.5).** In linear problems with σ² known there is
  "nothing to be gained by designing sequentially", because the posterior
  utility depends on the design and not on the data. In nonlinear
  problems there should be a gain.
- **Turning point (§7.2).** For estimating −θ₁/(2θ₂) in a quadratic
  regression on [−1, 1] with best guess ½, the locally optimal design
  puts half its mass at 0 and half at 1. That gives a singular nM of rank
  2, from which the quadratic, and hence the turning point, cannot be
  fitted: "The locally optimal design is therefore useless". Re-derived in
  [THEORY-954](../theory.d/THEORY-954.md).
- **Model discrimination (§8.3).** Spezzaferri's Bayesian criterion for
  choosing between two linear models minimizes the expected posterior
  probability of one model when the other is true. For nested normal
  models under diffuse priors it coincides with D-optimality for testing
  that the extra parameters are zero. For discrimination and estimation
  together, the criterion is a product of the smaller model's
  information determinant and that expected posterior probability.
- **Mixtures of models (§8.2).** From a Bayesian standpoint one averages
  expected utilities, not efficiencies: "expected utility should be
  maximized, not expected efficiency."
- **Examples 2 and 3 (§§6.2, 6.4).** The designs the laboratories actually
  used were close to Bayes-optimal under the more dispersed priors. For
  example, the six-dose bioassay had a φ₂ value 1.13 times the optimum
  under a uniform prior, against 1.52 under a Beta(4,4) prior. Designs
  efficient for one quantity, such as AUC, can be very inefficient for
  others (t_max, C_max).

## Concepts

- **Design measure η**: the proportions of observations at each design
  point. It is approximate when the integrality of nη_i is relaxed.
- **Information matrix nM(η)**: XᵀX = n Σ η_i x_i x_iᵀ. The Fisher
  information is σ⁻²nM. In nonlinear models nI(θ, η) depends on θ.
- **Bayes D-, A-, c-, E-, G-optimality**: the criteria above, with R
  inside.
- **Local optimality**: optimal for a single best guess θ₀.
- **Equivalence theorem**: the directional-derivative check of global
  optimality over ℋ.

## Connections

It follows Raiffa and Schlaifer (1961) and Lindley (1956, 1972), with
Stone, DeGroot and Bernardo (1979) on Shannon information as utility. It
gives the Bayesian side of Kiefer and Wolfowitz's alphabetical theory
(Fedorov 1972, Silvey 1980, Pukelsheim 1993) and of Elfving's theorem
(Chaloner 1984), and Whittle's (1973) form of the equivalence theorem.
DasGupta (1995) is named as a complementary review. Berry and Fristedt
(1985) on bandit problems is the sequential literature it does not
review.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-031](../questions.d/QUESTION-031.md)'s note of 2026-10-25, checked.** The note's
  G_ξ = Σ_c ξ(c)G_c is the review's nM(η) = n Σ η_i x_i x_iᵀ, generalized
  to the expected Fisher information nI(θ, η) of §4.2. Its log det is
  Bayes D-optimality ([THEORY-956](../theory.d/THEORY-956.md)). A29's point that the task fixes
  the criterion is the review's central thesis. Distinguishing two
  effects, rather than estimating every parameter, is c- or Ds-
  optimality, or Spezzaferri's discrimination criterion. The review does
  not answer the question as posed. Design chooses among probe families
  by a criterion. It gives no condition under which a family separates
  what a decision family needs.
- **[CASE-159](../cases.d/CASE-159.md)'s design problem.** The case is a nonlinear design
  problem in the review's sense: its information depends on the share
  being estimated, I_rare(π) = 0.64/(p(1 − p)) with p = 0.1 + 0.8π. So a
  design must average over a prior on π (§4.2) or fix a best guess
  (§4.4). In the two-specialist case the answer does not depend on π:
  the routine context has zero information at every π, so every criterion
  that increases with information puts all observations on the rare
  context. With three processes the question is nontrivial. In an
  illustration computed here, with four binary contexts (one where all
  agree, two that separate different pairs, one nearly redundant) and
  π = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2), the D-optimal design puts weight ½ on each of the
  two separating contexts. Its sensitivities tr(G_ξ⁻¹G_c) are 2, 2, 0 and
  0.01 against the dimension 2, which is the equivalence theorem's
  certificate. Its log det is −0.42, against −1.80 for the uniform
  design and −5.02 for a design spending 85% on the routine context.
  Each binary context contributes information of rank one, so at least
  k − 1 contexts with non-parallel profile differences are needed. The
  construction is the record's.
- **Sequential probing.** Because [CASE-159](../cases.d/CASE-159.md) is nonlinear in this
  sense, choosing probes adaptively should gain over a fixed design
  (§5.5). That is the design-side counterpart of the exploration results
  in [NOTE-1164](NOTE-1164.md) and [NOTE-1159](NOTE-1159.md).
- **[CLAIM-130](../claims.d/CLAIM-130.md).** The review treats ethical constraints only through the
  clinical examples ("the choice of the treatments to be compared is a
  central ethical issue"). It has nothing on a probe that changes the
  act it measures, the caution A29 raised.
- **[CLAIM-450](../claims.d/CLAIM-450.md), [CLAIM-444](../claims.d/CLAIM-444.md).** Nothing here bears on the garbling
  inequality. That nM(η) adds over independent observations is the
  additivity [CASE-159](../cases.d/CASE-159.md)'s sum over contexts assumes.

Statistical theory read for itself. No instruction for ML practice.

## Limitations

- **A 1995 review.** Simulation-based and MCMC design (Müller and
  Parmigiani are named as undemonstrated) and everything after are
  absent.
- **Mostly normal linear models.** Nonlinear results are approximations
  whose accuracy is "not much known" in general.
- **No general theory of support points** for nonlinear Bayesian designs.
- **Discrimination is brief**: two linear models, Spezzaferri's utility.
- **Technical-report copy**, with equations lost to OCR. It was not
  compared with the published version.

## Open questions

- Which utility, if any, justifies E-optimality? The review leaves it
  open.
- For a mixture model whose components' response profiles are known, as
  in [CASE-159](../cases.d/CASE-159.md), what is the Bayes-optimal adaptive probing policy,
  and how much does it gain over the best fixed design?
