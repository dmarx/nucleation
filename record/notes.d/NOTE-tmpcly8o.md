# NOTE-tmpcly8o: Schwitzgebel & Sinnott-Armstrong — Sacrificing humans

## Contribution

Scientists increasingly take seriously that insects may be sentient and that AI systems may soon be. If sentience or consciousness grounds moral standing, insects in aggregate, or near-future AI systems individually or in aggregate, might matter enough that their interests outweigh humanity's. That would radically deprioritize humans in ethics. The review examines Birch's The Edge of Sentience, Sebo's The Moral Circle and Keane's Animals, Robots, Gods. It finds that all three put forward arguments with apparently radical implications but play those implications down and offer fairly conservative near-term recommendations.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1 (The Possibly Radical Ethical Implications): we know little about what consciousness is or which entities have it, and little about its ethical role. Panpsychism and illusionism each break one direction of the consciousness–moral-status link. A worked example: an ethical premise (E1: concern proportional to capacity for valenced experience) plus a scientific premise (S1: insects have at least a millionth of human valenced capacity) makes the roughly 10^19 insects outweigh humanity.
- §2 (Birch's Principles) quotes Framework Principles 1–2: a duty to avoid gratuitous suffering, and "sentience candidature can warrant precautions". Birch's endpoint is a slow, democratic proportionality process.
- §3 (Sebo's Probabilities) sets out epistemically live views, including AI and insect moral patiency and the equal weight of moral patients. It discusses Sebo's "rebugnant conclusion" and "Pascal's bugging" (from his 2023 article). Sebo accepts in principle that small or large non-human populations may be the best future, but offers contingent practical grounds for prioritizing humans now.
- §4 (Keane's Cultures) describes the book as anthropological observation rather than argument, "giddy" in pace, and ending in caution against interfering with cultural practices.
- §5 (What Makes Humans Special?): consciousness, agency, intellect and creativity are all under pressure from AI and animal science as grounds of human specialness. The key conditional is plausible, and whether to accept the consequent or reject the antecedent (modus tollens) needs further argument. The reviewers explicitly do not endorse the radical conclusion.
