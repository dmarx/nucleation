# LIT-tmp8hcfz: Of Microperception and Micropolitics: An Interview with Brian Massumi, 15 August 2008

Massumi, B., & McKim, J. (2009). Of microperception and micropolitics: An
interview with Brian Massumi, 15 August 2008. *Inflexions: A Journal for
Research-Creation*, 3 (Micropolitics: Exploring Ethico-Aesthetics),
October 2009. https://www.inflexions.org/n3_massumihtml.html. Reprinted as
"Of microperception and micropolitics", ch. 2 of Massumi, *Politics of
Affect* (Polity, 2015), [LIT-tmpe2cnn](LIT-tmpe2cnn.md).

Read in full in [NOTE-tmpyf5pn](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpyf5pn.md).

## Key takeaways

- Affect is "an event, or a dimension of every event" (p. 1). The base
  definition is Spinoza's "ability to affect or be affected", with its
  second part as Deleuze took it up: "a power to affect and be affected
  governs a transition, where a body passes from one state of capacitation
  to a diminished or augmented state of capacitation. This comes with the
  corollary that the transition is felt" (p. 1).
- The definition is offered as a problematic field: "It's not a general
  definition that you can apply ... Rather than a definition, what you
  have is a proposition" (p. 3). "To start in the middle is precisely not
  to perform a phenomenological reduction" (p. 3).
- The subject emerges from affect, not the reverse. Calling affect
  "intersubjective" misleads if it assumes preconstituted subjects (p. 3).
- Bodies cued by the same microshock are "attuned – differentially"; there
  is "affective difference in the same event" (p. 6).
- Affect "is not a content that can be transported from one event to
  another ... It's an event logic, not a logic of transmission or
  communication" (p. 9).
- Micropolitics re-cues the "surplus of unacted-out potential that is
  collectively felt" (p. 7). Its aim is not consensus but to modulate
  contrasting tendencies "into a symbiosis: a cross-fertilization of
  capacitations" (p. 12).

## Why this is here

Bencherki, Brummans & Vézy ([LIT-tmp3mudg](LIT-tmp3mudg.md)) take their definition of affect
from Massumi 2015, p. 48, which reprints this interview. They quote it on
p. 1353: "a power to affect and be affected [that] governs a transition,
where a body passes from one state of capacitation to a diminished or
augmented state of capacitation. This comes with the corollary that the
transition is felt". They also quote "a dimension of every event" (p. 47).
Both are word for word here (p. 1); the bracketed "[that]" is theirs.
"Emphasis in original" could not be checked from the text extraction.

On reading, the quotation is accurate and the use is partly faithful:

- **Whose definition.** The parent says "Massumi consequently defines
  affect as". Massumi gives it as "the second part of the Spinozan
  definition taken up by Deleuze", and declines to treat it as a
  definition at all: "It's not a general definition that you can apply"
  (p. 3). The parent then applies it, "Extending Massumi's work, we define
  agency as the affective force that governs the transition from a lesser
  to an augmented ability to act" (p. 1354).
- **Direction.** Massumi's transition runs both ways, "diminished or
  augmented". The parent keeps only the augmenting direction for agency.
  That is the parent's choice, not Massumi's account ([THEORY-tmpcaccx](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpcaccx.md)).
- **Transmission.** The parent says that what "passes" or "is
  communicated" from one individuation to another is "excess action"
  (p. 1356). Massumi says affect "is not a content that can be
  transported from one event to another ... not a logic of transmission or
  communication" (p. 9). The parent's "communication" is transduction, not
  transmission (p. 1356), which narrows the gap, but its examples (an
  employee's indignation that "spills over into her inert colleagues", p.
  1354) read as transfer. Massumi's term for the shared case is
  differential "affective attunement" (p. 6). See [THEORY-tmpywwei](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpywwei.md).
- **Starting in the middle.** The parent's "start in the middle" (p. 1358)
  is cited to Manning 2013 and Massumi 2011; this interview states it
  directly (pp. 1, 3).
