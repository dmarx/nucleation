# LIT-tmp45885: True Believers: The Intentional Strategy and Why It Works

Daniel C. Dennett (1981), "True Believers: The Intentional Strategy and Why
It Works", in A. F. Heath (ed.), *Scientific Explanation: Papers Based on
Herbert Spencer Lectures Given in the University of Oxford*, Oxford:
Clarendon Press, 1981. Revised as chapter 2 of *The Intentional Stance*
(MIT Press, 1987; [LIT-tmpquosh](LIT-tmpquosh.md)), and reprinted in J. Haugeland (ed.), *Mind
Design II* (MIT Press, 1997), pp. 57–80, which is the text read here.

**On the citation.** The brief's citation (1981, Heath (ed.), *Scientific
Explanation*, Oxford; reprinted in *The Intentional Stance*) is right as far
as it could be checked: the volume, editor, publisher and year are confirmed
by its Open Library record, and the reprint by the 1987 book's catalogue
contents. Three things to add:

- **Pages.** The 1981 page range could not be confirmed; the 1981 volume
  was not reachable and PhilPapers was blocked.
- **Which text.** The text read is the revised 1987 version, as reprinted
  in 1997. Its own citations postdate 1981. What changed between the
  printings is not known here.
- **Identifier.** The 1981 Clarendon volume has no DOI. The DOI here is
  the *Mind Design II* chapter, the text actually read; `published:` is the
  first appearance, 1981, by year only, since no source gives a month.

## Key takeaways

- **The thesis (p. 59).** "While belief is a perfectly objective phenomenon
  (that apparently makes me a realist), it can be discerned only from the
  point of view of one who adopts a certain predictive strategy, and its
  existence can be confirmed only by an assessment of the success of that
  strategy (that apparently makes me an interpretationist)."
- **The criterion (pp. 59, 72).** "Any object … whose behavior is well
  predicted by this strategy is in the fullest sense of the word a
  believer. What it is to be a true believer is to be an intentional
  system, a system whose behavior is reliably and voluminously predictable
  via the intentional strategy."
- **Free choice, objective success (p. 67).** "The decision to adopt the
  intentional stance is free, but the facts about the success or failure
  of the stance, were one to adopt it, are perfectly objective." Dennett
  says his earlier presentations "sometimes invited" the radical
  interpretationist reading, and he "now want[s] to discourage it".
- **The lectern is excluded (p. 66)** because ascribing it beliefs gives no
  predictive power we did not already have. People, animals and computers
  are included because often "the only strategy that is at all practical is
  the intentional strategy".
- **Martians (pp. 68–71).** Laplacean super-physicists who predict Wall
  Street particle by particle, but do not see us as intentional systems,
  "would be missing something perfectly objective: the patterns in human
  behavior that are describable from the intentional stance, and only from
  that stance". Where there are intelligent beings, "the patterns must be
  there to be described".
- **Thermostat to person (pp. 72–75).** Enriching a thermostat's links to
  the world shrinks the class of satisfactory interpretations until one is
  "practically (but never in principle)" dictated. "There is no magic
  moment in the transition from a simple thermostat to a system that
  really has an internal representation of the world."
- **Why it works (pp. 75–77).** The easy answer is that evolution designed
  us to be rational. The hard answer, how the machinery works, "we just do
  not know". The language of thought is a good bet, "what else could it
  be?", but its rightness is "far from assured".

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request for Dennett. It sits between two
papers already here: "Intentional Systems" ([LIT-tmpt1t9z](LIT-tmpt1t9z.md), read in
[NOTE-tmpcpry5](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpcpry5.md)), whose observer-relative definition it retracts in favour of
objective success, and "Real Patterns" ([LIT-220](LIT-220.md), read in [NOTE-190](../notes.d/NOTE-190.md)), which
turns this essay's Martian moral, that the intentional patterns are real,
into a compression criterion. [NOTE-tmpfghc1](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpfghc1.md) is the close reading, and
placed it **Active**.

**On groups.** Its criterion is indifferent to what a system is made of,
and it is generous: clams, plants and, "a little bit", lightning are
systems on which the strategy works (pp. 65–66), and the lectern is out
only because the strategy buys no prediction. So on its own terms a nation
or a corporation is a true believer exactly if treating *it*, as a unit,
as a rational agent yields reliable and voluminous predictions that
nothing cheaper yields. The essay's examples of institutional prediction
(a Cabinet that "would support his decision", the consequences of letting
Oxford colleges grant degrees, a stock market whose "societal sockets"
absorb variation) are all reached through the reasons of individual
people, and none ascribes a belief to the institution as such. **Dennett
does not say, in this essay, that a nation or a corporation is a true
believer.** He never raises the question.

That matters for [THEORY-tmp31lxe](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp31lxe.md), which says the readings on the
society-of-mind bridge agree that agency survives in both directions. This
is the criterion that makes the agreement cheap: on it, the question whether
a group *believes* is the empirical question whether the group-level
stance pays. List ([LIT-tmp6phwb](LIT-tmp6phwb.md), read in [NOTE-tmp2ro60](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp2ro60.md)) grants organised
groups agency in a thin functionalist sense on independent grounds. It says nothing about experience, so it
leaves the bridge's dispute where that account puts it. Schwitzgebel
([LIT-159](LIT-159.md), read in [NOTE-131](../notes.d/NOTE-131.md)) answers in §6 a pragmatic objection Dennett sent
him in correspondence; this essay's insistence on predictive success
"reliably and voluminously" is the nearest published ancestor of that
objection, though the objection there concerns consciousness, not belief.

Other neighbours. Goldstein & Lederman ([LIT-206](LIT-206.md), read in [NOTE-138](../notes.d/NOTE-138.md)) state an
"objective interpretationism" for language models that builds on Dennett;
they deny thermostats beliefs, where this essay grants a thermostat "about
half a dozen" deinterpreted ones (p. 72). Roberts ([LIT-207](LIT-207.md), read in
[NOTE-157](../notes.d/NOTE-157.md)) argues the stance "does not go far enough" for chatbots, citing
this book's p. 49. Searle ([LIT-tmp543s0](LIT-tmp543s0.md)) answers McCarthy's thermostat
beliefs, which this essay recommends (n. 4), with "the mind is everywhere". Levin
([LIT-tmpsygq0](LIT-tmpsygq0.md)) takes the stance's level-choice rule from the 1987 book. Its
bearing on the AI-consciousness account [THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md) is the same as the 1971
paper's: belief ascription to a machine, however well earned, is not
evidence of experience on the essay's own terms. On Chalmers's hard problem
([LIT-tmpbxqmy](LIT-tmpbxqmy.md)) it is silent.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice, and the anthology
does not hold it. Its bearing on language models is through [LIT-206](LIT-206.md) and
[LIT-207](LIT-207.md), which are filed here.
