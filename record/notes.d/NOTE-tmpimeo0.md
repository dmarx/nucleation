# NOTE-tmpimeo0: Thürmer, Scheier & Carver — Coasting and shifting

## Contribution

The first experimental demonstration, the authors say, of both halves of
the control-process prediction for running ahead: less on the goal that is
ahead (coasting) and more on a competing goal (shifting). Goal progress was
manipulated by false feedback, and performance, not self-report, was the
outcome, which answers the common-method objection to Fulford et al.
(2010). A second experiment adds a manipulation aimed at affect.

## Key insight

With two goals that trade off, news that one is ahead moves performance to
the other, and the move seems to run through feeling: tell people to ignore
their feelings and it stops. Being ahead does not just relax effort; it
reorders which goal performance serves.

## Assumptions

- **The two-loop model** (Introduction): a rate loop whose output "is
  affective in nature: Negative affect is proposed to occur when progress
  is slower than expected or needed and positive affect when progress is
  faster than expected, needed, or desired ... Negative affect then leads
  to investing more effort (pushing) and positive affect leads to
  investing less effort (coasting)."
- **Carver 2003 for shifting** (Introduction): "Carver (2003) argued that
  people generally pursue many goals at once, even with respect to a single
  task. He suggested that fast goal progress toward one goal can lead to an
  openness toward alternative goals where progress is slower (shifting)."
  And (The Present Research): "Carver (2003 ...) suggests that coasting may
  occur mainly when one can shift to another goal."
- **The task's two goals are negatively interdependent** (speed–accuracy
  trade-off), and participants were told so (General Discussion).
- **Performance stands for effort.** Speed and accuracy were measured, not
  effort (General Discussion, first limitation).

## Key results

- **Experiment 1 design.** 151 MTurk workers (of 173), 2 progress
  (overshoot, undershoot) × 2 dimension (speed, accuracy) plus an on-target
  control. 200 lexical-decision trials with a target of 120 fast (< 550 ms)
  and 120 correct responses for a 50-cent bonus; false bar-graph feedback
  after 100 trials (90 against 30 against 60 on the manipulated
  dimension, 60 on the other). Mood (−3 to 3) and planned effort (0–6)
  rated before and after feedback.
- **Experiment 1, accuracy.** The planned contrast on Round 2 errors,
  covarying Round 1, F(1,145) = 15.67, p < .001, ηp² = .098. Overshoot on
  accuracy gave more errors than undershoot on accuracy (coasting),
  F = 7.01, p = .005, ηp² = .046; overshoot on speed gave fewer errors than
  undershoot on speed (shifting), F = 11.85, p < .001, ηp² = .076.
- **Experiment 1, speed.** Contrast F(1,145) = 7.29, p = .004, ηp² = .048.
  Overshoot on speed gave slower Round 2 responses (coasting), F = 3.27,
  p = .037, ηp² = .022; overshoot on accuracy gave faster ones (shifting),
  not significant, F = 2.02, p = .079. The control condition's regression
  slope differed; without it the contrast held, F(1,118) = 5.07, p = .013.
- **Experiment 1, mood and effort.** Overshoot left mood higher than
  undershoot at Round 2, covarying Round 1, T = −4.11, p < .001. In the raw
  means (Table 1) the change was −0.09 and −0.06 after overshoot, −0.96 and
  −0.69 after undershoot, and −0.04 in the control: being told one was
  behind lowered mood, and being told one was ahead did not raise it.
  Self-reported effort did not differ (p = .161), near ceiling (about 5.7
  of 6) throughout.
- **Experiment 2 design.** 222 CMU students, 2 progress (accuracy overshoot,
  undershoot) × 2 plan. Before feedback, an emotion-regulation plan ("When
  I'm working on the word recognition task, then I will ignore my
  feelings") or a control plan ("... I will ignore the pictures"), each
  typed three times after viewing Rothko paintings. Speed feedback on
  target in both. No mood or effort ratings.
- **Experiment 2, coasting.** Contrast (weights −1, 3, −1, −1) on Round 2
  errors, F(1,217) = 5.80, p = .009, ηp² = .026. With the control plan,
  overshoot gave more errors than undershoot, F = 5.89, p = .008; with the
  emotion-regulation plan, F = 1.10, p = .148.
- **Experiment 2, shifting.** Contrast on Round 2 reaction times,
  F(1,217) = 18.92, p < .001, ηp² = .080. With the control plan, overshoot
  on accuracy gave faster responses, F = 22.61, p < .001, ηp² = .094; with
  the emotion-regulation plan the difference was "smaller and
  non-significant", F = 2.67, p = .052.
- **Interpretation (General Discussion).** "The present two studies thus
  provide experimental evidence of the coasting and the shifting
  hypotheses, as well as the role that affect plays in their occurrence."
  Shifting is called adaptive. The authors explain the discrepancy with
  Gollwitzer and Rohloff, who found no coasting with a single goal, by the
  secondary goal: quoting them, "only when other important goals have to
  be served at the same time might one observe a decrease in effort".

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Feedback that a goal is ahead of target lowers later performance on it (coasting) | moderate | Exp. 1 accuracy and speed, Exp. 2 accuracy; ηp² .022–.046 for the simple effects, one-tailed |
| C2 | It raises performance on a competing goal (shifting) | moderate | Exp. 1 accuracy (p < .001), Exp. 2 speed (p < .001); Exp. 1 speed not significant |
| C3 | Ahead-of-target feedback produces better mood than behind-target feedback | moderate as a contrast | Exp. 1; but the raw means show the difference comes from undershoot lowering mood, not overshoot raising it |
| C4 | Affect is causal in coasting and shifting | weak | Exp. 2: an "ignore my feelings" plan removes the effects; no manipulation check, and the plan was not tested against the no-plan simple effects directly |
| C5 | Coasting needs a second goal to shift to | weak | reconciliation with Gollwitzer and Rohloff, not tested within these studies |

## Concepts

- **Coasting** — "unexpectedly fast goal progress leads to reduced effort at
  that goal" (Abstract); measured here as lower performance on that goal.
- **Shifting** — "shifting focus toward other goals" (Abstract); measured
  as higher performance on the alternative goal.
- **Pushing** — more effort after slower-than-expected progress.

## Connections

It extends Fulford et al. (2010, LIT-tmphhuac), which it cites as the "only
one published study" testing the hypotheses directly, and Louro, Pieters and
Zeelenberg (2007). It takes Carver and Scheier's control-process model
(1990, 1998; LIT-1441, read in the record through LIT-1449 and NOTE-1102)
and Carver 2003 (LIT-1445) as its theory. Phan and Beck (in press) is cited
for feedback's effect on affect. Carver died while it was under revision
(acknowledgements).

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-468: largely.** The objection's positive half needs evidence that
  positive affect lowers a goal's priority, and waits on Carver 2003 for it.
  This paper states the thesis's shifting form from Carver 2003 and tests
  it: running ahead lowers the overshot goal's hold on performance and moves
  performance to the rival goal, and a plan to ignore feelings removes
  both, which puts affect in the path. Two cautions keep it short of fully.
  Exp. 1's affect difference is mostly negative affect after undershoot, not
  positive affect after overshoot; and Exp. 2 shows that an
  emotion-regulation plan matters, not that positive affect was present and
  was what it regulated. Carver 2003 itself stays unread, so the claim's
  attribution to that paper should now cite the reading of this one.
- **CLAIM-440: partly, against clause 3 for positive valence.** Clause 3
  makes intensity how far the negotiation's decisions follow the
  prevailing ordering. Here, the state the theory calls positive goes with
  the overshot goal ceding performance to a rival, which is the difficulty
  the claim records against itself. Whether that is a weaker hold of the
  prevailing ordering, or that ordering moving resources to the goal now
  furthest behind, is for the claim to decide: on Carver's satisficing
  reading the shift serves the goals as a set, which the second reading
  fits. The paper gives no graded measure of affect, so it says nothing
  about intensity as such, only sign.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Performance, not effort,** and self-rated effort did not move (General
  Discussion; Table 1).
- **Demand.** Participants were told the goals trade off (General
  Discussion).
- **Small effects, one-tailed planned contrasts,** and one follow-up
  contrast not significant in each experiment.
- **No manipulation check in Experiment 2.** Mood was not measured, so the
  plan's effect on affect is assumed from prior work.
- **Exp. 1's control condition** had a different regression slope, possibly
  because "on target" was read as good by some and bad by others.
- **Online samples** and a laboratory task; the supplementary analyses were
  not read here.

## Open questions

- Does overshoot raise positive affect above baseline, and does that rise,
  graded, predict the size of coasting and shifting? Exp. 1's means suggest
  the affect contrast is driven by the undershoot side.
- Would the effects survive without telling participants the goals trade
  off, and with a direct measure of effort?
