# LIT-tmp3uosd: The Elephant and the Blind: The Experience of Pure Consciousness: Philosophy, Science, and 500+ Experiential Reports

Metzinger, T. (2024). *The Elephant and the Blind: The Experience of Pure
Consciousness: Philosophy, Science, and 500+ Experiential Reports*.
Cambridge, MA: The MIT Press. doi:10.7551/mitpress/15196.001.0001. Open
access, CC BY-NC-ND 4.0.

## Key takeaways

*From a partial reading. What was read is in the NOTE's history.*

- **The working hypothesis** (pp. xviii, 472). Minimal phenomenal
  experience (MPE), the simplest consciousness, "lacks time
  representation; self-location in a spatial frame of reference; the
  experience of ownership, agency, and autobiographical self-awareness;
  and a phenomenally experienced first-person perspective". Pure awareness
  in meditation is offered as the best candidate for it.
- **The model** (pp. 37, 42, 462–463, 476–477). Wakefulness is the
  phenomenal side of tonic alertness: "an organism can be tonically alert
  without knowing that it is alert: Consciousness is knowing that one is
  alert" (p. 37). In the summary's active-inference sketch (Figure 34.1),
  a fourth level models the system's own epistemic openness, "a single,
  integrated model of its own epistemic openness in the form of a
  highest-level but nonegoic self-model" (p. 477). A full-absorption
  episode is inference at that level only.
- **What a subject is** (pp. 302–306). The "epistemic agent model" is the
  layer of the self-model that represents a knowing, attending self.
  "Phenomenologically, a subject is an epistemic agent model that
  functions as the unit of identification" (p. 306). Full absorption and
  nondual states lack it.
- **The contraction principle** (p. 80). Phenomenality is a property of
  subpersonal brain states, which the brain "misrepresent[s] ... by
  contracting it into a transparent conscious self-model". Nondual states
  are offered as counterexamples. State consciousness can occur without
  system consciousness, "but not vice versa" (p. 81).
- **The evidence and its limits** (pp. xvi, 14–20). An online survey
  from early 2020 had 1,403 usable respondents and 841 usable free-text
  reports, of which more than 500 are printed. The author concedes
  self-selection, "embodied theory contamination" by practitioners'
  belief systems, unreliable recall and "response drift". He also grants
  that a report of a selfless state is "highly dubious from a
  methodological perspective", since autobiographical memory represents
  events as a self's (p. 19). He calls the qualitative analysis "not a
  truly systematic qualitative analysis at all" (p. 12).
- **Two fallacies he names** (pp. 416–418). The E-fallacy takes a felt
  certainty of knowing as knowledge. The C-fallacy takes "it felt like the
  nature of consciousness, or not like an experience" as having found it.
  Its mirror image: a report that something was "not an experience" does
  not show it was not one. "We cannot 'bootstrap a theory of consciousness
  out of pure consciousness itself'" (p. 418).
- **Ethics** (pp. 484–485). A minimal model of the simplest conscious
  state "may be the easiest to implement", which is an information hazard.
  The epilogue asks whether a machine kept in pure awareness, "nondual
  and without subjectivity", conscious but without suffering, would be
  wrong to build. It restates the case for a moratorium on synthetic
  phenomenology and cites his 2021 paper (LIT-tmpsk1p5).
