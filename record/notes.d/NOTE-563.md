# NOTE-563: Beyond Markets and States: Polycentric Governance of Complex Economic Systems

## Contribution

A synthesis lecture. Its contribution is the argument that the
mid-century framework of two organizational forms (market and state), two
kinds of good and one model of the individual does not fit the evidence. It
offers a polycentric, multi-scale account backed by meta-analysis,
experiments and field studies.

## Key insight

Successful self-governance is not explained by particular rules, which vary
endlessly across sites. It is explained by structural regularities: users
who make and monitor their own rules, sanctions that start low, cheap local
dispute resolution, and governance nested in layers when the resource is
embedded in something larger.

## Assumptions

- The design principles are inductive regularities from coded cases, not
  derived or deliberately designed: "I did not mean that the fishers,
  irrigators, pastoralists, and others overtly had these principles in
  their minds" (p. 422).
- Individuals are boundedly rational, learn in repeated settings with
  feedback, use heuristics, and hold norms (§7A).

## Key results

- **Policing** (§2A): "most efficient producers supply more output for given
  inputs in high multiplicity metropolitan areas" (quoting E. Ostrom & Parks
  1999).
- **Design principles** (§4E), updated from Cox, Arnold & Villamayor-Tomás
  (2009): 1A user and 1B resource boundaries; 2A congruence with local
  conditions, 2B costs proportional to benefits; 3 collective-choice
  arrangements; 4A monitoring users, 4B monitoring the resource; 5 graduated
  sanctions; 6 conflict-resolution mechanisms; 7 minimal recognition of
  rights; 8 nested enterprises. About two thirds of over 100 studies
  confirm them.
- **Lab** (§5A): baseline overuse beyond Nash; face-to-face communication
  raises returns; imposed costly sanctioning lowers net returns; self-chosen
  sanctioning reaches 90% of optimum.
- **Field experiment** (§5B): an imposed rule enforced with 1/16 inspection
  probability raised extraction relative to communication alone.
- **Nepal irrigation** (§6A): farmer-governed systems better on physical
  condition, tail-end water and productivity; confirmed on 229 systems.
- **Forests** (§6B): no difference in density between protected and other
  forests (163 forests); monitoring correlates with density across 178
  user groups.

## Concepts

- **polycentric** — many formally independent centres of decision; whether
  they form a system is empirical.
- **common-pool resource** — a good from which exclusion is costly and use
  by one subtracts from others.
- **design principle** — a structural regularity of long-enduring
  institutions; Ostrom suggests "best practices" might have been clearer
  (n. 5).

## Connections

Builds on V. Ostrom, Tiebout & Warren (1961) for polycentricity, and on
the IAD framework and the case meta-analyses behind *Governing the Commons*
([LIT-737](../literature.d/LIT-737.md)). It argues against Hardin's tragedy as a general
model.

## Bearing on the record

Source of [THEORY-149](../theory.d/THEORY-149.md). The owner's essay (§8.2) cites Ostrom for
"how distributed monitoring and authority can contribute to institutional
regulation". Principles 4 and 8, and the forest monitoring results, are the
specific support. Two cautions follow from the lecture itself. First, the
principles are about users governing a resource they share, and a firm's
governance of itself is a different structure. Second, Ostrom's lesson is
that fit and legitimacy matter more than form, so the essay should not
read polycentricity as good in itself.

## Limitations

A lecture: the evidence is summarised and cited, not presented. The
design principles are correlates of survival, not tested causes. Several
field results are correlational.

## Open questions

Which principles are causally necessary, and how they apply outside
natural-resource settings. The lecture flags the link between micro and
contextual variables as the main open task (§7D).
