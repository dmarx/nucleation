# Part A. Possibility

## 2. From regulating conduct to governing possibility

A familiar model of regulation treats an industrial ecosystem as a fixed set of technologically feasible actions. Regulation forbids some of them, taxes or subsidizes others, and requires reports on the rest. This section argues that the model is right about what a constraint can do inside it and wrong about where regulation does most of its work. It begins from the difference between the conduct a rule constrains and the outcomes it is meant to secure (§2.1), and shows that a constraint added at fixed dynamics can never enlarge what is attainable (§2.2). It then states the problem as a demand specification (§2.3), measures what a set of demands can achieve together while keeping reachability apart from that measure (§2.4), and separates three questions about any configuration that would meet the demands (§2.5), to which deadlines add a fourth (§2.6). Because every one of these measures is relative to a fixed list of demands, dropping a demand cannot count as success (§2.7). The thesis that regulation governs possibility gathers the three kinds of effect that remain, and the section ends by saying how firmly that thesis is held (§2.8).

### 2.1 Conduct and outcome

A demand and a regulation are different objects. A **demand** is a condition that the ecosystem's history must meet: worker exposure stays below a threshold, an essential service is not interrupted, a damaged watershed is restored within twenty years. A **regulation** is an enforceable rule on what participants do: a standard, a disclosure duty, a liability rule, a prohibition, an incentive, a monitoring procedure. Regulations are "constraints on conduct meant to induce constraints on outcomes, and a regime succeeds only if its conduct constraints make the demanded outcomes reliable under the participants' responses, so a rule can be perfectly enforced and fail its purpose" (CLAIM-tmplgnjf).

The two kinds of constraint can be written side by side. A conduct constraint has the form c_k(x, a) ≤ 0, on states x and actions a. An outcome constraint has the form ∫ f_j dμ_g ≤ b_j, on the distribution μ_g of histories that a regime g induces. The first says what participants must do; the second, what the ecosystem must achieve. Success is then a reliability condition, P_g(Γ_H ∈ V_j) ≥ 1 − ε_j for each demand j, where Γ_H is the history over horizon H and V_j the set of histories that meet demand j. This strengthens joint satisfiability, that some attainable history meets every demand, into reliability under strategic behaviour and disturbance, and it "makes regulation a problem of steering the distribution of possible futures, rather than specifying one ideal future" (CLAIM-tmplgnjf). Which kind of constraint to impose, on outcomes or on technology, "depends on observability, enforceability, and uncertainty": an outcome constraint leaves the technology open, and a technology prescription is sometimes needed because a risk cannot be measured by an outcome test (ibid.). Nothing here says that outcome constraints are better than conduct constraints, or that enforcement reduces to checking rules for consistency: "Legal interpretation, discretion, political legitimacy, and contested interests cannot be collapsed into automated consistency checking" (ibid.). The distinction is conceptual, an interpretation of what regulation is, not a result.

The gap between conduct and outcome is what makes the rest of this section necessary. If enforcing the rules guaranteed the outcomes, a regime could be judged by compliance. The distinction would fail for a regime "where meeting the conduct constraints is shown to entail the demanded outcomes whatever the participants' responses and the disturbances" (CLAIM-tmplgnjf). That a single rule happens to close the gap does not count against it: the claim is that whether the gap is open is a question for every regime, not that it is open in every one.

### 2.2 What a constraint at fixed dynamics cannot do

The familiar model has one exact consequence. "Under a fixed dynamical model an added hard constraint cannot enlarge the set of admissible trajectories, so it cannot enlarge what some admissible path reaches or lower the least cost of reaching it" (CLAIM-582). The derivation is a single inclusion: if K′ ⊆ K, then Traj(K′) ⊆ Traj(K). Any property that asks only that *some* admissible history exist can therefore only be lost when a constraint is added. The reachable set, the viably reachable set and the least cost of reaching a target are all of this kind.

What a constraint *can* improve is "a property required of every path a disturbance or a given process may take, such as robustness or reliability" (CLAIM-582). Removing another party's options weakens a requirement that must hold against all of them. Take ẏ = u + w, with control |u| ≤ 1 and a disturbance |w| ≤ 2, and the task of keeping |y| ≤ 0.1 over a unit of time whatever the disturbance does. No policy achieves it. Add a barrier that caps the disturbance at 1, and a simple feedback does. The robust property is gained, while what the controller alone can reach is unchanged (ibid.). "A constraint that does more has changed the mechanism" (ibid.).

Carried to demands, the inclusion becomes a statement about relief. "A regulation that only adds hard constraints to a fixed dynamical model can only shrink the compatibility complex of the public demands, so it cannot relieve their frustration; a regulation relieves frustration among the original demands only by changing the dynamics, through the technologies, infrastructure or access it brings about" (CLAIM-tmpk4bou). The compatibility complex is defined in §2.4; informally, it lists which sets of demands some attainable history meets together. The step is again one inclusion. A regime g′ that adds only hard constraints to g has R(g′) ⊆ R(g) for its attainable histories, so every face of the new complex is a face of the old, and no strict enlargement, hence no relief, is possible (ibid.; a brute-force check over 20,000 random instances found no exception). The result is a demonstration under its definitions. What would defeat it is a showing that the definitions misdescribe regulation: that "the attainable histories of an ecosystem under a regime are not the histories of the unregulated model that its constraints admit" (ibid.).

Three consequences follow (CLAIM-tmpk4bou). First, "incentives at fixed dynamics move realization, not possibility": a tax or subsidy that changes which admissible history participants choose leaves the complex where it was, and changes how reliably a jointly acceptable history is realized. Second, a hard constraint can still be worth having at fixed dynamics, for the reliability or robustness it buys, and because it can redistribute an unavoidable mismatch. That second aim is one of three design targets that need to be kept apart: "Relieving an incompatibility, redistributing an unavoidable mismatch and exploiting a mismatch dynamically are three design targets", and "the last two can be achieved without the first" (CLAIM-588). Only the first is relief, and relief is not the only aim of regulation (CLAIM-tmpk4bou). Third, changing the dynamics is necessary for relief and not sufficient: the new technology must actually enlarge what is attainable, and whether participants adopt it is the separate question of realization (ibid.).

Consider an interoperability requirement, which restricts how an infrastructure operator may exclude others and may let producers enter who could not before. Is it a constraint that enlarges what is possible? Insofar as it only forbids exclusionary conduct, it is a constraint, and it cannot enlarge what is attainable. Insofar as it lets new producers enter, it has changed the dynamics: it has created access, and with it histories that were not attainable before. The same rule does both, and its restriction and its generative effect should be assessed separately. This is no addition to the results above. It is what "a constraint that does more has changed the mechanism" means (CLAIM-582), and the case from which CLAIM-tmpk4bou was drawn is itself a rule with both effects, one that "may restrict immediate choices while expanding the ecosystem's longer-term repertoire of jointly viable configurations", as in "a rule that leads firms to develop cleaner processes so that environmental, safety and supply demands that were incompatible become jointly attainable" (CLAIM-tmpk4bou). History offers at least one instance of a restriction followed by a lasting change in what an economy could produce. Departments of the French Empire more shielded from British trade by the Napoleonic blockade "added more mechanized cotton-spinning capacity during it and kept a larger industry through 1887" (THEORY-919, a reading of Juhász). The study's long-run first stages are weak, and the restriction was a war blockade, not a regulation. It illustrates the generative half of the distinction and is no evidence for it.

### 2.3 The demand specification

The problem this paper addresses can be put in one sentence, the record owner's: "regulations as constraints which operationalize a demand specification to optimally steer harmonious governance across an industrial ecosystem" (CLAIM-tmpz67ew). Read in the terms of this section, its parts are these (ibid.). A **demand specification** is a set of demands, each "an acceptance set on the histories the ecosystem can realize". To **operationalize** a demand is to choose "the enforceable conduct constraint that is meant to bring it about", across the gap of §2.1. **Harmonious governance** is "a plurality whose constituents' demands are jointly satisfiable", with "coexistence not taken for cooperation" and "viability not taken for legitimacy". An **industrial ecosystem** is read as "a plurality of separately addressable firms, workers, communities and technologies, governed from outside by a regulator who is not one of the constituents whose demands are specified". That last reading is the paper's working gloss, since no settled term exists for it, and §13 questions its "from outside". The thesis itself is an interpretation, put forward as the paper's starting point rather than shown.

Two things qualify it. The first is §2.1's refinement: conduct constraint and demanded outcome are separate layers, and the regime is judged by the second (CLAIM-tmplgnjf). The second is an objection to the word "optimally": "There is no optimal regulation until the demand specification, the instruments, the horizon and the trade-offs among demands are declared, and a regime is better judged by a profile of possibility, realization, resilience, accountability and cost than by a scalar" (CLAIM-tmphjm0q). The objection qualifies "optimally" and leaves the rest of the thesis standing (CLAIM-tmpz67ew). It leaves two readings of the word. Optimality may be "relative to a declared objective", which presupposes a commitment the specification has to state, or it may be maximality, "undominated in the profile" with "no conversion between its dimensions assumed", which is weaker and may not single out a regime (ibid.). Which was meant is unsettled. This paper uses the second wherever it needs either (§§6, 9, 14), because the measure of compatibility introduced next orders regimes only partially (§2.4).

The sentence also leaves something open: "It does not say who sets the demand specification, or what makes it legitimate" (CLAIM-tmpz67ew). Throughout, the paper treats the specification as given, in order to say what a regime does with it, and marks the places where the given specification is itself in question (§§8.6, 12–13).

### 2.4 The compatibility complex, and reachability apart from it

How far a set of demands can be met together is an ordinal fact before it is a quantity. Let D be the declared demands and A_i the acceptance set of demand i. For an organization 𝒪, let ℛ(𝒪) be the histories it can attain. The **compatibility complex** is

  𝒦(𝒪; D) = {S ⊆ D : ℛ(𝒪) ∩ ⋂_{i∈S} A_i ≠ ∅},

the collection of sets of demands that some attainable history meets together (CLAIM-347). It is *downward closed*: a history that meets every demand in S meets every demand in any subset of S. Its *minimal non-faces* are the minimal conflicts, the smallest sets of demands that no attainable history meets together. Four consequences are drawn from the construction: frustration among demands "is an ordinal fact before it is a quantity"; "relief is strict enlargement of that complex for the same declared demands"; "reorganizations that relieve different conflicts are incomparable"; and "dropping or suppressing a demand is not relief" (CLAIM-347). The construction is a proposal about the right way to represent frustration, argued rather than proved; its elementary properties follow from the definition. The third consequence is why §2.3 reads "optimally" as maximality. Two regimes whose complexes each contain a set of demands the other lacks are not ordered by inclusion.

Indexing the complex by a resource budget gives a complex that grows with the budget "and never exceeds the complex with no budget" (CLAIM-586). This is demonstrated, and it splits a missing face in two. A set of demands outside the unbudgeted complex is structurally frustrated at every budget: only a change of architecture can relieve it. One inside the unbudgeted complex but outside the complex at budget b is "frustrated only relative to b, short of resources and not structurally frustrated" (ibid.). A small invented construction shows both at once. Four configurations meet three demands; the three pairs of demands enter the complex at budgets 1, 2 and 3, and the triple, which no configuration meets, enters at no budget, "so raising the budget relieves scarcity and leaves the frustration where it was" (CASE-212).

A satisfactory configuration may exist without being reachable from where an ecosystem is, and that distinction cannot be expressed in the complex just defined. If the complex is built from the histories attainable from the present arrangement, a history that cannot be attained from the present contributes no face, so "exists but is unreachable" never shows up in it. The distinction needs two objects. They are available in a second formulation, over configurations rather than histories. Fix an architecture with configurations Q, moves between them, and a capacity requirement r, and let Q(b) = {q : r(q) ≤ b} be the configurations that can be held at budget b. The **compatibility filtration** K(b) is "the sets of demands some q ∈ Q(b) meets together". The **reachability filtration** R(b) is "the pairs (q, q′) joined by a sequence of one or more moves through Q(b)" (CLAIM-560). Both grow with the budget, and "neither determines the other: a budget can make a transition between configurations already feasible affordable while adding no jointly satisfiable set of demands, and a configuration can be feasible at a budget at which it is unreachable from the start" (ibid.).

The example, which §9 builds on, is three configurations in a row, s₁ — s₂ — s₃, with moves between neighbours both ways, r(s₁) = r(s₃) = 1 and r(s₂) = 3. On budgets in [1, 3) both endpoints are feasible and neither reaches the other. At 3 "they become mutually reachable and the complex does not change" (CLAIM-560). Two further results, also demonstrated by construction, say how loosely the two layers are tied. Two systems can have the same compatibility complex, the same move rule and the same costs and differ in whether a configuration meeting one demand reaches one meeting another, so "compatibility equivalence does not imply equivalence of reachability" (CLAIM-583). And under directed moves, the least budget for going from one configuration to another "can differ from that for coming back" (CLAIM-591): a route open at some cost need not be open in reverse at the same cost.

The paper uses both formulations. The history complex says what regulation can and cannot relieve (CLAIM-347, CLAIM-tmpk4bou). The configuration filtrations say what a budget buys (CLAIM-560). The two coincide only under three conditions, which a derivation made for this paper states and checks by enumeration (CLAIM-tmpg5p8v):

1. each demand is a condition on the configuration held at the horizon H;
2. holding a configuration is an admissible move;
3. the attainable histories are exactly the sequences of moves that stay within the budget B at every moment.

Under these conditions "the history complex over horizon H at a capacity budget B is the configuration complex restricted to the configurations reachable from the start within H at B" (CLAIM-tmpg5p8v). Without them the two differ, and CLAIM-560's row of three configurations shows how. Without holding, a demand met only at the middle configuration is a face of the restricted configuration complex and not of the history complex, since no history ends there. With demands met at different times, a history can meet two demands that no single configuration meets. Demands on a whole history, "such as persistence throughout H ... or a response by a deadline, are not conditions on a configuration at all", and for such demands the configuration formulation cannot replace the history one (ibid.). The identification also fails for a budget that bounds a sum along the history rather than what is held at each moment, and, if the attainable histories are those of the organization's actual policy, the restriction is to the configurations that policy reaches, not to all that are reachable (ibid.). So the paper uses the identification only for demands on the configuration reached, as when §8 and §9 ask whether a route to a configuration is affordable. Where its demands have deadlines (§2.6, §§6–7) or ask that a configuration be kept (§2.5), it uses the history complex.

### 2.5 Three questions about a satisfactory organization

Once existence and reachability are separated, a third property appears. "That a target configuration is feasible, that it is reachable from the start under the admissible transformations, and that the process reliably reaches it are three properties, each implied by the next and none implying it" (CLAIM-567). A configuration is **feasible** when it meets the constraints. It is **reachable** when some admissible path leads to it from the start. It is **reliably reached** when the process as it actually runs ends there with probability at least 1 − ε, for a declared ε. Two constructions show that neither converse holds. A barrier of the kind in §2.4 makes a feasible target unreachable; a fork with unbiased actuation makes a reachable target reached only half the time. Both separations are "relative to the declared class" of admissible transformations (ibid.): what counts as unreachable depends on what moves are allowed.

The same ground can be cut more finely in two places. Within reachability, "permitted directions, actuated directions and transitions achievable at acceptable cost are three objects" (CLAIM-589). A motion the rules permit need not be one the available instruments can drive, and one they can drive "need not be cheap, reliable or sustainable" (ibid.). Within a regime, "Possibility and realization are separate dimensions" (CLAIM-tmpx9nrj). The complex says which sets of demands some attainable history meets; the probability of meeting them "under a model of participants' behaviour says how reliably the regime selects such a history" (ibid.). The construction is minimal. Take two attainable histories, one meeting every demand and one meeting none. An incentive that moves participants from the second to the first improves realization and leaves the complex unchanged. A new technology that makes a better history attainable, which no one adopts, enlarges the complex and leaves realization unchanged. The probability is relative to a behavioural model, and is not known (ibid.).

A regime that cares about its future therefore has three questions to ask of any configuration that would meet its demands:

1. **Can such an organization exist?** Is the set of demands a face of the complex, or at least of the unbudgeted complex? (CLAIM-347, CLAIM-586)
2. **Can the present organization reach it?** Is there an admissible, affordable, actuated route from here? (CLAIM-560, CLAIM-589)
3. **Can the ecosystem reliably reach it, and stay there, when the need arises?** (CLAIM-567, CLAIM-tmpx9nrj)

The third question asks more than reliable reaching. *Staying there* is a further requirement: that from the target some evolution keeps meeting the demands, which is viability in the sense of §8 (THEORY-134, a reading of Aubin's viability theory). The reached and the sustained are already distinct objects: among motions that can be driven, one "need not be cheap, reliable or sustainable", and in a one-line construction a state is reached under a bounded input and then cannot be held, because holding it would need more input than the bound allows (CLAIM-589). They are also joined in one object in the construction §8 uses, the kernel of states whose policies meet the demands "with declared reliability" and keep routes to readiness under alternative demands (TERM-tmpz28l1, note of 2026-11-23). That object is a construction, not yet a result, and §8 discusses its limits. The separation matters because a configuration can be reached reliably and then lost: "Stability does not entail viability" (CLAIM-079).

### 2.6 Deadlines

Most public demands have deadlines. A service must be restored within hours, a conversion finished by a compliance date, a watershed recovered within a generation. What a deadline does to the complex is demonstrated: "When demands are predicates on histories with deadlines, the sets of them an organization can jointly meet under its response architecture and budget form a downward-closed complex contained in the complex of the same demands without deadlines, so two demands can be compatible statically and incompatible in time, and a change in the response architecture, faster actuation, different sequencing, staged capacity or a new method, can enlarge the temporal complex without weakening any demand" (CLAIM-tmpdvgrf). The containment is immediate: every history that meets a demand by its deadline meets it without one, so the temporal complex lies inside the deadline-free complex for the same budget and safeguards (ibid.).

The example is industrial. A factory can cut its emissions and keep producing, and yet "lack the capacity to complete the conversion within the regulatory deadline while meeting uninterrupted supply requirements" (CLAIM-tmpdvgrf). The pair of demands is a face of the deadline-free complex and not of the complex with deadlines. Enlarging the temporal complex again requires a change in what is attainable, "not a hard constraint added at fixed dynamics" (ibid., by CLAIM-tmpk4bou), and a change of architecture is "one possible mechanism, not a guarantee of monotone expansion" (ibid.).

Deadlines add a fourth question to the three of §2.5: *can the ecosystem reach it in time?* Sections 6 and 7 develop it. There readiness becomes a curve over the deadline, and the ranking of two organizations can reverse as the deadline moves.

### 2.7 Eliminating a demand is not relief

Every measure in this section is relative to a fixed list of demands, and that is a safeguard, not a technicality. A regime can always make its remaining demands compatible by dropping the one that conflicts. That is not an improvement in the sense that matters. Relief "must be judged for an identified organization against a fixed reference set of its original demands, under an explicit correspondence between earlier and later demands: a demand excised or suppressed has changed the comparison and has not been satisfied, an endogenous revision of what the organization maintains is a transformation of it and not relief" (CLAIM-361). Four responses to demands that cannot all be met have to be told apart: **achievement**, where "More of the original demands become jointly attainable"; **reprioritization**, which changes which demands take precedence; **constitutive transformation**, which changes which demands the organization maintains and which processes bear them; and **suppression**, where "A demand or its evidence is excluded without demonstrating that the original requirement has been satisfied" (ibid.). Only achievement is relief. This is an interpretive requirement on how relief is judged, and it would fail if indexing relief to an identified organization and a correspondence of demands changed no prediction, intervention or attribution (ibid.).

The same safeguard covers a subtler move. Besides changing what is possible and shifting what is likely (§2.5), a regulation can "alter the test functions through which we determine whether a history is acceptable" (CLAIM-tmpx9nrj, note of 2026-11-22). That is not a third dimension of a regime. A change of test functions changes the demand sets V_j, and so changes the compatibility complex's reference set, which CLAIM-361 forbids calling relief (ibid.). The danger is real: "A regulation can improve measured outcomes while narrowing the ecosystem's future adaptive capabilities" (ibid.).

None of this says a demand may never be revised. A specification can be wrong, and §§8 and 13 need the capacity to change it. What is required is that a revision be called a revision, a transformation of what the regime maintains, and judged as one, not counted as success at the old task. Section 9 runs the difference in a model. An ecosystem that excises a newly visible harm scores no violations, either against its original specification or against its own, and only scoring against the true demands tells it apart from one that revises its specification and pays to meet the new demand. There the paper goes beyond CLAIM-361. The model's harm was never in the original specification, so "excising" it means never entering it, whereas CLAIM-361's case is a demand that was in the specification. Carrying the distinction to a demand never specified is this paper's extension; the synthesis of that line of argument lists a close form of it as not stated by any of its components (SYNTHESIS-049), so §9.8 offers it as the paper's reading, not as established.

### 2.8 The thesis: regulation governs possibility

The argument so far gives regulation three kinds of effect on an ecosystem. It constrains conduct within the attainable histories, which can improve reliability and robustness and never enlarges what is attainable (§2.2). It changes which histories are attainable, which is the only way to relieve a conflict among fixed demands (§§2.2, 2.6). And it changes how reliably participants select an acceptable history (§2.5).

These can be gathered under one thesis: "Regulation governs possibility: it shapes which actions, relationships, capabilities and forms of coexistence are admissible, attainable, sustainable and revisable, so it is generative when it makes forms of organization attainable that were not, stabilizing when it keeps valuable capabilities through disturbance, and reflexive when its own specifications stay correctable, and there is no choice between governed and ungoverned possibility, only between organizations of it" (CLAIM-tmptfpf7). It was put forward by ChatGPT in the exchange from which this paper comes. It rests on three entries: the condition for generative regulation, that it must change the dynamics (CLAIM-tmpk4bou); the separation of possibility from realization (CLAIM-tmpx9nrj); and the finding that removing a constraint "does not move it along one scale of more or less governance: it changes a profile of who holds governing positions" (CLAIM-410). Its last clause, that there is no ungoverned possibility, is close to the third (CLAIM-tmptfpf7).

This paper adopts the thesis as its organizing claim, and it is the least firm step of the section. The two results beneath it are demonstrations; the thesis is an interpretation, and it remains a proposal. Two things would defeat it (CLAIM-tmptfpf7). One is empirical: "An industrial ecosystem whose attainable futures are fixed by its technologies and participants alone, so that no rule, right, incentive or institution changes which histories are attainable, only which are chosen". The other is redundancy: "the finding that the claim only restates" its three premises "with nothing added by the generative, stabilizing and reflexive distinction". The thesis concedes how the first could be met. In an ecosystem where every regime acts only "by prices and penalties on a fixed set of options", all three premises hold "and regulation is never generative", and what the thesis adds is that "real regimes are not of this kind: rights, standards and institutions change the dynamics" (ibid.). Nothing in this paper or its sources shows that real regimes are not of that kind. The second defeater is open too. Sections 3 to 8 are in part an attempt to show that the stabilizing and reflexive clauses add something: the stabilizing clause is developed in §§3–5 and §8, the reflexive clause in §§8 and 13. Whether they succeed is for the reader to judge (§15).

Two features of the thesis matter for what follows.

**It is not a case for more possibility.** It does not say that every possible future deserves protection. Some possibilities, such as unsafe production, predatory exclusion and uncompensated environmental harm, may rightly be eliminated, and eliminating them by constraint is what constraints are for. Nor does it say that maximal flexibility is good: "A narrow path can be viable. A large option set can be nonviable" (CLAIM-079). The question is which possibilities to preserve, which to remove, and which to create.

**It ties regulation's levels to three achievements that can fail separately.** The thesis distinguishes three levels of governance: conduct, capabilities and governability. Read against the record, these are the regulatory case of three achievements that persist or fail separately: "an operative regime, the governing organization among regimes, and the capacity to transform it" (CLAIM-tmptfpf7, reading CLAIM-025). That is the bridge from this section to §8. If the three can fail separately (CLAIM-025, an interpretive claim shown in models), then a regime that keeps its conduct rules working can still lose the capacity to change them. A regime that meets every present demand can still be losing its future.
