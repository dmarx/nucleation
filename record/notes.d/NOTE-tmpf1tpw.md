# NOTE-tmpf1tpw: Marcuse (1964) — One-Dimensional Man

## Contribution

The classic statement that a society can dominate through satisfaction
rather than deprivation: through needs superimposed by particular
interests, a technological rationality that makes compliance rational,
and an administration that absorbs its own administrators. Part I
(chapters 1–4) is "One-Dimensional Society", Part II (5–7)
"One-Dimensional Thought", Part III (8–10) "The Chance of the
Alternatives".

## Key results

Quotations as the copy prints them.

1. Ch. 1, the first sentence: "A comfortable, smooth, reasonable, democratic unfreedom prevails in advanced industrial civilization, a token of technical progress."
2. Ch. 1, false needs: "'False' are those which are superimposed upon the individual by particular social interests in his repression: the needs which perpetuate toil, aggressiveness, misery, and injustice."
3. Ch. 1: "The most effective and enduring form of warfare against liberation is the implanting of material and intellectual needs that perpetuate obsolete forms of the struggle for existence"; "social control is anchored in the new needs which it has produced".
4. Ch. 1, who decides: the question of true and false needs "must be answered by the individuals themselves, but only in the last analysis; that is, if and when they are free to give their own answer"; "no tribunal can justly arrogate to itself the right to decide which needs should be developed and satisfied".
5. Ch. 1, the measure of freedom: "The range of choice open to the individual is not the decisive factor in determining the degree of human freedom, but what can be chosen and what is chosen by the individual." And: "Free election of masters does not abolish the masters or the slaves."
6. Introduction: the society conquers "the centrifugal social forces with Technology rather than Terror, on the dual basis of an overwhelming efficiency and an increasing standard of living"; "once the project has become operative in the basic institutions and relations, it tends to become exclusive", and the system "swallows up or repulses all alternatives".
7. Ch. 2: "Domination is transfigured into administration"; "the tangible source of exploitation disappears behind the facade of objective rationality"; "the technological veil conceals the reproduction of inequality and enslavement".
8. Ch. 2, the controllers enclosed: "the organizers and administrators themselves become increasingly dependent on the machinery which they organize and administer", "a vicious circle which encloses both the Master and the Servant".
9. Ch. 2: the growing output of goods and services makes "compliance a rational technological attitude". And on pluralism: "pluralistic administration is far better than total administration. One institution might protect him against the other; one organization might mitigate the impact of the other; possibilities of escape and redress can be calculated."
10. Ch. 3: "It is a rational universe which, by the mere weight and capabilities of its apparatus, blocks all escape." Ch. 1: "the more unimaginable the means and ways by which the administered individuals might break their servitude".
11. Ch. 4, the Hawthorne study: translating a general grievance into one worker's circumstances makes "a treatable and tractable incident"; "His case has been subsumed under another genus-that of personal hardship cases".
12. Ch. 6, impersonal dependence: "To be sure, the 'objective order of things' is itself the result of domination"; the apparatus "sustains and improves the life of the individuals while subordinating them to the masters of the apparatus"; "The world tends to become the stuff of total administration, which absorbs even the administrators."
13. Ch. 6: technology demonstrates "the 'technical' impossibility of being autonomous, of deter- mining one's own life" [hyphen as in the copy]; "The technological a priori is a political a priori".
14. Ch. 7: total mobilization made individuals "incapable of seeing 'behind' the machinery those who used it, those who profited from it, and those who paid for it".
15. Ch. 8, hypostatized wholes: "Their independence, although real, is a spurious one inasmuch as it is that of particular powers which have organized the whole I of society." [stray "I" as in the copy]
16. Ch. 8, the choice of a project: "The initial choice defines the range of possibilities open on this way, and precludes alternative possibilities incompatible with it." And: "The choice is primarily (but only primarily!) the privilege of those groups which have attained control over the productive process. Their control projects the war [sic: way] of life for the whole, and the ensuing and enslaving necessity is the result of their freedom."
17. Ch. 9: tolerance "enforced not by any terroristic agency but by the overwhelming. anonymous power and efficiency of the technological society" [punctuation as in the copy]; repressive productivity satisfies needs that include "enjoying freedom from using the brain"; "At the present stage, he is perhaps more powerless over his own apparatus than he ever was before."
18. Ch. 9: "liberation from the affluent society does not mean return to healthy and robust poverty, moral cleanliness, and simplicity".
19. Ch. 10: "Self-determination in the production and distribution of vital goods and services would be wasteful"; "centralized control is rational if it establishes the preconditions for meaningful self-determination"; "At its most advanced stage, domination functions as administration".

## Assumptions

- A critical theory may judge a society against its own real
  possibilities.
- Vital needs have an unqualified claim; other needs are historical and
  open to judgment as true or false, by a criterion of "the optimal
  development of the individual, of all individuals" (ch. 1).
- Technology is not neutral: "The technological a priori is a political
  a priori" (ch. 6).
- Of the Introduction's two hypotheses, that the society can contain
  change and that forces may break it, Marcuse takes the first as
  dominant.

## Concepts

One-dimensional society and thought; democratic unfreedom; true and false
needs; total administration; technological rationality as political
rationality; repressive desublimation; the Happy Consciousness;
operationalism and the closing of the universe of discourse; the
"project" and its initial choice; pacification of existence; the Great
Refusal.

## Bearing on the record

- **The stand-in, corrected.** Farr's entry (NOTE-tmpi8t8i, THEORY-tmpnydju)
  is confirmed on the first sentence (result 1) and, through Kellner, on
  false needs (results 2, 3). Three of its points are not the book's:
  "all domination assumes the form of administration" is from Eros and
  Civilization, and the book's own forms are results 7 and 19; "The
  system must make the citizens think that they are freer than they
  really are" is Farr's paraphrase, and no such sentence is in the copy;
  "domination no longer requires force or the presence of an authority
  figure" is Farr's wording. The book's nearest (results 6, 17, and ch.
  1's "non-terroristic economic-technical coordination which operates
  through the manipulation of needs by vested interests") are about the
  absence of terror, not of an authority figure.
- **CLAIM-tmpkjv7u and ARG-tmpsh7he.** The book does not support reading
  Marcuse as describing constraint that no one holds. The apparent
  impersonality is in part a veil over particular interests (results 7,
  12, 14, 15, 16), and those who control the apparatus are themselves
  enclosed by it (results 8, 12). What Marcuse adds against the claim is
  that control is exercised through the choice of a society's project
  (result 16), not over particular dependents, and that it is veiled, so
  the dependent cannot see whom to challenge. On the third condition,
  result 9 supports the value of plural means of redress, and results 5
  and 11 qualify it: a channel for complaint can itself integrate.
- **CLAIM-tmpbypt7** (ARG-tmpqo6eu). The book corroborates the mechanism: a
  project "tends to become exclusive" (result 6), the initial choice
  "precludes alternative possibilities" (16), compliance becomes rational
  (9), the apparatus "blocks all escape", and the ways out become
  "unimaginable" (10). It argues against the measure: "what can be chosen
  and what is chosen" (result 5), not the range of choice, decides how
  free a person is.
- **CLAIM-tmp6wuql.** Results 13 and 17, and ch. 2's loss of the worker's
  "professional autonomy", point the same way as the claim's premise,
  without dynamics or a model. Weak; no relation.
- **CLAIM-tmpsmg1c.** Results 6 and 16 give a reason irreversibility
  matters, and ch. 3 speaks of "the degree of its irreversibility". But
  Marcuse's remedy is "planning for the whole against these interests"
  (ch. 10), not a burden of justification. No relation.
- **QUESTION-071.** Result 3, and ch. 1: "No matter how much such needs
  may have become the individual's own", they remain "products of a
  society whose dominant interest demands repression". Marcuse's test is
  historical, whose interest produced the need, which is the question's
  premise and its "what in its history would have to be recorded".
- **QUESTION-057.** Result 4 and the same ch. 1 passage: avowal does not
  settle whether a need is the agent's own while the agent is not free
  to give her own answer.
- **QUESTION-461.** Ch. 2's integration of labour (the union "almost
  indistinguishable in its own eyes from the corporation"; workers with a
  "vested interest in the establishment") and result 11 are worked cases
  of an organization keeping indispensable contributions from becoming
  claims on it.
- **QUESTION-460.** Result 15 says the whole's independence is "spurious",
  that of the "particular powers" that organized it; ch. 8 quotes "One
  believes to die for the Fatherland, one dies for the Industrialists".
  Marcuse places the bearer in those who control or benefit, not in the
  whole.
- **THEORY-025** (Sen's adaptive preferences): kin, with a difference the
  book makes plain. False needs arise from affluence and satisfaction,
  not from deprivation.
- **Illich** (THEORY-tmpww16o): both hold that industrial production
  creates the needs it satisfies; they part on the remedy. Marcuse would
  plan necessities centrally (result 19) and refuses a return to
  "healthy and robust poverty" (18); Illich would give people tools to
  meet needs with their own energy.
- **Feenberg** (THEORY-tmpa59p3): his critical theory of technology
  descends from this book; result 13's "political a priori" is the
  ancestor of the technical code.
