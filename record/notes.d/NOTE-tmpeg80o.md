# NOTE-tmpeg80o: Group Identity, Social Influence, and Collective Action Online: Extensions and Applications of the SIDE Model

## Contribution

A handbook chapter that states the SIDE model (the Social Identity model
of Deindividuation Effects) and reviews twenty-five years of work on it.
It does three things the record lacked. It sets out the model's two
dimensions and the experiments behind each. It corrects readings the
authors call mistaken: SIDE does not endorse deindividuation, and it is
"not about anonymity" as such. And it extends the model to visible media,
online communities and collective action.

## Key insight

The crowd member has not lost a self; a group has come into it. "Whereas
deindividuation implies a loss of self in the group, depersonalization
refers to the emergence of group in the self: the tendency to see others
but also oneself in group terms" (p. 27). So anonymity does not loosen
regulation. It moves regulation to the group level: "behavior becomes
more social or socially regulated, not less so" (p. 26). What anonymity
amplifies depends on which self is salient. When personal identity is
salient, anonymity amplifies that instead.

## Assumptions

- **The social identity approach** (social identity theory and
  self-categorization theory) is the theory. SIDE is "a model rather than
  a theory": a more specific analysis of technological features within it
  (p. 31).
- **Two aspects of anonymity, taken from deindividuation theory**
  (pp. 26–27). Deindividuation theory had two routes to antinormative
  behaviour: anonymity cutting accountability, and submergence in the
  group cutting private self-awareness (Prentice-Dunn & Rogers 1989).
  SIDE keeps the distinction but changes both:
  - *anonymity of* others within the group bears on which identity is
    salient (the cognitive dimension);
  - *anonymity to* an audience bears on what can safely be expressed (the
    strategic dimension).
- **Salience is set in the experiment by instructions.** In the first
  study, participants were told the researchers were interested in them
  as psychology students, or in personality differences in communication
  style (p. 28).

## Key results

The chapter describes designs and directions of effects. It gives no
statistics.

- **Deindividuation theory** (pp. 25–26). From Le Bon through Festinger
  et al. (1952), Zimbardo (1969), Diener (1980) and Prentice-Dunn & Rogers
  (1989): "anonymity in the mass promotes a loss of self-awareness, which
  reduces inhibitions and increases the likelihood of antisocial
  behavior". Early work on computer-mediated communication borrowed it to
  explain "flaming" and extreme group decisions (Kiesler et al. 1984).
- **Group polarization as conformity** (p. 25). The prototypical position
  of a discussion group reflects both its mean and what distinguishes it
  from others. With no explicit out-group, the other end of the scale
  serves as an implicit one, so conformity to the prototype is
  polarization.
- **The cognitive dimension** (pp. 27–29).
  - Reicher (1982, 1987) argued that crowd members switch from individual
    to group identity and become more, not less, sensitive to norms.
    Crowd behaviour "is not antinormative and disinhibited ... but rather
    it is the result of conformity to explicit or implicit norms"
    (p. 27).
  - Reicher (1984): science and social science students dressed in
    overalls and hoods conformed more to in-group norms than students in
    their own clothes (p. 27).
  - Spears, Lea & Lee (1990): first-year psychology students discussed
    attitude topics, with the group norm made known. Group or personal
    identity salience was crossed with sitting in separate rooms or face
    to face. Conformity to the group norm was greatest with group identity
    salient and members isolated. "When personal identity was salient,
    however, participants actually contrasted their views away from the
    group norm under anonymity" (p. 28).
  - Postmes et al. (2001), two studies (pp. 28–29). Efficiency or
    patient-care norms were primed with a scrambled-sentence task before a
    discussion of hospital policy. Group influence was stronger when
    members were anonymous than when they were shown by portrait. When two
    of four members were primed, the norm spread to the two unprimed
    members, again mainly in anonymous groups.
  - Lee (2004) found that identification mediated the effect. Lee (2008)
    found that identifiable users attended to argument strength, while
    for anonymous users the group norm mattered more (p. 29).
- **Against deindividuation's process** (p. 29).
  - Postmes & Spears (1998) found that anonymity in the group "actually
    enhances conformity to local or specific group norms, rather than
    increasing antinormative or antisocial behavior".
  - In computer-mediated groups, anonymity does not reduce personal
    self-awareness and tends, if anything, to increase public
    self-awareness. Neither kind of self-awareness predicts disinhibition
    (Adrianson 2001; Lee 2007; Matheson & Zanna 1988; Yao & Flanagin 2006).
- **Conceptual recap** (pp. 29–31).
  - "Deindividuation" in the name is descriptive. It names the effects to
    be explained, and the model does not endorse "a state of
    deindividuation".
  - The name is not "Depersonalization Effects" because anonymity does
    not always bring a shared identity (p. 30).
  - Neither technology nor the social context determines the outcome;
    they interact.
  - "SIDE is not about anonymity but about the online representation of
    individuals and groups" (p. 31). Uniform representations of members
    have the same effect as anonymity.
- **The strategic dimension** (pp. 31–33).
  - SIDE makes "no blanket assumption that people will always act in
    line with individual self-interest when anonymous" (p. 32). What
    unaccountable people do depends on the salient identity.
  - Reicher & Levine (1994a, b) sorted student behaviours into three
    kinds: normative for students and acceptable to staff (missing the
    occasional lecture); normative but punishable (copying from another
    student's essay); and neither (cheating in an exam). Students
    identifiable to staff endorsed the second kind less, and students
    anonymous to staff endorsed it more. Copresence of other students had
    the same effect. All endorsed the first kind and none the third
    (p. 32).
  - Spears et al. (2002): visual anonymity raised influence on the
    unpunishable items (the cognitive effect). Being able to communicate by
    computer raised support for the punishable items (the strategic
    effect), since students could then gauge each other's support
    (p. 32).
  - Women more often concealed their gender online (Flanagin et al. 2002).
    They were less likely to choose female avatars on topics of male
    expertise, and less likely to reveal gender in Italy than in the UK or
    the Netherlands (Spears et al. 2011; p. 33).
- **Extensions** (pp. 34–37).
  - Depersonalized online groups showed more identification, cohesion,
    trust, satisfaction, knowledge sharing and, for high identifiers,
    performance. Members were more likely to perceive the group or dyad
    "as one" (Sassenberg & Postmes 2002; Tanis & Postmes 2008; p. 34).
  - Lea, Spears & Watt (2007). In mixed groups of British and Dutch men
    and women, depersonalization mediated influence under anonymity on
    nationality topics but under visibility on gender topics. Gender is
    visually cued, and visibility gives it comparative fit (p. 35).
  - Postmes & Spears (2002): adding individuating profiles weakened the
    salience and power differences of gender in text chat (p. 35).
  - Ren et al. (2007, 2012): in a movie database, users identifiable only
    as team members formed common-identity groups. These were the
    "stickiest", with the strongest commitment (p. 37).
  - Farzan et al.: Tetris players assigned to teams played more and
    returned more often (p. 37).
  - The authors speculate that environments that block credit-taking
    "foster more prosocial and community-oriented (even altruistic?)
    norms" (p. 37).
- **Collective action** (pp. 37–41).
  - Claims that Twitter and Facebook organized the 2011 London riots were
    disproven (Postmes et al. 2013). Egypt's uprising went on through an
    Internet and SMS shutdown (p. 38).
  - Brunsting & Postmes (2002) surveyed non-activists, sympathizers,
    hardcore activists and pressure-group members. The predictors of
    online and offline action intentions showed "no huge differences"
    (p. 38). Expected effectiveness counted slightly more online.
  - Three predictors are assumed: shared social identity, efficacy, and
    anger and injustice (Van Zomeren, Postmes & Spears 2008; p. 39).
  - Chan (2010): church groups gave more when a call for donations came
    by email than face to face, "particularly when Christian identity was
    salient, and particularly among low identifiers" (p. 39).
  - The authors reject the view that online action becomes personal
    identity expression (Bennett & Segerberg 2012): "This is not a view
    we share" (p. 39).
  - A Facebook event by three individuals drew an estimated 300,000
    people across 10 cities in Portugal on 12 March 2011, around the
    shared identity of the "endangered generation" (p. 40).
  - Rumours of police mistreating a 16-year-old girl circulated for a day
    and a half at up to 70 tweets an hour. Clearly false rumours were
    quickly quashed (pp. 40–41).

## Concepts

- **deindividuation (theory)**: loss of self-awareness under anonymity
  and immersion in a group, releasing antinormative behaviour. The model
  rejects it.
- **depersonalization**: seeing oneself and others in terms of group
  norms and stereotypes; "heightened social or group-level
  self-regulation" (p. 27).
- **cognitive SIDE ("anonymity of")**: anonymity within the group raising
  the salience of the group identity.
- **strategic SIDE ("anonymity to")**: anonymity to an audience changing
  what can be expressed without sanction.
- **pseudonymity**: traceable but not recognizable in the situation; the
  usual condition online (p. 30).
- **common-identity versus common-bond groups**: groups held together by
  a shared identity or by bonds between members (p. 37).

## Connections

- **Reicher, Spears & Postmes 1995 (LIT-tmpjavie)**, unread. Cited as a
  founding statement of the model and of its strategic dimension (pp. 23,
  31). The chapter gives the model's content and the experiments that
  preceded and followed it, but not the 1995 paper's own argument.
- **Postmes & Spears 1998 (LIT-tmpgdaz6)**, unread. Given one sentence
  (p. 29).
- **Turner et al. 1994 (NOTE-tmpf9mzh) and Hornsey 2008 (NOTE-tmp3dav9).**
  Turner et al. define depersonalization as a shift in self-perception
  (p. 455), which NOTE-tmpf9mzh glosses as "not a loss of self", but they
  do not discuss crowds. Hornsey gives SIDE one sentence (p. 210). This
  chapter supplies the model's structure, the strategic dimension (absent
  from both), and the evidence that anonymity does not reduce
  self-awareness.
- **Skorich (NOTE-tmplcvy8, THEORY-tmpdftev).** Skorich separates
  category effects, such as out-group homogeneity, from group-entity
  effects, such as crowd behaviour, and holds that accentuating likeness
  gives interchangeability, not unity. SIDE reports that depersonalized
  members see the group "as one" (p. 34). Its polarization account also
  relies on an implicit out-group (p. 25).
- **Spears 2021 (read alongside).** It covers SIDE more briefly and
  influence more widely, and cites this chapter as the fuller review.
- **León (THEORY-tmpsm03n).** SIDE's anonymous groups are the opposite of
  León's close dyads. Their members' individuating features are hidden,
  and that is what makes the "we" salient.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** This chapter answers the
  question the thesis needs answered about crowds. Is the crowd member
  selfless, or acting on a larger self? These are inferences; the authors
  do not discuss selflessness.
  - **Not selfless.** Deindividuation theory is the "selfless crowd": a
    loss of self-awareness and of the self's regulation. SIDE rejects it
    on evidence. Anonymity does not lower personal self-awareness, and
    crowd and online behaviour follows the group's norms (p. 29). The
    crowd member acts as a self, "the group in the self" (p. 27).
  - **Changed, more than larger.** Reicher's word is a "switch" from
    individual to group identity (p. 27). Spears et al. (1990) show that
    anonymity amplifies whichever identity is salient, and pushes people
    away from the group under personal salience. That is a choice of one
    self over another, not an enlargement. It confirms the thesis's own
    caveat that depersonalization is "a change of self, not plainly a
    larger one". Fusion (NOTE-tmp3g9n4) remains the record's case for
    "larger".
  - **Strategic action on a group self.** Unaccountable students did what
    their group's norm allowed and staff would punish, and nothing the
    norm forbade (p. 32). The authors say this means people do not always
    act "in line with individual self-interest when anonymous". The
    calculation of what one can get away with is kept, but it serves the
    salient self's norms. That fits "self-interest on a larger
    self-identity" well, provided the larger self is the salient one.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** SIDE experiments fix the
  self's level by instruction, not by what participants care about, and
  that level predicts the direction of influence (p. 28). That is the kind
  of independent criterion the objection asks for. Chan's donors are a
  sharper case. Salience raised giving "particularly among low
  identifiers" (p. 39), people who by their own report are less attached.
  If this holds, a salience criterion predicts prosocial action beyond
  measured attachment. But caring was not measured separately, the
  outcomes are attitudes and one field donation study, and all of it is at
  second hand.
- **Batson's objection (CLAIM-tmpybc4k).** Not addressed. Nothing here
  concerns empathic concern for an individual.
- **Self-categorization theory (THEORY-tmpo85ke).** The implicit
  out-group in the polarization account (p. 25) bears on the THEORY's
  proposed test against Skorich, as noted from Spears 2021. Lea et al.
  2007 is a candidate primary study, from the authors' own lab.
- **CASE-104 and QUESTION-119.** The strategic dimension gives a reading
  of fan crowds. Chants and boycott calls, made anonymously or among many
  other fans, express in-group norms that the club or police would
  sanction, so they show the norm, not each fan's view. Stated as a
  derivation, not a finding about fans.
- **A dated note is added to CLAIM-tmpjylq8.** Proposed text:

  > Note of 2026-10-29: the crowd member is not selfless. Spears and
  > Postmes (2015), read in place of Reicher et al. 1995 and Postmes &
  > Spears 1998, state the SIDE model's case against deindividuation:
  > anonymity and immersion do not remove the self but bring "the group
  > in the self" (p. 27), and behaviour follows the group's norms more
  > closely, not less. So the thesis's denial of selflessness holds for
  > crowds. Its word "larger" does not: anonymity amplifies whichever
  > identity is salient, which is a switch of self. Experimental salience
  > manipulations, which fix the self's level apart from caring, are the
  > kind of criterion CLAIM-tmprudek asks for.

- **No THEORY filed.** SIDE would be a THEORY in its own right. Its
  founding statement and every primary study are unread.

## Limitations

- **By the model's authors.** Every study described in detail is theirs
  or their collaborators'. The chapter does not report failures to
  replicate.
- **No statistics.** Designs and directions only; no sample sizes or
  effect sizes, including for the 1998 meta-analysis.
- **Laboratory attitudes, mostly.** Influence is measured as attitude
  change in discussion groups. The behavioural outcomes are a few field
  results (Chan 2010; Ren et al.; Farzan et al.).
- **Typos.**
  - "in Manchester the mid-1980s" (p. 24); "tends be more extreme"
    (p. 25); "might otherwise we sanctioned" (p. 27); "when the
    discussion topic were relevant" and "the Dutch were predicted be more
    critical" (p. 35); "connected though interpersonal relations"
    (p. 39); "a number extensions" (p. 41).
  - "Tanis & Postmes, Tanis & Postmes, 2005, 2007" (p. 34).
  - A full reference printed in the running text for Spears et al. 2002
    (p. 40).
  - Citations that disagree with the reference list. Spears, Lea & Lee is
    1990 in the text and 1992 in the list, with the 1990 volume. Matheson
    & Zanna is 1998 in the text and 1988 in the list. Farzan et al. is
    2012 in the text and 2011 in the list. Postmes et al. 2013 has "Van
    de Sande" in the text and "Van der Sande" in the list.
  - "Postmes, 2000" and "Spears & Lea, 2002" (p. 29) are not in the

  - In the references, "Anonymous (1998)" has the page range "3481–407".

## Open questions

- Is the depersonalized member's personal self lowered, or only
  outranked? A study measuring personal self-concept clarity and group
  identification together, under anonymity, would tell "switch" from
  "addition". That bears on whether SIDE's self is changed or larger.
- Does salience predict sacrifice, not only attitude change, when
  identification and caring are held fixed? Chan's design, with a measure
  of concern for the group's welfare added, would answer CLAIM-tmprudek.
