# NOTE-622: Making It Explicit

## Contribution

From the parts read: the book takes the meaning of linguistic expressions
to be conferred by their use, and describes use in normative, not
intentional or naturalistic, vocabulary. The norms are deontic statuses
(commitment and entitlement) instituted by practitioners' attitudes of
attributing and acknowledging them. Chapter 3 gives the model:
assertion's pragmatic significance is defined in scorekeeping terms, and
propositional content as inferential role. The Preface says the model's
building blocks (Kant, Frege, Wittgenstein, Sellars, Dummett, Lewis)
are not new; what is claimed new is working the model out in detail and
applying it.

## Key insight

To say something is to change what you and others are committed and
entitled to. A claim's content is whatever settles how it changes those
commitments and entitlements: what it follows from, what follows from
it, and what it rules out.

## Assumptions

- **Normative vocabulary is admissible and ineliminable** in specifying
  use, though it is explained by social practice, not taken as primitive.
- **Assertion is the fundamental speech act**: questions, orders and
  promises are intelligible only by their relation to assertions.
- **The model is an idealization** ("not our actual practice but an
  artificial idealization of it"): only semantic inferences, and Gricean
  implicature is left to an outer shell.
- **Default and challenge**: entitlement is attributed by default unless
  a challenge is itself entitled.

## Key results

The parts read give definitions and arguments, not theorems.

- **Assertion (§III.3).** It has three parts: undertaking a commitment;
  authorizing further commitments, one's own and the audience's; and a
  conditional responsibility to vindicate entitlement. Entitlement is
  inherited by justification (intrapersonal, across contents) and by
  deferral (interpersonal, within a content).
- **Regress (§III.4).** Default entitlement and challenges that need
  their own entitlement dissolve the foundationalist regress.
- **Sanction (§III.5).** An unentitled assertion loses its inferential
  and communicational authority; no external sanction is needed.
- **Scorekeeping (§IV.1–2).** Lewis's conversational score is recast as
  deontic score, kept for each interlocutor by each interlocutor. What C
  is committed to according to A can differ from what C is committed to
  according to B.
- **Score update (§IV.3–4).** When B asserts p, scorekeeper A adds p to
  B's commitments. A then closes them under commitment-preserving
  inferences, removes entitlements p's incompatibilities preclude, adds
  entitlements by entitlement-preserving inference, and assesses B's
  entitlement to p by inference, reliability or testimony. If B is
  entitled, others may inherit p.
- **Other forces (§IV.5).** Deferral, disavowal (which, if p follows
  from q, requires disavowing q), query and challenge attach different
  force to the same contents.

## Method

Philosophical model-building. Requirements on a theory of discursive
practice are stated, a simplified game of giving and asking for reasons
is specified in scorekeeping terms, and the claim is that the game is
sufficient (and in its main structure necessary) to confer
propositional content.

## Concepts

- **commitment / entitlement**: the two deontic statuses, the
  counterparts of obligation and permission without a hierarchy of
  authority.
- **doxastic (assertional) commitment**: what an assertion undertakes;
  the model's stand-in for belief.
- **deontic score**: the commitments and entitlements an interlocutor
  attributes to each participant at a stage of a conversation.
- **pragmatic significance**: how a speech act depends on, and changes,
  the score.
- **inferential role**: a sentence's place in commitment-preserving and
  entitlement-preserving inferences and in incompatibilities.
- **incompatibility**: commitment to one claim precludes entitlement to
  the other.

## Connections

Lewis's "Scorekeeping in a language game" supplies the score metaphor;
Dummett supplies circumstances and consequences of application and the
sense/force distinction; Stalnaker's contrast between the pragmatic and
linguistic pictures of intentionality is used to place the view;
Davidson's argument for the interdependence of belief and language is
given its form. The early paper "Asserting" (1983), the ancestor of
chapter 3, is what Krifka ([LIT-835](../literature.d/LIT-835.md)) cites for the commitment
theory of assertion. Krifka's performative update with a speaker's truth
guarantee is close to Brandom's undertaking of commitment. But Krifka
treats declarations and other acts as their own performative updates,
whereas Brandom makes assertion fundamental and the other acts
parasitic on it.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-014](../claims.d/CLAIM-014.md)** (which changes in conversational standing count).
  The deontic score is a worked-out answer to "what is a conversational
  standing": commitments and entitlements, kept perspectivally.
  Brandom's version is per scorekeeper, so it does not supply one
  objective state for an information order to compare, unless the
  comparison is made from one scorekeeper's perspective. Neither this
  book nor the claim says how to choose that perspective.
- **[CLAIM-054](../claims.d/CLAIM-054.md)** (interpretive and performative fidelity come
  apart). In the parts read, Brandom gives the means to separate what is
  claimed (content) from what is undertaken and licensed (commitments,
  entitlements, responsibility to defend). But he derives the second
  from the first "according to a uniform pattern" plus force, so for
  him two renderings with the same content and the same force cannot
  differ in deontic significance. That is a tension with the claim,
  unless the relational dimensions it names (complicity, solidarity)
  lie outside the doxastic score, which the parts read do not address.
- No THEORY is filed from a skim. No instruction for ML practice;
  nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- Skimmed: the arguments for the approach (chapters 1–2), the treatment
  of practical commitment (chapter 4) and of representation and
  communication (chapter 8) were not read.
- By the author's own account the model is an idealization limited to
  semantic inference.
- Scorekeeping is specified for assertion and its auxiliaries only, in
  the parts read.

## Open questions

- Whether chapter 8's account of communication across scorekeepers with
  different collateral commitments (de re ascription) gives a notion of
  sameness of content across perspectives that a translation could
  preserve or fail to preserve.
- How practical commitments (chapter 4) extend the score beyond
  assertion, and whether that reaches the non-doxastic standings
  (obligations, relationships) Krifka's performative updates create.
