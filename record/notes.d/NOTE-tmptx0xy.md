# NOTE-tmptx0xy: Kunz & Rittel — Issues as Elements of Information Systems

## Contribution

The paper names and specifies Issue-Based Information Systems: an
information system organised around the issues a planning group argues
about, rather than around a documentation thesaurus. It gives the
elements (topics, issues, questions of fact, positions, arguments, model
problems), the relations between issues, six subsystems, and the main
operating cycle. It reports three manually run systems in experimental
use. It is a specification with a field report of one sentence, not an
evaluation.

## Key insight

The unit of a planning record is the open question people disagree on,
not the topic or the document. "Issues are the organizational 'atoms' of
IBIS-type systems" (¶5). A discourse is a counterplay of questioning and
arguing in which "every formulation of the problem is also a statement
about a potential solution" (¶2), so the record has to follow the
argument rather than a fixed classification of its subject.

## Assumptions

- **Cooperatives with a problem complex.** The users are "governmental or
  administrative agencies or committees, planning groups, etc." that must
  arrive at a plan for decision (¶1).
- **An argumentative model of problem solving.** Problem solving by such
  groups is taken to be an argumentative process (¶1); the paper asserts
  this, citing the authors' own reports, and does not argue it.
- **Staffed operation.** The operating cycle (¶11) has issues "edited (in
  contact with Mₖ)" and files "processed by S-61", which implies staff
  who maintain the system for the participants; the paper does not say
  who they are.

## Key results

There are no results in the sense of findings. What the paper states:

- **The process (¶2).** A topic is named by a "trigger phrase" ("Urban
  Renewal in Baltimore", "The War", "Tax Reform"). "Issues are brought up
  and disputed because different positions are assumed. Arguments are
  constructed in defense or against the different positions until the
  issue is settled by convincing the opponents or decided by a formal
  decision procedure." Questions of fact go to experts or documentation;
  "Answers obtained can be questioned and turned into issues."
- **Why not a thesaurus (¶4).**
  - An issue such as "Shall we legalize private manpower banks?" is not
    represented by the descriptors "Legalization", "Manpower Banks",
    "Private".
  - "The similarity of an issue with issues dealt with previously and the
    information used in their treatment" matters more than the
    documentalist's description of the subject.
  - "The discourse developing is notoriously centering around concepts
    expressed by ad hoc vocabulary which is rapidly changing, defying
    thesaurization. Many central terms used are proper names for long
    stories specific of the particular situation, with their meaning
    depending very sensitively on the context in which they are used."
  - A further aim is "a more scrutinized style of reasoning which more
    explicitly reveals the arguments", and "fast and reliable information
    on the state of discourse at any time".
- **Properties of issues (¶5).** They have the form of questions; they
  originate in controversial statements; they are specific to situations;
  they are "raised, argued, settled, 'dodged', or substituted".
- **Relations between issues (¶6).**
  - I₂ is a *direct successor* of I₁: "I₂ challenges a statement made in
    support of one of the positions maintained in view of I₁."
  - I₂ is a *generalization* of I₁.
  - I₂ is a *relevant analogy* to I₁: its arguments transfer "mutatis
    mutandis".
  - Positions on different issues can be *compatible*, *consistent* or
    *incompatible*, by the same or another proponent.
- **Kinds of issue (¶7).** Factual ("Is X the case?"), deontic ("Shall X
  become the case?"), explanatory ("Is X the reason for Y?") and
  instrumental ("Is X the appropriate means to accomplish Y in this
  situation?"). Each may have a closed set or an open list of positions.
- **Other elements (¶8).** Topics are "a crude organization principle".
  Questions of fact are not assumed controversial; "Doubting the
  credibility of an answer leads to an issue." Model problems (location
  models, cost–benefit models) are general, and their usefulness "depends
  on the importance of those factors which have not been included".
- **Subsystems (¶9).** S-1 issue bank (living, settled or abandoned,
  latent); S-2 evidence bank (questions of fact, answered and open); S-3
  "Handbook" of model problems; S-4 topic list; S-5 issue map, a "graphic
  display of the state of argument"; S-6 documentation system.
- **Numbering (¶10).** "All the items are numbered consecutively to ease
  reference." Relations are kept as matrices (an I×I matrix per relation,
  an I×T matrix of issues to topics) used "for tracing connections" and
  for drawing issue maps; literature is linked by accession number, so
  "several layers of search networks are established".
- **Operation (¶11).** O-1 a participant raises issue I_r under topic Tⱼ
  on an "issue form"; O-2 it is edited and related to other issues; O-3 an
  "argument sheet" for each position; O-4 literature search; O-5 the issue
  map updated; O-6 discussion, ending in settlement, a request for
  evidence, a challenge to a supporting statement ("GOTO S-1"), or denial
  of the issue's significance with substitute issues.
- **Field report (¶12).** IBIS-1 for a supranational agency's information
  policy; IBIS-2 for an interdepartmental committee on a national plan for
  information networks; IBIS-3 for university planning. "The most
  astonishing result is the eager acceptance by the users although the
  implementation induced major changes in organization and working
  styles." All are manual; computerisation is being programmed.

## Concepts

- **Issue.** A question, arising from a controversial statement, specific
  to a situation, on which positions differ.
- **Position.** An answer taken on an issue.
- **Argument.** A statement for or against a position.
- **Question of fact (F-question).** A question not assumed
  controversial; doubting its answer turns it into an issue.
- **Model problem.** A general, situation-free model (location,
  cost–benefit) defined over a closed set of variables.
- **Direct successor.** An issue that challenges a statement made in
  support of a position on an earlier issue.
- **Latent issue.** An issue filed but not yet raised.

## Connections

The paper cites only the authors' own reports (Kunz and Rittel 1968,
1969, 1970) and Rittel (1966). gIBIS ([LIT-762](../literature.d/LIT-762.md)) is its best-known
computer implementation. The later design-rationale literature filed
under [ADR-030](../decisions.d/ADR-030.md) (Grudin, [LIT-759](../literature.d/LIT-759.md), among others) is largely an account of why
systems of this kind were abandoned, which this paper, written at the
start, does not anticipate.

## Bearing on the record

- **[ADR-032](../decisions.d/ADR-032.md)'s scheme against IBIS.** The record says "an objection is a
  claim". In IBIS a challenge to a statement supporting a position is a
  new *issue*, a direct successor (¶6), so objections are questions there,
  not claims. [ADR-030](../decisions.d/ADR-030.md)'s summary of the planned schemes as "questions,
  claims and objections, in the manner of IBIS" is loose on this point:
  IBIS has issues, positions and arguments, and no separate objection
  element. IBIS also has what the record lacks: questions of fact kept
  apart from issues, model problems, and the latent and dodged states of
  an issue. The record has what IBIS lacks: a credence on each claim, and
  retirement by status rather than an issue's being "substituted".
- **[CLAIM-180](../claims.d/CLAIM-180.md) and [CLAIM-182](../claims.d/CLAIM-182.md) have a 1970 precedent.** IBIS numbers every
  item "to ease reference" (¶10), and it does so because the discourse's
  terms are "rapidly changing, defying thesaurization" and are "proper
  names for long stories" whose meaning depends on context (¶4). That is
  the drift [CLAIM-180](../claims.d/CLAIM-180.md) describes, met with addresses as [CLAIM-182](../claims.d/CLAIM-182.md) proposes.
  The paper also wants retrieval by "similarity of an issue with issues
  dealt with previously" (¶4), so it pairs codes with retrieval by content
  rather than replacing one with the other. It argues none of this; it
  states it as design.
- **[CLAIM-179](../claims.d/CLAIM-179.md) and the cost of capture.** The founding paper reports
  adoption, not abandonment: "eager acceptance by the users", with no
  account of cost beyond "major changes in organization and working
  styles" (¶12). Its operating cycle implies staff who edit issues "in
  contact with" the participant who raised them (¶11), so capture in the
  original design was already done by someone other than the arguer. That
  bears on [CLAIM-179](../claims.d/CLAIM-179.md): the disparity Grudin describes may depend on who the
  filer is, and IBIS's first design separated filer and arguer, as an
  agent filer does. It is one sentence of design, not evidence. The
  question left open in [LIT-758](../literature.d/LIT-758.md)'s takeaways, whether the paper says what
  keeping an IBIS costs, is answered: it does not.
- **[QUESTION-037](../questions.d/QUESTION-037.md).** IBIS's answer to what makes an argument settle is
  procedural: an issue is settled "by convincing the opponents or decided
  by a formal decision procedure", or dodged, or substituted. It offers no
  account of an argument's structure stabilising over time, which is what
  the question asks. Its issue bank and issue map are the closest thing
  to a project memory, and the paper reports that they were used, not
  what they changed.
- **No THEORY filed.** The paper is a design specification. Its three
  general claims (C1–C3) are asserted, not supported, and nothing it
  establishes would hold as an account of what is true. C1 is the seed of
  Rittel and Webber's wicked problems, which the record should file from
  that paper if it is read.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- Seven typed pages, with no data, no description of the three systems'
  users or duration, and no measure of "acceptance".
- The theory of IBIS and the manuals are announced as "in preparation";
  this paper is an outline of them.
- The operating cycle is described for the "treatment of issues" only.

## Open questions

- Who edited issues and kept the matrices in IBIS-1 to IBIS-3, and at
  what cost, is not said; a later report on those systems would answer
  it.
- Whether "eager acceptance" survived past the experimental phase is the
  question the later abandonment literature answers for other systems,
  not this one.
