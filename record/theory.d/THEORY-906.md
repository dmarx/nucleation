# THEORY-906: In five formerly stable democracies selected because they later autocratized (South Korea, India, Hungary, Venezuela and Turkey), formal emergency powers, extraordinary powers functionally equivalent to them, and emergency rhetoric each contributed, on the authors' unsourced country accounts, to weakening democratic institutions

## Source

Dagan & Benvenisty, [LIT-1487](../literature.d/LIT-1487.md), the IDI's web summary of 26 February 2026,
read in full in [NOTE-1129](../notes.d/NOTE-1129.md). The full report was not found.

## What was actually shown

Country sketches. Hungary: "Since 2015, Hungary has operated under
successive, extended states of emergency, at times simultaneously" (para. 9),
with emergency provisions amended by the government that used them.
Turkey: the post-coup emergency of 2016–2018 "proved pivotal" (para. 11).
Venezuela: a temporary enabling law transferring legislative power
(para. 10). India: "an undeclared rule by executive decree" (para. 8). South
Korea: decree, enemy rhetoric and the failed martial law of December 2024
(paras. 6–7). Nothing that could have come out otherwise: the cases were
chosen because democracy eroded.

## What this does not say

- **That emergencies cause erosion.** "This convergence is not inevitable"
  (para. 5); no comparison cases.
- **That emergency regimes maintain themselves without intent.** The
  authors' account is mostly deliberate exploitation of crises.
- **That the accounts are sourced.** None is.

## Connections

- **[CLAIM-445](../claims.d/CLAIM-445.md).** Illustrates its political-institutional feedback
  (Hungary's emergencies renewed and redefined by those who hold the
  powers) and its authorizing move (emergency rhetoric). It is not evidence
  for the mechanism, which the claim says can run where the original
  threat was real and no one acts in bad faith.
