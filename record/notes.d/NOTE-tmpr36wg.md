# NOTE-tmpr36wg: Chen et al. — Need satisfaction, frustration and strength across four cultures

## Contribution

It builds and validates the BPNSFS, the 24-item scale that became the
standard measure of need *frustration* as well as satisfaction, with
measurement equivalence tested across four countries on four continents.
With it, it tests BPNT's universality claim at two levels: whether the
needs–well-being relations differ by country ("macro level"), and whether
they depend on how much a person values or desires the need ("micro
level"). Both answers are no (pp. 216, 230–232).

## Key insight

A need, unlike a preference, should matter whether or not you want it.
Chen et al. operationalize that by testing need strength as a moderator:
if satisfaction helps only those who value or desire it, the "need" is a
preference; if it helps everyone equally, it is a need. They find no
moderation, which they read as support for need-hood. The same logic is
applied to culture (p. 219).

## Assumptions

- **Universality is tested as non-moderation**: a non-significant χ²
  difference between constrained and unconstrained multigroup models, or
  non-significant interaction terms (pp. 222, 228–230). No equivalence test
  bounds the size of a possible difference.
- **Need strength is explicit self-report**: valuation, the same nine items
  reworded "It is important for me to feel ...", in Study 1; desire, the
  nine "Psychological Needs as Motives" items of Sheldon and Gunz 2009, in
  Study 2 (pp. 221, 224). Implicit motives are set aside as a different
  construct (pp. 231–232).
- **Study 1's need measure** is the nine-item scale of Sheldon et al. 2001
  ([LIT-tmpd3l9z](../literature.d/LIT-tmpd3l9z.md)), with Chinese autonomy α = .47 and Chinese autonomy
  valuation α = .41 (p. 221).
- **Study 2's main analyses** use saved latent factor scores in path
  models, because each country group in the hypothesis-testing half has
  N < 150 (p. 225).
- **Student samples**, convenience, cross-sectional (p. 233).

## Key results

### Study 1: Belgium (N = 359) and China (N = 324), late adolescents (pp. 220–223)

- The two samples sum to 683; the abstract gives 685.

- Metric equivalence held, ΔSBSχ²(15) = 23.26, p > .05.
- Well-being (self-esteem and CES-D depressive symptoms as one latent
  variable) regressed on the three satisfactions: autonomy β = .36,
  competence β = .41, relatedness β = .04 (ns); fit SBSχ²(75) = 304.93,
  CFI = .92, RMSEA = .07. Constraining paths equal across countries did
  not worsen fit, Δχ²(3) = 3.63.
- Valuation × satisfaction interactions: β = .01, .05, −.02 (ps = .91,
  .37, .72). Valuation correlated *positively* with satisfaction (Table 1).

### Study 2: scale construction (pp. 223–227)

- 42 items (16 autonomy, 12 relatedness, 14 competence), half satisfaction
  and half frustration, drafted across English, Dutch and Chinese,
  translated into Chinese, Dutch and Spanish. N = 1,051 split into
  a development half (525) and a test half (526).
- EFA within each need gave two factors, satisfaction and frustration. CFA:
  three factors SBSχ²(802) = 1,769.97, CFI = .87; six factors SBSχ²(790)
  = 1,319.18, CFI = .93; ΔSBSχ²(12) = 450.79, p < .01 (p. 226).
- Four items were dropped for non-equivalence, among them "I feel like I
  have a real say in the things I do" and "I feel free to do things my own
  way" in China, which "may reflect more independent functioning" (p. 226).
  Final 24 items, four per subscale (Table 3); six-factor fit SBSχ²(231)
  = 372.71, CFI = .97, and in the test half SBSχ²(231) = 441.99,
  CFI = .95. Subscale α .64–.89 across countries (Table 4).
- **Keying.** All twelve satisfaction items assert the positive experience
  ("I feel capable at what I do") and all twelve frustration items an
  aversive one ("I feel insecure about my abilities", "I feel like a
  failure because of the mistakes I make", "I have the impression that
  people I spend time with dislike me") (Table 3).

### Study 2: main hypotheses (pp. 227–230)

- Metric invariance of the six factors across the four countries,
  ΔSBSχ²(54) = 69.57, p > .05. One CES-D item was removed for
  non-equivalence (p. 227).
- Correlations (Table 6): same-need satisfaction–frustration −.58 (A),
  −.63 (R), −.64 (C); satisfaction with life satisfaction .48–.59; frustration
  with depressive symptoms .41–.48; frustration with life satisfaction
  −.43 to −.49. Need desire correlated *negatively* with satisfaction
  (−.11 to −.34) and positively with frustration (.16 to .41).
- Composite path model (Fig. 1), controlling gender, age, income, health
  and financial satisfaction: satisfaction related to life satisfaction and
  vitality and not to depressive symptoms; frustration to depressive
  symptoms and (weakly) life satisfaction, not vitality. The figure's
  coefficients are .51, .73, −.19, .60 and −.84; read with the text they
  are satisfaction → life satisfaction .51 and → vitality .73,
  frustration → life satisfaction −.19 and → depressive symptoms .60, which
  leaves −.84 as the covariance of the two latent composites. This
  assignment is reconstructed. Country constraints: Δχ²(18) = 27.74,
  p > .05.
- Per need: life satisfaction from autonomy (β = .42) and relatedness (.23)
  satisfaction, not competence; vitality from all three (.19, .22, .42);
  depressive symptoms from autonomy (.26), relatedness (.35) and competence
  (.17) frustration. Country invariant (Δχ²(18) = 20.29; Δχ²(9) = 3.26).
- Desire × satisfaction interactions β = .07, −.04, .01 (ps .09, .23, .87);
  desire × frustration β = .01, .03, −.08 (ps .80, .58, .18); none
  significant in any single country either (fn. 2).

## Concepts

- **Need frustration** — needs "actively blocked or thwarted", eliciting
  "defensiveness, ill-being, and even psychopathology", as opposed to low
  satisfaction (p. 217).
- **Need valuation** — "the stated importance of the need to the person";
  **need desire** — "the desire to get a need met" (abstract).
- **Autonomy** — "volition and willingness", contrasted with "external
  control, pressure, and coercion", not with dependence (p. 218).

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-tmpee04a](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpee04a.md): the paper's need-strength test is a partial answer
  of the kind the question asks for.** A preference helps those who hold
  it; a need should help regardless. Non-moderation by valuation and
  desire is evidence on that, and it does not pass through the well-being
  measure as the "essential" criterion does. But it is weak evidence: the
  test is null-hypothesis, the moderators are self-reports, and the
  outcome is still self-reported well-being. It cannot tell a need from a
  universal enhancer, since a universal enhancer also helps regardless of
  preference.
- **[THEORY-052](../theory.d/THEORY-052.md) and [LIT-tmpjcuzb](../literature.d/LIT-tmpjcuzb.md): the satisfaction/frustration split is
  confounded with item direction by construction.** All frustration items
  are negatively valenced experiences and all satisfaction items positive
  ones, so the six-factor model's better fit (ΔSBSχ²(12) = 450.79) is what
  a keying or valence factor would also produce. Murphy et al.'s objection
  ([THEORY-052](../theory.d/THEORY-052.md)'s note of 2026-11-02) applies to this scale as built. The
  latent composites are also close to collinear on the reconstructed
  Fig. 1 (−.84). [THEORY-tmpr67qi](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpr67qi.md) states this.
- **[THEORY-052](../theory.d/THEORY-052.md)'s "shared content" worry, sharpened.** Several frustration
  items are near-paraphrases of depressive cognitions ("I feel like a
  failure because of the mistakes I make"; "people I spend time with
  dislike me"), and the outcome is the CES-D. The 20-item CES-D includes
  "I felt that people disliked me" and "I thought my life had been a
  failure" (not in this text; which ten items were used is not stated), so
  some of the frustration–depression correlation may be item overlap.
- **[QUESTION-131](../questions.d/QUESTION-131.md).** The note of 2026-11-02 says this scale measures felt
  frustration. It does: every item is "I feel ..." or "I have the
  impression ...". Nothing in it identifies a constraint or countervailing
  process, so it measures the experience, not operative thwarting.
- **[THEORY-052](../theory.d/THEORY-052.md)'s promote_when** is not met: cross-sectional, one internalizing
  outcome, self-report.
- **[CLAIM-368](../claims.d/CLAIM-368.md)**, which uses frustration as a primitive, inherits the scale's
  keying problem wherever it relies on BPNSFS-measured frustration.
- Filed from it: [THEORY-tmpr67qi](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpr67qi.md) (Active) and [THEORY-tmp4qi8l](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp4qi8l.md) (Proposed).
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- University students only; cross-sectional; no measured cultural values
  (the authors', p. 233).
- **Universality by failure to reject.** With fewer than 150 per country in
  the hypothesis-testing half, non-significant constraint tests do not show
  that country differences are small.
- **Item removal tailors the scale to invariance.** Items were dropped
  where they failed equivalence, so invariance of the final scale is partly
  built in, on the same development half, and cross-validated only on the
  other half of the same samples.
- Low Chinese reliabilities in Study 1 (autonomy α = .47, autonomy
  valuation .41).
- Only depressive symptoms for ill-being.

## Open questions

- Does the satisfaction/frustration distinction survive a balanced-keying
  design, with reverse-worded items in each subscale ([LIT-tmpjcuzb](../literature.d/LIT-tmpjcuzb.md)'s test)?
- Does frustration predict ill-being with outcomes that share no content
  with the items: informant ratings, clinical diagnosis, behaviour?
- Would an equivalence test with adequate power confirm the absence of
  country moderation?
