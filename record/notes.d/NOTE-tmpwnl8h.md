# NOTE-tmpwnl8h: The Concept and Psychological Consequences of Symbolic Immortality

## Contribution

A test of the theory behind symbolic immortality, framed as Rank's with
Lifton's additions. It separates two things the earlier work ran
together: having a mode in one's life ("availability", such as having
children) and feeling that the mode lets one transcend death
("acceptance"). It measures both in one adult sample with a wide age
range and five religious groups, against death anxiety and Eysenck's
personality scales.

## Key insight

The modes work as consolation people draw from what their lives already
contain, not as goals they pursue. Availability was the strongest
predictor of acceptance, and the secular modes were accepted by the same
people who accepted death as final. Sturesteps concludes that people do
not consciously aim to make the modes part of their lives; a threat
raises the concern and they look for what they have (p. 127).

## Assumptions

- **A need, after Rank.** People have an unconscious need to feel they
  transcend death; failing to meet it should raise death anxiety and
  neuroticism (pp. 5, 43).
- **Vignettes measure acceptance.** Five paragraphs (O'Dowd's three for
  Rank's modes, two added for Lifton's), each rated 1–5 for how close it
  comes to one's feelings when imagining a terminal illness, plus a
  sixth, "death as finality" (p. 58).
- **Availability by single questions.** Children (yes or number); belief
  in life after death; whether one's main activity or a hobby is
  "meaningful, creative or long-lasting" (self-rated); "oneness with" or
  "communing with" nature; a mystical experience (pp. 56–57).

## Key results

- **The concept** (ch. 1).
  - Rank (1930) proposed three modes: biological (children; in modern
    form, living on in one's genes), afterlife, and creative work (being
    remembered for creations or exploits). Lifton (1973) added nature
    (the earth will go on) and experiential transcendence, a feeling of
    expansion of the self beyond the body (pp. 8–10).
  - The biological and creative modes need no belief in a soul (p. 9).
  - For Rank the need is subconscious: people who attain a mode are less
    anxious, not people who set out to attain one (p. 11).
- **Lifton, reported** (§4.2.1, p. 34).
  - From *The Broken Connection*: the prime human aim is a "struggle
    toward, or experience of, a sense of immortality" (1979: 17),
    "immortality hunger" (1979: 284).
  - Lifton held that the nuclear threat makes the biological, creative
    and nature modes untenable, and explains recent interest in
    experiential transcendence by it (1979: 149). Sturesteps objects that
    his own "psychic numbing" (1979: 144–145) would let people ignore the
    threat and go on seeking the modes.
  - Schmitt (1982) found all the modes still expressed, in obituaries,
    in Terkel's interviews and among 2,219 Catholic schoolchildren;
    Sturesteps lists his methodological flaws (pp. 34–35).
- **Earlier measures** (§§4.2.2–4.2.4).
  - Children as immortality was the third most often named advantage of
    having children in Hoffman et al. (p. 36).
  - Of 198 sociologists, 54% expected their work to survive them
    (Westie 1973); retired ones grew more optimistic (Westie and Kick
    1980) (p. 37).
  - O'Dowd (1985), 24 male professors: the death-as-finality paragraph
    was chosen most often (p. 40).
  - Scales: Hood and Morris (1983), rejected for method; Mathews and
    Mister (1987), who read experiential transcendence as seeking intense
    pleasures; Drolet (1990), whose factors recovered only Rank's three
    modes cleanly (pp. 41–42).
- **The sample** (§5.1.1). 610 distributed, 440 usable (72%): 186 men,
  254 women, mean age 44.43 (SD 15.57), range 18–88. Recruited through
  Christian, Jewish, Yoga and Spiritualist organizations, Skeptics, a
  voluntary euthanasia group, ANU classes and a University of the Third
  Age group. Highly educated (29% with a degree).
- **Acceptance** (Table 8-1, p. 88). Means on 1–5: biological 2.76,
  afterlife 3.18, creative work 2.97, nature 3.10, experiential
  transcendence 3.09; death as finality 3.56. Men rated death as finality
  above every mode (p. 89).
- **The modes and death as finality** (Table 6-13, p. 77). Accepting
  death as final correlated positively with accepting the biological
  (.30), creative work (.31) and nature (.33) modes, and negatively,
  non-significantly, with the afterlife and experiential transcendence
  modes (−.13 each). In the regression on accepting the biological mode,
  having children was the strongest predictor (β = .36) and accepting
  death as final the next (β = .21) (Table 9-1, p. 107).
- **Availability predicts acceptance** (§§6.2, 9.7). Total availability
  correlated .47 with total acceptance; number of children .37 with the
  biological mode (Table 6-12).
- **Religion** (Table 8-5, p. 99). Yogis gave experiential transcendence
  4.11 and the biological mode 1.61; Spiritualists gave the afterlife
  4.35; atheists gave death as finality 4.09 and the afterlife 1.36.
  Affiliation predicted acceptance of every mode (p. 126).
- **Rank's hypotheses** (chs. 6–10).
  - Death anxiety: lower with acceptance of the afterlife (r = −.15) and
    experiential transcendence (r = −.26) modes, but symbolic immortality
    variables were never the strongest predictors; in the regression,
    accepting the biological mode went with *more* death anxiety
    (β = .09, p = .06) (pp. 75, 82).
  - Neuroticism: no association with availability or acceptance; only
    with accepting death as final (pp. 74, 117).
  - Sex: women did not favour the biological mode nor men the creative;
    women rated the afterlife higher (pp. 88, 126).
  - Age: only women of 40 and over accepted the biological mode more
    (p. 126).
  - Education lowered acceptance of four modes but not of death as final
    (p. 127).
- **Conclusion** (pp. 128–130). Symbolic immortality is real but a weaker
  drive than Rank thought. Creative work might be split into creativity
  and notable achievements, making six modes.

## Concepts

- **symbolic immortality**: a sense of transcending death through
  something that stands for one's continuation, not survival itself.
- **modes**: biological (children, genes), afterlife, creative work
  (works and being remembered), nature, experiential transcendence (an
  expansion of the self).
- **availability / acceptance**: having a mode in one's life, versus
  feeling it lets one transcend death.
- **death as finality**: the vignette that rejects every mode.
- **psychic numbing** (Lifton): an incapacity to feel certain
  experiences, as Sturesteps reports it.

## Bearing on the record

- **The legacy question (QUESTION-tmpruhu4).** Symbolic immortality is the
  thesis's reading of legacy in psychological form: one lives on through
  children or works. Three results bear on it. These are inferences.
  - *It is not felt as survival.* Those who accept the biological,
    creative and nature modes tend to accept death as final. So whatever
    extension of the self these modes give, it is not believed to carry
    the person past death.
  - *It looks like consolation, not motive.* Acceptance follows what one
    already has; Sturesteps concludes the concern is raised by threat,
    not pursued. That fits terror management's use of the concept better
    than legacy motivation's, and says nothing about acting for future
    people.
  - *Only one mode is a self-extension by definition.* Experiential
    transcendence is defined as an expansion of the self; it is tied to
    religion and is about the present, not legacy.
- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** Nothing here tests whether
  concern for children or the future follows a felt continuity of self;
  no overlap or identification measure was used. The survey measures
  consolation under imagined dying. It neither supports nor threatens the
  thesis.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** The availability and
  acceptance split is a model for the test the objection asks for, at a
  distance: whether one *has* children is independent of how one feels
  about them. A study that added a self–child or self–future continuity
  measure could ask which predicts acting for one's legacy.

## Limitations

- **Skimmed**, not read: see the history note for the sections read.
- **Not Lifton.** The thesis builds on Rank and cites Lifton 1979 for
  four points.
- **One survey, self-selected.** Recruited through religious and
  sceptical organizations to fill religious groups; highly educated;
  Australian.
- **Imagined consolation.** Acceptance is a rating of how close a
  paragraph comes to one's feelings in an imagined terminal illness.
- **Self-rated availability** for creativity and nature, by the author's
  own account the weakest measures (p. 130).
- **Bonferroni not applied consistently.** §5.4.1 sets α at .006 for
  t-tests and .002 for correlations, but some results are reported as
  significant at p < .05 (e.g. pp. 63, 88), and regression predictors
  are kept at p < .10.
- **Internal inconsistencies.** Chapter 4 sets out three research
  issues; chapter 9 calls its topic "the fourth". §9.7 finds education
  significant for four modes; p. 127 says "every mode".
- **Typos** (on the page, not OCR):
  - "t(484)" for a comparison of 440 respondents (p. 88; checked on the
    scanned page);
  - "n = 40" for a correlation reported among n ≈ 400 (Table 6-12, p. 75);
  - "is that is that" (p. 42); "were was the same" (p. 57); "including
    these religious groups were included" (p. 129).
- **OCR.** The text layer has many misreadings ("patents" for parents,
  "Mterlife"); figures did not survive. Numbers cited here were taken
  from tables that extracted cleanly.

## Open questions

- Does acting for one's legacy, as distinct from consolation in it,
  follow a measured continuity between self and children or works?
- What does Lifton's own book say about the biological mode? The record's
  LIT summary gives it as living on "through one's children and people";
  the thesis treats it as children and genes, and reports that Hood and
  Morris and Drolet call it "biosocial", without saying what that adds.
