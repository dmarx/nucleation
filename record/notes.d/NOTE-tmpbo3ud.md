# NOTE-tmpbo3ud: Structuralism

## Contribution

From the pages seen, the book does two things. It proposes one
definition of structure that covers its uses across the sciences. And it
argues, domain by domain (mathematics and logic, physics and biology,
psychology), that structures are not static forms. They are systems of
transformations, and they are constructed. Ch. I says the synthesis is
possible only by separating two problems "always conjoined in fact" but
logically distinct. The first is the "affirmative ideal" that goes with
the idea of structure, and the page breaks off there; the second was on
an unserved page.

## Key insight

A structure is a set of elements under laws of transformation, and it
regulates itself. What it is can be read off what it does to itself: "all known structures—from
mathematical groups to kinship systems—are, without exception, systems
of transformation. But transformation need not be a temporal process: 1
+ 1 'make' 2" (p. 11).

## Assumptions

- **Wholes are not aggregates** (p. 7): "the elements of a structure are
  subordinated to laws, and it is in terms of these laws that the
  structure qua whole or system is defined". The laws "confer on the
  whole as such over-all properties distinct from the properties of its
  elements". The integers are the example: they "do not come upon the
  scene except as ordered".
- **The choice is not wholes or atoms** (p. 7). Piaget denies that the
  epistemological alternatives reduce to wholes defined by laws or
  atomistic compounding. The third option he argues for was on unserved
  pages.

## Key results

From the pages seen, in order:

- **Self-regulation, two grades** (p. 15). In logic and mathematics,
  "an operation is a 'perfect' regulation": an operational system
  "excludes errors before they are made, because every operation has its
  inverse in the system". But "an immense class of structures… whose
  transformations unfold in time: linguistic structures, sociological
  structures, psychological structures" are governed by "regulations"
  that "are not in the strict sense 'operations,' because they are not
  entirely reversible".
- **Why the group concept succeeds** (p. 19). Groups are "a kind of
  prototype of structures in general". The concept is obtained by
  "reflective abstraction", which derives properties "not… from things
  but from our ways of acting on things". It rests on coordinations such
  as the possibility of a "return to the starting point" (the inverse)
  and of reaching one goal by different routes.
- **Bourbaki's mother structures** (pp. 23, 27). Group structure "is
  quite independent of the intrinsic nature of its elements". Psychogenetically, topological
  structures come before metric and projective ones, so "psychogenesis
  inverts the historical development of geometry but matches the
  Bourbaki 'genealogy'". MacLane and Eilenberg's categories are
  mentioned as structuralism "in process of transformation", with an
  emphasis on morphisms.
- **Logic as general algebra** (p. 31). Boolean algebra can be read as a
  logic of classes, a propositional logic or a two-valued arithmetic. The
  INRC group (identity, negation, reciprocal, correlative) is a Klein
  four-group acting on propositional operations.
- **The limits of formalization are movable** (p. 35). Gödel shows
  limits that "are not laid down once and for all, like the walls of
  China". This is because "there is no 'form as such' or 'content as
  such'": each element is form to what it subsumes and content for a
  higher form.
- **Physical structures** (pp. 39, 43). Explanation places the real "in
  the context of possible transformations", as in least-action paths
  and probabilistic explanations of entropy. "There are physical
  structures which, though independent of us, correspond to our
  operational structures".
- **Organisms** (pp. 47, 51). Homeostasis makes the organism "a
  self-regulating system". Learning depends on "antecedent structures",
  which "need not be innate, nor are they necessarily immutable". Against
  Foucault's man as "a kind of rupture in the order of things", that
  rupture is "the product of a great upheaval… which is constituted by
  life as a whole".
- **Against Gestalt** (pp. 55, 59). Gestalt structures appeal to those
  who want structures "'pure,' unpolluted by history or genesis,
  functionless and detached from the subject". But logico-mathematical
  structures obey laws of wholeness without being Gestalten, since
  "their laws of composition are strictly additive". And the subject
  "performs in, sometimes even composes," what it perceives.
- **Sensori-motor origins** (p. 63). The functional factors are
  assimilation and accommodation. The structural elements are order
  relations, subordination of schemes and correspondences. Coordination
  yields structures with "a modicum of 'reversibility'", first of all
  the "practical" group of displacements.
- **The subject** (p. 69). "To be real, a structure must, in the literal
  sense, be governed from within". So "it is for our purposes sufficient
  to define this subject as the center of functional activity". The page
  ends as Piaget asks what role is left for the subject if each
  structure regulates itself.

## Concepts

- **structure**: a whole whose elements are subordinated to laws of
  composition, which is a system of transformations and regulates
  itself. The three defining ideas, from the contents and pp. 7–15, are
  wholeness, transformation and self-regulation. Piaget's summary
  definition of ch. I, §1 was on unserved pages and is not quoted
  here.
- **operation / regulation** (p. 15): an operation is a fully reversible
  transformation, a "perfect" regulation; a regulation in the
  cybernetic sense governs transformations in time without full
  reversibility.
- **reflective abstraction** (p. 19): abstraction from the coordination
  of one's own actions, not from properties of objects.
- **INRC group** (p. 31): identity, negation, reciprocal and
  correlative, acting on binary propositional operations.

## Bearing on the record

- The p. 15 distinction bears on the record's interest in which
  transformations of meaning are invertible. Logico-mathematical
  structures are groups, closed under inverses. Linguistic and social
  structures transform in time under regulations that are "not entirely
  reversible". That is Piaget's own line between group-like and
  merely self-regulating transformation systems, stated in 1968.
- p. 11 states that "all known structures… are… systems of
  transformation", where the transformation need not be temporal: a
  definition of structure through its transformations.
- No THEORY is filed. A skim of a quarter of four chapters cannot ground
  a claim about what is true, and the pages are definitional.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.
