# THEORY-tmptbvmq: In mind-wandering the capacity for veto over one's own mental processes is kept and its representation in the self-model is lost, and on Metzinger's principle a capacity can be deliberately exercised only while it is so represented; the loss of M-autonomy is then the loss of a condition of the capacity's efficacy, not of the capacity

## Source

Metzinger (2015), LIT-tmp0w298, pp. 280–283, 290–291 and 296, read in
NOTE-tmprpztl. Metzinger (2013), LIT-tmp23d7b, p. 3 note 4, p. 5 and
p. 13, read in NOTE-tmp97akw. Metzinger (2017), LIT-tmppbt0s, pp. 13–14,
read in NOTE-tmpk5ghu.

## What was actually shown

**The capacity is kept.** "It would perhaps be tempting to say that during
such periods we have altogether lost the functional ability to control our
own thought. I want to defend a more moderate, nuanced position: what we
have lost is a specific form of knowledge, and not the ability itself,
namely, conscious knowledge of our potential for second-order mental
action. We are still persons, because we have the relevant potential. But
we currently lack an explicit and globally available representation of an
existing functional ability for active epistemic self-control — because we
have not epistemically appropriated it" (2015, p. 282). The conclusion
restates it: "not in terms of a complete loss of the relevant ability, but
only as an epistemic deficit ... an absence of representation that is not
represented as an absence" (p. 296).

**Representation as a condition of exercise.** "Call this the principle of
'Autonomy by Phenomenal Self-Representation': one can only deliberately and
autonomously exert an ability if that ability is explicitly represented in
one's phenomenal self-model" (2015, p. 280). The 2013 paper had it in a
note: "As long as the EAM still represents the ability to become an active
attentional or cognitive agent, we have M-autonomy" (p. 3, note 4).

**Why it seems otherwise.** "we confuse our abstract, retrospective, and
purely intellectual knowledge that, in principle, we had the critical
mental ability all along with what actually was the case on the level of
concrete, inner phenomenology: the absence of an EAM" (2015, pp. 282–283).

**The return.** "in most cases the re-emergence of an EAM will have to be
caused by an unconscious event, perhaps by chance, perhaps based on an
implicit knowledge about the relevant potential" (2015, p. 283).

**What could have failed.** Nothing in the papers tests it. The argument is
from the conceptual point that the person remains a person across lapses
and from the phenomenology of the return.

## What this does not say

- **That the representation causes the control.** The 2017 chapter lists
  three options, the EAM as "a mere epiphenomenon", as "exactly the missing
  computational element that constitutes" control, or as an
  intermediate that does "not constitute, but represent" and "might
  causally enable relevant second-order functional properties like veto
  control", and says "Currently available empirical data do not help to
  decide" (pp. 13–14). The principle is compatible with the third and the
  second; on the first it would be false or true only by definition.
- **That control is impossible without the representation.** "Successful
  inhibition can be an entirely subpersonal process" (2013, p. 13). The
  principle concerns deliberate, autonomous exercise, and "deliberately"
  may be defined by the representation, which would make the principle
  analytic.
- **How the kept ability would be shown to be kept.** No measure of the
  ability during an episode is proposed.
- **Anything about overt action or valuation.**
