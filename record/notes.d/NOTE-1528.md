# NOTE-1528: Szczepanski et al. — Phase-amplitude coupling and spatial attention

## Contribution

The first test of whether phase-amplitude coupling (PAC) in human
fronto-parietal cortex tracks the allocation of visuospatial attention
(p. 2: "no study to date has investigated if PAC may serve as a mechanism
for visuospatial attention control within human fronto-parietal cortex").
With electrocorticography it shows that broadband high-gamma amplitude
locks to the trough of an endogenous 2–5 Hz rhythm while attention is held,
more strongly for the contralateral field, and that trial-by-trial coupling
strength predicts reaction time.

## Key insight

The slow rhythm is not entrained by the task. Targets were jittered
(1000–2000 ms), so "the slower rhythms that we report here appear to be
endogenous and are most likely determined by the local neurophysiology"
(p. 10). What co-varies with behaviour is the coupling, not either band's
amplitude: reaction times "did not significantly correlate with mean
amplitude of the delta/theta signal … or with mean amplitude of the HG
signal" (pp. 4–5).

## Assumptions

- **High gamma indexes local spiking**, and low-frequency phase indexes
  excitability (p. 2, citing refs 11, 12). The interpretation of PAC as
  "low-frequency phase controls neuronal excitability" is a proposed
  mechanism the paper adopts, not one it tests (p. 2).
- **Patients.** n = 8 (5 left, 3 right hemisphere), with epilepsy, off
  anti-epileptic medication for 2–3 days; channels with epileptiform
  activity or over later-resected tissue were removed (Table 1, pp. 10–11).
- **PAC measure.** Phase-locking value between the 2–5 Hz phase and the
  phase of the 100–150 Hz amplitude envelope refiltered at 2–5 Hz (Voytek
  et al.'s procedure, ref. 68), tested against 1000 time-shifted
  surrogates, FDR q < 0.01 (pp. 11–12). No control for the waveform shape
  of the slow rhythm is reported.
- **Task.** The Starry Night Test: covert attention to one hemifield,
  targets 62% on the cued side, dynamic distractors; 156 attended and 96
  unattended trials per subject (p. 11).

## Key results

- **Behaviour** (p. 2). d′ = 5.03 ± 0.68; hits 97.21%; mean RT
  601.31 ± 28.64 ms; RTs slower with eccentricity, F(1,7) = 13.22, p < 0.01.
- **High gamma tracks attention** (p. 3). 263 of 875 electrodes (30.06%)
  had sustained HG increases; 47 of 263 were larger for the contralateral
  field, 3 of 263 for the ipsilateral. Alpha/beta (10–30 Hz) fell in 119
  electrodes, all parietal or visual.
- **PAC** (p. 3). 123 of 273 electrodes (45.05%, all eight subjects) showed
  significant 2–5 Hz phase to 80–250 Hz amplitude coupling during the
  0–1000 ms allocation period, PLVs 0.16–0.57. (The paper gives the HG
  electrode count as 263 on p. 3 and as the 273 PAC was tested on, also
  on p. 3; the discrepancy is not explained.)
- **Laterality** (p. 4). 55 of 123 had higher PAC for contralateral
  attention; none higher for ipsilateral. Only 2 of the 55 showed a
  matching delta/theta power difference, so "power differences between
  conditions cannot account for the PAC differences".
- **Not an evoked-potential artefact** (pp. 4, 7–10). Using only the last
  1000 ms of long trials, 107 of 273 electrodes stayed significant, all
  among the original 123; HG locked to the trough in 117 of 123 for at
  least three cycles; ERPs and PAC overlapped in only 34 electrodes.
- **PAC predicts RT** (p. 4, Fig. 4). In 28 of 123 electrodes, in seven
  subjects (frontal 42.86%, parietal 32.14%, visual 25.00%), stronger PAC
  in the 1000 ms before the target went with faster RTs: S3
  r(41) = −0.51, p < 0.01; S5 r(47) = −0.37, p < 0.05 (FDR-corrected),
  for contralateral attention only; ipsilateral r(40) = −0.11 and
  r(47) = −0.10, n.s.
- **Microsaccades are not ruled out** (p. 10); argued unlikely because the
  effects are lateralized and above 70 Hz.

## Concepts

- **Phase-amplitude coupling (PAC)** — "the statistical dependence between
  the phase of a low-frequency rhythm and the amplitude (or power) of the
  high-frequency component" (p. 2). Defined as a dependence, not as
  control.
- **High gamma (HG)** — broadband 70–250 Hz power, taken as a correlate of
  local spiking (p. 2).
- **Trough-locking** — HG power peaking at the trough of the slow signal
  (p. 4).

## Connections

- Canolty and colleagues (ref. 19) first showed theta–HG coupling in
  humans; Lakatos and colleagues (ref. 43) showed delta-phase modulation in
  macaque V1 under rhythmic attention; the present study differs by
  jittered targets and association cortex (pp. 9–10).
- The PAC method is Voytek and colleagues' (ref. 68); Voytek is a co-author
  of Donoghue et al. 2021 ([LIT-1999](../literature.d/LIT-1999.md), [NOTE-1529](NOTE-1529.md)), which warns that
  waveform shape can produce PAC.
- Rodent theta–gamma coupling tracking learning (ref. 63) is cited as
  further evidence that "PAC serves a functional role" (p. 10).

## Bearing on the record

- **Jennings 2026 ([LIT-1963](../literature.d/LIT-1963.md), [NOTE-1508](NOTE-1508.md)) overstates it in one word.**
  Jennings writes that PAC "has been shown to be associated with and to
  improve attention (Szczepanski et al., 2014)" (manuscript p. 28). The
  paper shows association: coupling strength correlates with RT in 28
  electrodes. It does not manipulate coupling, so it cannot show that
  coupling improves attention; "facilitate" in its title is the authors'
  interpretation (C4). "Associated with" is faithful; "improve" is not.
- **Nor does it show that the slow wave constrains the fast one.** Jennings
  calls PAC "evidence that low frequency waves constrain high frequency
  waves, a form of power or control" (p. 28). The paper defines PAC as a
  statistical dependence (p. 2) and takes excitability control from
  earlier work as the "physiologically plausible mechanism", a mechanism
  wholly at the level of membrane potentials and spiking. Nothing in it
  favours the contextually emergent reading of [THEORY-1314](../theory.d/THEORY-1314.md) over a
  micro-level one; it bears out that entry's note that the signature fits
  weak emergence too.
- **[THEORY-1341](../theory.d/THEORY-1341.md).** The task is covert, cue-directed spatial attention,
  i.e. selection by an instructed goal. It says nothing about fluid
  interests, stalemates or dlPFC feedback as a criterion; its frontal
  electrodes are not localized to dlPFC as such.
- **The exchange's (A5) methodological caution is apt but untested here.**
  The paper reports no check of the slow rhythm's waveform shape
  ([NOTE-1529](NOTE-1529.md)). Its coupled band, 80–250 Hz against a 2–5 Hz phase,
  is broad and far above low harmonics, which by Donoghue et al.'s own
  heuristic points toward genuine coupling rather than a shape artefact,
  but that is an inference, not a control.
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- Eight patients with epilepsy, unequal hemisphere coverage; grids at
  1 cm spacing.
- Correlational throughout; no stimulation or perturbation of the rhythm.
- The behaviour correlation holds in 28 of 123 coupled electrodes; the
  paper reports two examples, not the distribution of r.
- No control for waveform shape or aperiodic activity; microsaccades not
  excluded (p. 10).
- The 263/273 electrode count discrepancy on p. 3.

## Open questions

- Does entraining or disrupting the 2–5 Hz rhythm (e.g. by rhythmic
  stimulation) change reaction times, as "facilitate" implies?
- Does the coupling survive cycle-by-cycle waveform-shape controls?
