# CASE-tmp25bmx: J. B. Phillips's "hearty handshake all round" for Paul's "holy kiss", and Nida's judgement that it keeps the greeting

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for: a documented case for
the half of [CLAIM-061](../claims.d/CLAIM-061.md) that [CLAIM-053](../claims.d/CLAIM-053.md) and [CLAIM-tmpfe37n](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpfe37n.md) record as having
none, a rendering that changes the proposition and keeps the act.

## The case

**The source.** Paul closes Romans with a directive to the congregation.
Rom 16:16, SBLGNT: "Ἀσπάσασθε ἀλλήλους ἐν φιλήματι ἁγίῳ"; KJV: "Salute one
another with an holy kiss." The same directive closes 1 Cor 16:20,
2 Cor 13:12 and 1 Thess 5:26, and, as "a kiss of love", 1 Pet 5:14.

**The rendering.** J. B. Phillips, *Letters to Young Churches* (1947) and
*The New Testament in Modern English* (1958, revised 1972), renders every one
as a handshake. Nida (1964, p. 160) quotes the pre-1972 text of Rom 16:16 as
"give one another a hearty handshake all around." The 1972 text, as Bible
Gateway gives it (version PHILLIPS, read 2026-10-10):

- Rom 16:16: "Give each other a hearty handshake all round for my sake."
- 1 Cor 16:20: "I should like you to shake hands all round as a sign of
  Christian love."
- 2 Cor 13:12: "A handshake all round, please!"
- 1 Thess 5:26: "Give a handshake all round among the brotherhood."
- 1 Pet 5:14: "Give each other a handshake all round as a sign of love."

The mapping is wider than the kiss. Phillips renders the plain greetings of
Rom 16:3–7, which have no kiss, the same way: "Shake hands for me with
Priscilla and Aquila", "A handshake too for Andronicus and Junias". He is
translating Paul's greeting, not his gesture.

**What changes.** The two directives have different satisfaction conditions.
A congregation that shakes hands has not done what the Greek says, and one
that kisses has not done what Phillips says. "Holy" is dropped; Rom 16:16
adds "for my sake"; two verses state the function outright ("as a sign of
Christian love").

**The judgement that the act survives.** Nida, *Toward a Science of
Translating* ([LIT-791](../literature.d/LIT-791.md)), pp. 159–60, as transcribed by Marlowe:

- the gloss alternative: "holy kiss" "would be rendered literally, and would
  probably be supplemented with a footnote explaining that this was a
  customary method of greeting in New Testament times";
- the principle: "the relationship between receptor and message should be
  substantially the same as that which existed between the original
  receptors and the message";
- the verdict: Phillips "quite naturally translates" the one as the other.

Munday (2016, p. 70) calls it "a notorious example", and grants that "some
may feel the loss of the source culture term/custom". Ellingworth and Nida's
UBS handbook on 1 Thessalonians, on 5:26, gives the act-reading ("the 'holy
kiss' was more like a spontaneous greeting between friends") and calls
Phillips "a brave attempt to find an equivalent custom in a western culture",
while preferring the Good News Translation's solution. The handbook's lemma
is "A brotherly kiss", which keeps the kiss; that this was the GNT text of
the day is inferred from the lemma, and the GNT now on Bible Gateway reads
"the kiss of peace". Gayle (2013) dissents: Phillips's handshakes, which he
also gives Priscilla and Junias, change who greets whom across the lines of
gender and culture, so the relation among the addressees is not kept.

**The sufficiency half, from the same handbooks.** Ellingworth and Nida:
"in many parts of the world kissing is regarded as exclusively an expression
of amorous or erotic interest. Under such circumstances one cannot use a
literal expression for 'kiss.'" The UBS note on Rom 16:16 reports, from van
Steenbergen, that in Pökoot "kissing in public, certainly between men, is
absolutely unacceptable", and gives "greet warmly". There the literal
rendering keeps the proposition and changes the act. One verse, in one body
of documented translation practice, carries both halves of [CLAIM-061](../claims.d/CLAIM-061.md).

## What it can show

That a published, attested rendering changed what a text directs, and that
the leading theorist of the move, the handbook tradition that applied it,
and the textbook that teaches it all took the communicative act to survive:
an apostolic directive in a letter's closing, telling the addressees to
perform their culture's ordinary warm greeting of fellowship. Measured against
the conditions the search set:

| | Met? | |
|---|---|---|
| (a) source and rendering attested | Yes | Greek and the 1972 English directly; the pre-1972 text through Nida and Munday |
| (b) proposition changed, not only wording | Yes | Different compliance conditions; "holy" dropped |
| (c) independent evidence the act was kept | Partly | Expert judgement, divided; no reader evidence |
| (d) interlingual | Yes | Greek to English |

So the necessity half of [CLAIM-061](../claims.d/CLAIM-061.md) has a documented case, not only
A12's sentence: A without P, in the notation of [CLAIM-tmpfe37n](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpfe37n.md), as
judged by competent third parties. Nida is a third party to Phillips's text
and to this exchange.

## What it cannot show

**That the act was in fact kept.** Condition (c) is met only by verdicts,
and they are divided on the residue. Nida is the advocate of the principle the
example illustrates. The UBS handbook calls the rendering brave and prefers
one that keeps the kiss. Munday notes the loss of the custom. Gayle denies
that the footing among the addressees survives. The holiness or ritual
weight, the intimacy, and the gendered footing are each disputed. The case
establishes that competent judges took the act to survive a change of
proposition. It does not establish how readers took it.

**That the defender of necessity is beaten.** The defender's reply: the
proposition worth keeping is the coarser "greet one another warmly", and the
handshake keeps it. The handbooks themselves offer that proposition as a
rendering ("greet fellow believers affectionately"; "greet warmly"), and
Phillips writes the function into two verses ("as a sign of Christian
love").

How far the reply reaches:

- **It reaches every necessity case.** Any rendering that keeps the act
  while changing the proposition can be redescribed as keeping some coarser
  proposition that names the act's function. Retreating to the coarse
  proposition is always available.
- **It costs the defender the distinction the claim is about.** "Greet one
  another warmly" is satisfied by a kiss in Corinth and a handshake in 1947
  London. Its content is fixed by what counts as a warm greeting where it
  is received. Its satisfaction conditions are those of the act. A defender
  who individuates propositions this way keeps the word "proposition" and
  concedes [CLAIM-061](../claims.d/CLAIM-061.md)'s point: what has to be kept is fixed by function,
  not by what is literally said.
- **It does not reach the fine-grained proposition.** The defender who means
  the proposition as the text's own satisfaction conditions, a kiss, has no
  reply. The case refutes necessity for that proposition, on the judges'
  verdict.

The case therefore settles nothing between the two individuations. It forces
the choice.

**Anything about frequency.** It bears on the modal claim only, an existence
proof with expert support. It does not supply the comparative test
[CLAIM-115](../claims.d/CLAIM-115.md) needs.

**What would close the gap.** A reader test on these texts. Readers of one
population would be shown the literal Rom 16:16 ("greet one another with a
holy kiss"), Phillips's handshake, the coarse "greet one another warmly", and
the kiss in a culture where it reads as amorous. They would judge force,
footing, warmth and the relation among the addressees. A separate set of
raters would judge propositional sameness, so that the proposition is
individuated before the act is measured. That is the independence the
defender's reply exploits. The texts are documented and the test is not
run. The verses could serve as items for the discriminating study,
[CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md), which rates its predictors with other raters and has an
interlingual arm. Until such a test is run, (c) rests on verdicts.

**Unverified.** The 1947 and 1958 printed texts were not seen: the Internet
Archive scans are lending-only. The pre-1972 wording rests on Nida's
quotation, as transcribed by Marlowe and cited by Munday. Nida's page is
inferred from Marlowe's page markers and confirmed by Munday's citation
("1964a: 160"). The UBS handbooks were read only as quoted on the UBS
translation-tips site. Phillips's own aim is stated in the 1955 discussion
with Rieu ("I do so agree with this principle of producing equivalent
effect"), which does not mention the kiss. Nida's text dates that discussion
1954.
