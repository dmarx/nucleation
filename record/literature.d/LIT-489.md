# LIT-489: Institutions

Douglass C. North (1991), *Journal of Economic Perspectives 5(1):97–112, Winter 1991* — DOI-10.1257/jep.5.1.97

## Key takeaways

- Institutions are humanly devised constraints, informal and formal, that
  reduce uncertainty in exchange. Together with the ordinary constraints
  of economics they set the choice set, and so transaction and production
  costs.
- Impersonal exchange among many players, with poor information and no
  repeated play, is where cooperation fails. It needs third-party
  enforcement, and in the end a state that is itself constrained.
- Institutional change is incremental and path dependent: organisations
  owe their existence to the existing framework, so the framework has
  increasing returns, and nothing guarantees that it evolves toward
  growth.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, as part of filling out the
record's coverage of `social-ontology` ([ADR-017](../decisions.d/ADR-017.md)). It is here as the
economists' definition of an institution, which [ADR-017](../decisions.d/ADR-017.md) names as on the
boundary between `social-science` and `social-ontology`.
[NOTE-392](../notes.d/NOTE-392.md) is the reading.

**Topic.** `social-science` is primary. The essay's subject is the role
of institutions in economic performance, argued from economic history.
Its definition of an institution is one paragraph, stipulated, and is the
reason `social-ontology` is the second tag. `society-and-governance`
holds its property rights, merchant courts and constitutional
constraints on the state.

**Neighbours.**

- Hindriks & Guala ([LIT-481](LIT-481.md)) open with North's definition, quoted
  from his 1990 book, as the exemplar of the "rules account". Their
  objection is that a rules account cannot say why some rules are
  followed and others are not. North's essay does not answer it: it
  explains compliance by the costs of enforcement and ends by asking how
  informal constraints come to bind.
- Guala's book ([LIT-472](LIT-472.md)) opens chapter 1 with the same definition
  (from the 1990 book) and, per its index, returns to North on p. 82.
- The SEP entry ([LIT-487](LIT-487.md), §5.3) places North 1990 in the "new
  institutional economics" programme, which it describes as largely
  game-theoretic. This essay uses game theory only in its second
  paragraph.
- Sen's papers on social choice ([LIT-285](LIT-285.md), [LIT-282](LIT-282.md)) concern how individual
  preferences aggregate into a social decision, not how the rules that
  constrain choice arise. Nothing in this essay connects to them.
