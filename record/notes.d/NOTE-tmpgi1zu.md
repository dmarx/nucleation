# NOTE-tmpgi1zu: James, Hynes, Whelan, Dreher and Humphry — From access and transparency to refusal

## Contribution

It names three responses to the harms of data-driven government and ranks
them. Access and inclusion, and transparency, "seek to remedy harms" while
working "largely within the prevailing logic of algorithmic governance".
Data justice, abolition and refusal contest that logic. It treats the
three as "a dialogue rather than a chronology". In the record's terms, its
sharpest point is that access and transparency each run in two
directions.

## Key insight

Being included is being made visible. "Access is generally angled 'up':
something is made available to those who otherwise 'lack' it. Access is
however bidirectional, and access 'down' for powerful players is
politically consequential." The same holds for transparency: in
Australia's Robo-debt "No credible claim to transparency was made ...,
though transparency was arguably demanded of welfare recipients through
the 'reverse onus'". So whether a remedy helps depends on who is made
legible to whom, which a demand for more access or more disclosure does
not settle.

## Assumptions

- Its object is "algorithmic governance": algorithms, and the data they
  rely on, "used within institutional government settings to assist in
  decision-making and service delivery" (after Danaher), with the focus on
  public-sector services and social welfare.
- Its normative starting point is data justice: begin from "impacted
  communities and social groups", and treat datafication as political
  rather than technological (Dencik and Kaun).
- The evidence is illustrative cases from the literature and the
  authors' research: LinkNYC kiosks, Australia's Digital Transformation
  Strategy, Robo-debt, algorithmic impact assessments, Australia's Smart
  Cities and Suburbs Program, #BlockSidewalk in Toronto, tech-worker
  refusals.

## Key results

- **Two theses.** Responses drawing on "traditional public administration
  values are not well formulated for addressing the various harms", and
  data justice and refusal "holds more promise than access, inclusion and
  transparency".
- **Access and inclusion.** It does not resolve the exclusions that the
  digital divide follows. It brings "exposure to increased monitoring,
  social sorting and surveillance", and it reduces non-digital
  alternatives, so that services can fail at a single point. LinkNYC's
  free Wi-Fi is read, after Green (2020), as a means to collect and
  monetize data. It quotes Benjamin: "what does it mean to be included,
  and hence more accurately identifiable, in an unjust set of social
  relations?" It also states the paper's charge against "horizontal
  neutrality": it "exacerbates existing inequities precisely by treating
  everybody the same."
- **Transparency.** In government "performance" it relies on "techniques
  of classification, measurement, evaluation and rank, which bring into
  view that which is to be rendered transparent and acted upon";
  "Transparency is a tautological value which justifies itself: it is good
  to see that it is good to see." Notice does not secure consent. Where
  comprehension and contestation cannot be assured, the limits "may render
  conventional notions of transparency trivial and incongruous". The
  "procedural management of opacity" hides a second, contractual opacity:
  public–private partnerships and proprietary algorithms.
- **Refusal.** "the capacity to say 'no' to the application and
  development of data-driven systems", drawn from Benjamin, the Feminist
  Data Manifest-No and Barabas. It is generative ("refusal and
  commitment"), collective, and "not reducible to voluntaristic forms of
  exit": "Refusal is not absence." Its cases are #BlockSidewalk, whose aim
  was to "put No on the table" and which outlasted Sidewalk Labs'
  withdrawal in May 2020; the Google Walkout; UK councils that abandoned
  tools; and Swedish strikes against automating benefit decisions.
- **Limits of refusal.** "Those reliant on government services are not
  afforded many concrete opportunities to refuse." Under Robo-debt it was
  "'impossible to refuse' the recovery of the assumed debt". Refusal "does
  not entail success", is also bidirectional, and may be co-opted, "where
  everyone could ... 'just say no'". The authors "have yet to find
  widespread collective refusals" in social welfare.

## Concepts

- **Algorithmic governance**: algorithms and their data, used in
  government settings for decisions and service delivery; also the "new
  modes of social ordering" these produce.
- **Response**: a theoretical and policy stance toward algorithmic
  governance, characterized by its alignment to the system it would
  change. The three responses overlap and do not form a sequence.
- **Bidirectionality**: access, transparency and refusal each run both
  ways between governed and governing.
- **Refusal**: "an informed practice of 'talking back'" (Garcia et al.),
  paired with a commitment to alternatives. Its two implications are the
  "distributed capacity to act on an objection" and its social iteration.

## Connections

It sets itself beside Pasquale's two "waves" of algorithmic
accountability (improve the system, or ask whether it is needed), and
builds on Dencik and colleagues' data justice, Benjamin's abolitionist
tools, Couldry and Mejias's data colonialism, Eubanks, Costanza-Chock's
design justice, and Barabas's refusal by computational practitioners.
It does not cite Scott, Espeland, or the epistemic-injustice literature.

## Bearing on the record

- **[TERM-tmp5lqn0](../terms.d/TERM-tmp5lqn0.md).** C2 is the term's directedness, argued from the
  governed side. "Society legible to an institution" is LinkNYC's access
  "down". Robo-debt shows transparency demanded of the observed and
  withheld by the observer. The paper adds that the two directions can be
  traded against each other: a remedy offered as legibility of the
  institution can be delivered as legibility of its subjects. Its line on
  transparency through "classification, measurement, evaluation and
  rank" is the term's regime in other words. A25's "counterweight" is
  accurate: the paper asks whether one should be inside the regime at all.
  Its answer is not "never". It is that the choice to be inside should be
  open, and that it often is not.

  itself be the harm, by making people "more accurately identifiable".

## Limitations

- **No evidence of comparative outcomes.** The claim that refusal "holds
  more promise" rests on argument and selected cases. No case is shown in
  which access or transparency failed where refusal succeeded, or the
  reverse. For #BlockSidewalk the paper itself reports that Sidewalk Labs
  withdrew "citing pandemic related business concerns", so the campaign's
  part in the outcome is not shown.
- **The responses are characterized at their weakest.** Transparency is
  discussed mostly in its procedural forms (notice, rights to
  explanation, impact assessments). Contestation built into transparency
  regimes is mentioned only as what they lack.
- **Refusal's operationalization is left open.** The authors say so: they
  "offer suggestions" and note the risk of co-optation.
- **Scope.** The case material is Australian, UK, US and Canadian public
  services and smart cities.

## Open questions

- When does a remedy offered as making the institution legible to the
  governed become, in practice, making the governed legible to the
  institution? The paper gives instances and no conditions.
- What does refusal require of an institution's procedures, rather than
  of its opponents? That would connect it to [CLAIM-tmpj163u](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpj163u.md)'s top rung.
