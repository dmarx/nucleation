## Summary

A PRISMA-ScR scoping review (protocol on OSF, January 2025). Searches of
PsycINFO, Scopus, Web of Science and DiGRA (6 Feb 2024, for 2017-2023), with a
Web of Science title update in March 2026, found 406 records; 72 empirical
studies were included. Two coders extracted every design feature the studies
treat as problematic and arranged them in three levels: 6 categories, 22
subcategories, 136 features (Cohen's kappa 0.78; three external game-design
experts consulted). Features are grouped by the kind of problem they cause
players, as the literature reports it, not by mechanics. Each feature's n is
the number of studies naming it.

## Contribution

The most granular literature-derived checklist of loot-box design features so
far (against Ballou et al.'s 32 features and Denoo et al.'s 51, p. 12), with a
coding scheme meant to standardize vocabulary and to let screening tools and
regulation address design variants rather than "the loot box" as one thing.

## Key results

1. Abstract, p. 1: "72 empirically based studies published between 2017 and
   2026 met the inclusion criteria. These studies revealed 136 distinct loot
   box design features organized into 22 subcategories across six broader
   dimensions".
2. Abstract: "Significant research gaps were identified, especially regarding
   the impact and interaction of these features."
3. p. 4: the five commonest: displayable cosmetic rewards (3.1.1, n = 38),
   openings requiring in-game currency conversions (1.1.1, n = 36), pay-to-win
   rewards (4.2.1, n = 33), virtual goods exchangeable for real money (6.2.1,
   n = 32), high odds disparity between rare and non-rare rewards (2.2.1,
   n = 23).
4. Category sizes (pp. 4, 12): Obfuscation 35, Psychological Manipulation 23,
   Social Influence and Promotion 23, Value/Data/Engagement Manipulation 28,
   Self-Regulation Obstacles 17, Gambling Resemblance 10 (sum 136, checked).
5. p. 4: the third digit of a code ranks prevalence in the literature: "A
   lower third number indicated a higher prevalence in the literature".
6. Table 2 (pp. 13-16), features bearing on cost measurement: 1.2.1 "Reward
   pool, availability, or odds change with progression or system
   conditions"; 1.2.6 "Odds vary across player accounts"; 4.1.2 "Dynamic LB
   odds, pricing, or offers based on player behavior." (n = 3); 2.3.1-2.3.3
   pity timers and guarantees; 4.5.1 bundles; 4.5.3 "Fixed purchase rates for
   in-game currency packs"; 4.5.8 "Higher odds for new or returning
   accounts"; 5.2.4 "Odds increase when purchasing in bulk"; 2.1.4 daily free
   boxes.
7. p. 17: the March 2026 update added mostly obfuscation features,
   "particularly odds changeability and information opacity", which "likely
   reflects the regulatory prominence of transparency-related measures".
8. p. 17: fixed currency bundles leave "leftover balances that encourage
   additional spending".
9. p. 18: pity timers "players often view as offering better value [104], yet
   they may also encourage additional spending".
10. p. 18 (limitations): "assessing the relative severity or harm of
    individual features remains difficult"; the review is "a catalog of
    features rather than a detailed evaluation of individual mechanics".
11. p. 18 (conclusion): "the risks associated with LBs stem from the
    interaction of multiple features within broader monetization systems,
    rather than a single design element."
12. p. 18: regulation: Belgium's ban circumvented by VPNs; China requires
    odds disclosure and caps daily openings; PEGI and ESRB allow loot boxes in
    all-ages games.

## Assumptions

That the literature's attention marks the features that matter (n counts
studies, not games or harm); that a feature can be placed by "its most
relevant problematic aspect" in one subcategory only; that what researchers
call problematic is the frame (it notes players may view the same features
favourably, p. 17).

## Verdict on the source's characterization

A (exchange "Medical Ethics and Media Enshitification", A29 §4, A31 §3):
"a scoping review identifying 136 loot-box design features across 72 studies,
with little known of their separate and joint effects." **Accurate.** Two
refinements: the 136 are features *studied as problematic*, ranked by how
often the literature names them, not a census of games; and "little known of
their effects" is the authors' own statement (results 2, 10). CASE-248's

## Bearing on named codes

- **CASE-248** (Proposed; loot-box cost of a standardized reward across
  releases). *Supports and sharpens.* The review backs the case's list of
  things to record (free draws 2.1.4, pity 2.3.x, bundles 4.5.1, currency
  rates 1.1.x, 4.5.3, direct purchase vs exclusive-to-box 4.2.5). It adds a
  measurement threat the case does not name: odds and prices that vary by
  account, behaviour, location, bulk size or account age (1.2.4-1.2.6, 4.1.2,
  4.5.8, 5.2.4). Where they apply, "the real-money cost of a standardized
  reward" is not one distribution per release but one per player segment,
  and disclosed odds "may not consistently reflect the actual chances players
  face" (Table 2, 1.2). The case's level-1 data (published odds) would miss
  this. It also confirms the case's choice to track the distribution, since
  the review stresses polarization and tail features (2.2.1, 2.3).
- **CLAIM-653 / CLAIM-658** (Proposed; heavy users or spenders set the design
  everyone is offered). *Bears weakly; no test.* The review catalogues
  features, not how designs come about, so it neither shows nor refutes
  revenue-weighted design drift. 4.1.2 (dynamic odds and offers by player
  behaviour) and 4.1.3 (personalized offers) are a mechanism by which design
  can target spenders *without* changing what everyone is offered: a rival to
  the claims' "shared product" premise, worth a sentence in CASE-248's
  hypotheses.
- **CASE-250** (Proposed; monetization design that lowers the efficacy of
  users' commitments). Self-Regulation Obstacles (5.1-5.3: no spending
  limits, no confirmation steps, notifications that cannot be turned off) are
  direct instances of the designs CASE-250 targets.

## Limitations

- The review counts mentions, not effects; prevalence in the literature is
  not prevalence in games or harm (the authors say so, p. 18).
- Internal inconsistencies: "55 published in peer-reviewed journals and 12 in
  conference proceedings" (p. 4) sums to 67, not 72; the update is "March
  2026" in Methods (pp. 1-2) and "February 2026" in the Discussion (p. 17);
  the PRISMA counts do reconcile (367 + 39 = 406; 87 − 11 − 9 + 5 = 72).
- The update was narrower than the main search (one database, titles only,
  articles only) and admitted only studies with new features, so the n
  values of features named after 2023 are undercounted.
- Placement of each feature in one subcategory is a judgment, and the
  ordering by problem type presupposes the harms that the review says are
  unmeasured (step 9a: the categories assert a harm mechanism the evidence
  does not show).
- Supplementary File 1 (the checklist with keywords) was not fetched.
