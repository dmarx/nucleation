# NOTE-385: Of Identity and Diversity

## Contribution

The chapter makes personal identity a question of its own, distinct from
the identity of substances and from the identity of the living human
animal. It gives an answer, sameness of consciousness, that is independent
of what the thinking substance is. It also supplies the cases the later
literature is built from. Before it, the identity of a person was assumed to
follow from the identity of a soul or a body. After it, there is a stated
psychological criterion, and a set of cases in which soul, body and person
come apart.

## Key insight

"Same" is incomplete until one says same *what*. Identity is fixed by the
idea the name stands for (§7, §28): a mass is the same while its particles
are, a plant or animal or man while its organised life continues, and a
person while its consciousness does. So the question "is this the same
person?" is answered by consciousness, whatever the answer to "is this the
same substance?" turns out to be.

## Assumptions

- **Ideas fix identity conditions.** "Such as is the idea belonging to that
  name, such must be the identity" (§7). The chapter's results depend on
  the reader sharing Locke's ideas of man (a body "of a certain form", §8)
  and of person (§9).
- **Consciousness accompanies all thinking.** "It being impossible for any
  one to perceive without perceiving that he does perceive" (§9).
- **Substance is unknown.** The chapter repeatedly brackets whether the
  thinking thing is material or immaterial, simple or compound (§§17, 23,
  27). Its conclusions are meant to hold on either hypothesis.
- **Divine goodness.** That consciousness of a past action is never
  transferred to a substance that did not do it cannot be shown "from the
  nature of things", and is "best resolved into the goodness of God" (§13).

## Key results

- **Principium individuationis (§3).** It is "existence itself; which
  determines a being of any sort to a particular time and place,
  incommunicable to two beings of the same kind". Two things of one kind
  cannot be in one place at one time, and one thing cannot have "two
  beginnings of existence" (§1).
- **Masses and living things (§§3–6).** A mass of atoms "is no longer the
  same mass" if one atom is added or taken away. An oak, a horse and a man
  are the same while they partake of "the same continued life" in
  "constantly fleeting particles of matter" (§6). A watch is the analogy for
  an animal, with the difference that in the animal "the motion coming from
  within" (§5).
- **Same man (§§6, 8).** If the same soul made the same man, Seth, Ismael,
  Socrates, Pilate and Caesar Borgia might be one man (§6). A rational
  parrot would still be called a parrot, and a creature of human shape
  without reason a man (§8, with Temple's account of Prince Maurice's
  parrot). So "man" is a body "so and so shaped" with a mind joined to it.
- **Person and personal identity (§9).** A person is "a thinking intelligent
  being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself,
  the same thinking thing, in different times and places". Its identity
  reaches "as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any
  past action or thought".
- **Substance does not decide it (§§10–14, 23–25).**
  - Different substances united "by the same consciousness" make one
    person, as different particles united by one life make one animal
    (§10).
  - Whether consciousness can pass between thinking substances cannot be
    settled by us; if it can, "two thinking substances may make but one
    person" (§13).
  - An immaterial spirit stripped of all memory of a former life begins a
    new person. The man who believed his soul had been Socrates's, or
    Nestor's, is not the same person with them (§14).
  - Day-man and night-man: two "incommunicable consciousnesses" acting one
    body by turns are two persons, and one consciousness acting two bodies
    by turns is one person (§23).
- **Man and person come apart (§15).** The prince's soul, "carrying with it
  the consciousness of the prince's past life", in the cobbler's body is
  "the same person with the prince", but "the same cobbler to every one
  besides himself".
- **The boundary of the self (§§11, 17, 18, 25).** A limb is part of the
  self while consciousness reaches it, and no part once cut off. If
  consciousness went with a severed little finger, "the little finger would
  be the person, the same person" (§17). If the body kept its own separate
  consciousness after the separation, it "would not at all be concerned"
  for the finger (§18).
- **Forgetting and drunkenness (§§20, 22).** Total, irretrievable forgetting
  separates the forgotten actions from the person but not from the man, and
  "human laws not punishing the mad man for the sober man's actions" show
  the sense of mankind. Drunkards are punished anyway "because the fact is
  proved against him, but want of consciousness cannot be proved for him"
  (§22); at the Great Day, no one will answer for what he knows nothing of.
- **Person a forensic term (§26).** "It is a forensic term, appropriating
  actions and their merit; and so belongs only to intelligent agents,
  capable of a law, and happiness, and misery." Punishment for an act one
  cannot be made conscious of differs not at all from "being created
  miserable".
- **Conclusion (§§28–29).** "The difficulty or obscurity that has been
  about this matter rather rises from the names ill-used, than from any
  obscurity in things themselves." Whatever complex idea one takes "man" to
  be, "the same existence continued preserves it the same individual under
  the same denomination".

## Concepts

- **identity / diversity** — the ideas formed by comparing a thing existing
  at one time and place with itself existing at another (§1).
- **principium individuationis** — existence itself, fixing a being to a
  time and place (§3).
- **man** — an animal of a certain shape; the same man is the same
  continued life in one organised body (§§6, 8).
- **person** — a thinking intelligent being that can consider itself as
  itself in different times and places (§9); "the name for this self"
  (§26).
- **self** — "that conscious thinking thing … which is sensible or
  conscious of pleasure and pain, capable of happiness or misery, and so is
  concerned for itself, as far as that consciousness extends" (§17).
- **consciousness** — never defined. It is "inseparable from thinking"
  (§9), "a present representation of a past action" (§13), and the thing
  that can be "extended" backwards and forwards.
- **forensic** — of persons: the term that appropriates actions and their
  merit (§26).

## Connections

- **Hume's "Of Personal Identity"**, filed beside this reading, takes up
  Locke's analogy between the identity of plants and animals and the
  identity of persons, and treats both as fictions of the imagination.
  Hume names "of late years in England" as the place the question became
  great, and in his Appendix he says "most philosophers seem inclin'd to
  think, that personal identity arises from consciousness", which is this
  chapter's thesis.
- **Parfit's "Personal Identity"**, filed beside it, cites §18 (in Yolton's
  edition) as the place where division is "implicit". The finger that keeps
  its own consciousness is a case of one person becoming two. Parfit's
  quasi-memory answers Butler's charge, which Parfit names, that Locke's
  account is circular.
- **Survey** ([LIT-130](../literature.d/LIT-130.md), [NOTE-166](NOTE-166.md)). The survey begins its
  psychological criterion and its responsibility section with Locke, Butler
  and Reid.
- **Velleman** ([LIT-292](../literature.d/LIT-292.md), [NOTE-268](NOTE-268.md)). Velleman reads Locke as making
  survival "a matter of memory alone". The chapter speaks of
  "consciousness" of "thoughts and actions" and of concern for one's own
  happiness (§§17, 25–26). It is wider than memory in what it says, though
  every case it uses is a case of remembering.
- **Floridi** ([LIT-136](../literature.d/LIT-136.md), [NOTE-130](NOTE-130.md)). That reading reports Floridi holding
  that Locke was right to distinguish kinds of sameness question and wrong
  to commit ontologically. §28's
  diagnosis, that the difficulty lies in names, not in things, is already
  half of Floridi's deflation.
- **Dennett** ([LIT-405](../literature.d/LIT-405.md)). The brain-in-a-vat story is the prince and the
  cobbler with radio links. Dennett's narrator says a legal argument "might
  have appealed to" Locke; the chapter's forensic person is the reason.

## Bearing on the record

No THEORY document in the record cites this chapter. It bears on the
record's treatment of AI subjects only through its descendants:
Birch's persisting-interlocutor argument ([LIT-111](../literature.d/LIT-111.md), [NOTE-183](NOTE-183.md)) rests on
Parfit's Relation R, which is Locke's criterion with quasi-memory in place
of memory and with causal continuity added. The chapter carries no
instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

What is shown and what is asserted:

- **Shown, as conceptual separation:** that "same man", "same substance" and
  "same person" can be given different conditions, and that cases can be
  described in which they diverge (C5).
- **Asserted, not shown:** that the conditions of personal identity are those
  of consciousness (C3). The cases make the criterion vivid; they rely on
  the reader's agreeing that the prince is in the cobbler.
- **Undefined:** "the same consciousness". Locke grants that it is "not the
  same individual act" (§13), so it is a relation between acts, and he does
  not say which. This is the gap Butler and Reid pressed, which the chapter
  does not anticipate.
- **Closed by theology:** whether false consciousness of another's act could
  make one the same person is left to "the goodness of God" (§13).
- **No transitivity.** The chapter does not consider that consciousness
  reaching back from A to B and from B to C need not reach from A to C. That
  is Reid's brave-officer objection, later the reason for Parfit's
  distinction between connectedness and continuity.

## Open questions

- What relation between a present act and a past one makes them "the same
  consciousness"? The chapter's only gloss, "a present representation of a
  past action", would include false memory, which §13 sees and does not
  resolve.
- Does Locke's person persist through gaps in consciousness, such as sleep?
  §10 says the doubt such gaps raise "concerns not personal identity at
  all", but the criterion as stated in §9 would need overlapping chains to
  say why.
