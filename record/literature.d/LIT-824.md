# LIT-824: Informative Communication in Word Production and Word Learning

Michael C. Frank, Noah D. Goodman, Peter Lai and Joshua B. Tenenbaum (2009),
in N. Taatgen and H. van Rijn (eds), *Proceedings of the 31st Annual
Conference of the Cognitive Science Society*, Amsterdam, pp. 1228–1233.

## Key takeaways

- **The informative speaker.** Meanings are distributions over the objects
  in a context. A word's literal meaning w̃_C is uniform over its extension.
  The speaker chooses w with P(w | M_S, C) ∝ exp(α U), where
  U = −D_KL(M_S ‖ w̃_C) + F, and F stands for other costs (set to 0 here).
  With α = 1 this is the Luce choice rule.
- **The size principle falls out.** When the intended meaning is a single
  object, D_KL reduces to −log w̃_C(o_S), so P(w | M_S, C) ∝ |w|^(−α) over
  the true words (Eq. 10): a speaker prefers words that pick out smaller
  parts of the context. The authors offer this as a communicative
  derivation of Shepard's and Tenenbaum and Griffiths's size principle.
- **The learner inverts the speaker.** A learner who knows the referent but
  not the lexicon updates P(L | w, M_S, C) ∝ P(w | L, M_S, C) P(L). With two
  features and two candidate lexicons, the probability that the word names
  feature f₁ is |f₁|⁻¹ / (|f₁|⁻¹ + |f₂|⁻¹) (Eq. 12): $67 on "red" in the
  one-red-circle display.
- **Experiment 1 (learning).** 700 web participants split $100 between two
  candidate meanings of a novel word across displays of six objects. Mean
  bets track the model at Pearson r = .93 (Spearman .92). They sit near $50
  when the two features are equally frequent, and the Figure 1 case gave
  $70 against a predicted $67.
- **Experiment 2 (production).** 44 participants wrote descriptions of 50 of
  304 Flickr "superball" photos, so that someone could pick each out. The
  probability of using a ball's tag-word correlates with Eq. 10 at r = .19
  overall. Without basic colour terms it is .51, and for colour terms
  alone .02. Adding a Google-Images frequency estimate of F barely helps
  (.22 and .52). The authors blame how well a tag fits the ball.
- **No recursion.** The authors contrast their framework with game-theoretic
  pragmatics, which "rel[ies] on complex, recursive computations". There is
  one speaker and one learner who inverts it, and no pragmatic listener
  who infers a referent.

Read on 2026-10-09 ([NOTE-627](../notes.d/NOTE-627.md)). It is the informative-speaker
precursor of the rational-speech-act line the record already holds. The same
authors' 2009 *Psychological Science* paper ([LIT-815](LIT-815.md)) is a different
work: a word learner that infers referential intentions, with no
informative speaker. The listener who inverts this speaker to find a
referent, with a salience prior, is Frank and Goodman 2012 ([LIT-821](LIT-821.md)).
The lexical-uncertainty recursion is Bergen, Goodman and Levy 2012
([LIT-809](LIT-809.md)), and the framework's statement is Goodman and Frank 2016
([LIT-816](LIT-816.md)). Experiment 1 adds to the evidence for
[THEORY-172](../theory.d/THEORY-172.md) in its word-learning form, with the referent given and the
lexicon inferred. Experiment 2 is the record's only evidence on the speaker
side, and it is weak.
