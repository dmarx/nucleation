# NOTE-tmpvh2sy: Nisbett and Wilson — Telling more than we can know

## Contribution

Before it, cognitive psychologists had asserted, mostly from research on
perception and memory, that people cannot observe higher mental
processes. The paper supplies evidence for higher-order processes
(evaluation, choice, inference, problem solving) and answers the question
the bare assertion left open: if not from introspection, where do
people's fluent reports about why they acted come from? Its answer is
that they come from causal theories about what kind of stimulus produces
what kind of response, which an observer who has only a description of
the situation holds as well. It then says when such reports will be right
and when wrong.

## Key insight

A report of why one responded is a judgment of plausibility, not a
readout. It feels like introspection and is not. Polanyi's "we can know
more than we can tell" has a converse: people "sometimes tell more than
we can know". The proof is that observers who never met the stimulus
predict the same effects that the subjects report, so the subjects need
no "fount of privileged knowledge" (Bem's phrase) to produce them.

## Assumptions

- **The target is process, not content.** The paper grants that people
  know their own history, current sensations, focus of attention, and
  much about their emotions, evaluations and plans. What it denies is
  access to how a stimulus produced a response.
- **"Awareness" is redefined.** Correct report is not enough. The
  criterion proposed is a verbal report "which exceeds in accuracy that
  obtained from observers" given a general description of stimulus and
  response.
- **Reports were taken soon after.** Its own studies ask within seconds
  or minutes, so the claim is that such access as exists is insufficient
  at that interval, not that access is impossible under coaching or
  interruption.
- **The situations were chosen to mislead.** The authors say so: they
  picked stimuli they expected subjects to be wrong about, and add that
  their own predictions of which stimuli would matter were no better
  than the subjects' reports.

## Key results

**Three conclusions** (stated at the outset). People often cannot report
accurately on the effects of stimuli on higher-order responses, and
sometimes cannot report the stimulus, the response or that any process
occurred. Their reports rest on a priori theories. When reports are
right, it is because the theory happened to be right.

**From the literature.**

- **Dissonance and attribution.** Of the studies that measured both
  behaviour and verbal report at about the same time, most found no
  verbal-report difference at all (17 entries, one of them five
  experiments), and in most of the
  rest the behavioural effect was the more reliable. Where the
  correlation between report and behaviour was reported (two studies) or
  obtained by writing to the authors (three), it was near zero. Bem and
  McConnell's and Goethals and Reckman's subjects changed their
  attitudes and recalled their earlier attitudes as their new ones; in
  the busing study, pro-busing subjects converted by a confederate
  recalled having been more anti-busing than the anti-busing subjects
  recalled being.
- **Interviews.** In Nisbett and Schachter (1966), pill-attribution
  subjects took four times as much amperage, and 3 of 12 reported the
  attribution to the pill. Storms and Nisbett's insomniacs fell asleep
  28% faster (arousal pill) or took 42% longer (relaxation pill) and
  explained the change by exams, roommates and the day of the week.
  Aronson and Zimbardo, asked by letter, reported the same.
- **Subliminal perception** (W. R. Wilson 1975): tone sequences played to
  the unattended ear could not be recognized above chance but were liked
  more.
- **Maier (1931).** After the experimenter set a cord swinging, subjects
  solved the two-cord problem, usually within 45 seconds, and few named
  the hint. A useless hint given first was credited instead by every
  subject who received it.
- **Latané and Darley (1970).** Subjects denied that bystanders affected
  their helping, and observers predicted no effect.

**Its own studies** (subjects introductory psychology students unless
noted).

- **Word pairs.** 81 men memorized pairs such as "ocean–moon"; the cues
  doubled target responses ("Tide") from 10% to 20% (p < .001), and
  subjects almost never named the pairs as reasons. "Awareness ratios"
  ranged from 0 to 244%.
- **Position.** Passersby in shops chose among four nightgowns (378) or
  four identical pairs of stockings (52); the right-most was heavily
  favoured, by almost four to one over the left-most for the stockings.
  No one named position, and almost all denied it when asked.
- **Anchoring** (Borgida, Nisbett and Tversky, unpublished; 60 subjects).
  Subjects reported the same moderate use of the anchor whether it had
  had a large effect or none.
- **Halo.** A teacher shown warm or cold changed ratings of his
  appearance, mannerisms and accent (each p < .001). Subjects denied
  this; subjects in the cold condition said instead that disliking the
  attributes had lowered their liking of him, inverting the cause.
- **Ineffective factors.** Deleting passages from an Updike excerpt (152
  subjects) changed rated impact by less than .10 on a 7-point scale, yet
  86% said the crib passage had increased it. Noise from a power saw
  during a film (90 subjects) had no effect on ratings, yet 55% said it
  lowered at least one; for poor focus most reported correctly that it
  had no effect, which the authors call their only case of reasonably
  good accuracy. A "no permanent damage" reassurance (75 men) did not
  change predicted shock-taking, yet most said it did. In each case
  subjects not exposed to the factor predicted the same erroneous effect.
- **Nisbett and Bellows** (unpublished). Five factors in a job
  applicant's portfolio were varied factorially for women subjects:
  attractiveness, academic credentials, a spilled cup of coffee, a past
  auto accident, and whether they expected to meet her. Mean subject and
  observer reports of each factor's influence correlated .89 (liking),
  .84 (sympathy), .99 (intelligence) and .77 (flexibility). For
  intelligence, reports correlated .94 with true effects and observer
  predictions .98. For the other three, reports correlated −.31, .14 and
  .11 with true effects, and observers did no better or worse.

**When reports will be wrong**: when the report is removed in time from
the process; for "mechanics of judgment" (order, position, contrast,
anchoring); for context; for nonevents; for nonverbal behaviour; and
when a small cause has a large effect. **When right**: when the
influential stimuli are available and plausible and few plausible
noninfluential ones are, as in verbal conditioning, and in judgment
tasks where clinicians and stockbrokers apply rules they were taught.

**Why we think we introspect**: we confuse access to content with access
to process; we report intermediate results (Maier's professor's
"monkeys swinging from trees") as if they were the process; feedback that
would disconfirm our theories of ourselves is rare; and it is frightening
to think we know our minds no better than an informed outsider.

| C2 | Subjects' reports match observers' predictions from a description alone | strong | Nisbett and Bellows (r .77 to .99), the Updike, reassurance and word-pair studies, Latané and Darley |

## Concepts

- **Higher-order cognitive process**: the mediating process between a
  stimulus and an evaluation, judgment, choice, inference or behaviour;
  contrasted with perception and memory, and with content.
- **A priori causal theory**: a belief, held before the event, about what
  kind of stimulus produces what kind of response; from explicit cultural
  rules, implicit theories ("psychological implication", Abelson; "causal
  schemata", Kelley), observed covariation, or connotative similarity.
- **Representativeness of a cause**: judging a stimulus influential
  because it resembles the kind of thing that produces the response, by
  analogy with Tversky and Kahneman's heuristic.
- **Awareness (proposed criterion)**: report more accurate than an
  observer's prediction from a general description.
- **Causal theory controls**: comparing a subject's reported weights not
  with actual weights directly but with what the subject, or a peer,
  would have predicted beforehand.

## Connections

It builds on Bem's self-perception theory (1967, 1972), which had argued
that people infer their own attitudes as observers do; on Kelley's and
Abelson's work on causal schemata; and on Tversky and Kahneman's
representativeness and availability heuristics, which it applies to
causal reports. It reads the dissonance and attribution literature
against itself, through what those papers did not publish. Johansson,
Hall, Sikström and Olsson ([LIT-1417](../literature.d/LIT-1417.md), [NOTE-1398](NOTE-1398.md)) carry the question from
group-level comparisons to single reports shown false of the person who
gave them.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-114](../cases.d/CASE-114.md), the imagined owner.** The case's note of 2026-10-23 quotes
  A5 that this paper shows "reported authorship and actual causal history
  can diverge". It does, in a specific form: reports of why one responded
  diverge from what caused the response, and match what observers would
  guess. The paper is about the influence of stimuli on responses, not
  about attributions of agency or control; it never discusses who did
  something. So it supports the case's premise, that a self-report of why
  one acted can be a construction from a theory, and is silent on the
  case's thesis, that the content of a self-model changes error
  correction. Its causal-theory account suggests one prediction for the
  case: a system whose self-model is centralized should explain its
  errors as an observer holding the same model would, whatever its actual
  division of labour.

  people do and the reasons they give was shown before only in

  evidence, since every comparison here is of condition means. The
  paper's interview data are individual (the insomniacs' explanations,
  Maier's professor), but none of them can be shown false of the person
  who gave it.

## Limitations

- **Selected situations.** The authors chose stimuli they expected
  subjects to misjudge. The paper shows that reports fail often, not how
  often in daily life.
- **Unpublished and small studies.** The studies of its own are reported
  briefly, several with no test statistics. Nisbett and Bellows is an
  unpublished manuscript, and the paper gives its correlations with no
  number of subjects.
- **Necessary versus sufficient causes.** The authors grant that an
  effective stimulus may have been necessary but not the main cause, so
  a subject who named another cause may not have been wrong; and that
  demand or embarrassment may explain some denials (position, noise).
- **The causal-theory account is not tested against a rival.** That
  subjects and observers agree is consistent with both using the same
  theory; it does not exclude subjects having some access that happens to
  agree with the theory.
- **Errors and slips.** The awareness ratio as defined (reporters over an
  estimate of the influenced) can exceed 100%, as its reported 244% does;
  the text does not say so. The text cites Kornhauser and Lazarsfeld as
  1935 and the reference list as 1955 (the 1955 being a reprint in *The
  Language of Social Research*). The reference list gives Storms and
  Nisbett (1970) as JPSP volume 2; the article is in volume 16,
  issue 2, so the list gives the issue for the volume.

## Open questions

- Does any procedure yield reports of process that beat an observer's
  prediction? The paper's criterion makes this the test and runs it
  only on judgments where both fail or both succeed.
- Do reports become more stereotyped with delay, as it predicts?
