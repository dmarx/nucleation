# ARG-tmpxsh8r: Leffler's plight-inescapability argument, carried by analogy from agents to self-maintaining organizations, shows that a constitutive explanation of the force of an organization's norms makes that force relative to the organization's constitution

## The inference

1. Leffler: if the force of norms implicit in an agent's constitution is
   explained by the agent's being continuously positioned to exercise its
   agency fully, absent external limits, then the explanation passes
   invariance, ought-implies-can, ought-implies-can-fail and
   criticizability, and the force is relative to the agent's
   constitution ([THEORY-tmpdrt3r](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpdrt3r.md)).
2. A self-maintaining organization, on the record's account, has a
   constitution that fixes what counts as its success and failure
   ([THEORY-220](../theory.d/THEORY-220.md), [THEORY-628](../theory.d/THEORY-628.md), [CLAIM-648](../claims.d/CLAIM-648.md)), and while it persists it is
   continuously positioned to go on maintaining itself, well or badly,
   unless something outside it stops it.
3. So an explanation of the force of its norms by that position has the
   structure of 1, with self-maintenance in place of agency, and the same
   consequence: the force is relative to the organization's constitution.

## Critical questions

- **In what respect are they alike, and is it the respect the conclusion
  needs?** In the structure Leffler's relativism step uses: a standard
  of success set by the thing's own constitution, a continuous position
  to meet or miss it, room to fail without ceasing to be the thing
  (Di Paolo's adaptivity is the organizational form of OICF), and
  constitutions that vary. The conclusion needs only these. Leffler
  himself counts corporate agents among the agents whose constitutions
  vary ("Some corporate agents are democratic, others are dictatorial",
  p. 19), so for organizations that are agents this is not an analogy but
  a case.
- **What relevant difference is there?** Two. First, Leffler's agents can
  take up their norms in deliberation; most of the record's organizations
  are not agents, and their "position" is a dynamical fact, not a
  practical one. That weakens criticizability: there may be no one to
  criticize, only a failure to register. It does not weaken the
  relativity, which follows from the standard's being set by the
  constitution whoever meets it. Second, for an organization failure can
  end it, and Leffler's agent who fails remains an agent. Di Paolo's
  graded norm ([THEORY-628](../theory.d/THEORY-628.md)) is what keeps failure short of dissolution, so
  the analogy holds only for adaptive organizations, which is where the
  record's account places norms anyway.
- **What transfers, and what does not?** The relativity of force
  transfers, and with it the absence of constitution independence. What
  does not transfer is the claim that the explanation is adequate: for
  non-agent organizations Leffler's criticizability may be too weak to
  count as force at all, in which case the constitutive route gives them
  less than relative force, and the conclusion holds a fortiori.
