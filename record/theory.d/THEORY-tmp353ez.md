# THEORY-tmp353ez: Khan and Dhar find that knowing the same choice will recur lowers self-control in the present choice, which they explain by optimism about the next choice lowering the anticipated guilt of the present one, and that telling choosers the present choice is the best indicator of the next takes most of the effect away

## Source

Khan and Dhar (2007), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*
136(2): 277-288, LIT-tmpgi12m, read in NOTE-tmpq33n0.

## What was actually shown

Across five experiments with real choices (movies, magazines, snacks,
money), the share choosing the vice or the smaller sooner reward was
higher when participants knew the same choice would come again (70-90%,
64.5% for money) than in isolation (46.6-57.5%, 37.1% for money).
Predictions of a virtuous next choice exceeded actual virtuous choices
(67% against 36%). Anticipated guilt for the vice was lower with a similar
future choice and mediated the effect (Experiment 3). Fixing next week's
outcome as the virtue raised the present vice share to 90%; fixing it as
the vice left it at 42.5%, near isolation (Experiment 4). With Kirby and
Guastello's linking instruction the vice share was 53%, against 90%
without it and 46.6% in isolation (General Discussion, p. 286).

## The account

The authors' explanation: a broad choice frame lets people "optimistically
believe that they will choose a virtue in the future choice and hence
provides them with a guilt-reducing justification to not exercise
self-control in the present" (abstract). The broad-frame wisdom (adding-up,
bundling) assumes the present choice is seen as predictive; where it is
not, a series lowers self-control.

## What it does not show

That the chooser's own prediction, rather than an experimenter's
assertion, makes the present choice a precedent; or that perceived
precedent raises self-control above the isolated choice.
