# NOTE-2045: Moynihan, Herd and Harvey — Administrative Burden

## Contribution

A three-part decomposition of administrative burden from the citizen's
side, and a move of burden from a question of administrative efficiency
to one of political choice and distribution.

## Key insight

Who bears the cost of reaching an entitlement is set separately from the
entitlement itself, often by choice and often out of view.

## Assumptions

- The citizen's experience is the unit of analysis.
- Political actors' preferences about a program become preferences about
  its burdens: proponents reduce them, opponents add them (p. 44).
- Take-up is the main measurable outcome of burden.

## Key results

- **Definition** (abstract, p. 43): "Administrative burden is
  conceptualized as a function of learning, psychological, and
  compliance costs that citizens experience in their interactions with
  government."
- **Burden as politics** (abstract, p. 43): "the level of administrative
  burden placed on an individual, as well as the distribution of burden
  between the state and the individual, will often be a function of
  deliberate political choice rather than simply a product of historical
  accident or neglect."
- **Opacity** (p. 44): "Changes to burdens may be presented as technical
  fixes without any specific policy intent".
- **Learning costs** (p. 45): "Learning costs arise from engaging in
  search processes to collect information about public services, and
  assessing how they are relevant to the individual."
- **Compliance costs** (p. 46): "Compliance costs are the burdens of
  following administrative rules and requirements."
- **Behavioral amplification** (p. 46): "This basic insight explains why
  burdens that seem minor and defensible when designed by the
  administrator may exert dramatic effects when experienced by citizens."
- **Not red tape** (p. 47): "By contrast, we assume that administrative
  burdens will often serve legitimate purposes and are not inherently
  bad."
- **Strength of evidence** (pp. 50–51): "Of the three different aspects of
  burden identified in table 1, there is the strongest empirical evidence
  on the effects of compliance costs."
- **Auto-enrolment** (p. 52): the "most dramatic way by which the state
  can reduce application compliance burden is to autoenroll eligible
  individuals into a program based on administrative data".

## Concepts

Administrative burden; learning, psychological and compliance costs;
take-up; red tape as distinct; stigma and autonomy; hidden politics
(Hacker); policymaking by other means (Lineberry); auto-enrolment.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-731](../claims.d/CLAIM-731.md)** (the cost of imposing a decision and the cost of
  challenging it are separate). Qualifies; not filed as a relation. The
  burdens here are costs of obtaining an entitlement (applying,
  documenting, recertifying), not of contesting a decision. What they
  share with the claim is structural: who bears the costs in a
  citizen-state interaction is set, often deliberately and out of view,
  apart from the formal entitlement.
- **[CLAIM-669](../claims.d/CLAIM-669.md)** (exploitation through access granted on encumbering
  terms). No direct bearing: burdens bar access rather than encumber it.
- **[CLAIM-681](../claims.d/CLAIM-681.md)** (time taken by activities one cannot reasonably refuse).
  Adjacent: learning and compliance costs are such time.
- **[LIT-1609](../literature.d/LIT-1609.md)**, the authors' 2018 book *Administrative Burden:
  Policymaking by Other Means*, is held and Deferred.

## Connections

## Limitations

Only pp. 43–52 were read; the Wisconsin case, which is the evidence for
burden as politics, was not. A 2015 article, so the authors' later
revisions are not reflected.
