## 1. Killen & Smetana 2015
- Bibliographic details: Melanie Killen and Judith G. Smetana, "Origins and Development of Morality", Chapter 17 of Handbook of Child Psychology and Developmental Science, Socioemotional Processes, John Wiley & Sons, 2015, pp. 701-749. Checked against the page footer and pagination. The DOI is not printed in the copy and is not verified (it is probably 10.1002/9781118963418.childpsy317; this needs checking).
- Provenance: Read in full: pp. 701-715 (introduction, classical theories, false dichotomies, roots and early emergence), 717-718 (intentionality), 721-725 (moral judgments; fair distribution of resources; start of the section on prejudice), 733-735 (peers), 741-742 (conclusions). Skipped: the rest of the mental-states section, family, the rest of intergroup, civic engagement, neuroscience, references.
- Summary: A handbook review. It sets out the classical theories (Freud, behaviourism, social learning, Piaget's constructivism, Kohlberg, prosocial development, social domain theory, evolutionary theory) and then reviews findings on infancy and early childhood, mental states, moral judgment (harm, fair distribution, prejudice, rights), family, peers and civic life. Its thesis is that morality emerges very early, and that what develops is the skill to apply it to complex situations.
- Contribution: A verdict on Piaget 1932 from the social-domain side: peers matter, but early heteronomy and the late ages are refuted. It also brings the 3-year-old sharing studies into a constructivist frame.
- Key results:
  - p. 705, Piaget's account: "Piaget proposed that children are initially heteronomous (defining morality as compliance with rules set by an authority) and then become autonomous by 8–10 years of age". On the source: "Rather, Piaget viewed peer interactions as equal, mutual relationships that promote conceptions of equality and fairness".
  - p. 705, what held: "Research has clearly established that peer relations and particularly friendships play important roles in children’s moral understanding". What did not: "Piaget’s description of early morality as heteronomous has not survived empirical scrutiny". Children "understand the intrinsic basis for rules", and adult-child relations can be mutual as well as unilateral.
  - p. 718, intentions: "Contrary to Piaget’s findings, research has consistently shown that children as young as 3 years of age can take intentions into account".
  - p. 714 (no quote): moral and conventional violations are distinguished "by about 3 years of age and more consistently by the age of 4", across cultures.
  - p. 712, fairness early: "children younger than the age of 6 years have a preliminary understanding of the importance of distributing resources equally or equitably". On Warneken et al. 2011 (3-year-olds sharing equally after joint work): "However, the researchers did not examine why 3-year-olds shared equitably".
  - p. 713, equality before merit: in Baumard et al. 2012, 4-year-olds showed rudimentary merit, with "most preferring equal allocation when given the opportunity to do so". From 3.5 to 6 years, children back an ingroup member who wants equal division: "Young children are willing to challenge an ingroup norm in order to maintain their preference for equality".
  - p. 723, equality then equity: "the age-related shift from a focus on strict equality" to merit and need (Damon 1977) is "one of the most robust developmental findings". Sigelman & Waitzman: "Younger children preferred equality in all three conditions, but with age, children considered both equity and need". On enforcement: "Recent studies have found that 6- to 8-year-olds will discard additional resources provided in a distribution task" (Blake & McAuliffe 2011; Shaw & Olson 2012).
  - p. 713, Piaget against early cooperation: Brownell found that peers coordinate only at 24-27 months, whereas 14-month-olds cooperate with adults. This is "Consistent with Piaget’s (1932) claims", and "in the experimental studies demonstrating early cooperation in adult–child encounters, adult experimenters asked children for help in solving a problem and thus functioned more like peers".
  - p. 733, peers revised: "Peer relationships may also be hierarchical, however, just as parent–child relationships can reflect mutuality and reciprocity".
  - p. 710, on collaboration studies as morality: "Because these behaviors lack a prescriptive, obligatory basis, questions have been raised about whether these constitute moral behaviors".
  - pp. 741-742, conclusion: "what develops is not the ability to appreciate morality but rather the social" cognitive competence to apply it to complex situations.
- Assumptions: Social domain theory. Morality means prescriptive norms of fairness and welfare, recognised by generalizability and by independence from authority and rules. Judgments and reasoning are needed as evidence, not only behaviour.
- Concepts: heteronomous/autonomous morality; unilateral vs mutual respect; moral, conventional and personal domains; equality, equity and merit; straightforward vs complex contexts; ingroup deviant.
- Treatment of Tomasello: as evidence of early cooperation and helping (Warneken & Tomasello 2006, 2007, 2009; Vaish et al. 2010, 2011; Warneken et al. 2011). It does not discuss shared intentionality theory as an account of fairness. It doubts that cooperation and helping count as moral, and says the "why" behind 3-year-olds' equal sharing has not been examined.
- Bearing on the codes:
  - THEORY-495: qualifies. It confirms equal sharing at 3 after joint work (Warneken et al. 2011) but says the motive was not tested. It places obligation at the moral/conventional distinction in toddlers (p. 711, Smetana 1984), not specifically in joint commitments.
  - NOTE-185: parallel and background on the sharing studies; it cites the same Warneken et al. 2011, not Hamann 2011.
  - LIT-837 (Piaget, Structuralism): background. It supplies the 1932 moral theory that the record lacks.
  - THEORY-tmpqxwyk (Schelling) and THEORY-1630 (Sugden): no direct bearing. Its constructivist "equality is the straightforward rule" (p. 721, p. 723: equality "involves relatively straightforward decision-making") is a parallel to equality as the salient default.
- Limitations: A broad review that cites rather than analyses studies. It does not separate collaborative from windfall contexts. Equality is "straightforward" without any account of why. It is partisan to social domain theory.
- Candidate THEORY: Killen and Smetana (2015) hold that Piaget was right that peer relations and friendships foster concepts of fairness and equality, but wrong that early morality is heteronomous: by about age 3 to 4 children judge moral transgressions wrong independent of authority and rules, take intentions into account, and prefer equal distribution, shifting toward merit and need with age.
  - promote_when: two or more independent reviews or meta-analyses report (a) moral/conventional distinctions independent of authority by age 3-4, (b) a dominant equality preference in preschoolers giving way to merit and need in middle childhood, and (c) a role for peer relations and friendship in fairness concepts.

## 2. McAuliffe, Blake, Steinbeis & Warneken 2017
- Bibliographic details: Katherine McAuliffe, Peter R. Blake, Nikolaus Steinbeis and Felix Warneken, "The developmental foundations of human fairness", Nature Human Behaviour 1, 0042 (2017; online 2017/02/08 per the repository metadata), doi:10.1038/s41562-016-0042. Pages below are manuscript pages of the Leiden copy, not journal pages.
- Provenance: Read in full (text, Boxes 1-3, figure legends; references skimmed). The figures themselves are not in the manuscript.
- Summary: A review built on four "signatures" of adult fairness, each a cost paid against inequality: disadvantageous inequity aversion (DI), second-party punishment, third-party punishment and advantageous inequity aversion (AI). It traces the order in which each appears in children and proposes neurocognitive mechanisms for each: envy and spite, behavioural control (DLPFC-VMPFC), and the value of fair outcomes (insula connectivity).
- Contribution: A dated timeline for when children enforce equality at a cost, and a dissociation between knowing the norm (by 3) and acting on it (6-8).
- Key results:
  - MS p. 4: "Equality acts as an objective benchmark in investigations of fairness". The review's definition is "admittedly a lean one" (MS p. 14): costly responses to unequal distributions.
  - MS p. 6, at 3 years: "by three years of age children will state explicitly that equality is a norm that should be followed in distribution tasks". DI at 4: "Children as young as four rejected allocations that placed them at a disadvantage relative to a peer". DI was found across seven societies, so it "may be a universal response".
  - MS p. 7, second-party punishment: 4-5-year-olds accept even highly unfair Ultimatum offers, and "the first clear signs of children being willing to pay a cost to punish unfair treatment around 6-8 years of age".
  - MS p. 8, third-party punishment at 6: "six-year-olds will sacrifice their own resources to take candies away from the selfish individual".
  - MS p. 8, AI at 8: "by 8 years of age children begin to reject advantageous allocations in the Inequity Game". On culture (MS p. 9): "advantageous inequity aversion is tied to local norms of fairness and expectations of child behavior that vary by culture" (found in the US, Canada and Uganda; not in India, Mexico, Peru or Senegal).
  - MS p. 12, knowing vs doing: "in Dictator Games children as young as 3 years recognize an equal split as a norm but keep more for themselves, suggesting that young children do not view fairness norms as obligatory". Compliance comes at about 6-8 and is tied to behavioural control.
  - MS p. 18, Box 2: "5-6 year olds were willing to accept a lower payoff overall to achieve a relative advantage over their peer".
  - MS p. 9, order: "concerns for fairness to self emerging first, a willingness to punish unfairness in others emerging next and, finally, a willingness to reject advantageous unfairness emerging last".
- Assumptions: Behavioural-economic tasks with real costs; equality as the benchmark; a uniquely human fairness defined by enforcement against self-interest; adult neural data used to suggest developmental mechanisms.
- Concepts: disadvantageous and advantageous inequity aversion; second- and third-party punishment; the Inequity Game; Ultimatum and Dictator Games; behavioural control; norm compliance; the judgment-behaviour gap.
- Piaget: not mentioned at all. Its ages nonetheless line up with Piaget's late ages for costly egalitarian action (AI at about 8) while placing knowledge of the norm at 3.
- Treatment of Tomasello and collaboration: none. The collaborative sharing studies (Hamann 2011, Warneken 2011) are not cited. Tomasello appears only in references on chimpanzees and on Wittig 2013 and Grocke 2015 (the latter as procedural justice). Its tasks are all windfall or experimenter allocations, not jointly earned spoils.
- Bearing on the codes:
  - THEORY-495: contrast or qualification. In windfall settings 3-year-olds know the equal norm but do not treat it as obligatory, and give in their own favour. This sharpens the contrast THEORY-495 draws: the 3-year-olds' equal splits after collaboration stand out against the lean picture.
  - NOTE-185: parallel. It supplies the non-collaborative baseline for the sharing studies.
  - THEORY-tmpqxwyk (Schelling): parallel. Equality as "objective benchmark" fits salience, though the paper gives no salience argument.
  - THEORY-1630 (Sugden): no bearing.
- Limitations: Only distributive fairness, and only under cost. Neural mechanisms are inferred from adults (the authors say so). There are no collaborative contexts. The ages are task-bound (e.g. Ultimatum rejection at 6-8).
- Candidate THEORY: McAuliffe, Blake, Steinbeis and Warneken (2017) argue that children show the four signatures of human fairness in a fixed order: aversion to getting less than a peer by about 4, costly second- and third-party punishment of unfairness by about 6-8, and rejection of advantageous inequality by about 8, which is culture-dependent. Three-year-olds already state that equal division is the norm but do not follow it when it costs them.
  - promote_when: independent studies replicate the ordering, DI by 4 and second- and third-party punishment and AI at 6-8 or later, together with the gap between stating and following the equality norm at age 3.

## 3. Killen 2018
- Bibliographic details: Melanie Killen, "The origins of morality: Social equality, fairness, and justice", Philosophical Psychology 31(5): 767-803 (2018), doi:10.1080/09515089.2018.1486612. Received 31 Oct 2016; accepted 22 Feb 2017; published online 19 Aug 2018.
- Provenance: Read in full, pp. 767-798 (all text; references skimmed).
- Summary: A commentary on Tomasello's A Natural History of Human Morality (2016; cited as 2015). Killen accepts that cooperation and interdependence are a basis for morality. She argues against three things: (1) that cooperation is morality, since cooperation can serve unjust ends; (2) that interdependence is enough, since independent, autonomous thinking must coexist with it from infancy; and (3) that objective morality is conformity to group norms, since it is the capacity to challenge groups for fairness. She sets out development after age 5 and the social reasoning developmental (SRD) model.
- Contribution: The most direct Piagetian/social-domain answer to Tomasello's collaboration-based account. It reinstates Piaget's peer conflict and autonomy against Tomasello's "we".
- Key results:
  - p. 767, thesis: "interdependence coexists with independent autonomous thinking, which is necessary for challenging group norms, authority, and institutional mandates".
  - p. 776, Tomasello's evidence reported: "By ages 3–4 years, children will share the fruits of collaborative labor with a same-age playmate rather than take all the rewards for themselves" (Warneken et al. 2011; not in apes, Hamann et al. 2011).
  - p. 777: "cooperation and morality are not one and the same". The forms of cooperation are morally repugnant, socially condoned, or morally determined (pp. 776-778).
  - p. 773, on the me-you-we sequence: "Underlying this trajectory is the notion that individuals are originally selfish and then become oriented to dyads and then to the group".
  - p. 771, on transmission: "Tomasello appears to agree with the cognitive-developmental viewpoint in many places throughout his book, even referring to peer interaction as being central for morality", but he also invokes adult teaching ("the authoritative voice").
  - p. 783, Piaget held and revised: "Piaget’s (1932) moral judgment theory and research demonstrated how children constructed theories of justice and fairness out of peer interactions between 4 and 10 years of age". But "current research has revealed how the construction of moral concepts emerges prior to Piaget’s (1932) claims", "reflecting a range of judgments by age 4 years (Piaget’s proposed onset of moral judgment)". Also p. 783: "Tomasello’s (2015) claim is that cooperation and collaboration, the recognition of “we,” precedes moral judgment".
  - p. 786, Piaget's mechanism kept: "Young children’s social conflicts reflect a form of disequilibrium, distinct from cooperation, which motivates change due to the desire to resolve conflicts (Piaget, 1932; Turiel, 1983)". Pp. 787-788 give a preschool excerpt in which children negotiate equal shares ("then we’ll all have one").
  - p. 792: "even preschool children are willing to go against an in-group norm" to keep equal distribution between groups (Cooley & Killen 2015).
  - p. 793: "Objective morality is not evidenced by conformity to norms and institutions, as Tomasello (2015) proposes, but by challenging groups when their behavior violates the fair and equal treatment of others".
  - p. 794, equality before equity: "Younger children allocated resources equally between the two recipients, but supported equity in their judgments and reasoning". And "It is not until 7–8 years of age that children consistently prioritize equity over equality in behavior, judgment, and reasoning (Rizzo & Killen, 2016)".
  - p. 796: "Along with cooperation, conflict is essential for the change to occur".
- Assumptions: A deontological notion of morality (Kant, Rawls, Gewirth, Sen). Constructivism through reflective abstraction. Social domain theory and the SRD model. Morality is shown by judgment and reasoning, not by behaviour alone.
- Concepts: interdependence vs independent (autonomous) thinking; second-personal and objective morality (Tomasello, via Darwall); me-you-we; disequilibrium; SRD model; ingroup deviant; rectifying inequality; equity vs equality.
- Bearing on the codes:
  - THEORY-495: contrast. Killen denies that the "we" is the source of fairness or obligation, and holds that independent judgment coexists with it and is needed to tell good cooperation from bad. She accepts the 3-4-year-old sharing data but not the reading of them. She does not address joint commitment as such.
  - NOTE-185: contrast or parallel. This is the "Killen & Dahl" line NOTE-185 records against in-group scoping, here at full length (an in-group can cooperate unjustly).
  - Owner's affordance view: parallel. Her preschool excerpt ("we’ll all have one") shows equal division reached by negotiation in peer conflict, not by reading a mutual expectation. That fits a plain affordance or a construction through conflict better than recursive focal-point reasoning, but she makes no Schelling argument.
  - THEORY-1630 (Sugden, team reasoning): contrast in spirit, since she resists group-level thinking as the basis of morality. No direct discussion.
  - LIT-504 (Natural History of Human Morality, Unavailable): this is a commentary on it, and can stand in for part of its content (the three moralities and me-you-we, pp. 768, 775), quoted with page numbers from Tomasello.
  - LIT-837: background.
- Limitations: A commentary, not a systematic review. The evidence comes mostly from the author's own lab. It does not engage joint commitment or obligation as Tomasello defines them. Its charge that Tomasello equates objective morality with conformity is contestable. It gives no account of why equality is the default.
- Candidate THEORY: Killen (2018) argues against Tomasello that cooperation and interdependence are a basis for morality but not sufficient for it: from early childhood, children's independent judgments of fairness coexist with group-minded cooperation and let them oppose cooperative or group norms that treat others unequally. The Piagetian mechanism of peer conflict and its resolution, not collaboration alone, drives moral change.
  - promote_when: studies show preschool children (by about 3.5-5) favour an ingroup member who advocates equal division against the group's norm, and show peer conflict or negotiation predicting later fairness judgments independently of collaborative experience.

## Synthesis for the task
- Held from Piaget: peers, and especially friendship, matter to fairness (KS p. 705, 733-734; K18 p. 783). Conflict and negotiation among peers as an engine of change (K18 pp. 786-788). Strict equality before equity, merit and need, which is "one of the most robust developmental findings" (KS p. 723; K18 p. 794).
- Revised: early heteronomy is rejected. By about 3-4, moral rules are judged independent of authority and rules, and intentions are weighed (KS pp. 705, 714, 718). Adult-child relations can be mutual and peer relations hierarchical (KS p. 733). The onset of moral judgment is pushed below Piaget's 4 to infancy and toddlerhood (K18 p. 783).
- Ages for equality: preferred and stated as a norm by 3 (MC MS p. 6; KS p. 712-713). 3-year-olds share equally after joint work (Warneken et al. 2011, in KS p. 712 and K18 p. 776). But in windfall Dictator tasks 3-year-olds keep more (MC MS p. 12). Enforcement at a cost: DI at 4, second- and third-party punishment at 6-8, AI at about 8 and culture-dependent (MC). Equity consistently over equality only at 7-8 (K18 p. 794). So Piaget's later ages (8-10 autonomy; equality against authority) survive roughly for costly enforcement and for equity, not for knowing and preferring equality.
- Collaboration-based accounts: KS treats the Tomasello-lab studies as evidence of early cooperation, says why 3-year-olds share equally was not examined, and doubts that cooperation is moral. MC ignores collaborative contexts. K18 engages Tomasello directly and rejects interdependence or the "we" as sufficient, putting autonomy and peer conflict in its place.
