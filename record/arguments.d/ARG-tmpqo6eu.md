# ARG-tmpqo6eu: One-Dimensional Man corroborates the mechanism of practical compulsion, a project that once operative tends to become exclusive and makes compliance rational, but argues against measuring freedom by the options left open, since what can be chosen and what is chosen are shaped as well, an argument of the kind the compulsion claim's second defeater describes

## The inference

1. **The mechanism.** "once the project has become operative in the basic
   institutions and relations, it tends to become exclusive", and the
   system "swallows up or repulses all alternatives" (Introduction). "The
   initial choice defines the range of possibilities open on this way,
   and precludes alternative possibilities incompatible with it" (ch. 8).
   The growing output of goods makes "compliance a rational technological
   attitude" (ch. 2); the apparatus "blocks all escape" (ch. 3); the ways
   out become "unimaginable" (ch. 1). These are [CLAIM-tmpbypt7](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpbypt7.md)'s three
   mechanisms in Marcuse's terms: alternatives precluded as the
   arrangement settles, compliance made rational, and cultural
   normalization. So far the book corroborates.
2. **The measure.** "The range of choice open to the individual is not
   the decisive factor in determining the degree of human freedom, but
   what can be chosen and what is chosen by the individual" (ch. 1). On
   Marcuse's account the options a person has, and the wants by which she
   chooses among them, are both shaped by the arrangement. Two
   arrangements could then leave a non-adopter the same options and
   differ in freedom, because they form needs differently. That is an
   argument of the form the claim's second defeater describes, and the
   difference lies in the arrangements, not in what adopters gain.

## Critical questions

- **Does the work say this, read in context?** Yes on the mechanism: the
  passages are about technological society as a whole and are central to
  the Introduction and chapter 8. Yes on the measure: the sentence is
  Marcuse's own thesis in chapter 1, not a passing remark.
- **Is it said within the scope the claim needs?** The mechanism, only in
  part. Marcuse's mechanism is needs and satisfaction, not
  infrastructure; his automobile appears as a need ("Between the
  automobile as nuisance and as convenience?", ch. 1), not as a road
  system. Illich's excerpt carries the road mechanism ([THEORY-tmpww16o](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpww16o.md)).
  The measure: Marcuse argues against range of choice as such, for
  adopters and non-adopters alike, not specifically against comparing
  arrangements by a non-adopter's remaining options.
- **Does the claim lean on the reading for more than it holds?** The claim
  does not rest on Marcuse; the relation is `corroborates`, for the
  mechanism only.
- **Does it meet the second defeater?** Not as stated. The defeater asks
  for two arrangements that leave non-adopters the same options and
  differ in the freedom they leave in a way the measure cannot register.
  Marcuse supplies the kind of argument, but no such pair, and his
  criterion for telling the needs apart (true and false needs, judged
  "only in the last analysis" by the individuals) is itself contested.
  The argument presses the measure: what remains open to a non-adopter
  may be necessary to the freedom an arrangement leaves without being
  sufficient.

## Verdict

Proposed. The book corroborates [CLAIM-tmpbypt7](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpbypt7.md)'s mechanism and argues
against its measure. The claim keeps its status; a note on it records the
pressure. Whether the measure should be narrowed, to a necessary condition
of freedom, is the owner's call.
