# NOTE-036: Lindahl et al. 2017, varieties of contemplative experience

## Contribution

The paper sets out the method and first results of the Varieties of Contemplative Experience (VCE) study, a qualitative interview study aimed at under-reported meditation-related experiences, especially challenging, distressing or impairing ones. It delivers two codebooks: 59 phenomenology categories in 7 domains (Table 4) and 26 influencing-factor categories in 4 domains (Table 6). It also documents that phenomenologically similar experiences were appraised anywhere from very positive to very negative. Before this paper, the adverse-effects literature consisted mostly of case reports and small, theory-driven or single-community studies (Introduction, pp. 3–5).

## Key insight

Whether a meditation-related experience counts as "progress" or "adverse" is largely a matter of appraisal. Practitioners, teachers, texts and clinicians often disagree about it. So the study codes *what happened* (phenomenology) separately from *what it was taken to mean, what caused it, and what helped* (influencing factors) (Methods, "Influencing factors coding", p. 12). The taxonomy is valence-neutral on purpose.

## Assumptions

These are premises of the design, not formal assumptions:
- **Purposive "deviant case" sampling** (p. 7). Inclusion required a challenging, difficult or impairing meditation-related experience. The target was 10 men and 10 women in each of three traditions (Theravāda, Zen, Tibetan). Recruitment went through teachers, then snowball sampling.
- **Exclusions** (p. 7): prior unusual experiences closely resembling the reported ones; a mixed or non-Buddhist practice history; an alternative cause that could "wholly" account for the symptoms.
- **Attribution-theory framing** (Taves) (p. 6). "Meditation-related experiences" are the effects participants *attributed* to meditation.
- **Retrospective self-report.** Interviews ran from Dec 2010 to Mar 2016, typically 45–120 minutes. A follow-up questionnaire was sent after all interviews were done; 53/60 (88%) completed it, and for the other 7 researchers extracted the answers from transcripts (p. 13).
- **Saturation.** It was judged after 30 and after 60 interviews (pp. 7–8).

## Key results

- **Sample (Table 1, pp. 13–14).** 73 interviewed, 13 excluded: 5 for mixed practice, 4 for reporting no challenge, 4 for alternative causes. That leaves n = 60: 43% female, mean age 48.9 (SD 13.1), 85% US, 94% White (n = 59). 60% were meditation teachers, and 43% had more than 10,000 lifetime practice hours. There were 32 experts (40% Theravāda, 25% Zen, 19% Tibetan, 16% clinical), 11 of whom also gave practitioner interviews.
- **Onset context (Table 3, p. 15).**
  - Practice before onset: mean 7.1 years (SD 8.0), range 1 day to 25 years. 12% reported challenges within their first 10 days and 18% within their first 50 hours.
  - 72% had onset on or right after retreat and 28% in daily practice.
  - At onset, 25% practised less than 1 h/day, 34% 1–9 h/day and 41% 10 or more h/day.
- **Phenomenology (Table 4, pp. 15–16).**
  - Participants reported a mean of 6.1 domains (SD 1.0) and 19.6 categories (SD 7.2, range 7–40). Each category was reported by an average of 20 practitioners, ranging from 2 (scrupulosity) to 49 (fear).
  - Top categories: fear/anxiety/panic/paranoia 82%; changes in motivation or goal 78%; positive affect 75%; somatic energy 63%; sleep changes 62%; depression/dysphoria/grief 57%; changes in self–other boundaries 53%.
  - Experts reported 56 of the 59 categories.
- **Causality (Table 5, p. 21).** Mean score 4.2 ± 1.1 of 6. Subjective attribution 93%, challenge 85%, consistency 88%, de-challenge 60%, re-challenge 61%, exacerbation 58%.
- **Impact (p. 21).** 88% said effects extended into daily life. Median symptom duration was 1–3 years (days to more than 10 years). 10% reported minimal impairment. 73% reported moderate-to-severe impairment in at least one domain, 17% suicidality and 17% inpatient hospitalisation.
- **Influencing factors (Table 6, p. 22).** The most-cited categories, with practitioner % and expert % in that order:
  - relationship to teacher 97 / 97
  - worldview or explanatory frameworks 97 / 85
  - amount, intensity or consistency of practice 93 / 94
  - practice approach 93 / 94

  The authors say explicitly that these are participants' views, "not ... a theory or hypothesis about risk factors ... put forth by the authors" (p. 22).

## Concepts

- **Meditation-related experience.** An effect a practitioner attributed to meditation (attribution-theory sense, p. 6).
- **Phenomenology vs influencing factors.** Two separate codebooks: what was experienced, versus interpretation, causal attribution and remedy (pp. 12–13).
- **Deviant-case sampling.** Sampling from the extreme of a distribution in order to study rare phenomena (p. 7).
- **Causality score.** The number of six endorsed criteria. ≥2 means "possibly related" (p. 10).
- **Severity grades** (p. 11). Mild: less than 25% reduction in functioning. Moderate: 25–50% reduction, or outpatient treatment. Severe: 50% or more reduction, persistent, or inpatient treatment.
- **Influencing factor.** Anything reported as a risk factor or a remedy. The two are coded together because the same factor, such as the teacher, is reported as either (p. 13).

## Connections

The paper builds on Kornfield (1979), Shapiro (1992: 63% of retreatants reported at least one adverse effect and 7.4% stopped meditating), VanderKooi (1997) and Lomas et al. (2014). It criticises the Chen et al. (2011) and Full et al. (2013) studies for being theory-driven and all-Asian (pp. 4–5). The frame comes from religious studies (Taves; Sharf) and cultural psychiatry (Kirmayer). The companion papers it points to are Lindahl et al. (2014) on light experiences and Britton et al. (2014) on sleep. It announces a VCE-based questionnaire piloted in an MBCT trial (NCT01831362).

In this record, the "sense of self" domain (loss of agency 25%, loss of basic self 25%, self–other boundaries 53%) connects to the consciousness cluster. [LIT-080](../literature.d/LIT-080.md)'s "entropic brain" and REBUS frame for psychedelic states has an obvious phenomenological parallel, but neither paper cites the other, and the link is my inference.

## Bearing on the record

- It is a citable, carefully hedged source for the claim that meditation has non-trivial adverse effects in some practitioners. It is **not** a source for rates.
- Its "sense of self" categories are fine-grained operational definitions: agency, ownership, basic self, embodiment, narrative self. They are usable by any consciousness note that needs them.
- Nothing here bears on ML practice. No ANTH- document is implicated.

## Limitations

- Frequencies are within a purposively selected, highly educated, 94%-White, mostly US sample. The authors call the 100% rate of challenge a sampling artefact (p. 27).
- Retrospective interviews, some many years after onset. The authors note that more distant interviewees under-report phenomenology (p. 28).
- Category frequencies depend on how broadly each code is drawn and on what was salient to the interviewee, because most categories were not prompted (p. 27).
- The causality evidence is self-report and, in part, built into inclusion (see corrections).
- The expert sample overlaps the practitioner sample (11 of 32). The authors report that removing them does not change the similarities, but give no numbers.
- Reliability was assessed on only 15% of transcripts, with κ of .70 and .56.
- Minor internal inconsistencies: the education figure (73% vs 67%) and the causality cutoff (">2" vs "≥2").

## Open questions

- What are the actual prevalence and incidence in unselected meditators and in MBI participants? The authors propose a structured questionnaire based on the codebook (piloted in NCT01831362).
- Do any influencing factors (practice intensity, trauma history, teacher relationship) predict duration or severity under controlled comparison? The paper supplies only a list of candidates.
- Would the planned comparisons (Abrahamic traditions, European and Indian samples) change the taxonomy itself, or only the frequencies?

## Corrections to the seeded skim

- **The dossier omits the two most severe outcomes.** 17% reported suicidality and 17% required inpatient hospitalisation (Results, "Duration, severity and associated impairment", p. 21).
- **The causality assessment is weaker than "11 of 13 regulatory criteria" sounds, and the dossier does not look at it.**
  - Only six criteria were scored per person, all by self-report or transcript: subjective attribution, challenge, exacerbation, consistency, de-challenge, re-challenge. The score is the sum of endorsements, with ≥2 ("possibly related") as the cutoff.
  - A score of ≥2 was an inclusion condition, so "100% of respondents met causality assessment criteria" (p. 13) is guaranteed by the design.
  - "Inter-subjective consistency" is satisfied by each category having been reported by several people (mean 20). "Expert judgment" is satisfied by the expert interviews. "Prior published reports" is a literature list.
  - The paper also states the cutoff inconsistently: "score>2" on p. 13 against "score of two or greater" on p. 10 and a reported range of 2–6 on p. 20.
  - The Limitations section concedes the needed design is a prospective RCT with multi-year follow-up. Even so, the paper says the results "provided causality-related evidence for all 11 criteria" (p. 28). That is literally true, but the evidence is thin.
- **An internal inconsistency on education.** The Limitations section says "73% earning graduate or doctoral degrees" (p. 27). Table 1 gives 42% Master's plus 25% doctoral, which is 67%.
- **The exclusion criterion is narrower than the dossier states.** The dossier says "people whose symptoms predated meditation" were excluded. The actual criterion is a prior history of unusual experiences that "closely resembled" the meditation-associated ones. So 32% of the retained sample had a psychiatric history and 43% a trauma history (Table 3).
- **The inter-rater figures need their kappas.** Phenomenology coding showed 99.5% average agreement but weighted κ = .70. Influencing-factors coding showed 97.5% agreement but κ = .56 (moderate). Percentage agreement is inflated by rare codes.
