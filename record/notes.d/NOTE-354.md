# NOTE-354: Chalmers — The Meta-Problem of Consciousness

## Contribution

The paper isolates a problem every side of the consciousness debate has to
solve: explaining our problem reports and intuitions without mentioning
consciousness. It argues that this problem is empirically tractable. It
organises a scattered literature of thirteen proposals under one problem,
with one recurring test, "the problem of belief". It then uses the problem
in two ways. Against realist theories, it poses a challenge: the basis of
consciousness must explain the reports. Against illusionism, it argues that
only the strong form dissolves the hard problem, and that the strong form
is false.

## Key insight

Problem reports are behaviour, so the meta-problem is an easy problem in
the 1995 sense ([LIT-406](../literature.d/LIT-406.md)). But it is the easy problem nearest to the
hard one. If consciousness is real, whatever is its basis should also be
what makes us say it is puzzling. If the reports can be explained
completely without it, then either consciousness is an illusion, or its
connection to our talk about it looks like luck. Every theory has to say
which.

## Assumptions

- **Physical explicability of behaviour (intro).** "At least if we accept
  that all human behaviour can be explained in physical and functional
  terms", problem reports can be. §1.2 widens this to topic-neutral
  explanations (representational, rational, historical, structural), so
  that interactionists and panpsychists are covered.
- **Wide sharing (§1.1).** Problem intuitions are taken to be widely
  shared, or at least to have a widely shared basis. This is called an
  "empirically defeasible assumption". The evidence cited is thin: work on
  intuitive dualism (Bloom; Chudek et al.), Gottlieb and Lombrozo on what
  is hard to explain, and the PhilPapers Survey (fn 8: 56% physicalism,
  27% non-physicalism; 36% say zombies are conceivable but not
  metaphysically possible).
- **Explananda are dispositions (§1.1).** The targets are dispositions to
  report and judge. They are reconstrued topic-neutrally as
  "quasi-phenomenal judgments".
- **A design stance (§2).** Mechanisms are assessed by asking what a
  well-designed self-monitoring robot would need.

## Key results

All are conceptual arguments and surveys. No data are produced and no model
is built; one built model by others is reported (fn 26 and p. 34).

- **Taxonomy of problem intuitions (§1.1).** Explanatory intuitions (gap,
  anti-functionalist) and metaphysical ones (dualist, fundamentality) are
  most central. Knowledge intuitions (first-person knowledge, third-person
  ignorance) and modal ones (zombies, inversion) complete the central four.
  There are also value, distribution ("robots are not conscious"), self,
  quality and presentation intuitions. Footnote 7 argues that Sytsma and
  Machery's robot-sees-red versus robot-feels-pain finding was predicted by
  *The Conscious Mind*'s dual readings of mental terms.
- **Candidate solutions (§2).** Each is tested against the belief case.
  Beliefs are introspected, opaque and immediately known too, and they
  raise no hard problem.
  1. *Introspective models*: necessary, not sufficient.
  2. *Phenomenal concepts*: indexical and recognitional accounts are "too
     thin".
  3. *Independent roles* (Nagel; Hill and McLaughlin): belief concepts
     are independent too.
  4. *Introspective opacity* (Armstrong's headless woman): the move from
     "not seen as physical" to "seen as non-physical" is unexplained.
  5. *Immediate knowledge*, sharpened to acquaintance, meaning presentation
     and revelation.
  6. *Primitive quality attribution* (Pereboom; Hall's dummy properties):
     works for qualia-style views. It must explain why illusionism about
     experience meets more resistance than illusionism about colour
     (Kammerer's problem, renamed "the resistance problem").
  7. *Primitive relation attribution*, the attention-schema idea
     generalised: "consciousness is a model of representation".
  8. *Introjection* (Place's phenomenological fallacy; Jackson; Rosenthal):
     the hard problem survives rejecting sense-data.
  9. *The user illusion* (Dennett): a slogan collecting 4, 6 and 8.
  10. *Use–mention fallacy* (Loar, Tye, Papineau): uncharitable, and it
      over-generates.
  11. *Underestimating the physical* (Stoljar, Strawson): does not touch
      the anti-functionalist intuitions.
  12. *Historical and cultural*: evolutionary design explanations are
      favoured.
  13. *Others*: Humphrey, Fiala et al., Molyneux, Drescher. These are
      dismissed "for fairly predictable reasons" that are not given.
- **Summary picture (p. 34).** Opaque introspective concepts, independent
  of physical ones, attribute primitive mental relations to primitive
  perceptual qualities, with a sense of immediate knowledge and
  acquaintance. It is "neutral between realism and illusionism". The only
  computational test known is Muehlhauser and Shlegeris's theorem-prover
  agent.
- **The meta-problem challenge (§3).** "If a theory says that mechanism M
  is the basis of consciousness, then it needs to explain how mechanism M
  plays a central role in bringing about judgments about consciousness."
  It descends from the 1990 "coherence test" (fn 28).
  - *IIT*: "for every system with high integrated information there will
    be a computationally isomorphic simulated system with zero integrated
    information" that reports alike. So "high integrated information plays
    no essential role in explaining phenomenal intuitions". Not a
    "knockdown".
  - *Biological theories* (Block 2009): non-conscious silicon duplicates
    share the intuitions.
  - *Global workspace and first-order representationalism*: they can
    begin to answer, since only globally available information is
    reported, but problem reports are unexplained.
  - *Higher-order thought*: Goldman's regress.
  - *Quantum collapse*: unselected branches also report.
  - *Panpsychism*: the combination problem gains a reporting aspect.
  - *Analytic functionalism*: fails outright, because by Nagel's point it
    makes the hard problem as unpuzzling as the easy ones.
  - *The author's lean:* acquaintance, developed as a first-order property
    F that disposes states to be objects of primitive higher-order
    representation. Footnote 31 notes that his 1996 candidate, any
    informational state, faces the belief objection.
- **Six reactions (§4).** Non-reductive: no solution ("meta-problem
  nihilism"), correlationism, realizationism. Reductive, each with some
  illusionism: strong illusionism, consciousness as a lower-order
  meta-problem state, consciousness as a higher-order one. Realizationism
  and strong illusionism are judged the important two.
- **Debunking (§5).** Argument A: there is a consciousness-independent
  explanation of our beliefs, so they are unjustified. Three replies:
  - meta-problem solutions explain *intuitions*, but phenomenal *beliefs*
    are partly constituted by consciousness (the 2003 paper);
  - justification is by acquaintance, not by causal history;
  - "independent" equivocates: descriptive or modal independence makes
    premise 1 true, but premise 2 needs causal and constitutive
    independence. Compare explanations of table beliefs that do not
    mention tables.

  Argument B, the coincidence argument, survives better. With strong modal
  independence "it can seem lucky" that the meta-problem processes and
  consciousness coincide. Mørch's phenomenal-powers view is offered as a
  way out.
- **Illusionism (§§6–7).** Weak illusionism (consciousness exists but is
  not intrinsic, non-physical, primitive, and so on) leaves the hard
  problem intact, because it needs only "something it is like". A
  functionalised "phenomenal consciousness" is in effect strong illusionism
  about the ordinary concept. The lower-order version is type-B
  materialism. The distinctive illusionist view is type-A. Strong
  illusionism is coherent, since an illusion can be a non-experiential
  judgment, as in the "grand illusion" of visual detail. But it is false:
  "People sometimes feel pain." A sample dialogue ends with the concession
  that "dialectically, the illusionist side is much more interesting".
- **Conclusion (§8).** Neither side has "a truly satisfactory response to
  the charge of absurdity". The illusionist needs to explain how a mind
  without consciousness could be *like this*. The realist needs to show
  why the meta-problem processes are "by their nature grounded in
  consciousness".

## Concepts

- **Meta-problem** — explain why we think there is a problem of
  consciousness; strictly, explain problem intuitions in topic-neutral
  terms, or explain why that is impossible (§1).
- **Problem intuitions / problem reports** — dispositions to judge and say
  that consciousness is puzzling (§1.1).
- **Topic-neutral explanation** — an explanation that does not mention
  consciousness or its cognates (§1.2).
- **Quasi-phenomenal judgments** — problem judgments reconstrued up to,
  but not including, their being about consciousness (§1.2).
- **The problem of belief** — the recurring test that a mechanism must not
  equally generate problem intuitions about beliefs (§2).
- **Resistance problem** — Kammerer's "illusion meta-problem", renamed:
  why illusionism meets so much intuitive resistance (§2).
- **Meta-problem challenge** — the basis of consciousness must explain our
  judgments about it (§3).
- **Meta-problem processes; lower-order and higher-order meta-problem
  states** — what the topic-neutral explanation invokes, split into the
  first-order states modelled and the introspective states that do the
  modelling (§4).
- **Realizationism / correlationism** — consciousness realises, or merely
  accompanies, the meta-problem processes (§4).
- **Strong / weak illusionism** — consciousness does not exist / exists
  but lacks properties it seems to have (§6).

**Composition and nesting.** The paper does not discuss groups or wholes
with minded parts. It touches composition only through panpsychism. The
meta-problem challenge adds a reporting aspect to the combination problem:
explain "how these combination states play a central role in bringing
about reports of consciousness" (§3).

## Connections

- **The 1995 paper ([LIT-406](../literature.d/LIT-406.md))** supplies the easy/hard distinction the
  whole paper depends on. *The Conscious Mind* ([LIT-441](../literature.d/LIT-441.md)) is where the
  author's first solution appeared (fn 5: pp. 184–8, 289–92) and where he
  wrote that if he were a materialist he would be an illusionist (p. 189).
- **Frankish ([LIT-103](../literature.d/LIT-103.md))** named the view "illusionism" (Frankish 2016),
  and the "illusion problem" is his term, though Chalmers says he
  suggested the name (fn 6). §6's argument agrees with
  Frankish that dissolving illusionists must be strong illusionists, for
  different reasons.
- **IIT ([LIT-445](../literature.d/LIT-445.md), [LIT-185](../literature.d/LIT-185.md)).** The §3 challenge rests on IIT's own
  claim that a zero-Φ computational isomorph is possible ([NOTE-365](NOTE-365.md),
  C7).
- **Organizational invariance ([LIT-392](../literature.d/LIT-392.md)).** Not cited. But the §3
  argument against IIT and biological theories has the same shape as the
  1995 dancing-qualia argument: a functional duplicate makes the same
  judgments. Here the conclusion drawn is only that M plays "no essential
  role" in the judgments, not that the duplicate is conscious.
- **Hofstadter ([LIT-423](../literature.d/LIT-423.md), unread)**, cited in fn 13 for self-model
  illusionism about the self.
- **Kammerer** is cited for the resistance problem and as a possible
  illusionist (Kammerer 2016, 2018a). The record's Kammerer is the 2015
  reply to Schwitzgebel ([LIT-409](../literature.d/LIT-409.md)), a different paper.
- **Seth ([LIT-135](../literature.d/LIT-135.md)).** His real-problem approach is among the strategies
  that try to solve easy problems and expect the hard one to dissolve. The
  paper does not cite him.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md).** C10 (fn 26) is a direct, if small, support for the
  account's mimicry point. Even puzzled phenomenal reports, the behaviour
  most often proposed as hard to fake, can be produced by a trivially
  simple agent. C5 adds a second consideration: indicator properties taken
  from theories whose basis does not explain the reports (IIT, biological
  theories) carry that weakness into any indicator list ([LIT-056](../literature.d/LIT-056.md)). The
  second point is my application.
- **[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md).** (1) C5 gives a reason to doubt IIT as the basis of
  consciousness that is independent of IIT's verdict on groups. That is
  relevant to the account's `promote_when`, which asks for a criterion
  motivated independently of the verdict it gives. It weakens IIT as the
  architectural criterion, rather than supplying one. (2) The challenge
  suggests a test nobody in the bridge has applied: does a candidate group
  mind produce problem reports of its own, over and above its members'
  reports? My inference; the paper is silent on groups.
- **[LIT-097](../literature.d/LIT-097.md).** No bearing.
- No ML instruction. Its proposed computational models of phenomenal
  reports are a research programme for cognitive science, not a practice
  instruction. It does not belong in the anthology.

## Limitations

- The data the programme needs mostly do not exist yet, by the author's own
  account (§1.1).
- The favoured solution is an explanandum sketch, not a mechanism (p. 34).
- Several rejections are brief: proposal 13 is dismissed without reasons.
- The anti-illusionist conclusion rests on a Moorean premise that the
  illusionist explicitly predicts the realist will find compelling. The
  paper concedes the dialectic does not settle it.
- The IIT challenge assumes that a zero-Φ simulation has the same
  intuitions, on a functional reading of intuition. An IIT theorist who
  denies that the simulation *judges* anything would resist it. The paper
  notes that "various ways" of responding exist and does not pursue them.

## Open questions

- What experimental results would discriminate between primitive-quality
  and primitive-relation accounts, or between realizationism and strong
  illusionism?
- Does any group, AI system or modular architecture produce problem reports
  that are its own? And would that count, by the §3 challenge, as evidence
  bearing on its consciousness?
- Can realizationism be made to remove the coincidence (C7)? The paper
  points to Mørch's phenomenal powers as one route and leaves it there.
