# NOTE-1951: Van Gorder — Quantum Hasimoto transformation on a superfluid vortex filament

## Contribution

A dissipative generalization of the Hasimoto transformation. It shows what
of Hasimoto's filament soliton survives weak loss: the curvature envelope
persists, the torsion changes, and decay is slow.

## Key insight

A thin vortex line can be described entirely by its curvature and torsion.
In that description, its motion under local self-induction is a nonlinear
wave equation whose solitons are localized patterns of the line's shape.
They are patterns on a structure, not structures of the fluid.

## Assumptions

- The local induction approximation in place of the full Biot–Savart
  integral.
- A negligible normal-fluid velocity, without which the transformation
  does not exist.
- Small mutual friction coefficients α and α′, treated to first order.
- For the soliton, validity "for small timescales": "dissipative effects
  likely dominate any nonlinear effects for large time" (p. 12).

## Key results

- **Hasimoto's result as restated** (§1, p. 2): "the 1-soliton solution
  found by Hasimoto (1972), by way of what is now referred to as the
  Hasimoto transformation, which puts the classical LIA into
  correspondence with the cubic NLS".
- **The transformation** (§2, eq. 2.4): ψ(s,t) = κ(s,t) exp(i∫₀ˢ τ dŝ).
  With α = α′ = 0 the scalar equation "reduces to a cubic NLS", and the
  results "are completely consistent with those of Hasimoto for the
  classical fluid LIA" (p. 6).
- **A condition** (§1, p. 3): "if this assumption is not made, the quantum
  Hasimoto transformation does not exist" (the negligible normal-fluid
  velocity).
- **The soliton** (§4): q0(S) = sech(S), "so in the ε → 0 limit, the
  result corresponds to the Hasimoto soliton". The stationary Hasimoto
  soliton is "torsionless". Its frictional counterpart has torsion.
- **Persistence under loss** (§10, p. 28): "while the soliton is not
  eternal (as is true in the classical case), the soliton would dissipate
  at a rate proportional to at most α², which is rather slow".
- **Other solutions** (§3): the classical ring "will gradually collapse
  under mutual friction effects". A static helix needs a normal-fluid drive
  to balance the dissipation (§3.1).
- **Experiment, reported** (§1): Hopfinger and Browand (1982) found that
  "the theoretical solutions predicted by Hasimoto (1972) do actually occur
  along vortex filaments in practical experiments". Not read here.

## Concepts

The local induction approximation; the Da Rios equations; binormal motion;
the Hasimoto variable (modulus the curvature, phase the integrated
torsion); mutual friction; a complex Ginzburg–Landau-type equation;
standing and travelling solitons.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-606](../claims.d/CLAIM-606.md), the integrable route.** The classical case is the route on
  a filament: no supply, and the soliton is "eternal". Under weak loss the
  sech envelope stays while the soliton slowly decays. That fits the
  claim's "under loss it shrinks while keeping its shape", with one
  refinement: friction shows first in the torsion, the phase, not in the
  curvature. On the same carrier, the static helix needs a drive to exist,
  which is the claim's dissipative route.
- **[THEORY-1509](../theory.d/THEORY-1509.md).** Another instance of a weakly damped soliton running
  down along its family of shapes rather than breaking up, here for an
  NLS-type equation, with the rate bounded at order α².
- **[TERM-088](../terms.d/TERM-088.md).** The soliton is individuated as a pattern of the filament's
  shape. The filament carrying it is presupposed throughout and is not
  derived. The two are individuated at different levels and on different
  grounds.

## Limitations

limits and summaries, not Hasimoto's text. The travelling waves of §6 were
not read past the opening. The equations were taken from extracted text.
