# LIT-tmp9t0oy: Is the folk concept of pain polyeidic?

Borg, E., Harrison, R., Stazicker, J. & Salomons, T. (2020). Is the folk concept of pain polyeidic? *Mind & Language* 35(1), 29–47 (online 3 February 2019). DOI 10.1111/mila.12227. Read from the revise-and-resubmit manuscript in CentAUR (University of Reading), item 79706.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmpak4tr](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpak4tr.md).*

- **The target.** The "Bodily view": the folk hold that pains have
  bodily locations and are "not in the mind", because participants allow
  shared pains, unfelt pains and hallucinated pains (Sytsma 2010; Reuter,
  Phillips & Sytsma 2014).
- **Four methodological objections.** The samples are small and partly
  recruited online. Every vignette concerns acute pain, while chronic,
  diffuse pain fits the view poorly. "Is the pain in his mind?" is likely
  heard as "is it imagined?". And the means are close to the neutral
  point (3.29 and 3.42 for shared pain; 5.4 for two pains in the
  three-legged race).
- **Two structural objections.** Denying pains the Cartesian marks is
  consistent with a non-Cartesian mental view, which Sytsma & Reuter
  (2018) concede. And from "folk respond in bodily ways" only "the folk
  conception of pain is (at least in part) Bodily" follows.
- **Counter-evidence, pilot only.** In vignettes where damage never hurt,
  participants disagreed that the person "has pain" (means 5.70 and
  5.79, where 7 is "clearly disagree"). The details are deferred to an
  unpublished manuscript, and no sample size is given.
- **The polyeidic view.** The folk concept is an amalgam of
  dimensions that "can conflict", on which subjects hold "latent
  positions" that are "subject to framing effects" and have
  "behavioural effects". It is offered as a programme. The paper prefers,
  provisionally, single scales between poles over separate dimensions.
- **Clinical hypothesis.** Patients and clinicians may weight the
  experiential and the bodily dimensions differently, which would
  explain part of the difficulty they have in communicating about chronic
  pain.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-20 at the owner's request, in a round of experimental
work near the rubber hand illusion. It is a philosophical critique of
strict-sense experimental philosophy, with a little pilot data of its
own. Read in full from a pre-acceptance manuscript: **Active**.

It **does not bear on** [THEORY-tmpfufvf](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpfufvf.md), [THEORY-tmpxdoe8](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpxdoe8.md), [THEORY-tmpom3z2](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpom3z2.md),
[THEORY-tmpuehzc](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpuehzc.md) or [THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md). It is about the concept of pain, not
about ownership, felt location under multisensory conflict, or
interoceptive signals. Its footnotes on referred and phantom pain touch
the topic of felt location, and the NOTE says why that is not evidence
for or against [THEORY-tmpfufvf](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpfufvf.md)'s referred-touch clause.

Its place is with the record's holdings on pain, affect and phenomenal
consciousness. Its affective, aversive and motivational dimensions
sit beside [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md)'s claim that felt affect, pain included, is the
experience of the body's regulatory state. It does not test that claim.
For [QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md) it is a reminder, from inside the x-phi literature,
that judgements about a concept move with how the question is asked, and
that they are not evidence about the experience.

Read against Reuter, Sienhold & Sytsma (2019), filed in the same round
([LIT-tmpvrrs0](LIT-tmpvrrs0.md)), one of the bodily-view papers it opposes. It names
that paper's authors and cites it, but does not discuss its
pain-in-mouth argument. Each paper uses conversational implicature as
its tool. Reuter et al. use it to explain away the intuition that pain
is not spatially in the mouth. Borg et al. use it to explain away
the bodily view's "not in the mind" answers.
