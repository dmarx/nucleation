# NOTE-tmphivx5: Brouillet and Friston — Relative fluency (unfelt vs felt) in active inference

## Contribution

The paper proposes that the psychology of processing fluency can be
restated in active inference. Fluency is read as the brain's recognition
of the precision of its own predictions. What is first registered is not
fluency but a *change* in it relative to expectation. The paper's own
addition is the pairing of Whittlesea's discrepancy-attribution hypothesis
with precision-weighting, and a two-level vocabulary for it: unfelt
fluency below, felt fluency above.

## Key insight

The feeling of ease is an inference about one's own inference. Its cause
is the precision of internal processing, not anything in the world, and
introspection does not reveal that cause. So when expected and experienced
ease come apart, the surprise is attributed to the most salient candidate,
usually the stimulus, and shows up as familiarity, liking, nearness or a
sense of error.

## Assumptions

- **The predictive-brain premise.** The brain "predicts the content of the
  sensorium and crucially the precision of … its own predictions". It is
  taken as accepted, not argued.
- **Precision is attention.** "Psychologically, this kind of precision
  weighting is often equated with attentional set"; physiologically it is
  synaptic gain under neuromodulation (dopamine, noradrenaline).
- **Fluency is a feeling by definition.** Fluency is "the subjective
  (metacognitive or qualitative) experience of the level of ease with
  which our own cognitive processes proceed" (after Alter and
  Oppenheimer 2009). "Unfelt fluency" is therefore a new, extended use.
- **Bayesian beliefs are subpersonal**: "implicit probability
  distributions encoded by neuronal activity and synaptic gain", not
  propositional attitudes.
- **Helmholtz's unconscious inference**: "any mental state is realised by
  unconscious inference", whence "the causes of the subjective
  (conscious) inference must have unconscious perceptual precursors".

## Key results

The paper reports no simulation and no experiment of its own. Its results
are a review and a proposal.

- **Absolute and relative fluency (pp. 8–9).** After Whittlesea and Leboe
  (2003): absolute fluency is the degree of ease, as in priming; relative
  fluency is ease against expectation, as when something expected at once
  appears after a delay and "will seem more fluid than if it had appeared
  immediately".
- **The hension effect (pp. 9–10), as reported.** In Whittlesea and
  Williams (1998, Exp. 3), new orthographically regular non-words
  ("hension") drew more false recognitions than new real words or
  irregular non-words, although real words were read fastest. Read as
  surprise: easy to pronounce against hard to identify. "Participants
  cannot attribute surprise to its source, so they attribute the fluency
  felt to the most obvious source, the stimulus".
- **The same pattern, extended (pp. 10–13), as reported.**
  - Conceptual against perceptual fluency (Brouillet et al. 2023): the
    discrepancy acts "both anteriorly (forward) and posteriorly
    (backward)".
  - Motor fluency of an unrelated preceding gesture (Brouillet et al.
    2017) and of a colour associated with a fluent gesture (Brouillet,
    Rousset and Perrin 2022) raises "old" responses to new items.
  - Typing (Wilbert and Haider 2012, Exp. 4): the sense of having made an
    error comes from "a discrepancy between expected fluency and
    experienced fluency", resolved by searching for a cause.
  - Conclusion drawn: "the phenomenological perception of a gap directs
    attention, and … the fluency experienced is merely the consequence of
    a non-felt fluency or an expected fluency."
- **Unfelt fluency (pp. 14–16).** Surprise "is not a state (i.e., an
  emotional state of mind …)" but self-information. It drives a
  stimulus-bound change in attentional set. "These subpersonal changes
  can then be recognised as unfelt fluency; namely non-conscient
  recognising a change in attentional set". When precision is optimal,
  it "constitutes unfelt fluency", likened to exogenous attention.
- **Felt fluency (pp. 15–16).** Unfelt fluency is "phenomenologically
  transparent (surprise) until it is rendered opaque by the mental action
  that attends its recognition, felt fluency". Mental action is
  "top-down or endogenous attention". In a hierarchy, felt fluency is "a
  hierarchically deeper representation of fluency": it infers fluent
  processing below and sends priors on precision down. It is compared to
  Solms's felt uncertainty, mediated by ascending neuromodulation.
- **The summary claim (pp. 16–18).** Felt fluency "supervenes on—or is an
  attributional inference about—the resulting optimisation of active
  inference", experienced as "a precise grip on the world of affordances".
  It is both cause (a prior on precision) and consequence (a "prediction
  error of prediction errors") of fluent processing. "Changes in unfelt
  fluency (i.e., precision) induce felt fluency."
- **Why misattribution follows (p. 18).** Because felt fluency "arises
  from internal inferences about the precision of predictive processing,
  not the sensorium", it "cannot be attributed to its subpersonal
  cause—and is therefore plausibly attributed to the sensorium".
- **Affect, in passing (p. 8).** Hesp et al. (2021) are cited for
  formalising emotional valence as a state of self inferred from changes
  in confidence in one's generative model, and for proposing "that it
  would be changes in processing fluency across different domains that …
  would be at the root of affective states". Brielmann and Dayan (2022)
  are cited for a model in which fluency, as likelihood, predicts
  aesthetic pleasure.

## Concepts

- **Fluency.** The subjective experience of the ease of one's own
  processing (the field's definition, which the paper adopts).
- **Absolute / relative fluency.** Degree of ease / ease relative to
  expectation (Whittlesea and Leboe). Westerman's different sense of
  "relative" is noted and set aside (footnote 4).
- **Unfelt (non-felt) fluency.** A non-conscious recognition of a change
  in attentional set, i.e. in precision. Treated as like exogenous
  attention.
- **Felt fluency.** The opaque, reportable recognition of unfelt fluency,
  made by mental action (endogenous attention); like felt uncertainty in
  Solms.
- **Transparent / opaque.** Metzinger's sense: a transparent state is not
  represented as a representation, so its source is not given.
- **Surprise.** Self-information of sensations, explicitly not an emotion.

## Connections

The paper joins Whittlesea's SCAPE model and discrepancy-attribution
hypothesis to active inference as in Feldman and Friston (2010), Parr and
Friston, and Kanai et al. (2015). Its nearest formal neighbours are Hesp
et al. (2021, "Deeply felt affect") for valence and Sandved-Smith et al.
(2021) for meta-awareness, neither held in the record. It shares the
predictive-coding scheme that Barrett's constructed-emotion paper uses
([LIT-542](../literature.d/LIT-542.md), [NOTE-434](NOTE-434.md)), not the Markov-blanket theorem of [LIT-526](../literature.d/LIT-526.md). James's
"fringe of consciousness" is invoked for unfelt fluency.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-059](../questions.d/QUESTION-059.md) (Deferred).** The LIT was filed as separating "felt from
  unfelt fluency inside one active-inference framework", and as "a
  candidate mechanism for what feeling adds to a functional state". The
  reading weakens both.
  - *Fluency is not affect.* The paper treats fluency as a metacognitive
    feeling of ease. It touches valence only by citing Hesp et al. and
    the hedonic-fluency literature.
  - *Felt versus unfelt is access, not phenomenality.* "Felt" fluency is
    the state that endogenous attention makes opaque and reportable ("I
    lost a fluent grip on this task because my attention was
    distracted"); unfelt fluency is likened to exogenous attention. That
    is a difference of functional position in a hierarchy, of exactly the
    kind [QUESTION-059](../questions.d/QUESTION-059.md) asks whether a felt state can be reduced to. The
    paper simply identifies feeling with the higher position and gives
    no argument that the lower one lacks experience. It even speaks of
    "a phenomenal sense of non-felt fluency" (p. 11), so its own usage
    does not keep the two apart.
  So it is, at most, an instance of the identity side, as [THEORY-101](../theory.d/THEORY-101.md) is:
  feeling is the occupant of a functional role. It is not a mechanism
  that could tell the two sides apart, and nothing in it would show a
  regulatory role met without feeling.
- **[THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md).** Barrett's felt affect is a prediction about the body;
  Brouillet and Friston's felt fluency is an inference about the
  precision of one's own processing. If both are right, some felt states
  are about the brain's own processing and not about the body, which is
  the dissent from Minsky that [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md) already records. The paper does
  not claim that fluency is affect, so it does not contradict [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md).
- **[THEORY-093](../theory.d/THEORY-093.md).** Damasio's feelings are "hybrid", made partly of the
  body state. Felt fluency on this account is wholly an inference within
  the cortical hierarchy, with no body in the loop. Again not a
  contradiction, since fluency is not offered as a homeostatic feeling.
- **Misattribution.** Its claim that a feeling with an internal cause is
  attributed to the most salient external source is the same structure
  as Barrett's categorization of affect by context ([THEORY-057](../theory.d/THEORY-057.md)). The
  paper does not make the connection.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- **No model.** Nothing is simulated or derived; "unfelt" and "felt"
  fluency are not given variables in a generative model. The formal
  claims rest on cited simulations of attention and meta-awareness.
- **No new data.** The empirical case is a review, much of it the first
  author's own experiments, reported briefly and without effect sizes.
- **Terms shift.** Fluency is defined as a subjective experience, then
  extended to an unfelt case; unfelt fluency is "phenomenologically
  transparent", yet the gap between it and felt fluency is "phenomenal".
- **Neuromodulation is assigned, not shown.** The link of felt fluency to
  monoaminergic precision control is by citation to Moran et al. and
  Solms.
- **What would refute it is not stated.**

## Open questions

- Can unfelt and felt fluency be written as two levels of a generative
  model, with a prediction that the discrepancy-attribution findings do
  not already fix? A simulation that reproduced the hension effect and
  predicted a new reversal would make the proposal testable.
- Is "felt" here more than "reportable after endogenous attention"? A case
  where fluency is attended and reported yet not felt, or felt without
  being attended, would separate them.
