summary: >-
  Collins & Shester (2013; NBER 2011, revised 2012). A city-level
  instrumental-variable study of federal urban renewal grants under Title
  I of the Housing Act of 1949 in 458 US cities, the instrument being the
  years a city could take part, set by when its state passed enabling
  legislation. Each year of eligibility brought about $10 of grants per
  capita (first-stage F 13.8), and rural counties show no relationship
  between the legislation and outcomes. In 1980 "a $100 per capita
  difference in grant funding is associated with a 2.4 percent difference
  in 1980 median income and a 6.9 percent difference in 1980 median
  property value", with 9–11% more population and housing units; the
  effects "are not driven by changes in demographic composition", appear
  for income by 1960 and property value by 1970, and in 2000 "do not
  dissipate with time". The authors read this as raised local
  productivity, and say the design cannot price the costs borne by the
  more than 300,000 families the projects displaced.

# LIT-tmpdtq25: Slum Clearance and Urban Renewal in the United States

Collins, W. J. & Shester, K. L. (2013). Slum Clearance and Urban Renewal in the United States. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics* 5(1): 239–273. doi:10.1257/app.5.1.239. First circulated as NBER Working Paper 17458, September 2011, revised November 2012, doi:10.3386/w17458.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, NOTE-tmpvwtsg. Page numbers are the NBER working paper's (November 2012 revision).*

- **Scale.** "more than 2,100 distinct urban renewal projects with grants
  totaling approximately $53 billion (in 2009 dollars)" (p. 2); new
  funding ended in 1974.
- **Displacement.** To mid-1966 projects "cleared (or intended to clear)
  over 400,000 housing units, forcing the relocation of over 300,000
  families, just over half of whom were nonwhite" (p. 5).
- **City gains.** "a $100 per capita difference in grant funding is
  associated with a 2.4 percent difference in 1980 median income and a 6.9
  percent difference in 1980 median property value" (p. 17), and 9–11% more
  population and housing units (p. 25). Not returns: "They are not
  estimates of the social returns on investment or local fiscal
  multipliers." (p. 17)
- **Not composition.** "there is no reduction in the black population"
  (p. 24), although black residents were disproportionately displaced, and
  with 1980 composition controlled "The coefficients on urban renewal are
  slightly larger in the augmented regressions" (p. 25).
- **After the money stopped.** "the estimated urban renewal effects do not
  dissipate with time" (p. 26): the 2000 estimates exceed 1980's, though
  "Given the large standard errors, we hesitate to make strong
  inferences".
- **Concentration, not spillover.** County effects are weak, "suggesting a
  limited scope for positive geographic spillovers" (pp. 22–23).

## Standing in the record

Filed for the owner's question of what makes support safe or hazardous
to remove. It is a case of large, one-off, time-limited outside support,
the clearing and rebuilding done by public agencies and private
developers rather than by residents, whose city-level effect persisted
and may have grown after the support stopped. That qualifies
CLAIM-tmpc3x0c on the unit of analysis: what persisted is the city as a
place, measured in aggregates, while the population nominally served was
removed from the cleared areas. Read as upkeep, it runs against
CLAIM-597; read as formation drawing on private investment, it is outside
that claim's scope. Its threshold and agglomeration stories fit
CLAIM-tmpw4ai2 without testing it, and its note that cities' agencies
learned by doing proposals bears on CLAIM-tmpoh07b for the agencies, not
for residents. It answers QUESTION-398 at city scale in part, and
parallels THEORY-919 (Juhász) in design. It does not see the displaced or
their neighbourhoods; LaVoice (2024), read alongside as a complement,
does. Filed from it: THEORY-tmp7ch1y.
