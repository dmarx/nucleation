# CLAIM-708: Responsibility for one's own conduct and responsibility for the conditions under which another acts are distinct and can both hold of one outcome: a party who wrongfully structures another's circumstances so that a wrong becomes likely, hard to avoid or apparently necessary can answer for that wrong while the one who commits it still answers for committing it, and the structuring is also a wrong of its own against the person structured, whether or not they comply

## The claim

The exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afa2a8d-2598-83ea-8d1a-d9ceb992a99c). A7 §8,
after Young's social connection model and a workplace in which a manager's
abusive culture leads some employees to bully others:

> But relationships don't literally make decisions. People do. [...] A
> satisfactory account needs both levels.
>
> **People are responsible for their conduct, but they can also be
> responsible for creating the conditions that constrain and corrupt other
> people's conduct.**

The owner quoted the bold sentence at U5 and said "expand on this". A9
answers with what it calls "The principle I'd ultimately defend":

> A person is ordinarily accountable for the wrongs they intentionally
> commit. But another person may also bear responsibility for those wrongs
> when they have deliberately or culpably created the conditions that made
> the wrongdoing likely, difficult to avoid, or apparently necessary. This
> responsibility is especially serious when the conditions undermine the
> actor's autonomy, moral judgment, or capacity to act according to their
> own values.

A9 §1 names the two layers: "**First-order wrongdoing:** Doing something
impermissible" and "**Second-order wrongdoing:** Wrongfully structuring
another person's circumstances so that they are pressured, incentivized, or
conditioned to commit the first-order wrong."

## Its limit

A9 §7 refuses to attribute "everything bad that happens downstream to the
person who established the original conditions". It grades the structuring
party's share by six questions: control over the conditions, knowledge,
how costly the actor's non-wrongful alternatives were, how directly the
pressure was created, whether the structuring party benefited from or
weaponized the wrong, and proportionality. And it blocks the regress:
"everyone acts under influences created by other people", and what makes
coercion, domination and manipulation special is that they "**wrongfully
interfere with another person's capacity to govern their own conduct**".

## The wrong against the person structured

Carried from the continuation's filing of the same turns (share
https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b). A9 §1
works through a manager and an employee asked to falsify records, from
ordinary to coerced wrongdoing to "Manufactured dependency", and calls its
two layers "analytical labels, rather than as the names of a universally
accepted philosophical taxonomy". Three further parts:

- **It is wrong without success.** A9 §1: "**Creating a coercive moral
  trap can itself be wrong, whether or not the trap succeeds.**"
- **It is not zero-sum.** A9 §4: "responsibility isn't necessarily
  zero-sum." A salesperson lies to a customer under a manager's deceptive
  incentive scheme: the salesperson wrongs the customer, the manager wrongs
  the customer, and "The manager may also have wronged the salesperson".
  "They may be **several distinct wrongs connected through a single
  event**." A9 §4 grades what the structuring party does, from "Influences
  B" through "Creates foreseeable pressure" and "Exploits B's
  vulnerability" to "Deliberately engineers wrongdoing".
- **It wrongs the person structured.** A9's principle separates a
  distributive claim from a relational one: "deliberately compromising
  another person's moral agency may be a wrong *against that person*,
  quite apart from whatever harms subsequently occur." A11 §6 states it for
  the reflexive-control case as A's responsibility "for appropriating B's
  agency". A9 §9: the arranger may owe "acknowledgment of the dilemma they
  created, restitution for resulting losses, and changes that restore the
  other person's ability to act independently."

## Where it sits

- [CLAIM-624](CLAIM-624.md) holds the organizational form: an organization's
  wrong beyond violation in reproducing the representations and allocations
  of authority that keep a justified standard from constraining it.
- [CLAIM-445](CLAIM-445.md): a protective regime becomes self-maintaining
  when its interventions shift the evidence used to judge its necessity.
  A9 §3's "feedback loop" is the interpersonal case: the controller's
  conditions produce conduct that is then cited to justify more control.
  That claim leaves out the point A9 adds, that the controller gains no
  authority from conduct it induced.
- [QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md) asks whose agency a
  collective's manipulation runs through. This claim answers a version of
  it for an individual structuring party: through the actor's, without
  relieving either.
- [THEORY-147](../theory.d/THEORY-147.md), from Wolf: attributability and
  accountability are independent. A9 §5 draws on the same distinction for
  the actor's side.
- The same exchange's [CLAIM-709](CLAIM-709.md) (equality of wrongdoing and of power
  are separate questions) uses this claim for its second half.
- [CASE-151](../cases.d/CASE-151.md) (Wells Fargo) is a documented arrangement of the kind:
  a sales model and performance management that the board's investigation
  traced the misconduct to, with leadership blaming individual employees.
  The claim does not rest on it; whether the report shows the relational
  wrong, against the employees, is not settled here.
- [QUESTION-449](../questions.d/QUESTION-449.md) asks whether it matters that another installed a
  mechanism, or only who maintains it now. A9 §3 answers for persons that
  responsibility "for the formation of the dispositions that produced it"
  can lie with someone other than the actor: "These responsibilities can
  belong partly to different people." The claim bears on the question; it
  does not answer it for organizations.
- [CLAIM-671](CLAIM-671.md) strengthens a responsible agent's obligations where a
  transformation increases another's vulnerability to it; this claim names
  the wrong of using such vulnerability to produce wrongdoing.
- The senses of responsibility kept apart are [TERM-127](../terms.d/TERM-127.md)'s. The
  continuation's filing read [CLAIM-624](CLAIM-624.md)'s "second-order" as a duty about
  keeping violations correctable rather than a wrong of producing another's
  wrong; on either reading [CLAIM-624](CLAIM-624.md) is not this claim restated.

## What it does not say

- **That the actor is excused.** "None of these conclusions cancels the
  others" (A7 §8).
- **How much.** A9 §7: "None of these questions has a mechanical answer."
- **That it is new.** Young's *Responsibility for Justice*
  ([LIT-2246](../literature.d/LIT-2246.md), Deferred) and the literature on derivative and complicitous responsibility
  hold versions of it; A states it as a synthesis, not a discovery.
- **That an explanation excuses.** A9 §5: "We have to be especially careful
  about the phrase *You made me do it.*" "An explanation involving another
  person's conduct is not automatically an excuse." "we should judge their
  choices in light of the choices they actually had."
- **That responsibility is fixed.** A9 §5: "**A person's responsibility can
  change as their capacity for doing otherwise changes.**"
- **That the second-order wrong is always the worse.** A9 §8: "Not
  everyone would agree that the second wrong is categorically worse, but
  there is good reason to regard it as qualitatively different."

## Note of 2026-11-16: Gaboriaud and colleagues, read

The LIT registered this experiment as bearing on the claim. Read, it does not. It manipulates whether a harm was caused by the acting agent or "by another independent source". Participants judged the agent less harshly when the other source caused it ("M = 3.72" vs "M = 2.82"). That concerns an agent's own causal role in an outcome. No scenario has a party who wrongfully structures another's circumstances, and there is no measure of how responsibility is shared between such a party and the actor. Nor does the paper give A5 §5's distinction between kinds of responsibility an empirical basis. Its own conclusion is that lay wrongness and blame judgments differ little ("the distinction between types of judgment (particularly wrongness versus blame) must not be overestimated"). Relation: none. The LIT's link should be dropped from its summary.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Mazinani and O'Connor, read

The paper affirms the claim's first-order half: responsibility for an act "lies with the person who commits it". It is silent on whether a partner who structures the conditions also answers for it. Its relationship-context markers ("controlling behavior (r=0.30), and greater perpetrator power within the relationship (r=0.18)") are associations with the controlling partner's own perpetration, not with the other partner's. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven, read

Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018, [LIT-2705](../literature.d/LIT-2705.md), [NOTE-2024](../notes.d/NOTE-2024.md)) support the claim's first half from outside the exchange. Their tracing condition carries responsibility from the agent who acts to whoever shaped the conditions of the act: in Kamm's reading of Jim and the captain, the threat "originates with (and therefore the responsibility traces back to)" the captain, because "The captain is the choice architect, who" "creates Jim’s dilemma in which there are only tragic choices to" be made (p. 8); and those in a system's design and maintenance carry "their second-order responsibility for the first-order responsibility" of the operator (p. 9). They qualify the claim's second half. Where the operator's conditions fail through no fault of hers, "the pilot may not be responsible for an accident" and responsibility "may trace back to other agents upstream": tracing here relocates responsibility rather than adding a second bearer. The paper does not take up cases in which both answer, which the claim asserts are possible; nor does it ask whether the structuring was wrongful.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tognazzini and Coates, read

The SEP entry "Blame" ([LIT-2720](../literature.d/LIT-2720.md), read in [NOTE-1998](../notes.d/NOTE-1998.md)) supports the claim from
the side of blame, by a route independent of the exchange. Among the facts
about a blamer that undermine standing is complicity. It quotes Cohen's list,
"you ordered me to do it, you asked me to do it, you forced me to do it, you
left me with no reasonable alternative, you gave me the means to do it", and
draws the conclusion: "The superior officer who orders a subordinate to do
something morally reprehensible is not in a position to blame the
subordinate for carrying out the order, even if civilians are." The actor
remains blameworthy ("even if civilians are"). The party who structured the
circumstances is implicated in the very act, so both responsibilities hold
of one wrong. That is the claim's thesis, seen through who may blame. The
entry does not say the complicit party answers for the act itself, only
that it lacks standing to condemn it. Relation: corroborates (on the new
THEORY).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Smith, read

This claim cites A9 §5, which rests the actor's side on Watson's
distinction between attribution and accountability. Smith ([LIT-2658](../literature.d/LIT-2658.md)) offers
a rival reading of the same cases. Conduct that reflects the agent's
evaluative judgments is answerable. How harshly to respond depends on "(1)
the seriousness of the moral failure, (2) the nature of our relationship
with the agent, and perhaps even (3) our own moral standing", which is "the
ethics of blame". On that reading the claim still holds: the actor answers
for committing the wrong. But the moderating role A9 §5 gives the actor's
history is not a second kind of responsibility. It is a reason bearing on
the fitting response. The claim does not depend on the distinction being
one of kinds. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Anderson, read

Anderson's SEP entry on coercion ([LIT-2673](../literature.d/LIT-2673.md), skimmed in [NOTE-2014](../notes.d/NOTE-2014.md))
supports the claim's structure from the coerced party's side and leaves its
other side open. §3.2 assesses the coercee's responsibility as a question
of its own, never as a share subtracted from a fixed total. What changes
that responsibility is how the coercer structured the situation: the
coercee is justified where "the coercer has structured a coercee’s
situation in such a way that the coercee’s will becomes essentially
redundant to the outcome". In coercive control, it reports a proposed
defence "when the coercer has usurped the target’s decisional authority"
(Galoob and Sheley 2021, 314). The entry also observes that "Coercion
involves a strategic and dynamic interaction between two agents", so the
rule we adopt for the coercee's responsibility shapes what the coercer
attempts. That is an argument, independent of the exchange, that the two
responsibilities cannot be fixed one at a time. The entry does not address
whether the coercer answers for the coercee's act, which is the claim's
distinctive half, and it excludes manipulation by stipulation. It also
qualifies the claim's vocabulary of excuse: most diminished responsibility
under coercion "will appeal to justification rather than excuse".
Relation: corroborates (on [THEORY-1919](../theory.d/THEORY-1919.md)), partial and second-hand.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Nagel, read

Nagel's "Moral Luck" ([LIT-2662](../literature.d/LIT-2662.md), read in [NOTE-2015](../notes.d/NOTE-2015.md)) states the principle
that would pull against the claim's second half, that the actor "still
answers for committing it". On the condition of control, "action can be
judged only to the extent that it goes beyond these conditions and does not
just result from them". On that view, conditions another party structured
are subtracted from what the actor answers for. Nagel shows that ordinary
judgment does not subtract this way: citizens of Nazi Germany faced a test
others did not, and "most of them are culpable for having failed this test".
He also argues that no principled line stops the subtraction once begun. So
the claim's position, that the actor answers in full while the structuring
party also answers, is the ordinary judgment Nagel describes. It holds only
by refusing the control condition, which Nagel calls "This compatibilist
account of our moral judgments". It inherits his objection to that account, its "failure to explain
how skeptical problems arise". Nagel does not discuss whoever set the test.
Relation: none. The reading neither corroborates nor bounds the claim, but
it names the condition the claim's `defeated_if` (the proximate-agent and
fixed-quantity views) does not mention.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tessman (via Bell's review), read

A7 cites Burdened Virtues on the way to this claim. As Bell reports it, the book holds that
"oppression interferes with the ability of the oppressed to achieve moral goodness", by preventing some
virtues and by requiring burdened ones whose cost falls on their bearer (p. 95). It does not address
who answers for a wrong that a burdened person commits, or responsibility for structuring another's
conditions. The book gives the claim no support beyond the general point that conditions shape
character. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tessman, read

Tessman's A/B/C case (p. 14) keeps the claim's two responsibilities apart for one outcome. A's wrong
"functions like luck" in the relationship between B and C, where B must still meet C's need, and is the
act of a responsible agent in the relationship between A and B, where "A owes all kinds of reparations
to B". The structuring party answers for the predicament, and the structured party still answers for
her conduct within it. In Tessman's case the structured party does no wrong; she bears an unfair duty.
The claim is about a wrong made likely, so the support is for its structure, not its central case.
Relation: corroborates ([THEORY-1914](../theory.d/THEORY-1914.md)).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tronto (2018), read

An illustration at the scale of states. If every nation is responsible for development everywhere,
then France "bears no special responsibility for the underdevelopment" of its colonies, and "the
effect is to excuse the former colonizers for any failures on their part to create just
institutions" (p. 11). A diffusion of responsibility excuses the party whose conduct set the
conditions. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Elish, read

Consistent. Elish holds that the operators at Three Mile Island did err (p. 44) while the account
centred on them "misses other sites of interaction and dimensions of control exercised by other
actors", "the plant managers who created the conditions within which the operators could act"
(p. 45). That is the claim's structure: answering for conduct and answering for conditions both
apply to one outcome. She does not state it as a thesis about two kinds of responsibility.
Relation: none (bears, cited in the NOTE).

## Note of 2026-11-16: NTSB HAR-19/03, read

An official finding with this claim's structure, in causal terms. The operator's own conduct is
the probable cause ("a clear example of deliberate engagement in visual distraction", p. 43). The
party that set her conditions is found to have contributed to that very conduct: ATG's safety
culture "created conditions—including inadequate oversight of vehicle operators—that contributed
to the circumstances of the crash and specifically to the vehicle operator’s extended distraction
during the crash trip" (p. viii). The NTSB speaks of causes, not answerability, so this supports
the claim's factual premise, not its normative conclusion. Relation: none on the claim. A CASE
drawn from the report would be a fair specimen, if the owner wants one.
