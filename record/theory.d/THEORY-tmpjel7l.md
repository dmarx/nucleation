# THEORY-tmpjel7l: On Campbell and colleagues' comparison of cicadas, hosts whose endosymbiont Hodgkinia has split into many interdependent lineages compensate by sending more Hodgkinia cells to each egg, up to about six times as many, with Sulcia numbers and the transmission route unchanged, so that every egg still receives every lineage; the authors take the raised titre as a host adaptation driven by the symbiont's fragmentation and a cost of it

## Source

Campbell, Łukasik, Meyer, Buckner, Simon, Veloso, Michalik & McCutcheon
(2018), *mBio* 9(6): e02104-18, LIT-1590, read in full in NOTE-tmp6a57o
from the PubMed Central copy (PMC6234865). The four results subsections,
the discussion, Figures 1–4 from their captions, Table 1, and the
methods with their caveats.

## What was actually shown

- **The problem, by simulation (Fig. 1).** With random transmission, the
  cells needed to give every egg every lineage grow steeply with lineage
  number and unevenness, "by more than 2,000-fold" at *Magicicada*'s
  complexity. The authors call this an upper bound.
- **Titres (Fig. 2).** Four species, five or six eggs each, counted by
  FISH and confocal microscopy.
  - *Hodgkinia*: 4,889 (*T. ulnaria*, one lineage) to 30,154
    (*M. septendecim*, many), about 6-fold. Only *M. septendecim* differs
    significantly from the others. *T. chilensis* (six lineages, about
    12,000) does not.
  - *Sulcia*: 2,572 to 5,643, about 2-fold, and if anything lower in
    *M. septendecim*.
- **Transmission complete (Fig. 3).** Every sampled egg of *T. chilensis*
  and *T. limbata* carried every rpoB lineage, and every *M. septendecim*
  egg carried both universal etfD genotypes. Eggs of one nest share their
  mother's lineage proportions.
- **Route unchanged (Fig. 4).** The same stages under light microscopy in
  *T. lacertosa* and *M. septendecim*, with more cells migrating in
  *Magicicada*.

What could have failed: the *Hodgkinia* titre could have been the same
across species, with complex cicadas laying eggs that lack lineages; or
both symbionts' titres could have risen together, pointing to a general
change in the host's transmission. Neither was found.

## What this does not say

- **Not a trend across complexity.** One species of four carries the
  difference. One species stands for each level of complexity, and there
  is no phylogenetic control.
- **Not that splitting came first.** The authors raise the reverse, a
  high titre enabling splitting, and argue against it only because some
  cicadas keep an unsplit *Hodgkinia*.
- **Not that no egg ever lacks a lineage.** Presence was tracked by one or
  two marker genes, and the authors say rare losses could escape
  detection.
- **Not that hosts do not sort lineages.** Active sorting is untested.
- **Not the feedback.** That larger transmitted populations weaken host
  control and drive further splitting is, in the authors' words,
  "speculative".

## Connections

- **QUESTION-277.** The one worked case in the record of host
  compensation at transmission. The host maintains reproductive cohesion
  (R) while the number of essential parts, and so dependence (D), grows.
  On the authors' speculation, the same compensation may weaken host
  control (C) and selection alignment (S), which would make R and S
  trade off. The mapping to the dimensions is mine.
- **CASE-177** is the case this account documents. The splitting it
  compensates for is reported in LIT-1563, LIT-1586, LIT-1612 and
  LIT-1602, all unread.
- **THEORY-1049.** The authors note that *Hodgkinia* cannot make its own
  membranes, so "the host must define Hodgkinia's envelope". That makes
  active sorting by the host conceivable. It is not shown.
