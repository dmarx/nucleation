# QUESTION-325: Is persistent avoidance in anxiety maintained by an inflated uncertainty estimate about the avoided states, as Brown, Price and Dombrovski propose, or by a pessimistic evaluation of one's own future avoidance, as Zorowitz, Momennejad and Daw propose, rather than by an inflated threat value?

## Why it is a question

[THEORY-910](../theory.d/THEORY-910.md) holds what Brown, Price and Dombrovski propose, without a
model run or data; [THEORY-915](../theory.d/THEORY-915.md) holds what Zorowitz, Momennejad and Daw's
simulations showed, with no data fitted. Whether either mechanism
operates in people, and which, is untested.

## What would answer it

The former promote_when of each, unchanged.

**[THEORY-910](../theory.d/THEORY-910.md).** Two results. First, a simulation: an
uncertainty-sensitive temporal-difference agent in an aversive Markov
decision process, with noise misattributed to volatility or estimation
uncertainty, shows avoidance that persists because the avoided state's
uncertainty is never reduced, and stops when that uncertainty is reduced
by forced sampling with the threat estimate held fixed. Second, people:
in an instrumental avoidance task, measured uncertainty-learning
parameters predict how much people avoid, and a manipulation that lowers
uncertainty about the avoided option without changing its expected
threat reduces avoidance. It is refuted if avoidance in such tasks
tracks the expected threat and not the uncertainty, or if reducing
uncertainty with threat fixed leaves avoidance unchanged. Consistency
with clinical observations about exposure cannot settle it.

**[THEORY-915](../theory.d/THEORY-915.md).** Data, not more simulation: in a sequential avoidance
task with known transition structure, people's choices are fit better by
a model with a pessimistic weight on their own future actions than by an
optimal agent with inflated threat values or altered transition beliefs,
and that weight relates to anxiety; or the model's own prediction
confirmed, a free-choice premium reduced or reversed in anxious people.
It is refuted if anxious avoidance in such tasks is explained as well by
altered threat values with an optimal policy, or if the bias appears
where no future avoidance is at stake. A qualitative match of new
simulations to further published effects cannot settle it.

## Note of 2026-11-04: Gershman, Blei & Niv, "Context, learning, and extinction", read

Gershman, Blei & Niv (2010), [LIT-tmpdnjkj](../literature.d/LIT-tmpdnjkj.md), read in [NOTE-tmpn30ni](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpn30ni.md), suggest a third option beside an inflated uncertainty estimate and governance. Safety experience may be filed under a new latent cause, leaving the estimate for the avoided state untouched. That needs no inflated uncertainty, only the inference that the safe trials came from a different cause. The paper models no avoidance, so it raises the alternative without bearing on which holds. No status changes.

## Note of 2026-11-04: Mathys, Daunizeau, Friston & Stephan, "A Bayesian foundation for individual learning under uncertainty", read

Read in NOTE-tmpwdo6q (LIT-tmpld7ct). The paper is a formal source for the uncertainty side of this question. It separates informational uncertainty (the posterior variance) from environmental uncertainty (volatility), and derives a learning rate that rises with inferred volatility. It also offers its reduced-κ agent as an anthropomorphic picture of anxiety, to "interpret underconfidence about estimates of environmental volatility (i.e., high σ3) as a possible cause of anxiety". That is a metaphor drawn from one simulation, in a paper that says it is "of a purely theoretical nature". It supplies the construct, not evidence that inflated uncertainty maintains avoidance. It leaves open whether anxious people's fitted volatility parameters differ, and whether any difference drives avoidance. No status changes.

## Note of 2026-11-04: Mathys et al., "Uncertainty in perception and the Hierarchical Gaussian Filter", read

Read in NOTE-tmpnn27v (LIT-tmpkft30). The paper qualifies the question indirectly. It supplies the machinery that uncertainty accounts of anxiety fit: learning rates specific to each kind of uncertainty, and response models that include the volatility estimate μ3. Its sensory-uncertainty simulation shows that high input noise drives the volatility estimate down, so misreading noise as change corresponds, under the HGF, to underestimating sensory noise, which inflates μ3. The paper has no anxiety model and no anxious participants. Its Appendix F warns that "there is a fundamental ambiguity between individual differences in coupling (i.e., κ) and individual differences in priors for x3 (i.e., μ(0)3)", so group differences in fitted volatility parameters need one of these fixed before they can be read as evidence here. No status changes.
