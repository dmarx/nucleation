# LIT-481: Institutions, rules, and equilibria: a unified theory

Frank Hindriks, Francesco Guala (2015), *Journal of Institutional Economics 11(3):459–480; first published online 16 October 2014. Reply to commentators: JIE 11(3):515–522, DOI 10.1017/S1744137415000120* — DOI-10.1017/S1744137414000496

## Key takeaways

- An institution is a **rule in equilibrium**. Rules alone cannot say why
  some rules are followed and others are not (the 65 mph limit that
  everyone treats as 75). Equilibria alone let in too much: the butterfly
  *Pararge aegeria* plays a correlated equilibrium over sunspots but has
  no institution. Institutions are correlated equilibria of coordination
  games with several equilibria, represented by the players as rules.
- **Constitutive rules are regulative rules with a name.** A regulative
  rule [R] ("if one occupies land first, one has the right to its
  exclusive use") is split by a new term into a base rule [B] ("first
  occupant's land is her property*") and a status rule [S] ("property*
  gives the right to exclusive use"). [B] and [S] together have Searle's
  "X counts as Y in C" form, unpacked as "if C then X is Y, and if Y then
  Z". Because the new term is conservative and eliminable in Belnap's
  sense, nothing is added but cognitive economy and a name.
- **Consequences drawn.** Multiple realisability of institutional kinds
  (shells or coins as money) corresponds to multiple equilibria. Searle's
  view would wrongly deny that etiquette or driving on the right are
  institutions. By Lewis's account of theoretical terms, "property" refers
  if the rules that introduce it are true.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, as part of filling out the
record's coverage of `social-ontology` ([ADR-017](../decisions.d/ADR-017.md)), which names rules in
equilibrium as one of the literatures [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)'s anti-nesting question
can draw on. It is the record's main text for how the economists'
equilibrium account and the philosophers' constitutive-rule account fit
together. [NOTE-395](../notes.d/NOTE-395.md) is the reading.

**How it relates Searle to Lewis.** Lewis's conventions
([LIT-473](LIT-473.md)) are its starting point: "the first major breakthrough in
the equilibria approach" (§3). It generalises Lewis's symmetric
coordination equilibria to correlated equilibria, where a signal such as
first occupancy tells each player what to do. It adds a representation
condition, that the players encode their strategies as rules. Searle's
constitutive rules then appear as a way of *naming* such an equilibrium,
not as a different kind of rule. The authors say Lewis "would agree that
accepting regulative rules (strategies) suffices for their existence"
(§6). That is their reading of Lewis, not a quotation of him. The Searle being
answered here is the Searle of *The Construction of Social Reality*
([LIT-485](LIT-485.md)), filed alongside this paper with Searle's 2006 article
"Social ontology: Some basic principles" ([LIT-482](LIT-482.md)). That is a
different subject from Searle on the Chinese Room ([LIT-400](LIT-400.md)), and
nothing here bears on that paper.

**North** ([LIT-489](LIT-489.md)). Section 2 opens with North's definition of
institutions as "the rules of the game", quoted from his 1990 book, which
is not in the record. The 1991 essay filed here gives the same
definition. This paper's objection to the rules account, that it cannot
separate effective from nominal rules, is an objection North's essay
leaves open: North treats compliance as a matter of enforcement cost,
and his essay ends by listing how informal constraints work as an open
question.

**Guala's book** ([LIT-472](LIT-472.md)) is the book-length version. Its chapter
1, the publisher's sample, repeats this paper's §2 examples (the French
trousers law, the speed limit, marriage) in the same order.

**Epstein** ([LIT-478](LIT-478.md), [LIT-487](LIT-487.md)). Epstein's frame principles
generalise Searle's constitutive rules, and his anchors generalise
collective acceptance. This paper keeps the "counts as" form and makes
acceptance whatever attitudes an equilibrium needs. The two projects
agree that Searle's single form is too narrow and disagree about what
replaces it: for this paper an equilibrium, for Epstein a separate
metaphysical relation of anchoring. The SEP entry places Guala and
Hindriks 2020 in the new-institutional-economics tradition (§5.3).

**Group minds.** The paper says nothing about group agency or group
minds. Its neutrality on collective intentionality (n. 14) is deliberate.
For [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) its bearing is indirect: institutional facts, on this
view, are patterns of individual strategies that people represent and
name. Nothing in that requires, or rules out, a group-level subject.

No instruction for machine-learning practice appears, so nothing here
points to the Anthology of the SOTA.
