# NOTE-2034: NTSB (2019) — Collision Between Vehicle Controlled by Developmental Automated Driving System and Pedestrian, Tempe, Arizona (HAR-19/03)

## Contribution

The authoritative factual record of the Tempe crash, with a structured
allocation of causes across the operator, the company, the pedestrian and
the state, made by an investigation that "does not assign fault or blame".

## Key results

- **Probable cause** (p. v; §3.2, p. 59): "the failure of the vehicle
  operator to monitor the driving environment and the operation of the
  automated driving system because she was visually distracted throughout
  the trip by her personal cell phone. Contributing to the crash were the
  Uber Advanced Technologies Group's (1) inadequate safety risk assessment
  procedures, (2) ineffective oversight of vehicle operators, and (3) lack
  of adequate mechanisms for addressing operators' automation
  complacency—all a consequence of its inadequate safety culture." Further
  factors: the impaired pedestrian's crossing outside a crosswalk, and
  "the Arizona Department of Transportation's insufficient oversight of
  automated vehicle testing."
- **The design relied on the operator** (p. v): "The system design
  precluded activation of emergency braking for collision mitigation,
  relying instead on the operator's intervention to avoid a collision or
  mitigate an impact." Uber's deactivation of the Volvo's forward
  collision warning and automatic emergency braking "removed a layer of
  safety redundancy" (findings).
- **The operator's conduct** (§2.2.2.1, p. 42): "the operator was streaming
  a video for the entire trip, including the moments before the crash";
  her longest downward gaze was 26.5 seconds. Attentive, she "would likely
  have had sufficient time to detect and react" (p. 43).
- **Complacency is typical** (p. 44): "humans are very poor at this task";
  her distraction was "a typical effect of automation complacency", and
  Uber "did not adequately recognize the risk of automation complacency
  and develop effective countermeasures".
- **The second operator removed** (p. 45): "the decision to remove the
  second vehicle operator increased the task demands on the sole operator
  and also reduced the safety redundancies"; Uber "had the means to
  retroactively monitor the behavior of vehicle operators", but "it rarely
  did so".
- **Culture produced the distraction** (p. 46): "the Uber ATG's inadequate
  safety culture created conditions—including inadequate oversight of
  vehicle operators—that contributed to the circumstances of the crash and
  specifically to the vehicle operator's extended distraction". There was
  no corporate safety plan; the head of operations, "lacking experience in
  safety management", was also safety manager.
- **After the crash** (§2.2.3): Uber's postcrash second operator and
  real-time attentiveness monitoring "begins to address the oversight
  deficiencies that contributed to the crash".
- **The concurrence** (p. 62): Vice Chairman Landsberg: "The safety driver
  knew that cell phone use was prohibited while the vehicle was moving";
  she had made the same trip 73 times: "Automation complacency!"

## Assumptions

The NTSB's convention: one probable cause and contributing factors, a
ranking for safety, in "fact-finding proceedings with no formal issues and
no adverse parties". The report says nothing about criminal charges.

## Concepts

- **Automation complacency**.
- **Safety culture** and **safety management system**.
- **Action suppression**: a 1-second period in which the system suppressed
  braking while it verified a hazard.
- **Layers of safety redundancy**.

## Bearing on the record

Filed as [THEORY-1932](../theory.d/THEORY-1932.md), and the case as [CASE-tmpnrxpi](../cases.d/CASE-tmpnrxpi.md).

- **[CLAIM-707](../claims.d/CLAIM-707.md)**, the control-and-duty part, with an official finding:
  a design that relied on the operator for emergency braking, a removed
  second operator that raised her task demands, and a culture that
  contributed "specifically to the vehicle operator's extended
  distraction". It also fits the claim's carve-out of a frontline failure
  that was partly her own. It does not show blame landing downstream: the
  NTSB itself carries cause upstream, to Uber and the state. As an
  attribution it sits nearer the second branch of the claim's
  `defeated_if` (blame first placed where failure is visible, carried
  upstream by the same institutions) than a crumple zone. Whether the
  operator became one is a question about the prosecution and the press,
  which the report does not cover.
- **[QUESTION-tmp2t6ve](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmp2t6ve.md)** (blameless post-incident practice): the report is
  an instance, a no-blame investigation that keeps upstream provenance by
  naming contributing factors in the company and the state.
- **Elish ([LIT-2665](../literature.d/LIT-2665.md), [NOTE-2019](NOTE-2019.md))** wrote from the preliminary report,
  which left open that the operator was "looking down at the touch-screen";
  the final report rules that out.
- **[CLAIM-422](../claims.d/CLAIM-422.md)** (a corrective act, a procedure and a maintained capacity
  differ): the NTSB judges Uber's postcrash changes only to "begin to
  address" the deficiencies. A small instance; no relation written.

## Limitations

- Read for responsibility, not engineering.
- A safety finding, not an adjudication of blame or liability.

## From the reading of 2026-11-16

*Quotations here are carried from the reading of 2026-11-16, whose filer verified them against its copy; they were not rechecked at the reconciliation.*

- **What happened to the operator** (p. 27): "She was suspended after the
  crash and was laid off after ATG ceased Tempe operations in May 2018."
- **Why one operator** (p. 29): the move to a single operator was "an
  operational decision that allowed the company to increase the number of
  test vehicles"; Landsberg (pp. 62–63): "since everything was going so
  well and the tagging process was made easier, ATG decided that a
  dedicated observer was not needed."
- **Not excused** (p. 43): her distraction was "a clear example of
  deliberate engagement in visual distraction".
- **[CLAIM-708](../claims.d/CLAIM-708.md)**: a clean specimen of its structure in causal terms: the
  operator answers for her conduct, and the conditions-setter is found to
  have contributed to that conduct itself.
- **[CLAIM-707](../claims.d/CLAIM-707.md)**, its blame clause: the official account attributed
  contribution upstream, though its probable cause names the operator and
  she was suspended and laid off; and the operator had adequate time, which
  meets the claim's own exclusion.
- **[CLAIM-422](../claims.d/CLAIM-422.md)**: ATG had the policies and the cameras but not the
  maintained capacity that would make them work; an instance of procedure
  without maintained capacity.
- **[CLAIM-700](../claims.d/CLAIM-700.md)**: an analogue, not an instance. Apparent success was read as
  licence to remove a redundancy, but the success was the automation's, not
  members' compensation.
- **The exchange** (A17 §4) is accurate except that it calls the operator's
  distraction contributing when it was the probable cause, and omits the
  pedestrian's impaired crossing.
