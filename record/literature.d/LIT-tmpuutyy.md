# LIT-tmpuutyy: Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person

Harry G. Frankfurt, "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person",
*The Journal of Philosophy* 68(1), 14 January 1971, pp. 5–20 —
DOI-10.2307/2024717. Reprinted as chapter 2 of *The Importance of What We
Care About* (Cambridge University Press, 1988), pp. 11–25 ([LIT-tmpwnjgf](LIT-tmpwnjgf.md)),
and in Watson (ed.), *Free Will* (Oxford, 1982), whose pagination the SEP
uses.

## Key takeaways

- **Persons are defined by the structure of the will, not by mind and
  body.** Against Strawson's concept of a person (p. 5), "one essential
  difference between persons and other creatures is to be found in the
  structure of a person's will" (p. 6). Only humans appear able to form
  "second-order desires", desires to have or not have desires, which
  manifest "reflective self-evaluation" (pp. 6–7).
- **The apparatus (§§I–II).** The will is not every first-order desire but
  "an effective desire—one that moves (or will or would move) a person all
  the way to action" (p. 8). A physician who wants to feel an addict's
  craving without acting on it has a second-order desire that is not about
  his will (p. 9). Wanting a desire "to be his will" is a "second-order
  volition", and "it is having second-order volitions, and not having
  second-order desires generally, that I regard as essential to being a
  person" (p. 10). A wanton has first-order desires and no second-order
  volitions; "The essential characteristic of a wanton is that he does not
  care about his will" (p. 11). Wantons include animals, very young
  children "and perhaps … some adult human beings", and a wanton may
  reason well about means (p. 11).
- **Identification.** The unwilling addict "identifies himself, however,
  through the formation of a second-order volition, with one rather than
  with the other of his conflicting first-order desires. He makes one of
  them more truly his own and, in so doing, he withdraws himself from the
  other" (p. 13). Second-order volitions need not be moral, and "There is
  no essential restriction on the kind of basis, if any, upon which they
  are formed" (n. 6).
- **Freedom of the will (§III).** Freedom of action is "the freedom to do
  what one wants to do"; freedom of the will is, analogously, being free
  "to have the will he wants" (p. 15). "It is in securing the conformity of
  his will to his second-order volitions, then, that a person exercises
  freedom of the will" (p. 15). Conflict among second-order desires can
  leave a person "a helpless bystander to the forces that move him" (p.
  16). The series of higher orders can be ended "without cutting it off
  arbitrarily": when a person identifies "decisively", "without
  reservation or conflict", the commitment "'resounds' throughout the
  potentially endless array of higher orders", so that no further question
  "remains to be asked" (p. 16).
- **Responsibility (§IV).** "It is not true that a person is morally
  responsible for what he has done only if his will was free when he did
  it" (p. 18). One acts "of his own free will" if the will that moved him
  "was his will because it was the will he wanted", and then "it is quite
  irrelevant … to inquire whether the alternatives that he opted against
  were actually available to him" (p. 19, citing [LIT-tmpwts2i](LIT-tmpwts2i.md) in n. 9). The
  willing addict's will is not free, but he takes the drug "freely and of
  his own free will" and "may be morally responsible" (pp. 19–20). The
  conception is "neutral with regard to the problem of determinism" (p.
  20), and an addict made so "by the deliberate and calculated work of
  another" may be "fully responsible" along with the other (n. 10).

`Active` from a full reading ([NOTE-tmpnpe9o](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpnpe9o.md)). The record has built three
theories on this paper while holding it only through McKenna and Coates
([LIT-291](LIT-291.md)), Buss and Westlund ([LIT-296](LIT-296.md)), Dryden ([LIT-297](LIT-297.md)) and Robb ([LIT-294](LIT-294.md)).
The reading settles three of the disputes those readings left open:

- **Decisive identification is in 1971.** Supplement §A of [LIT-291](LIT-291.md) says
  identification "previously rested solely" on a higher-order volition,
  with the first answer to the regress in 1987. Dryden ([LIT-297](LIT-297.md)) and
  Velleman ([LIT-292](LIT-292.md)) cite the 1971 paper for a decisive commitment that
  resounds up the hierarchy. The paper bears them out (p. 16).
- **Freedom of the will and of action are distinguished** (pp. 14–15),
  which no revision of [LIT-291](LIT-291.md) states.
- **The bullet on manipulation is bitten in 1971.** [LIT-291](LIT-291.md) quotes
  Frankfurt accepting a manipulated character in 2002. Note 10 already
  says that an addict made so by another's "deliberate and calculated
  work" may be fully responsible. That is first-hand evidence for the
  first branch of [THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md)'s forced choice.

**Relations.**

- **`extends` [LIT-tmpwts2i](LIT-tmpwts2i.md).** The last section rests on the 1969 paper,
  cited in n. 9: responsibility does not require that "the alternatives
  that he opted against were actually available". The willing addict is
  this paper's version of an agent who could not have done otherwise and
  did what he "really wanted to do", which is the 1969 paper's exempting
  condition.
- **`extended_by` [LIT-293](LIT-293.md).** Frankfurt's Tanner Lectures, read in [NOTE-264](../notes.d/NOTE-264.md),
  restate this paper's definition, "we will freely when what we want is
  what we want to want" (p. 177), and add to it that there be "no conflict
  … between this motive and any desire at any higher order" (p. 178),
  wholeheartedness. They also restate the free will/free action
  distinction and the compatibilist conclusion (p. 178). The lectures do
  not cite the paper, but their account of free will is this one,
  extended.
- **`corrected_by` [LIT-295](LIT-295.md).** Velleman's "What Happens When Someone Acts?"
  (read, [NOTE-265](../notes.d/NOTE-265.md)) quotes this paper's identification passage (IWWCA 18,
  p. 13 here) and argues that a second-order desire cannot be what makes
  the agent participate, since the agent can be alienated from it as from
  a first-order one; he reports that Watson made the point and Frankfurt
  conceded it (pp. 112–113). That shows this paper wrong on a claim it
  makes: that forming a second-order volition is how a person "makes one
  of them more truly his own". Velleman also argues against decisive
  commitment, but from a 1976 footnote, not from p. 16 here. The record
  holds the general claim as [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md), still Proposed.
