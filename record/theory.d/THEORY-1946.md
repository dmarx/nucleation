# THEORY-1946: Rubel, Castro and Pham argue that agency laundering is a distinct wrong of accountability: an agent responsible for a domain makes a process, person or technology partly causally responsible for outcomes in it, ascribes it morally relevant qualities, and so obscures its own causal role and forestalls others' demands for an account, a wrong possible while the agent meets its substantive role responsibilities and one that ordinary algorithm use need not commit

## Source

Rubel, Castro and Pham (2021), ch. 7 of *Algorithms and Autonomy*, [LIT-2691](../literature.d/LIT-2691.md), read in full; the reading is [NOTE-2031](../notes.d/NOTE-2031.md). The 2019 article, [LIT-2659](../literature.d/LIT-2659.md), states the same account by its abstract and was not reached.

## What was actually shown

An analysis, not a finding. The definition (p. 143): a party (1) morally responsible for a domain (2) ensures that some process, person or entity has causal responsibility in it, (3) "ascribes (implicitly or explicitly) morally relevant qualities to b’s actions", (4) thereby obscures the scope of its own causal responsibility, and (5) "fails to adequately account for events within X". The defining wrong is "forestalling others’ ability to demand an account" (p. 143), and it does "not require that one infringe one’s substantive role responsibilities" (n. 21). Applied: Facebook, Uber's surge prompts and Houston's EVAAS launder; Uber's goal reminders and the Loomis court do not. Invoking a responsibility gap can itself launder (p. 157), and humans in the loop can launder "just as well as automation itself" (p. 160).

Corroborates [CLAIM-624](../claims.d/CLAIM-624.md) for its structure (a wrong of answerability beyond the substantive violation), from a source independent of the exchange that produced [CLAIM-624](../claims.d/CLAIM-624.md). Bears on [QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md): a firm's laundering does not need an individual who intends it.

## What this does not say

That automation launders by its nature, or that embedding values in a model is laundering. That anyone is blamed in the place of the launderer: the wrong is the forestalled account, not misplaced blame. It leaves open whether laundering can be inadvertent and whether social processes can launder.

## From the second reading (2026-11-17)

The second reading adds two relations, to claims that do not rest on it.
It corroborates [CLAIM-472](../claims.d/CLAIM-472.md): the account owed lies in "the decision to deploy
the system" (p. 157). It corroborates the upstream half of [CLAIM-707](../claims.d/CLAIM-707.md) only:
an agent responsible in a domain can obscure its causal role through a
process, a human in the loop among them, and escape the demand for an
account. It does not show anyone blamed in the launderer's place (above),
and laundering needs a misleading ascription and a failed account, where
the claim needs no mastermind.

- **That every interposed process launders**: two of the four cases fail
