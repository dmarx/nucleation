# NOTE-1659: Watson — Agency-customer interactions in condominial sewerage in seven Brazilian cities

## Contribution

A field survey of condominial sewerage, the Brazilian low-cost design in
which residents choose, block by block, among backyard, front-yard and
sidewalk feeder layouts, pay reduced tariffs and, in the design, keep the
block feeders, while agencies build and keep the trunk lines and
treatment. Across Petrolina, Natal, Brasília, Itapissuma, Recife,
Joinville and Cuiabá, it asks how agencies learn a different service
approach for the urban poor and how communities organise to work with
them. For the record it is direct evidence on what happens when support
for a coproduced service is partly withdrawn.

## Key insight

The agencies' retreat from the feeder network "created a void of service
provision" (p. 33). That void was filled not by residents' self-help but
by a renegotiated, more interactive agency role, prompted by residents'
complaints: "increased resident responsibility requires increased
interactian [sic] with agencies, not less" (p. 33). The service lasted
where the joint arrangement was kept and decayed where the agency stepped
back or refused it.

## Assumptions

- **Qualitative evidence.** Performance is judged from interviews, visits
  to more than 20 neighbourhoods and agency records; the author says it
  "is not a systematic study" of finance or performance (abstract).
- **Seven non-random cities**, no counterfactual and no health outcome.
- **The design's division of labour**: agencies on trunk lines and
  treatment, residents on feeders, the arrangement being tested.

## Key results

- **Cost and performance (abstract).** Conventional sewerage "costs three
  to four times more"; in about half the cases condominial projects
  "performed as well as conventional sewerage, but cost between a third to
  a quarter of those systems."
- **Consultation (abstract).** "Where community consultation was
  sufficient, connection rates of 95 to 98 percent were observed", against
  "less than 40 percent of the intended beneficiary population" where
  customers did not understand their systems.
- **Tariffs (p. 16).** Backyard customers keep their own feeder and pay a
  reduced tariff, 40 to 60 percent of the water bill; front-yard customers
  40 to 80 percent.
- **Devolution failed (p. 33).** Devolving feeder upkeep to residents "has
  not worked well even in the best cases."
- **The void and its filling (p. 33).** The arrangement "activated
  previously dormant neighborhood associations", giving "a dynamic of
  customer complaint and agency responses that ultimately improved agency
  performance."
- **Blocks (p. 34).** In Natal, "Only four percent of residents, however,
  knew who the representative was ten years later"; "blocks are not
  necessarily a natural form of association or cooperation in urban
  neighborhoods in Brazil." Turnover and house extensions follow
  servicing, because "housing values increase", and add to upkeep
  problems.
- **Self-clearing by layout (p. 35, Table 6).** In Petrolina, a sample of
  425 of 12,729 households: residents removing their own blockages, 59 %
  with conventional sewers, 25 % sidewalk, 63 % front yard, 85 % backyard.
- **Reversion (p. 35).** In Olinda's Ilha de Santana, when a poorly built
  system backed up "just months after construction", "nearly all
  residents rapidly reverted to individual sanitation solutions".
- **The one common clearing (p. 37).** A Recife association's campaign
  "was the only example of common blockage removal encountered" in the
  seven cities; when its funds ran out the lines refilled with sand.
  Associations "play a regulatory role".
- **Refusal (pp. 38–39).** Recife's state water company had not accepted
  80 percent of the city's condominial systems (Table 7). "Non-maintenance
  can be a form of passive sabotage. A receiving agency that has not been
  induded [sic] in the construction of (or the credit for) the new system
  has no stake in its success." In Cuiabá it worked: the municipality
  built no more condominial systems.
- **Good agency performance (p. 39).** Four elements: "staff continuity
  between the construction and operations phases", "a specialized
  condominial maintenance crew", face-to-face contact with residents, and
  "ongoing network monitoring and repairs and customer education".
- **Natal (pp. 40, 42).** Trained and mandated, the team "was able to run
  essentially on auto-pilot, innovating and perfecting its service
  approach"; rainwater connections to the system rose "from around four
  percent shortly after implantation, to nearly 20 percent today", a
  problem the team found and met with an education campaign. Built
  in 1980–82, the system was by 1988 so troubled that residents "demanded
  the whole system be removed", the cause a trunk line of "zero or
  negative slope" [OCR runs the words together]; after the repair the
  original pact was not restored.
- **Conclusion (pp. 46–47).** Good agency performance comes not from
  strengthening sector agencies "but from increasing their responsiveness
  to customers"; "where there has been little negotiation
  with customers, the condominial system has performed poorly." The
  failures argue for "continued agency involvement with feeder line
  maintenance", if only as technical backup.
- **Spread (p. 48).** Condominial systems were "a sixth of all sewerage
  construction in Brazil, and the majority of all new sewerage in squatter
  and low-income neighborhoods" over the decade.

## Concepts

- **Condominial sewerage**: block-level feeder lines under yards or
  sidewalks, joined to agency trunk lines, with the layout chosen by the
  block.
- **Trunk and feeder**: the division of labour between agency and
  residents.
- **Devolution of maintenance**; **third-party maintenance**.
- **Neighbourhood associations as regulators**: residents holding the
  agency to account by complaint.
- **Non-maintenance as passive sabotage**: a receiving agency that had no
  part in building a system letting it fail.
- **Staff continuity** from construction to operation.

## Connections

Evans (1996), [LIT-2143](../literature.d/LIT-2143.md), read in [NOTE-1665](NOTE-1665.md), says the Recife sewers
are Ostrom's (1996) case, [LIT-2140](../literature.d/LIT-2140.md); Watson acknowledges John Briscoe
and Judith Tendler as advisers. The failure of devolved upkeep and the
agencies' "passive sabotage" parallel the upkeep problem in Ostrom,
Gibson, Shivakumar and Andersson's Sida report, [THEORY-1480](../theory.d/THEORY-1480.md): here the
beneficiaries do contribute their own assets, yet upkeep still needs the
official side. The block groups lacked what [THEORY-149](../theory.d/THEORY-149.md) names for durable
self-governance, user-made rules and monitoring, and did not
self-organise; the associations that did act took a monitoring role.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md) (when transient support is safe to withdraw):
  qualifies.** Residents did part of the work throughout, yet withdrawing
  the agency's feeder support failed. Doing the work is not enough when
  the party's share depends on a complementary input, and when damage
  keeps coming from turnover and building. What became safe to withdraw
  was the founding team: Natal's crew ran "on auto-pilot".
- **[CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md) (ignition threshold, collapse, hysteresis): weak
  qualification.** No measured threshold. Olinda's reversion to individual
  solutions after backups is collapse after supply failure; Natal's kept
  arrangement after repair is lock-in of a division of labour, not the
  restored-supply failure the claim models.
- **[CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md) (support that does the work crowds out capacity):
  qualifies.** Support confined to what residents could not do, the trunk
  lines, did not leave a capable resident body: block upkeep rarely
  formed even with the agencies out of it. Where agencies took over feeder
  clearing (Natal), service improved without evident crowding out.
- **[CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md) (supplied upkeep never becomes optional): supports.** The
  agency's input, trunk upkeep, repair and education, was needed
  continually, and its withdrawal produced decay (Recife, Cuiabá).
- **[CLAIM-603](../claims.d/CLAIM-603.md) (one-way against reciprocal supply): supports.** The
  reciprocal arrangement, residents paying and complaining, agencies
  maintaining, was the stable form; one-way devolution was not.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md) (independence from the founding intervention):
  qualifies.** What became independent of its founders was Natal's
  maintenance team, not the service, which stayed dependent on the agency.
- **[THEORY-919](../theory.d/THEORY-919.md) (temporary protection): none.**
- Filed: [THEORY-1473](../theory.d/THEORY-1473.md). No instruction for machine-learning practice;
  nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- Seven non-random cities; qualitative evidence; no counterfactual and no
  measured health outcome; cost data thin by the author's own account.
- OCR text: words run together and some figures and tables are garbled;
  Figure 4's costs were not usable.
- Only the operation-and-maintenance part and the conclusion were read;
  the implementation chapter and the annexes were not, so the cost and
  performance figures are the abstract's.

## Open questions

- Does a second study of condominial or similar shared-maintenance
  sewerage find the same contrast between continued agency involvement and
  devolved upkeep? The PROSANEAR follow-up data might tell.
- Do the implementation chapter and annexes bear out the abstract's cost
  and performance figures city by city? Reading them would tell.
