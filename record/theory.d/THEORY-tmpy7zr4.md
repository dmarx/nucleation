# THEORY-tmpy7zr4: Emotion regulation is a set of processes distinct from emotion generation, by which people influence which emotions they have, when, and how they are experienced and expressed; regulatory acts can be sorted by the point in the generative sequence where they act, and acting before response tendencies form (reappraisal) has different consequences from acting after (suppression)

## Source

Gross (1998), [LIT-554](../literature.d/LIT-554.md) (*Review of General Psychology* 2(3), from p. 271),
read in full in [NOTE-429](../notes.d/NOTE-429.md), pp. 271–288, through the conclusions. The experiments it cites
(Gross 1998 JPSP; Gross and Levenson 1993, 1997) were not read and are
taken as the review states them.

## What was actually shown

**The definition (p. 275).** "Emotion regulation refers to the processes by
which individuals influence which emotions they have, when they have them,
and how they experience and express these emotions." It covers increase
and decrease, positive and negative emotion, conscious and automatic
processes, and only one's own emotions. It makes no assumption that
regulation is good or bad.

**Its place (p. 276, Fig. 2).** Affect regulation is the superordinate
category, over coping, emotion regulation, mood regulation and defense.

**The process model (pp. 281–285, Fig. 4).** Emotion is taken as a sequence:
situation, attention, meaning, response tendencies, response. Regulatory
acts are sorted by where they act: situation selection, situation
modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change (reappraisal
included) and response modulation (suppression of expression included).
The first four are antecedent-focused, the last response-focused (p. 282).

**The cited contrast (p. 285).** Reappraisal of negative films lowered
negative experience. Suppression lowered reported experience of some
emotions (pride, amusement) but not others (disgust, sadness), and raised
sympathetic responding. Rumination prolongs depressive symptoms and worry
prolongs anxiety (p. 284).

**What could have come out otherwise.** Strategies acting at different
points could have had the same effects. In the cited studies they did not.
The classification itself could not have come out otherwise; it is a
taxonomy, offered as "working distinctions".

## What this does not say

- **That regulation can be measured apart from generation.** The paper's
  first open challenge (pp. 285–286): inferring regulation needs a
  counterfactual unregulated response, so "thresholds for inferring emotion
  regulation should be high". It names Frijda as holding that regulation is
  "part and parcel of emotion" and sets that view aside without refuting it.
- **That the families differ in mechanism.** The model is "silent" on
  mechanisms, relations among strategies and their relative efficacy
  (p. 283). The boundaries are permeable, as the paper concedes.
- **That reappraisal is better in general.** The evidence in 1998 was a few
  laboratory film studies, and for disgust films physiology did not always
  fall under reappraisal (p. 285).
- **That dysregulation figures in over half of Axis I disorders.** The
  figure is asserted with three citations and no count (C5, weak).
- **That emotions have a true unregulated course.** The model assumes one,
  and the paper admits it is hard to know (p. 286).

## Connections

- **Rival.** [THEORY-056](THEORY-056.md) (Frijda, Ridderinkhof and Rietveld; Barrett) takes
  the side Gross set aside: most regulation is one motive state checking
  another, with no distinct regulating system. Both accounts predict the
  reappraisal and suppression contrast, so it cannot decide between them.
- Psychological inflexibility ([THEORY-050](THEORY-050.md)) treats experiential avoidance,
  close to situation selection and suppression here, as a transdiagnostic
  process. Rumination, worry, avoidance and suppression appear in this
  review as regulation strategies with costs.
- The appraisal account of emotion that the review's consensual model
