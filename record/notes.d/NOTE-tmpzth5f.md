# NOTE-tmpzth5f: Wolf (2007) — Meaning in Life and Why It Matters (Tanner Lectures I and II)

## Contribution

A hybrid account of meaningfulness as a value distinct from well-being and morality, with an argument that it needs a standard of value that is not radically subjective. Wolf disclaims practical guidance: what she says "will be of little or no practical use".

## Key results

1. Lecture I: "meaning arises when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness."
2. Lecture I, the activity condition: "mere passive recognition and a positive attitude toward an object's or activity's value are not sufficient for a meaningful life."
3. Lecture I, "The Fitting Fulfillment View Defended": "meaning in life consists in and arises from actively engaging in projects of worth."
4. Lecture I, Sisyphus Fulfilled: if the gods' drug has "reduced his intelligence and his imaginative capacity", he is worse off in at least one respect, however fulfilled he feels.
5. Lecture II, "Who's to say?": her answer is "no one in particular", with fallibilism and pooled judgement.
6. Lecture II: an adequate account of value's objectivity "is an unsolved problem in philosophy".
7. Lecture II: "we can recognize a paradox of meaningfulness, similar to but deeper than the paradox of hedonism."
8. Lecture II, a note: "many people, through no fault of their own, simply lack the opportunity for meaning".
9. Lecture II: "morality is no better suited to serve as an absolute standard for practical reason than self-interest."

## Assumptions

- Some value is not radically subjective, though no theory of it is given.
- Fulfillment has a cognitive component: seeing its object as good.
- The wish to see one's life as valuable from outside is pervasive, though not universal.

## Concepts

Reasons of love; meaningfulness as a third category of value; the Fulfillment, Larger-than-oneself, Bipartite and Fitting Fulfillment views; Sisyphus Fulfilled; the paradox of meaningfulness; a "wider moral berth" for meaning-giving projects.

## Bearing on the record

- **The exchange's use.** A9 made Wolf the synthesis's normative centre for a critique of technological civilization. The lectures say nothing about technology and disclaim practical use; the use is the exchange's. A9's "subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness" and its active-engagement condition are accurate (results 1–2).
- **The worry that a demand to live meaningfully defeats itself** (A9 §VI, A13 §IV), which the exchange took from "Moral Saints" ([LIT-tmphwzap](../literature.d/LIT-tmphwzap.md)), has its closer support here, in the paradox of meaningfulness (result 7): meaning pursued for one's own sake is self-undermining. "Moral Saints" was not reached.
- **[CLAIM-tmp6wuql](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp6wuql.md)**: leaves open. Result 4 counts a lost capacity as a loss even when felt as fulfillment, which agrees with the claim's premise that losing a capability is a loss whatever present satisfaction says; nothing here is about dynamics or support.
- **[CLAIM-352](../claims.d/CLAIM-352.md)**: result 8 states a lack of meaning as a lack of opportunity owed to circumstance, an opportunity-aspect idea in Sen's sense, without his vocabulary.
- **Neighbouring readings on meaning in life**, found at the recheck: Hiekel on Wolf's view ([LIT-183](../literature.d/LIT-183.md), Unavailable); Scheffler's Tanner Lectures, *The Afterlife* ([LIT-1779](../literature.d/LIT-1779.md)), on how projects keep their point; Fischer, "Stories and the Meaning of Life" ([LIT-689](../literature.d/LIT-689.md)); Browning and Veit ([LIT-116](../literature.d/LIT-116.md)). None states the fitting-fulfillment view.
