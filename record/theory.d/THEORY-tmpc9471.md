# THEORY-tmpc9471: Politics is more prone than markets to increasing returns, and so to path dependence, because it is dense with compulsory institutions and policies, rests on collective action and is opaque, so that institutions, collective action and shared interpretations of politics all feed back on themselves; and once on a path political actors leave it less readily than market actors, because competition and learning correct weakly, elected officials discount the future at the electoral cycle, and institutions are built to be hard to change

## Source

Pierson (1997), [LIT-1372](../literature.d/LIT-1372.md), read in full in [NOTE-tmp78852](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp78852.md) from the EUI
working paper that preceded the APSR article: Part III (pp. 23–36) for the
claim, Part IV (pp. 36–41) for its implications. Page numbers are the
working paper's. The 2000 article was not read and may state the list of
features differently.

## What was actually shown

Nothing was tested. The account is argued in two steps.

- **Prevalence.** Arthur's conditions for increasing returns (set-up
  costs, learning, coordination, adaptive expectations) are found in three
  features of politics: institutional density, collective action, and the
  complexity and opacity that make "mental maps" self-reinforcing.
  "Increasing Returns in fact characterize three of the most prominent
  features of political environments: processes of institutional
  development, processes of collective action, and processes of social
  interpretation" (p. 30).
- **Intensity.** The correctives that Liebowitz and Margolis rely on in
  markets are weaker in politics: competition and learning, long horizons
  supplied by firms and capital markets, and flexible authority within
  firms. Elected officials "employ a high discount rate" because voters'
  decisions "are taken in the short-run" (p. 32), so they pick paths with
  early payoffs and rarely leave one whose exit costs come now. Institutions
  are made sticky on purpose, to bind successors (Moe) or oneself (credible
  commitment).
- **What could have come out otherwise.** Only the illustration: the Clinton
  plan (1993–94) and the Republican Medicare and Medicaid cuts (1995–96)
  both failed against interests grown up around existing policy and against
  veto points (pp. 35–36). Two opposite reforms failing is what the account
  predicts; one succeeding would have counted against it. It was chosen
  after the event.

## What this does not say

- **Not that politics is path dependent everywhere.** "Not all aspects of
  political life are subject to increasing returns" (p. 41).
- **Not that persistence shows path dependence.** An arrangement that lasts
  because the interests behind it still hold is not locked in by early
  events. The account names mechanisms; it gives no test for telling the
  two apart in a case.
- **Not a claim about which actor governs.** Short horizons explain why
  paths are kept, not which component of a polity holds authority over
  another. That is [CLAIM-423](../claims.d/CLAIM-423.md)'s question, which Pierson names as open
  ("we know relatively little about the time horizons of different
  political actors", p. 32) and does not answer.
- **Not efficiency.** It says inferior paths are more likely and harder to
  leave in politics. It does not say any given political path is inferior,
  and it calls the remediability test too demanding for explanation.
