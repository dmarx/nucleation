# NOTE-tmp2yypk: Alía and Esteban — Weaning from mechanical ventilation

## Contribution

It sets out the randomized evidence of the 1990s on discontinuing
ventilation: who can stop at once, how readiness is tested, how long the
test should last, and how to withdraw support from those who fail it. Its
practical upshot is the protocol later codified by the 2007 consensus.

## Key insight

Readiness is best found by trying: "a direct method of assessing readiness
to maintain spontaneous breathing is simply to initiate a trial of
unassisted breathing" (introduction). Most patients pass and should be
extubated at once; tapering them only delays. Gradual withdrawal is for
the minority who fail, and even then the pace depends on how the method is
applied, not on the method's name.

## Assumptions

- **The cause has improved.** Any trial presupposes that the underlying
  cause of respiratory failure "has either improved or been resolved"
  (introduction).
- **Failure is an imbalance** "between the load faced by the respiratory
  muscles and their neuromuscular competence", and "Failure to wean is
  usually multifactorial" (patients failing the first attempt).
- **A failed trial costs little if watched**, asserted, not tested.
- **Support rests fatigued muscles**: "Resting the respiratory muscles with
  mechanical ventilation is the only method of treating muscle fatigue";
  diaphragm contractility stays depressed "for at least 24 h" after induced
  fatigue in healthy subjects (patients failing the first attempt).

## Key results

- **Abrupt withdrawal (introduction).** Ventilation "can be discontinued
  abruptly in approximately 75% of mechanically ventilated patients whose
  underlying cause of respiratory failure has either improved or been
  resolved."
- **Protocols (introduction).** In Ely's trial (n = 300), weaning came "2
  days earlier in the protocol-directed group".
- **After a passed trial (abstract; sustaining spontaneous breathing).**
  "immediate extubation after successful trials of spontaneous breathing
  expedites weaning and reduces the duration of mechanical ventilation as
  compared with a more gradual discontinuation of ventilatory support";
  "60-80% of mechanically ventilated patients can be successfully extubated
  after passing a trial of spontaneous breathing."
- **The trial (sustaining spontaneous breathing).** T-tube against
  pressure support of 7 cmH2O, still extubated at 48 h 63% and 70% (P =
  0.14); 30 and 120 min equivalent, with "reintubation rates of
  approximately 13% at 48 h".
- **Judging a trial (sustaining spontaneous breathing).** "strict criteria
  might increase the occurrence of unnecessarily prolonged mechanical
  ventilation but ... permissive criteria might increase the occurrence of
  reintubation."
- **Support is not rest by default (patients failing the first attempt).**
  "ventilator support should not be considered synonymous with respiratory
  rest"; poorly set, the patient's work may exceed that of unassisted
  breathing.
- **Brochard 1994 (n = 456).** 76% extubated after one 2-h T-piece trial; in
  the 24% who failed, pressure support gave shorter weaning than T-piece
  and SIMV combined, "5.7 ± 3.7 days versus 9.3 ± 8.2 days".
- **Esteban 1995 (n = 546).** 76% extubated on the first day; in the 130
  who failed, "weaning success rate was significantly better with once
  daily and multiple T-trials than with PSV and SIMV"; median weaning 5 days
  for SIMV, 4 for pressure support, 3 for T-piece.
- **What both trials share.** "the pace of weaning depends on the manner in
  which the technique is applied" and "SIMV is the least efficient
  technique of weaning".
- **Noninvasive ventilation (NIV section).** In Nava's COPD trial (n = 50),
  60-day weaning "88% versus 68%" and 60-day mortality "8% versus 28%".
- **Conclusion.** "Patients failing the initial spontaneous breathing
  trial need a gradual withdrawal of ventilatory support"; once-daily
  trials lead "to extubation twice as quickly as PSV" and allow "a
  prolonged period of rest".
- **The contradiction.** The abstract: "Two randomized studies found that,
  in difficult-to-wean patients, synchronized intermittent mandatory
  ventilation (SIMV) is the most effective method of weaning." The body says
  the reverse twice, and the conclusion: "It is known that SIMV is the most
  ineffective method of weaning those patients." The trials as summarized
  in the body support the body. A correction appeared in 2006
  (doi:10.1186/cc4996), not read; that it corrects the abstract is likely
  and unverified.

## Concepts

- **Trial of spontaneous breathing**: a timed period on T-tube or low
  pressure support, after which a patient who tolerates it is extubated.
- **Abrupt and gradual withdrawal**: extubation straight after a passed
  trial, against stepwise reduction of support (pressure support by 2–4
  cmH2O, SIMV by 2–4 breaths a minute) or intermittent trials with rest
  between.
- **SIMV**: mandatory breaths synchronized with the patient's own, reduced
  in number to wean.

## Connections

It summarizes the trials behind Boles et al.'s (2007), [LIT-tmp1tvad](../literature.d/LIT-tmp1tvad.md),
recommendations on initial weaning and on weaning after a failed trial:
Brochard 1994, Esteban 1995, Ely 1996 and the Spanish group's trials of
trial mode and duration. It does not restate the consensus' three groups or
its grading; and on modes after a failed trial it is more favourable to
T-tube trials than the consensus' abstract, which favours pressure support
or assist-control. Its doctrine of rest is the one that Jaber, Jung,
Matecki & Petrof (2011), [LIT-tmp0huzr](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0huzr.md) ([THEORY-tmp2eczh](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp2eczh.md)), say "has now been
completely turned on its head", though Alía and Esteban mean rest for a
fatigued muscle by assisted modes or nightly support, not fully controlled
ventilation throughout. Shah, Hart & Kaltsakas (2024), [LIT-tmpujjvl](../literature.d/LIT-tmpujjvl.md), carry
the later evidence.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md).** Supports the condition that the threat the support stood
  in for has gone: a resolved cause is the precondition of any trial.
  Qualifies the claim on schedule: where the party can do the work, abrupt
  removal beats a taper, and tapering only delays.
- **[CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md).** Partly contradicts it in this setting: support doing all
  the work is prescribed as rest to restore a fatigued capacity. It does not
  test whether support erodes capacity; with Jaber et al. it suggests the
  order effect depends on whether the capacity is fatigued or unused.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md).** The trial measures the effect of withdrawal at the
  present state. That 30 and 120 min trials are equivalent says the measure
  saturates quickly; the strict-versus-permissive tradeoff is the cost of
  reading it from a short window, in false failures and false passes.
- **[CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md), [CLAIM-558](../claims.d/CLAIM-558.md), [CLAIM-603](../claims.d/CLAIM-603.md), [CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md).** None directly.

## Limitations

- A narrative review by an interested trialist; the trials are from the
  1990s.
- The abstract's error on SIMV; the correction was not read.
- Brochard's and Esteban's trials disagree on pressure support against
  T-tube trials, which the authors put down to differing criteria.
- Fatigue as the mechanism of failure rests on small studies in COPD.

## Open questions

- Is rest by full support right for a fatigued diaphragm, given the disuse
  loss found later? A trial of rest regimes with diaphragm measures would
  tell.
- What does the 2006 correction change? Reading it would settle it.
