# ARG-tmpttrcb: Locke's Second Treatise carries the form of the claim that no delegation can confer what the delegator lacks, but grounds it in the absence of power over one's own life and admits forfeiture by fault, so it does not carry inalienable standing in general

## The inference

From Locke, Second Treatise §§6, 22–23, 135 and 168, to [CLAIM-tmp6vck6](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp6vck6.md):
inalienability is a limit on normative powers, not on exercise; a person's
standing cannot be removed by delegation, contract, collective decision or
success in imposing an arrangement.

## Critical questions (form: source)

- **Does the source say it?** In form, yes. §23 and §135 state the
  no-greater-power principle. §168 keeps the people's judgement of breach
  as one "they cannot part with". [THEORY-tmp50zaf](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp50zaf.md) corroborates.
- **On what ground?** The absence of power over one's own life, a
  theological premise (§6). The claim's ground is standing in general.
  Locke's text does not reach that far.
- **Is there an exception the claim omits?** Yes: forfeiture "by his fault"
  (§23). The claim should say whether standing can be forfeited, or that it
  departs from Locke here.
- **Do other readings bear on it?** Calabresi and Melamed's inalienability
  rests on efficiency and can be partial ([NOTE-tmpv28va](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpv28va.md)). Hohfeld's
  disability and immunity give the analysis without a ground. Neither
  supplies the claim's general ground. The dated notes on the claim record
  both.

## Verdict

The source supports the claim's form and not its scope. The claim needs a
ground of its own, or a narrower statement, and a word on forfeiture.
