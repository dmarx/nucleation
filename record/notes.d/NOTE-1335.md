# NOTE-1335: Redko et al. — CO-Optimal Transport

## Contribution

It defines an optimal transport problem between two matrices X ∈ ℝ^{n×d}
and X′ ∈ ℝ^{n′×d′} that need not share rows, columns or sizes. It fits a
sample coupling π^s and a feature coupling π^v together, under a cost that
compares the entry X_{i,k} with X′_{j,l}. It proves that the value is a
distance between datasets up to row and column permutations, that it lower-
bounds Gromov–Wasserstein (GW) on symmetric matrices with equality in the
squared-Euclidean case, and that its block coordinate descent then reduces
to the known Frank–Wolfe solver for GW. Experiments on heterogeneous domain
adaptation and co-clustering report the best accuracy among the baselines.

## Key insight

GW compares two sets only through their within-set similarity matrices,
so it can forget what the features were. COOT keeps the raw features and
pays for that with a second unknown: it must also decide which feature
corresponds to which. The invariances shrink accordingly. COOT is "not
invariant neither to feature rotations nor to the change of signs" (pp.
5–6), so it cannot confuse a configuration with its mirror image as GW on
distances can. But it is invariant to any simultaneous relabelling of rows
and columns that leaves a matrix unchanged. Those joint symmetries are
exactly what it cannot resolve.

## Assumptions

- **The problem (eq. 1, p. 2):** min over π^s ∈ Π(w, w′), π^v ∈ Π(v, v′) of
  Σ_{i,j,k,l} L(X_{i,k}, X′_{j,l}) π^s_{i,j} π^v_{k,l}, with L a divergence
  between scalars, w, w′, v, v′ histograms on the rows and columns. "when
  no additional information is available about the data, all the weights'
  vectors can be set as uniform" (p. 2).
- **Entry-wise comparability.** The cost compares X_{i,k} with X′_{j,l}
  directly, so entries of the two matrices must be on a common scale. That
  is a real assumption for response matrices from two populations.
- **Proposition 1 (p. 4):** L = |·|^p, p ≥ 1, n = n′, d = d′, uniform
  weights, for the identity of indiscernibles; the triangle inequality needs
  only that L satisfy one.
- **Propositions 2–3 (p. 5):** L = |·|², and for the equality, C and C′ are
  squared Euclidean distance matrices; COOT is then run with the same
  weights on rows and columns, COOT(C, C′, w, w′, w, w′).

## Key results

- **Bilinear program (pp. 3–4).** COOT is "an indefinite Bilinear Program
  (BP) problem", for which "there exists an optimal solution lying on
  extremal points" of the two polytopes. With equal sizes and uniform
  weights, Birkhoff's theorem gives an optimal pair of permutations. "Even
  though solving COOT exactly may be NP-hard" (p. 4), via the bilinear
  assignment problem.
- **Proposition 1 (p. 4; proof pp. 11–13).** Under its hypotheses
  "COOT(X, X′) = 0 iff there exists a permutation of the samples σ1 ∈ Sn
  and of the features σ2 ∈ Sd, s.t, ∀i, k Xi,k = X′σ1(i),σ2(k)". It is
  symmetric, and the triangle inequality is proved for general sizes and
  weights by gluing couplings.
- **Proposition 2 (p. 5; Lemma 1, p. 14).** For squared Euclidean C, C′, GW
  is a concave quadratic program with Hessian −4 XXᵀ ⊗_K X′X′ᵀ.
- **Proposition 3 (p. 5; proof p. 15).** For any symmetric C, C′,
  COOT(C, C′, w, w′, w, w′) ≤ GW(C, C′, w, w′). "The converse is also true
  under the hypothesis of Proposition 2. In this case, if (π∗s , π∗v ) is an
  optimal solution of (1), then both π∗s , π∗v are solutions of (3).
  Conversely, if π∗s is an optimal solution of (3), then (π∗s , π∗s ) is an
  optimal solution for (1)." The proof applies Konno's theorem relating a
  concave QP to its bilinear form (Theorem 1, p. 13). I checked equality on
  five random pairs of 5-point squared-distance matrices (exact, by
  enumerating permutation pairs) and found strict inequality in three of
  five pairs of random symmetric non-Euclidean matrices.
- **Algorithms (p. 4; pp. 15–16).** Block coordinate descent alternates two
  exact OT problems and "is guaranteed to decrease the loss after each update
  and so to converge within a finite number of iterations". In the
  squared-Euclidean case, setting π^s = π^v gives a DC algorithm that equals
  the Frank–Wolfe GW solver of [LIT-1813](../literature.d/LIT-1813.md), whose line search is always 1
  (Algorithms 3–5).
- **InvOT and election isomorphism (pp. 17–18).** Proposition 4: under
  cosine similarities and a whitened X′, InvOT, COOT and GW share optimal
  couplings. §C.5 shows COOT solves the election-isomorphism distance of
  Faliszewski et al. up to scaling.
- **Experiments (pp. 6–9; Tables 1–9).** Semi-supervised HDA, Decaf to
  GoogleNet, n_t = 3: mean accuracy 89.47 against SGW's 84.65 (Table 1).
  Unsupervised: 33.28 against CCA's 13.66, KCCA's 18.72 and EGW's 11.42
  (Table 2). At n_t = 5 COOT's lead over SGW narrows to 91.03 against 89.21
  and 94.53 against 94.26 (Tables 5, 6), and SGW wins several domain pairs.
  Co-clustering: lowest error on D1, D2 and D4, behind CCOT-GW on D3; the
  baselines' numbers are copied from another paper (Table 3).

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | With equal sizes, uniform weights and L = \|·\|^p, COOT is zero iff the matrices agree up to a row permutation and a column permutation | strong | Prop. 1, proof pp. 12–13; checked numerically |
| C2 | COOT satisfies the triangle inequality when L does, for any sizes and weights | strong | Prop. 1, gluing proof pp. 11–12 |
| C3 | When n ≠ n′ or d ≠ d′, COOT is strictly positive | wrong | asserted p. 4 and p. 13; false: a matrix with two equal rows against one copy of that row costs 0 |

| C5 | BCD decreases the objective monotonically and stops in finitely many steps | strong, but only to a block-wise optimum | p. 4, citing Horst and Tuy; in my check 12% of 200 random starts reached the global value |
| C6 | COOT gives better HDA accuracy than CCA, KCCA, EGW and SGW | moderate | Tables 1, 2, 5–7; 20 samples per class, 10 random draws; the margin over SGW shrinks as labels grow |
| C7 | COOT's feature coupling recovers a meaningful spatial transformation between MNIST and USPS pixels | weak | Fig. 1, qualitative; 67% of sample mappings within class |

## Method

Algorithm 1 (p. 4): start from the product couplings; then repeatedly solve
an OT problem for π^v with cost L(X, X′) ⊗ π^s, and one for π^s with cost
L(X, X′) ⊗ π^v. Each step is an exact OT problem (network simplex) or, in
the entropic version (eq. 2, p. 3), a Sinkhorn problem. For L = |·|² the
cost tensor product costs O((n + n′)dd′ + n′²n) by the factorization of
[LIT-1797](../literature.d/LIT-1797.md) (p. 4; §B.2, p. 13). The comments in Algorithm 1 are swapped:
the step that computes π^v is labelled "OT problem on the samples" and the
step that computes π^s "OT problem on the features" (p. 4).

## Concepts

- **Samples and features.** Rows and columns of a data matrix (p. 2). Each
  carries its own histogram.
- **Sample coupling π^s, feature coupling π^v.** Couplings of rows with rows
  and of columns with columns. No coupling ever pairs a row with a column.
- **Bilinear assignment problem (BAP).** The same problem with π^s, π^v
  restricted to permutation matrices (p. 4).

## Connections

- **Gromov–Wasserstein.** COOT contains GW as a lower bound and, for squared
  Euclidean inputs, as a special case (Prop. 3). The FGW solver of
  [LIT-1813](../literature.d/LIT-1813.md) is recovered as the symmetric BCD (pp. 15–16). The tensor
  factorization is [LIT-1797](../literature.d/LIT-1797.md)'s Proposition 1.
- **InvOT** (Alvarez-Melis, Jegelka and Jaakkola 2019, [LIT-1805](../literature.d/LIT-1805.md)): equal
  couplings under cosine similarity and whitening (Prop. 4, p. 17).
- **Co-clustering by OT** (Laclau et al. 2017): the CCOT and CCOT-GW
  baselines (Table 3).
- **Election isomorphism** (Faliszewski et al. 2019): a special case (§C.5).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-185](../cases.d/CASE-185.md), the correspondence arm: whether COOT's joint plans are
  identifiable.** No. The paper proves no uniqueness result for (π^s, π^v),
  and its Proposition 1 implies the limit. With R and R′ two utterance ×
  probe matrices of equal shape and uniform weights, every pair (σ1, σ2)
  with R′_{σ1(i),σ2(k)} = R_{i,k} is a zero-cost, optimal pair. Take any
  optimal pair and compose it with a joint symmetry of R′ (permutations P
  of utterances and Q of probes with R′ = P R′ Qᵀ). The result costs
  exactly the same, because the objective depends on the plans only
  through the entries they pair. Such a symmetry need not exist for P or
  Q alone. In my check, the matrix with rows e₁, e₂, e₃ and (½, ½, ½)
  compared with itself has six optimal pairs, each permuting rows and
  columns together. Swapping two utterances alone costs 1/3. A 4 × 4
  matrix with two communities of two utterances, each answering its own
  two probes, has 32 zero-cost pairs of 576. That is 4 within-block row
  swaps times 4 within-block column swaps times 2 for exchanging the
  communities' utterances and probes together. That last factor is the
  exchange's "utterances and probes can be swapped together at equal
  cost", precisely: a community's items and its probes move as a block.
  With noise of sd 0.05 the tie breaks, but the second-best pair costs
  only 5 × 10⁻⁴ more. So real data give near-ties, not uniqueness. The
  "Why this is here" wording, "utterances and probes can be swapped
  together", should not be read as a row being matched with a column.
  COOT never does that. The swap is a joint relabelling within each side.
  Two more limits bear on the arm. The global optimum is NP-hard in
  general (p. 4), and BCD finds block-wise optima: only 12% of my random
  starts reached the global value. And the entry-wise cost presupposes
  that the two communities' response scales are comparable, which is
  [CASE-055](../cases.d/CASE-055.md)'s measurement-invariance question.
- **What COOT does identify that GW does not.** Against GW on distances
  ([THEORY-1133](../theory.d/THEORY-1133.md)), COOT removes rotation and sign ambiguity ("contrary to
  GW, COOT is not invariant neither to feature rotations nor to the change
  of signs", pp. 5–6). Its residual ambiguity is the joint
  permutation-symmetry group of the raw matrix. That group is usually
  smaller than the isometry group of the derived distance matrix
  ([THEORY-1155](../theory.d/THEORY-1155.md)).
- **[CLAIM-257](../claims.d/CLAIM-257.md) (change of cover).** COOT does fit a probe correspondence
  rather than assume it, as the "Why this is here" says. But π^v is a
  coupling with fixed marginals v, v′. Every probe on each side must send
  and receive its full mass, so a question one side cannot decide cannot be
  left unmatched. A change of cover would be absorbed into the coupling,
  not reported separately as the claim asks. The paper's note that "this
  procedure can be easily adapted ... to include unbalanced OT problems"
  (p. 5) points to the needed variant, which it does not develop.
- **[CLAIM-098](../claims.d/CLAIM-098.md).** The CASE's outcome "anchored fused GW or co-optimal
  transport beating orthogonal maps" fits COOT's structure. COOT's maps are
  couplings, neither linear nor invertible in general (n ≠ n′).
- **[TERM-092](../terms.d/TERM-092.md).** The "Why this is here" calls COOT [TERM-092](../terms.d/TERM-092.md)'s frontier "in
  fittable form". Nothing in the paper bears on that reading, which is the
  exchange's.
- **Instruction for ML practice.** The HDA and co-clustering results imply
  method recommendations; that part belongs to the Anthology of the SOTA,
  and the LIT carries `anthology-candidate`.

## Limitations

- **No uniqueness or stability result** for the optimal couplings. The
  paper's identifiability statement is only the zero case of Prop. 1.
- **Proposition 1's remark on unequal sizes is false.** "If n ≠ n′, d ≠ d′
  the COOT cost is always strictly positive as there exists a strictly
  positive element outside the diagonal" (p. 13; also p. 4). Duplicated rows
  give zero. X = (1 2; 1 2) with uniform w and X′ = (1 2) has the
  admissible plan π^s = (½, ½)ᵀ, π^v = I/2 with cost 0, which I computed.
  COOT is therefore a distance only up to such replications, not between
  datasets in general.
- **Exact computation is NP-hard in general**, and the BCD gives no global
  guarantee. The supplement's §E reports variance over random
  initializations in the co-clustering task (p. 18).
- **Small experiments.** HDA uses 20 samples per class and 10 random draws.
  The co-clustering baselines are quoted from another paper, so the
  conditions may differ.
- **Proposition 3's equality needs squared Euclidean matrices.** For other
  symmetric matrices it is a strict bound in general. In three of five of
  my random trials the exact COOT value was below the best GW value found
  over the coupling polytope by 40 restarts.

## Open questions

- Under what conditions on R, R′ (for example, no nontrivial joint
  permutation symmetry and a gap condition) is the COOT plan unique and
  stable under noise? A perturbation bound would answer it.
- Does an unbalanced COOT leave unmatched exactly the probes that only one
  side can decide? That would make it a measurement of [CLAIM-257](../claims.d/CLAIM-257.md)'s change of
  cover.
