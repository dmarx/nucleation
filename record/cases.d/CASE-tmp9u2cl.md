# CASE-tmp9u2cl: Two identical unnumbered ballots in one tally: two votes that no relation in the structure of the vote tells apart

Filed on 2026-10-10 from a search the owner asked for. The search found no
documented case of two communicative relata that no relation differentiates,
even weakly, and counted as two. Ordinary candidates fail for one reason.
Identical utterances in unison are made by different speakers, and a relation
such as "speaks in unison with", holding only between distinct performers, is
irreflexive and so discerns them weakly. That is Leitgeb and Ladyman's graph
with an edge, not the edgeless one ([LIT-154](../literature.d/LIT-154.md), read in [NOTE-133](../notes.d/NOTE-133.md)).

**This case is constructed.** What rests on a documented source is the
institution's norm: identifying marks void a ballot. The papers, the voters
and the marks are stipulated. It is built to test which version of [CLAIM-136](../claims.d/CLAIM-136.md)
the owner holds.

## The case

An election uses identical, unnumbered ballot papers. Two voters each mark a
single cross for candidate C, and both papers go into one box. At the count
both are valid, both are for C, and both are tallied. The result records two
votes for C, and the returning officer and the candidates' agents count them
as two.

Take as the structure the relations that make a paper a vote in this
election: it is an official paper of this election, deposited in this box,
marked for C, valid, counted toward C. In that structure each ballot bears the
same relations to everything else, the election, the box, C and the tally, and
no relation to the other ballot. Formally the structure is a star. The ballots
b₁ and b₂ are leaves joined only to shared nodes. They have the same atomic
relations to every element, and to each other, as each has to itself, which
is none. So merging them is a strong homomorphism, and every identity-free
formula φ gives φ(b₁, b₂) ↔ φ(b₁, b₁). No irreflexive relation holds between
them: they are not even weakly discernible. This is Leitgeb and Ladyman's
edgeless two-node graph ([LIT-154](../literature.d/LIT-154.md)), realized by an institution.

**The documented anchor.** The institution strips the relation that would
discern the papers. UK Representation of the People Act 1983, Sch. 1,
r. 47(1)(c): any ballot paper "on which anything is written or marked by which
the voter can be identified except the printed number on the back … shall …
be void and not counted." The exception matters. UK papers carry a printed
number, and a sealed record links it to the voter, so UK ballots are
discernible and the UK is not the case. That is why the case is stipulated
with unnumbered papers. Which jurisdictions use unnumbered papers was not
checked.

## What it can show

It forces the choice between [CLAIM-136](../claims.d/CLAIM-136.md)'s two versions, which the claim says
the owner has not made.

- **What the strict version must say.** A relatum exists as an individual only
  in so far as its relations differentiate it. The ballots are counted as two,
  so the strict reading must find a relation that tells them apart. None lies
  inside the structure that makes a paper a vote, so it must go outside it: to
  the causal history of each paper (who marked it), or to its position in the
  box. That is [CLAIM-138](../claims.d/CLAIM-138.md)'s cost. Identity is then fixed by relations specified
  independently of the act's constitutive structure, and r. 47 shows that the
  institution treats those relations as no part of the vote, since a mark
  that discerns the paper voids it. The alternative for the strict reading is
  to count the votes as one, which the tally refutes.
- **What the weak version must say.** The ballots are two, and nothing
  intrinsic grounds the plurality. That is Esfeld and Lam's move, "one can
  simply accept a numerical distinction (diversity) … as primitive" ([LIT-895](../literature.d/LIT-895.md),
  read in [NOTE-695](../notes.d/NOTE-695.md), p. 33), and Leitgeb and Ladyman's edgeless graph. It
  concedes what [CLAIM-134](../claims.d/CLAIM-134.md) says the weak version concedes, a non-relational
  ground of numerical identity.

So the case does not refute [CLAIM-136](../claims.d/CLAIM-136.md). It makes its dilemma concrete, on an
example in which the institution, not the theorist, removes the
differentiating relations.

**For [CLAIM-138](../claims.d/CLAIM-138.md).** The strict version's only way out is the remedy [CLAIM-138](../claims.d/CLAIM-138.md)
says is costly: relations picked out from outside the structure. Here those
relations are causal and spatial, and the institution says they are not part
of what the vote is. So the case shows the cost of that remedy in a setting
where the act's own constitutive structure is fixed by rule.

## What it cannot show

**That a non-institutional communicative act has undifferentiated tokens.** A
vote is an institutional act and an expressive one. Whether it is a
"communicative relatum" in [CLAIM-136](../claims.d/CLAIM-136.md)'s sense is arguable.

**The strongest reply.** A vote is a status assigned to a physical token, and
the tokens are discerned physically, by position, by paper fibre, by who
marked them. Secrecy hides the relation "cast by"; it does not abolish it. On
this reply the strict version needs no help from outside the act, because
the act includes its token. The case answers only if the structure of the
vote is taken to be the rule-given relations. Which structure counts is the
point at issue, and that is [CLAIM-138](../claims.d/CLAIM-138.md)'s question again.

**That it happened.** It is stipulated, and not documented as an episode. Two
identical valid ballots for one candidate in one count are commonplace, but
no particular count was examined.

**What would close the gap.** A documented count under an unnumbered-ballot
system, with its rules on identifying marks, would make the anchor exact. A
judgement test could ask competent officials whether two such ballots are
"two votes" and whether anything about the vote, as opposed to the paper,
tells them apart. That would show which structure participants take the act
to have.
