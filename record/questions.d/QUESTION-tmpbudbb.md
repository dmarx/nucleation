# QUESTION-tmpbudbb: When fans of one club perceive their rivalry with another as reciprocated, do the rival's fans in fact return it, and do the identity benefits of rivalry depend on the perception or on the fact?

## Why it is a question

In Berendt and Uhrich's survey of 571 Bundesliga supporters, the benefits
of a rivalry flow through the belief that the rival returns the
antagonism ([THEORY-tmpk1fow](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpk1fow.md)). Tyler and Cobbs ([LIT-tmp1tphp](../literature.d/LIT-tmp1tphp.md)) document
one-sided rivalries, where A's fans name B but B's do not name A. So the
belief can be false.

[CASE-098](../cases.d/CASE-098.md) is about the rivalry as a relation that outlives its carriers.
If identity benefits track the perception, the rivalry's persistence can
be carried by each side's beliefs about the other. If they track the
fact, the relation itself does work.

## What would count as an answer

Paired surveys of fans of both clubs in a set of rivalries, one-sided and
mutual. The measures:

- each side's rivalry intensity;
- each side's perceived reciprocity;
- each side's distinctiveness, cohesion and regard.

The answer is whether one side's outcomes depend on the other side's
*actual* intensity once its own perceived reciprocity is held fixed.
Kilduff et al.'s relational account of rivalry ([LIT-tmpl8672](../literature.d/LIT-tmpl8672.md)) predicts
that the relation matters.

## Partial evidence, 2026-10-29

Kilduff et al. (NOTE-tmpiffjj) measured rivalry from both sides for 389
pairs of NCAA basketball teams, from student sportswriters validated
against players and coaches. Rivalry was largely reciprocated: r = .64
raw, .85 with each team's general tendencies removed. Where it was one
sided, lower-status teams felt it toward higher-status ones. That is
teams, not fans, so the question stays open. It does predict where fans'
perceived reciprocity would be wrong if fans follow their teams: at clubs
of lower status, toward rivals above them.
