# LIT-119: Conspiracy Theories and Public Trust

Brian L. Keeley (2023), *In D. Collins, I. Vidmar Jovanović and M. Alfano (eds.), The Moral Psychology of Trust, Lexington Books (Rowman & Littlefield), pp. 197–214* — DOI-10.5040/9781978734494.ch-011

## Key takeaways

- Public Trust Skepticism — discounting conspiracy theories because they erode trust in institutions — gets things backwards: for an important class of theories it is the sweeping distrust they presuppose that undermines their own credibility, by leading to "epistemic nihilism".

*Seeded from the abstract alone, not a reading. What follows is what the work says about itself.*

Keeley asks how belief in, or engagement with, conspiracy theories relates to trust, and whether it erodes trust in others and especially in public institutions. He targets "Public Trust Skepticism", the thesis that conspiracy theories deserve less credibility because they undermine citizens' trust in democratic institutions. He argues that for an important class of conspiracy theories the reverse holds: the broad distrust of public sources that such theories presuppose is what undermines them, because it makes much of what their proponents would otherwise believe incoherent — a condition he calls epistemic nihilism.
