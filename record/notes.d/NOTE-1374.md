# NOTE-1374: Friedkin — A formal theory of reflected appraisals in the evolution of power

## Contribution

It turns the looking-glass self into a rule for updating an influence
network. Cooley's and Mead's premise, that self-appraisal follows others'
appraisals, is formalized as one equation: a member's openness to
influence on the next issue reflects how much of the last issue's outcome
was theirs. Put in the Friedkin–Johnsen model of a single issue, the rule
drives almost every group to a single leader. The paper calls this a near
"iron law", gives a laboratory test of the rule (not of the end state),
and offers it as a micro-level route to Michels's oligarchy that needs no
bureaucracy.

## Key insight

If those whose positions carry become more closed and those whose
positions do not become more open, the inequality compounds across
issues. The leader's control in the end runs through the network of the
others, who stay connected to each other and fully open: the end state
is not everyone deferring to one person directly (form (a)) but one
self-weight of 1 with an accommodative mass whose interpersonal weights
carry it on (form (b)).

## Assumptions

- **Influence network.** Each member i allots weights w_ij ≥ 0, Σ_j w_ij
  = 1; w_ii is the self-weight. W_s = A_s C + I − A_s (9), with C the
  relative interpersonal weights c_ij = w_ij/(1 − w_ii), fixed across
  issues, and A_s = diag(1 − w_sii).
- **One issue (Friedkin–Johnsen).** y(t+1) = AWy(t) + (I − A)y(1) (2):
  an ongoing anchorage on initial positions. At equilibrium y(∞) =
  Vy(1), with V = (I − AW)⁻¹(I − A) (6) when I − AW is nonsingular.
- **Reflected appraisal across issues.** w_(s+1)ii = (1/n) Σ_j v_sji
  (10): the self-weight becomes the member's share of all total influence
  in V_s. Appraisals are assumed accurate, "like the assumptions of
  perfect information and rational action", and members are assumed
  keenly interested in the group's outcomes.
- **Scope.** W_1 strongly connected, with an equilibrium V_1 that shows
  some inequality; one issue domain; fixed membership and fixed C.

Note on (2) and (9) together: since W already holds I − A on its
diagonal, AW gives the self-weight a second role, so a member's own
initial position enters both through the anchorage and through their own
diagonal weight. This is the specification the paper states; it is not
the DeGroot step that [LIT-1146](../literature.d/LIT-1146.md) analyses.

## Key results

- **Experiment.** 50 tetrads (25 all-female, 25 all-male), each through
  five issues (three risky-shift, two monetary awards), in five
  communication networks, by telephone, with self-weights derived from
  observed position changes by (12). Multilevel regression of the next
  self-weight on prior control and prior self-weight, 800 cases (50
  groups × 4 members × 4 transitions): control on prior issue .199 (SE
  .079, p = .011); self-weight on prior issue −.124 (SE .054, p = .021);
  constant .198 (SE .021). Prior control raises the next self-weight;
  extreme self-weights regress.
- **Simulation (Table 2).** 10,000 random systems for each of two types
  and five sizes (5–25): Type 1 complete, Type 2 with random densities
  and arrangements of zero weights. The share that evolves to a single
  leader is 0.9996–1.0000 for Type 1 and 0.9900–0.9987 for Type 2. The
  rest end bilateral, w_kk + w_mm = 1, and in those one member holds on
  average 0.70–1.00 of the control (panel c). The member with the most
  initial control becomes the leader in about 0.56 of five-person groups and
  0.22–0.26 of 25-person ones (panel b).
- **Not established.** The end state itself is shown only in simulation;
  the experiment tests one step of the rule, over five issues.

## Method

1. Fix C and an initial self-weight vector, giving W_1.
2. For issue s, form W_s from (9) and compute V_s from (6).
3. Set each member's next self-weight to the column mean of V_s, (10).
4. Repeat across issues; report the end state.

The behavioural self-weight (12) inverts the model on each member's
observed movement, so that the experiment can be scored.

## Concepts

- **Self-weight** (w_ii): openness or closure to influence on an issue;
  "a social construction, as opposed to fixed personality
  characteristics".
- **Control matrix** V: the total direct and indirect contribution of each
  member's initial position to each member's settled one.
- **Reflected appraisal**: as Eq. (10), the self-weight set to one's
  appraised share of control.
- **Self-appropriation**: Cooley's premise, as Friedkin reads it, that a
  member accorded influence keeps it rather than passing it on.

## Connections

The DeGroot–Friedkin model of Jia, MirTabatabaei, Friedkin and Bullo
([LIT-1146](../literature.d/LIT-1146.md), [NOTE-1135](NOTE-1135.md), [THEORY-916](../theory.d/THEORY-916.md)) is the same reflected-appraisal rule on
a different single-issue step: DeGroot averaging to consensus, so that
control is the dominant left eigenvector of W, with no anchorage on
initial positions. Its result is not this one. Under it, self-weights go
from every non-autocratic start to one equilibrium ordered by
eigenvector centrality, autocratic exactly for a star. The re-run below
puts the two side by side. The paper cites French (1956), Harary (1959)
and DeGroot (1974) as the single-issue tradition, Michels and Mayhew and
Levinger for oligarchy, and Cooley (*Human Nature and the Social Order*,
[LIT-1379](../literature.d/LIT-1379.md)), Mead and Gecas and Schwalbe for reflected appraisal.

## Bearing on the record

- **The re-run.** Rule (10) with (6) and (9) as printed, on 300 random
  complete networks each of sizes 5, 10 and 20 (scratch script,
  2026-10-29): every one ended with one self-weight above 0.99 and the
  rest below 0.01, which matches Table 2's Type 1. On 300 random
  strongly connected networks of size 10 and density 0.4, 93% ended with
  a single leader and the rest bilateral, a larger bilateral share than
  Table 2's Type 2 (under 1%). The Type 2 densities were random, so the
  two are not the same population. On 100 random complete networks of
  sizes 5 and 10, the DeGroot–Friedkin update gave a median largest
  self-weight of 0.29 and 0.13, and never above 0.40, where this
  paper's update gave 1.0. **The concentration depends on the
  single-issue model.** With Friedkin–Johnsen anchorage, as here, power
  concentrates almost always; with DeGroot consensus, as in [LIT-1146](../literature.d/LIT-1146.md), it
  does not. Filed as [THEORY-1161](../theory.d/THEORY-1161.md) (Proposed).
- **[CASE-092](../cases.d/CASE-092.md)'s note of 2026-10-23** says, from the abstract, that
  reflected appraisal "generates transitions of the influence network

  baseline. The reading says they are two baselines with different

  sense in which a person takes up what others take them to be: the
  self-weight is set by "appraisals of others" of one's control,
  "assumed accurate". It formalizes the held and appropriated senses

  assumes they do.

## Limitations

- The experiment tests one step of the rule over five issues in
  four-person groups; the iron law is the simulation's.
- Appraisals are assumed accurate and C fixed; the paper says other
  specifications "are possible" and gives none.
- Footnote 6 says the construct gives 0 ≤ w_ii < 1, which "excludes the
  extreme case of a completely self-weighted individual", yet the end
  state is "a single completely self-weighted, inner-directed
  individual". The end state is a limit the construct approaches and
  does not reach; the paper does not say so.
- **Errors and slips.** The reference to Krackhardt (1990) gives
  *Administrative Science Quarterly* "50: 505–535"; the 1990 volume of
  ASQ is 35, and Crossref gives the paper as 35, first page 342
  (doi:10.2307/2393394, checked 2026-10-29). "what has been referred to
  as a ''becalming' (Zald and Ash, 1966)" lacks a closing quotation
  mark. "a concentrated form of power exits or not" for "exists". In the
  extracted text two displays carry the number (8), the vector of
  settled positions and the matrix C.

## Open questions

- Which single-issue step do real groups follow, anchored or consensual?
  The two predict opposite end states under the same reflected
  appraisal, and the paper's experiment, scored by the anchored model,
  cannot tell them apart.
