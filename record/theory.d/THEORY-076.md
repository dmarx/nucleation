# THEORY-076: Diachronic self-governance is constituted by cross-temporal links of intention that are intrapersonal analogues of shared intention, not by narrative unity and not by intertemporal bargaining

## Which unity this is about

Of the four unities the record keeps apart ([ADR-024](../decisions.d/ADR-024.md)), this account is about
**self-governance**: what makes an agent's conduct over time governed by
her, and not merely managed. It says something about the other two only by
denying that they constitute it. Intertemporal conflict and its resolution
belong to `behavioral-integration`, and the bargaining account is an
account of that. A life's unity as a story belongs to `narrative-unity`.
The claim is that neither is what makes agency over time self-governed. It
is not a claim that either does not exist.

## Source

- Bratman (2018), [LIT-567](../literature.d/LIT-567.md), read in [NOTE-464](../notes.d/NOTE-464.md) in the author's draft
  of 10 July 2017. Page references are to that draft.

## What was actually shown

**Self-management is not self-governance.** Diachronic self-governance needs
synchronic self-governance at the relevant times, guidance by a standpoint
"sufficiently coherent to constitute a clear place where the agent stands"
(p. 2), but it is "not merely a concatenation" of it (p. 3). Ulysses at the mast
manages himself over time "thereby blocking his self-governance when the
sirens call" (p. 2). Sartre's young man, shuffling between options he
judges non-comparable, is self-governing at each time and still less
self-governing over time (§§2, 7). So something links the times.

**The link is the structure of acting together.** Bratman's conditions for
shared intention are (A) interlocking intentions, (B) intended mutual
responsiveness and meshing sub-plans, (C) correct belief in persistence
interdependence, (D) expectation of success and (E) openness (pp. 8–9). In
planned activity, my intention at t1 to A at t2 is meant to work through my
later intention, my sub-plans then are meant to mesh with those I formed
earlier, and so on. These are "intra-personal analogues", so that the agent
at each time "is acting 'together' with ... herself -- at other times"
(p. 9). He grants that the analogy "will at most only be partial" (p. 10):
there is no two-way causation across time, and condition (C) holds only in
some cases ([NOTE-464](../notes.d/NOTE-464.md), C3).

**Willpower without a bargain.** In temptation, a prior resolution and a
present evaluation cannot by themselves say why the resolution speaks for
the agent. Bratman adds a third element, a "conditional, reflexive end" of
one's own diachronic self-governance in the standpoint, which is not a
taste for "mere cross-temporal psychic tidiness" (§§6–7). The plan's
time-frame runs to "plan's end" and includes later reflection. So sticking
with a resolution is supported because one anticipates no regret at the end,
and drinking Kavka's toxin is excluded because one anticipates regret
(§8; [NOTE-464](../notes.d/NOTE-464.md), C4–C5).

**Against the two alternatives.**

- *Bargaining.* McClennen's "fair bargain between one's different,
  time-defined selves", which the essay groups with Ainslie's picoeconomics,
  "seems to involve a kind of reciprocal inter-relation that is not possible
  here", since "the person-stage at t1 will not be around" to respond when
  the later stage acts (p. 8). That is the whole argument ([NOTE-464](../notes.d/NOTE-464.md),
  C6).
- *Narrative.* A "story" that ties a life together "may apply to many
  cases. But according to the shared agency model, the more basic
  cross-temporal unity at work in diachronic self-governance is the unity
  involved when the agent is acting 'together' with herself" (p. 9). That is
  the whole treatment.

So the positive model is argued at length and by cases, and the two denials
in the title are asserted. The account is the record's statement of the
positive model, with the denials kept at the strength the essay gives them.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say the analogy is exact.** Bratman says it is partial. The
  account claims only that the analogues the essay names are what constitute
  the cross-temporal link, not that a person over time is a group.
- **It does not say narrative unity is unreal or unimportant.** Only that it
  is not what makes agency over time self-governed. McAdams's narrative
  identity ([THEORY-060](THEORY-060.md)) is a claim about a different unity, and the two
  can both be true.
- **It does not refute the bargaining account as an account of
  self-control.** Bratman's objection is that a bargain needs reciprocity.
  Ainslie's interests are successive and do not respond to each other
  either; his "bargain" is a repeated game played through self-prediction.
  Whether reciprocity is needed for that is not argued. The two accounts are
  rivals over what makes resolve *self-governed*, and over what carries it
  in practice they make different predictions (see `promote_when`).
- **It does not show that agents have the reflexive end.** It is posited by
  inference to the best explanation, and other ends can "trump" it when it
  is not "sufficiently important" to the agent (p. 22).
- **It does not settle manipulation.** The model is wholly structural. It has
  no condition on how the plans were acquired ([THEORY-040](THEORY-040.md)).

## Connections

- **Ainslie's picoeconomics,** [THEORY-067](THEORY-067.md), declared this theory's
  rival: a theory that self-control over time needs no faculty of will, because
  interests dominating at different delays bargain and resolve is the
  recursive staking of future choices on the present one. It rests on the
  précis [LIT-586](../literature.d/LIT-586.md), which itself rivals this essay in the LIT scheme. The
  two are rival accounts of the same phenomenon, resolve against a
  temptation one foresaw. On Ainslie's account resolve is credibility in an
  intertemporal game. On this one it is acting together with oneself, backed
  by an end of self-governance. The précis returns the challenge: it
  suggests Bratman's "resolute choice" "may turn out to be another name for
  intertemporal bargaining" ([NOTE-443](../notes.d/NOTE-443.md)). Neither tests the other. The
  `rivals` relation should be declared once that theory is complete.
- **[THEORY-029](THEORY-029.md)** (the regress). Bratman's stopping point is structural: a
  coherent standpoint plus a reflexive end. Because the end can be
  outweighed, it is one weight among others and not a guarantor of
  ownership, which fits [THEORY-029](THEORY-029.md)'s claim. Whether the end can be specified
  without presupposing the self-governance it is an end of is the question
  [THEORY-029](THEORY-029.md) asks, and this essay does not ask it.
- **Korsgaard's normative constitution ([THEORY-065](THEORY-065.md)).** Korsgaard reads
  Bratman's Lockean claims as saying that "by conforming to the norms
  consequent upon intention, we constitute our identities" ([LIT-587](../literature.d/LIT-587.md),
  ms. p. 23). On that reading the interlocking intentions here constitute
  self-governance *because* of the norms of consistency and means–end
  coherence they commit the agent to, and the two accounts agree. They part
  where Korsgaard reports that Bratman counts a post-hypnotically installed
  intention as the agent's own (ms. p. 21), and where she holds that being
  active needs conformity to the categorical imperative, which nothing here
  requires. That is a disagreement about agency at a time, not about the
  link across times, so no `rivals` is declared.
- **The empirical psychology of self-control** (the behavioral-integration
  holdings) can tell this account from the bargaining one only through a
  test like the one in `promote_when`. The record holds none.
