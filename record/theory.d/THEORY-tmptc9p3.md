# THEORY-tmptc9p3: Treating openness in science as the unlimited sharing of research objects, with transparency ranked before reproducibility and inclusion, can narrow the epistemic diversity of research and entrench epistemic injustice, and this lowers the reliability of what is learned, so sharing is neither a necessary starting point nor a sufficient condition for reliable open science

## Source

Leonelli (2023), [LIT-189](../literature.d/LIT-189.md) (*Philosophy of Open Science*, Cambridge Elements
in the Philosophy of Science), read in full in [NOTE-098](../notes.d/NOTE-098.md). Page references
are the Element's printed pages.

## What was actually shown

**The sharing view (§2, Table 1, Figure 1).** Mainstream institutional
Open Science reads openness as sharing that is unlimited, digital, good,
global and equal, with a value ordering transparency → quality
(reproducibility) → inclusion. The EC 2016 definition is the exemplar. The
author calls her reading "opinionated and … by no means comprehensive".

**Four cases (§3).**
- GISAID: access conditions (a user agreement, credit, limits on linkage)
  protected low-resourced producers and drew wide participation, 240
  countries and 15 million sequences by February 2023 (p. 21). Calls for
  fully open data rank transparency over inclusion.
- Software: in a Global Young Academy survey in Bangladesh, Ghana and
  Tanzania, researchers preferred expensive proprietary software, fearing
  reviewers would read free software as low quality (p. 23).
- Crop data: open linkage favours decontextualised genomic data over
  phenotypic and local knowledge, and can become "yet another form of
  bioprospecting" (p. 26).
- Reproducibility: taking controlled experiments as the universal model
  damages exploratory, field-based and qualitative research (pp. 26–28).

**The mechanism (§§4–5).** Systems of practice are local, entrenched in
repertoires, more or less permeable, and "systems of demarcation and
exclusion" (p. 35). Epistemic injustice is "typically, inversely
correlated" with epistemic diversity (p. 39). The sharing view rests on an
object-oriented epistemology in which outputs are self-standing
commodities. But "the further a research object travels from its context
of origin, the more difficult it becomes to assess" whether it fits a new
use (p. 46). Hence sharing is "neither a necessary starting point nor a
sufficient condition" for reliable open science (p. 8), and the ordering
should start from inclusion (Figure 2).

**What could have come out otherwise.** In the cases, open access could
have widened participation and preserved local expertise. As the author
describes them, it did not. No case measures reliability.

## What this does not say

- **That sharing-first open science has in fact lowered reliability.** The
  reading grades that link weak to moderate (C3): it is argued through
  cases and standpoint theory, not measured.
- **That diversity and injustice are inversely correlated in general.**
  That is an assertion with two examples (C4).
- **That sharing is bad.** It is not a sufficient condition and not a
  necessary starting point. Leonelli calls sharing as reuse "prima facie …
  a much better foundation" than sharing as access, while arguing both
  share the object-oriented epistemology.
- **How to decide what is judicious.** "How this vision of openness can be
  effectively realized remains itself an open question" (p. 66). There is
  no decision rule, only things to attend to.
- **That it transfers to fully computational work.** The base is biology and
  biomedicine. Computer simulations are the case where "both procedures and
  results are expected to be fully reproducible" (p. 27), and the book never
  discusses machine-learning model release.
- **That every open-science initiative holds the sharing view.** Many
  grassroots initiatives already embody the view she favours (pp. 2–3).
