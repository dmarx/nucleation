# THEORY-879: Representing a sensory quality, even in the distinctively sensory way, does not make there be something it is like for the subject to have it: a quality can occur unnoticed, as for an absent-minded driver, so what it is like to experience a quality is a further property that needs awareness, and a representational theory of sensory qualities leaves open what makes a state experienced

## Source

Lycan (2000; substantive revision 19 October 2023), [LIT-1355](../literature.d/LIT-1355.md),
*Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, read in full in [NOTE-1068](../notes.d/NOTE-1068.md): the
introduction's four senses of "conscious", §2 and §2.1 (representation is
not enough), §4.2 (unconscious representation), §4.5.1 and §5.1 ("what
it's like"). The works it reports were not read.

## What was actually shown

An argument in three steps, made in the entry's own voice.

1. **Representation is not enough for a quality.** Saying "red" or
   pointing to a colour chip represents redness without anything looking
   red, so the representation must be "specifically a visual
   representation" (§2). "representation alone is cheap and ubiquitous"
   (§2.1). Strong representationalism adds a sensory or functional
   condition: Dretske's "systemic", Tye's "poised", Lycan's functional
   role.
2. **A quality represented in that way can still go unnoticed.** Most
   representationalists "already hold that a sensory quality can occur
   without being noticed by its host", as in the driver "on autopilot" who
   "obviously saw the red light, and saw its redness in particular, but who
   was daydreaming and quite unaware of the redness" (§4.2).
3. **What it is like is a further property.** In the unnoticed case "it
   would not be like anything for the subject to experience that quality",
   so what it is like to experience it "requires awareness and so is
   something distinct from the quality itself" (§5.1). It is "itself a
   property of the quality" (§5), and it is ineffable where the quality is
   describable.

Hence the scope the entry gives its subject: "As always, the
representational theory addresses only sense (3)" (§4.5.1). And the
reply to the objection from unconscious representation, that it refutes
only pure representationalism (§4.2).

What could have come out otherwise: if every case in which a quality is
represented in the sensory way were also a case the subject is aware of,
step 2 would have no instance. The entry offers one instance, and it is
contested.

## What this does not say

- **It does not show the driver's perception was unexperienced.** That is
  asserted. Block reads such cases as phenomenal consciousness without
  access ([LIT-643](../literature.d/LIT-643.md); [THEORY-103](THEORY-103.md) is his overflow claim), and the
  higher-order entry ([LIT-1362](../literature.d/LIT-1362.md), §2) grants that one "may be possible
  to get oneself to believe" the driver's perceptions are phenomenally
  conscious but forgotten. Telling the two apart is [QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md)'s
  problem, and nothing here solves it.
- **It does not say what awareness is.** The entry lists the options
  (higher-order representation, self-representation, attention,
  acquaintance) and leaves them to other theories (§3.6).
- **It is not representationalism.** The THEORY holds only the split
  between a quality and its being experienced. Whether qualities are
  represented properties, and whether they are wide or narrow, is the
  entry's subject and stays unsettled there (§4.6).
- **It is not the claim that there is no phenomenal consciousness.** It
  keeps "what it's like" as a real higher-order property. Illusionism
  ([THEORY-633](THEORY-633.md)) denies that property; this THEORY assumes it.
- **It does not speak to affect.** The entry's split of a pain into a
  sensory quality and an affective aspect that morphine blocks (§4.5.1) is
  a different distinction, reported from Lycan (1998), and is not part of
  this claim.
- **The usage is disputed.** Dretske and Tye use "what it's like" for the
  quality itself (introduction, §5). On their usage step 3 is false by
  definition, and the dispute is partly verbal.

## Connections

No `answers:` relation is declared. The THEORY says that awareness
separates an experienced quality from an unnoticed one. It does not say
what awareness is, so it does not answer [QUESTION-254](../questions.d/QUESTION-254.md) even in part.

- **[CLAIM-412](../claims.d/CLAIM-412.md).** If this THEORY holds, then a theory that fixes
  the character of an experience by an organized representation has not yet
  said what makes it experienced. The claim is meant to do both. So the
  THEORY marks where the claim goes beyond its nearest standing relative.
- **[CLAIM-411](../claims.d/CLAIM-411.md).** This is the same objection put to representationalism,
  and the representationalist's reply concedes its structure: the theory owes
  a further condition, and it locates that condition in awareness.
- **[QUESTION-254](../questions.d/QUESTION-254.md).** The question asks what separates experiential
  from merely processing organization. On this THEORY, for perception the
  separating factor is awareness of the state. That is one candidate
  answer, and it is the higher-order family's.
- **[THEORY-103](THEORY-103.md).** Block's overflow puts experience beyond access. This
  THEORY puts a quality short of experience. The two disagree over the
  same unnoticed-perception cases.
