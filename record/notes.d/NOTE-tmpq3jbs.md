# NOTE-tmpq3jbs: Berdini — Rationality as the Normative Dimension of Speech Acts

## Contribution

A short programmatic paper. It takes Searle's thesis that "language is the
basic form of public deontology" (Searle 2010, p. 82) and denies that the
deontology comes from constitutive rules. It puts in their place Bach and
Harnish's inferential model, with a presumption of rationality, read through
Grice's late lectures, as the minimal normative base. It closes by calling
itself a "quessertion" (p. 206), a hedged suggestion.

## Key insight

If which act an utterance performs is settled by inference to the speaker's
attitude, not by rules, then any obligation a speech act creates has to come
from what the inference presupposes. Berdini's candidate is that the parties
treat each other as rational.

## Assumptions

- **Searle's three sources of commitment**: "(i) the social character of
  the communication situation, (ii) the conventional character of the
  devices used, (iii) the intentionality of speaker meaning" (Searle 2010,
  p. 80, quoted p. 201). Berdini keeps (i) and drops (ii).
- **Bach and Harnish's inferential model** is taken as given.
- **Rationality is evaluative.** From Grice (1991, 2001) and Kim (1988):
  "the terms within the scope of rationality are normative, since
  rationality itself is essentially normative" (p. 205).

## Key results

- **Against the rule-based account (pp. 202–203).** "Meaning never
  exhausts illocutionary force" (Bach and Harnish 1979, p. 132, quoted p.
  202). "The door is open" may be an assertion, an order, a threat or a
  piece of advice. Searle limits his account to serious, literal, direct
  discourse, and his account of indirect acts relies on background
  information and inference that his rule-based core lacks (p. 202). His own
  "Background of Meaning" (1980) argues that literal meaning fixes truth
  conditions only against background practices (pp. 202–203).
- **The inferential model (pp. 203–204).** The hearer understands an
  illocutionary act by "inferring the speaker's attitude from 'what is
  said', together with mutual contextual beliefs, two general mutual
  beliefs peculiar to the communication situation (linguistic and
  communicative presumptions), and a set of conversational presumptions
  (drawn from Grice's maxims)" (p. 203). The presumptions are defeasible
  mutual beliefs. Communication is the speaker's expression of an attitude
  "by means of reflexive-intending ... 'that the hearer take the
  [speaker]'s utterance as reason to think [she] has that attitude'" (Bach
  and Harnish 1979, p. 39, quoted p. 204).
- **Bach and Harnish on rights and obligations (p. 204).** "I distance
  myself from Bach and Harnish, who define a 'moral question' whether
  illocutionary acts create a deontology, stating that 'at best they create
  mutual beliefs between speaker and hearer about rights and obligations'
  (Bach & Harnish 1979, 124)." With Searle, Berdini holds deontology
  "internal to the performance of speech acts". Against him, she holds that
  it does not come from compliance with semantic rules (p. 204). Footnote 6
  points to Harnish (2005) as facing normativity differently.
- **Rationality as normativity (pp. 205–206).** "The presumption about the
  interlocutor's rationality could be regarded as the basic form of
  normativity deriving from the social character of the communication
  situation." Treating someone as rational means expecting things of them,
  which constrains one's own conduct. These expectations and constraints
  could be "an ultra-minimalist notion of normativity", from which
  "commitments, obligations, rights, licenses" might be accounted for (p.
  205). Making the presumption explicit would let the model drop the
  presumption of literalness and define conversational appropriateness
  without circularity (p. 205, n. 7).

## Concepts

- **Inferentialist model**: Bach and Harnish's account of understanding
  as inference from what is said, mutual contextual beliefs and
  presumptions to the speaker's expressed attitude (p. 203).
- **Presumptions**: linguistic, communicative, literalness and
  conversational. They are defeasible mutual beliefs that make
  communication possible (pp. 203–204).
- **Deontology / deontic powers**: commitments, obligations, rights and
  licenses created in speech, after Searle (2010) (pp. 201, 205).
- **Quessertion**: Grandy's word for a hedged suggestion, "It is perhaps
  possible that someone might assert that ..." (p. 206, n. 8).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md): partly, through one quoted sentence.** The claim's open
  point after 2026-10-13 is whether standing that enters through the
  content of the speaker's intention counts as a relation, and it waits on
  Bach and Harnish for that. Berdini quotes them: whether illocutionary acts
  create a deontology is "a moral question", and "at best they create
  mutual beliefs between speaker and hearer about rights and obligations"
  (1979, p. 124). On that sentence, Bach and Harnish do not count rights,
  obligations or standing among the constituents of an illocutionary act.
  Such things enter as mutual beliefs, which in their model are inputs to
  the hearer's inference (p. 203). That is the intention-based answer the
  claim attributes to the line, and the reading confirms it is Bach and
  Harnish's. It does not show how they treat teasing or reproach. It is one
  sentence at second hand, from an author who rejects it. It does not meet
  what the claim waits on: a reading of the book.
- **[QUESTION-073](../questions.d/QUESTION-073.md): partly, the same passage.** On Bach and Harnish, as
  Berdini quotes them, standing would enter the act only as mutual belief
  about rights and obligations, and a frame the parties hold together would
  enter as mutual contextual belief and presumption (p. 203). Both are
  evidence for the inference, not parts of the act. That states the
  intention-based side of the question in its authors' terms. It supplies no
  case and does not mention teasing.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **Programmatic.** The positive thesis is stated, not argued, and the
  author says so (p. 206).
- **Second-hand Bach and Harnish.** The three quotations of the 1979 book
  (pp. 39, 124, 132) are Berdini's, unchecked against the book. The
  definition of expressing is cited to p. 39 here and to p. 15 in Harnish
  (2005, [LIT-tmp7ybhu](../literature.d/LIT-tmp7ybhu.md)), with slightly different wording. Either the book
  states it twice or one citation is wrong.
- **Pagination.** The PDF prints pp. 201–207. The journal's metadata and
  Crossref give pp. 160–165.
