# NOTE-tmpf65in: Powers, Clark & McFarland — General Feedback Theory, Part I

## Contribution

The founding paper of perceptual control theory, in two parts; this is the
first, the model without its psychological application. It defines a
control unit (Feedback, Comparator and Output Functions plus an
Environment Function), stacks such units so that a higher order's output
is a lower order's reference, and adds three things the 1973 Science
paper ([NOTE-tmpxdsyb](NOTE-tmpxdsyb.md)) leaves out: a recording function at every unit that
supplies references as replays of past perceptions (memory), an
"imagination connection" by which those replays reach the higher order's
perception without passing through the world, and an inherited
"negentropy" or N-system that reorganizes the network, input functions
included, at a rate set by intrinsic error. There are no data.

## Key insight

Every goal is a remembered perception, and a goal sent down can be
perceived on the way. "the reference-signals which control a given
feedback unit are examples of its own past sensory signals, and one could
now express the task of the control system as being that of reproducing
in present-time experience some previously-experienced perceptual field"
(p. 18). Let the higher system perceive the reference it sends instead of
the feedback it gets, and "the 'match' is automatically ensured" (p. 20):
that is imagination, memory, planning and dreaming. How much of each
perception comes from the world and how much from the replay is set by a
"mixing control" whose seat the authors leave open. Separately, what
changes the perceiving apparatus itself is not this loop but the
N-system, an inherited controller of bodily variables whose output is
random reorganization.

## Assumptions

- **Analogue, magnitude-only signals.** "we are concerned only with
  information flow, and not with the means by which the information is
  transmitted" (p. 5).
- **Stable functions between reorganizations.** A function is "a stable
  relationship, which does not alter its form over reasonable periods of
  time" (p. 4). The learned hierarchy is "a model of those human systems
  which produce learned behavior, after learning has taken place" (p. 12).
  Change of form is the N-system's alone.
- **External loops first.** "We will be primarily concerned with
  externally connected feedback loops" (p. 6), and the summary calls the
  hierarchy "composed of elementary feedback control systems of the
  external-loop type" (p. 21). The imagination connection is the one
  internal loop added, and is drawn dotted because "its introduction must
  be qualified" (p. 19).
- **Stability by fiat.** "by leaving the details of the functions
  unspecified, we have by definition a stable system" (p. 9).
- **Quasi-static equilibrium.** Only steady-state relations; transients
  are neglected on a time scale chosen per system, from 0.1 s to "even
  days" (p. 9).
- **Outputs only at the first order.** "all its output boundary functions
  belong to first-order control systems" (p. 10); "Only first-order
  systems act directly on the (non-CNS) environment" (p. 22).
- **Genetically fixed top references.** The N-system's references "must be
  genetically determined, not determined by experience" (p. 14).

## Key results

There are no theorems and no data; these are the model's stated
properties.

- **The control unit (pp. 6–8).** f = F(v_i), o = O(e) = O(r − f),
  v_i = E(o). With an unresisting environment the system settles with f =
  r and makes f "track" a slowly changing r; under disturbance a small
  residual error holds the output (p. 8).
- **Internal loops are invisible from outside (p. 6).** "only the external
  type is unequivocally perceivable as a feedback loop by an external
  observer. The behavior of any system with internal feedback could be
  simulated exactly by another system with no internal loops, so such
  internal loops cannot be firmly identified by external observations."
- **The hierarchy (pp. 10–12).** First-order systems are the spinal loops.
  Output signals of higher orders are identified with lower references,
  because tying outputs together sends feedback systems "into violent
  conflict" (p. 11). "the output of a second-order system is not a
  muscular force, but a goal toward which first-order systems
  automatically adjust their input-signals" (p. 11). A system of any one
  order "perceives an environment made up of the feedback-signals of the
  systems in the next lower order" (p. 12).
- **What the system knows (pp. 11–12).** "it is of no concern at all to the
  feedback system what actual effects are produced in the environment";
  the systems "cannot even know what is going on 'out there.' They perform
  the sole function of bringing their feedback-signals, the only reality
  they can perceive, to some reference-level, the only goal they know."
- **The limit of the learned hierarchy (p. 12).** It "cannot do a thing
  about an environment which changes its properties ... We still lack
  something to account for non-rote learning, for that requires altering
  the structure of the system, not merely its information content."
- **The N-system (pp. 12–16).** Its output, "the processes which alter
  organization in uncommitted neurones" (p. 13), does four things: (1)
  uncommitted neurones "become tentatively organized to process a number of
  feedback signals from the highest existing order"; (2) others become
  tentative output organizations generating references for the next lower
  order; (3) at a variable rate; (4) an organization that occurs "often
  enough" persists, where "'Often enough' means one or more times" (p. 13,
  note 1). The input side is perceptual: "The continuing reorganization
  occurring in the new input function does not have such
  externally-observable results, but is subjectively recognized as a kind
  of trial-and-error effort to perceive new patterns ... The 'hypotheses'
  here should be thought of as tentative definitions of new variables,
  which may or may not prove to repeat themselves in experience, depending
  on the organization of lower-order perceptual functions and the
  properties and nature of the environment" (p. 14). Its inputs are
  "intrinsic signals ... measures of a set of physiological states" (p.
  14). "the rate of learning is approximately proportional to the
  intrinsic error-signal, and this is a fundamental property of the human
  organism" (p. 15). Reorganization stops not because anything "tells" it
  to, but because "this particular organization produces behavior which
  results in a lessening of the intrinsic error" (pp. 15–16). "The output
  action of the N-system is conceived of as essentially random" (p. 21).
- **Memory (pp. 16–18).** Imagining is defined as "the ability to perceive
  sensory events generated internally rather than generated by
  present-time interactions at the input boundary of the whole system"
  (p. 16). A recording function R at each unit records its feedback
  signal and, when stimulated from outside the unit, replays "a facsimile
  of the signal that was recorded ... To all intents, it is a sensory
  signal, but one arising from a past event rather than a present one" (p.
  17). Higher outputs now stimulate recordings rather than set references
  directly (p. 18). "Rote learning could occur in the form of new
  recordings and hence an enlarged repertoire of reference-signals" (p.
  18). Forgetting is undecided: "There is no present way to tell whether
  forgetting is due to fading of the recordings or to failure of the
  recovery apparatus" (p. 17).
- **Why the replay must go up, not sideways (pp. 18–19).** Perception is
  of feedback signals only, so the model so far "can reproduce a past
  experience without being able to perceive that experience" (p. 18). The
  replay cannot be fed back into the same order's F, since F would process
  it twice (the y = x² example: the result represents x⁴, p. 19). It bears
  the same relation to lower signals as present feedback does, so "if the
  reference-signal were to enter a Feedback Function of the next higher
  order, it would always be interpreted properly" (p. 19). Hence the
  imagination connection.
- **What it does (pp. 19–20).** The higher system "would find its feedback
  signal at the required reference-level solely on the basis of the
  imagination-signals from lower order, even though the lower-order
  signals might be quite far from the reference-levels in the lower-order
  systems" (p. 19); then "a condition like dreaming or fantasy would
  occur, in which every goal set for the lower-order systems appeared to
  be immediately satisfied-in imagination, of course ... if the
  reference-signal substitutes for the feedback-signal, the 'match' is
  automatically ensured" (p. 20). "The imagination-signal makes it
  possible for our system to perceive reproductions of past perceptual
  signals (that is, to remember as well as record), to plan an action
  'mentally' without actually performing it, to hallucinate, and as
  mentioned, to dream" (p. 20).
- **The mixing control (p. 20).** "Obviously, the hierarchy could not
  perform very reliably in a real and sometimes dangerous environment if
  its actions were completely 'short-circuited' by the imagination
  connections. Somehow this configuration must contribute more information
  to the perceptual field at some times, less at others. Under conditions
  of sensory deprivation, it apparently provides a great deal of
  information, while under conditions of, e.g., immediate danger (barring
  pathology) it contributes little." "There appears to be a kind of mixing
  control, which can be adjusted to full imagination (as when asleep) to
  full present-time perception. This might be a property of the Feedback
  Functions, corresponding to a shift in perceptual attention, or of the
  manner in which Output Functions stimulate lower-order recordings. We
  are open to suggestions."
- **The normal mix (p. 20).** "The normal condition is probably one in
  which most information is present-time perceptual information, and small
  errors are filled in by the imagination connection-this would be a
  pro-survival property, in that it would allow the feedback systems to be
  very exact in their control-actions, while not tying them up over
  trivial discrepancies. The phenomenon of 'filling in' small
  discrepancies is well-known under the label 'closure.'"
- **The test of the significant variable (pp. 22–24).** An observer finds
  another system's controlled variables by disturbing candidate
  abstractions and seeing which are held constant; introspection supplies
  candidate classes ("sequence" is the example), to be checked by the
  test.

## Concepts

- **Percept, variable, function, system.** A percept is "the basic unit of
  experience"; a variable combines an unvarying class of percepts
  (identity) and a varying one (magnitude); a function is a stable
  relation imposed by some physical device; a system is "a collection of
  functions (not, as is often proposed, a collection of variables)" (pp.
  2–4).
- **Feedback-signal f, reference-signal r, error-signal e.** "signal" is
  reserved for variables inside the system (p. 7).
- **Order.** First-order systems are the boundary loops (spinal
  reflexes); an nth-order system perceives (n−1)th-order feedback signals
  and outputs (n−1)th-order references (pp. 10–12).
- **Recording function R.** Records a unit's feedback signal and replays it
  on stimulation; the replay serves as the unit's reference (pp. 16–18).
- **Imagination connection.** A branch from a unit's reference signal into
  the Feedback Function of the higher system that set it (p. 19).
- **Mixing control.** Whatever sets the proportion of imagination to
  present-time perception (p. 20).
- **N-system, intrinsic signals, intrinsic state.** The inherited
  reorganizing controller; its inputs, "measures of a set of physiological
  states"; and the state in which all its references are met (p. 14).
- **Test of the significant variable.** Disturb a candidate variable and
  see whether the system keeps it constant (pp. 22–23).

## Connections

Table 1 names twelve sources. Ashby's Design for a Brain (with "defects in
2/3 and 2/4" noted), Wiener's Cybernetics and the 1948 Teleological
Mechanisms symposium supply the control concepts; Hebb's cell assembly
and phase sequence are called "primitive feedback concepts"; Lorente de
Nó's cortical diagram in Fulton (1949) is read as showing "upgoing
feedback signals ..., outgoing output signals, and imagination
connections"; Shannon and Weaver and analogue-computing texts supply the
signal language; a 1954 Air Force frequency-response report is "A good
example of what we are not trying to accomplish." "Uncommitted neurones"
is McCulloch's term (p. 13). The 1973 Science paper ([LIT-tmp626gh](../literature.d/LIT-tmp626gh.md), read in
[NOTE-tmpxdsyb](NOTE-tmpxdsyb.md)) restates the hierarchy and the reorganizing top level and
drops the recording function, the imagination connection and the mixing
control. Part II ([LIT-tmpuh84t](../literature.d/LIT-tmpuh84t.md), [NOTE-tmplcbcz](NOTE-tmplcbcz.md)) names the orders and works
out reorganization, consciousness and conflict.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-tmpq9do8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpq9do8.md) (thesis).**
  - *The Rivals bullet's quotations are accurate.* "plan an action
    'mentally' without actually performing it", "match", "automatically
    ensured", "could not perform very reliably" and "completely
    'short-circuited'" are all on p. 20 (the last two in one sentence).
  - *The imagination loop does not reshape the observer.* It replays
    recordings into an unchanged Feedback Function, and rote learning only
    enlarges "the repertoire of reference-signals" (p. 18). So the claim
    is right to call it the synchronic, self-satisfying form. The paper
    adds that this form is not only for dreaming: in "the normal
    condition", "small errors are filled in by the imagination
    connection", which the authors identify with closure (p. 20). In this
    model the self-confirming loop runs all the time, at a small share.
  - *But the theory does reshape the observer, and the claim's contrast is
    with the wrong paper.* The claim says "Powers's sensors are assumed
    constant", quoting the 1973 paper. In 1960 the functions are stable
    only "over reasonable periods of time" (p. 4), and the N-system's
    first act is to organize new input functions, "tentative definitions
    of new variables", experienced as "a kind of trial-and-error effort to
    perceive new patterns" and as "tentative formulation of hypotheses"
    (p. 14). That is the claim's diachronic shape: a change to what the
    observer can perceive, felt as hypothesis-making. The difference lies
    in what drives the change and what keeps it, not in whether perception
    changes.
    - *What drives it* is error in inherited "intrinsic signals ...
      measures of a set of physiological states" (p. 14), and its output
      is "essentially random" (p. 21). No judgement enters.
    - *What keeps it* is behaviour: an organization persists when it
      "produces behavior which results in a lessening of the intrinsic
      error" (pp. 15–16), in "the lower-order systems and the environment".
      So in Part I the selection of a new observer runs through action and
      the world. Part II qualifies this: a new organization can be vetoed
      by "a moment spent imagining" ([NOTE-tmplcbcz](NOTE-tmplcbcz.md)).
    The claim's loop, judgement reshaping the observer, sits between the
    paper's two mechanisms. The imagination loop has judgement-like
    content but changes nothing. Reorganization changes the observer, but
    it is driven from the body and kept by behaviour (my observation).
  - *Only the lowest order acts on the world* ("Only first-order systems
    act directly on the (non-CNS) environment", p. 22), and each higher
    order "does not act directly on the external world, but on the only
    environment with which it is in immediate contact, the set of
    next-lower-order systems" (p. 22). This is a stronger statement of
    [CLAIM-tmp9cjyo](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp9cjyo.md)'s "the lower system is the higher one's proximal
    environment" than the 1973 paper gives, where higher systems sense
    quantities in the world. Here they perceive only lower signals.
  - *`supports: CLAIM-tmpq9do8` on the LIT is doubtful.* The paper's
    hierarchy is "of the external-loop type" (p. 21); its one internal
    loop is hedged for exactly the reason [CLAIM-tmpki5gp](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpki5gp.md) gives; and its
    reshaping of the observer is selected through behaviour. It is a
    rival that contains the claim's ingredients, not support for it. The
    LIT's relation was left as filed, for the owner to decide.
- **[CLAIM-tmpki5gp](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpki5gp.md) (counter).** The paper states its worry in its own
  terms: a hierarchy "completely 'short-circuited' by the imagination
  connections" could not "perform very reliably in a real and sometimes
  dangerous environment" (p. 20). Its remedy is not action as such but a
  mixing control that keeps present-time perception dominant. In locked-in
  syndrome present-time perception is intact while first-order output is
  gone. On this model every higher goal that closes through movement then
  stays in error in present-time perception, and the only place it can be
  satisfied is imagination (my observation; the paper does not discuss
  paralysis). Part II names a full switch to imagination as a defence
  against conflict ([NOTE-tmplcbcz](NOTE-tmplcbcz.md)).
- **[CASE-tmpxgcq2](../cases.d/CASE-tmpxgcq2.md).**
  - *Sensory deprivation.* The paper says one sentence: under it the
    imagination connection "apparently provides a great deal of
    information" (p. 20). No source and no data are given. Locked-in
    syndrome is not sensory deprivation; it is deprivation of output with
    input spared, a case the paper does not consider.
  - *The asymmetry.* The CASE says lucidity in the completely locked-in
    state cannot be shown without a channel that is itself an action. The
    paper makes the general point in 1960: internal loops "cannot be
    firmly identified by external observations", since a system with
    internal feedback "could be simulated exactly by another system with
    no internal loops" (p. 6).
- **[QUESTION-tmpr5tck](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpr5tck.md).**
  - *Candidate 2, the body.* The N-system is a scoring channel the learned
    hierarchy does not set: its references "must be genetically determined,
    not determined by experience" (p. 14), and its inputs are physiological.
    This is the 1973 paper's top level, stated earlier and in more detail.
    The same caution applies: its scores select organizations through
    behaviour (pp. 15–16).
  - *The mixing control is where an anchor would have to sit.* What keeps
    imagination from confirming itself is whatever sets the share of
    present-time perception. The authors do not know what it is, offering
    attention in the Feedback Functions or the way outputs stimulate
    recordings, and add "We are open to suggestions" (p. 20). The paper
    has no answer to the question, and locates where it lies.
- **Checks of [LIT-tmpgo33i](../literature.d/LIT-tmpgo33i.md).** The summary is correct against pp. 19–20.
  "Why this is here" is correct in what it quotes, and should add that the
  1960 theory reshapes perceptual input functions through reorganization,
  so the contrast with "constant sensors" holds for the 1973 paper only.
  Crossref lists the third author as "Farland"; the reprint gives
  "McFarland".
- **For the anthology.** No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- No data. The empirical remarks (brain stimulation, sensory deprivation,
  closure) are uncited asides.
- Stability, the forms of the functions and the mixing control are left
  unspecified, the first by definition.
- The N-system's output is "defined simply in terms of what it must do"
  (p. 13); its specificity is left open (Part II, p. 35).
- How a higher output selects which recording to replay is left open: it
  "might be a function of R, or it might result from some property of
  higher-order Output Functions" (p. 18).
- Read from an OCR extraction of a reset reprint; figures not legible in
  the extraction; journal pagination not checked.

## Open questions

- What the mixing control is, and whether anything other than intrinsic
  error or danger sets it. That is the place in this model where
  [QUESTION-tmpr5tck](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpr5tck.md)'s answer would go.
- Whether reorganization of input functions can be triggered by error in
  the learned hierarchy rather than only by intrinsic error. Part II
  allows "mean error signal in the hierarchy" as an intrinsic variable (p.
  35), which would make a failed judgement a cause of reorganization
  ([NOTE-tmplcbcz](NOTE-tmplcbcz.md)).
- Whether, in output deprivation with input spared, the model predicts a
  shift of the mix toward imagination, and so the self-confirming regime
  [CLAIM-tmpki5gp](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpki5gp.md) fears.
