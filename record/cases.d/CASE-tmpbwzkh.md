# CASE-tmpbwzkh: The English localization of Ace Attorney: a Japanese courtroom game moved to an alternate Los Angeles, with "textually accurate" set against "emotionally accurate" by its localizer

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for: a documented
adaptation that moves an act into a new situation, with the correspondence
of situations stated by the adapter and its fidelity contested on record,
for [CLAIM-tmp95pjv](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp95pjv.md), whose two targets (report and re-performance) had no
documented case.

## The case

Capcom's courtroom game *Gyakuten Saiban* (2001) was localized into English
as *Phoenix Wright: Ace Attorney* (2005) by Alexander O. Smith, and by Janet
Hsu from the second game on. The localization moved the setting from an
unnamed Japan to Los Angeles, renamed the characters (Naruhodō Ryūichi
became Phoenix Wright) and swapped cultural items: a character's miso ramen
became hamburgers. Most graphics were left as they were, so Japanese
traces remain in a nominally American world, which fans call
"Japanifornia" (Mangiron 2021, §6, citing Hsu).

**The two targets, named by the localizer.** Janet Hsu, "Localization and
Ace Attorney", Capcom blog, 21 Nov 2014,
https://news.capcomusa.com/zeroobjections/blog/2014/11/21/%0blocalization-and-ace-attorney:

> "Translations are not concerned with how the reader will feel or react to
> the information. The primary objective of a good translation is
> accuracy."

and a localization "must strike a balance between what is 'textually
accurate' and what is what I call 'emotionally accurate'." Her example is a
developer's blog she had translated, which kept a reference to grilled
chicken skin (yakitori): "did it give you the reader the same emotional
experience as a Japanese reader? Probably not. In other words, that blog
was translated to be 'textually accurate' regardless of how 'emotionally
inaccurate' some parts of it may have been to the reader."

**The correspondence of situations, stated.** Same post. The relocation was
forced by a plot device: "Because of the time zone difference trick in the
first episode, there was a need to decide on where the game was going to
take place." The world she works to is one where "a number of things can
only be possible in a world where Japanese people were allowed to own land
because the California Alien Land Law of 1913 was never passed". She claims
re-performance as the truer fidelity: a good localization "will be truer
to the intentions of the original creators than a strict translation",
with players "laughing at the same points, and crying your eyes out at the
same points, too." She concedes "it's unusual for people to like, let
alone accept, such drastic changes to the source material".

**The criterion for re-performance.** Smith, interviewed by Stephen
Mandiberg on 8 Nov 2012, on the ramen that became hamburgers, quoted in
Mandiberg, "Playing (with) the Trace: Localized Culture in Phoenix
Wright", *Kinephanos* 5(1), December 2015,
https://www.kinephanos.ca/2015/playing-with-the-trace/: if a translation
creates "an iwakan [incongruity] that doesn't exist in the original ... if
it's creating confusion that the original doesn't create with its original
audience then you are doing a disservice to the goal of the scene or the
line."

**Where the correspondence has no image.** In the third game Phoenix wears a
white face mask. Asked by an American fan in a 2007 interview "What's up
with the… face mask that Phoenix sports?", the producer, Matsukawa Minae,
answered about a different masked character. Mandiberg: "To the Japanese
producer, the question could not be something as banal as why does Phoenix
Wright wear a mask. He wears a mask because he is sick." The interview
(tiny dancer, gaygamer.net, 31 Jul 2007) is a dead page and is known here
only through Mandiberg.

**Fidelity contested.** Mandiberg's abstract argues that the residual
traces "should be seen as successes of responsible localization practice",
"far from being failures of localization, as industry professionals
contend". Carme Mangiron, "Found in Translation: Evolving Approaches for
the Localization of Japanese Video Games", *Arts* 10(1):9, 2021,
doi:10.3390/arts10010009, §6, records both sides. The localization "has
been acclaimed by players and critics because of its hybrid nature"
(citing Consalvo 2016), and a critic, Scott Baird (2019), says it "managed
to capture the spirit of the original". But "some of the more hardcore
fans of Japanese culture consider it to be one of the worst translations
ever made due to the extreme level of adaptation", which Mangiron sources
to forum debates (her note 9). Mangiron spells Hsu's name "Tsu"; it is the
same 2014 post.

**A runner-up that cuts the other way: "Son of God" for Muslim readers.**
Some SIL- and Wycliffe-associated translations for Muslim audiences
rendered "Son of God" and "Father" with non-biological terms. Their
translators defend this as the *more faithful report*, not as
re-performance. Rick Brown, Leith Gray and Andrea Gray, "A New Look at
Translating Familial Biblical Terms", *International Journal of Frontier
Missiology* 28(3), 2011,
https://www.ijfm.org/PDFs_IJFM/28_3_PDFs/IJFM_28_3-BrownGrayGray-NewLook.pdf:
in some target languages the Lord's Prayer "is misunderstood as meaning
'Our Begetter, who is in heaven'", and "Such wordings are inaccurate
because they add a procreative meaning that was absent from the original".
The renderings themselves (*Stories of the Prophets*, Arabic: "the Messiah
of God" for "the Son of God" at Luke 4:9, "Our loving heavenly Lord" for
"Father" at Luke 11:2) are known only through a critic, Michael F. Ross,
"The Son of God and Muslim Idiom Translations", *Christian Research
Journal* 36(6), 2013,
https://www.equip.org/articles/the-son-of-god-and-muslim-idiom-translations/;
the translations were not seen. The World Evangelical Alliance panel's
report of 29 Apr 2013 is known only as quoted in *The Gospel Coalition*
(Michael Clark, 8 May 2013): divine "father" and "son" should "always be
translated with the most direct equivalent familial words", with
explanation in "paratextual material". The report itself could not be
fetched and is unverified.

## What it can show

That a practitioner draws [CLAIM-tmp95pjv](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp95pjv.md)'s distinction in nearly its own
terms, and on single items. "Textually accurate" is the report target: the
yakitori is kept, and the reader learns what the source's situation held.
"Emotionally accurate" is the re-performance target: the line should do,
for a player in Los Angeles, what it did for a player in Japan. Smith's
iwakan criterion is a statement of re-performance fidelity: no reaction in
the target audience that the source did not produce in its own.

It gives the correspondence of situations z ↦ z′ that [QUESTION-005](../questions.d/QUESTION-005.md) says
nothing fixes, fixed here by the adapter's fiat (an alternate history) and
kept across several games. It also shows what happens where the
correspondence has no image. The mask is carried over, so it is reported,
but its convention is not, so it is not re-performed, and a target reader
cannot answer "why the mask?", which a source reader answers at once. That
is a documented question decidable on one side only, and it bears on
[CLAIM-tmp1rlu5](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp1rlu5.md) as well, though as a cultural convention in an image, not as
a linguistic form.

The case is the claim's own kind of re-performance, compensation for
changed social coordinates ([CLAIM-091](../claims.d/CLAIM-091.md)), with the correspondence on record.
House's overt and covert translation ([LIT-789](../literature.d/LIT-789.md)) and Nida's dynamic
equivalence ([LIT-791](../literature.d/LIT-791.md)) are near the same distinction; neither is read
here.

## What it cannot show

- **The defeat condition is met only in part.** [CLAIM-tmp95pjv](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp95pjv.md) is defeated
  if receivers' verdicts on the same renderings do not differ between a
  report task and a re-performance task. No study has put both tasks to
  the same receivers for these renderings. The documented split, critics
  who say the spirit was captured against fans who call it one of the
  worst translations, fits the distinction. It fits equally a difference
  of weights over the same profile ([CLAIM-tmpnttsk](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpnttsk.md)), and the record cannot
  tell the two apart. The fan side is known only through Mangiron's
  citation of forum threads.
- **Nothing about the order.** The case shows two targets, not that
  Blackwell's order ([THEORY-156](../theory.d/THEORY-156.md)) applies to the report target. No
  probability model of any item is available.
- **The strongest reply.** Hsu herself says localization "must strike a
  balance" between the two targets. A defender of [CLAIM-tmpyeik3](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpyeik3.md) would
  say that practitioners optimize a mixture, so that report and
  re-performance are not separable decision targets in practice, and the
  order still reaches no actual rendering. The case cannot rebut that. It
  shows that the targets can be named apart and traded, not that any
  rendering is judged on one alone.
- **The runner-up cuts against one line of the claim.** [CLAIM-tmp95pjv](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp95pjv.md)
  says that compensation "is a re-performance notion". The "Son of God"
  translators defend a compensating rendering as the better *report*: for
  readers whose word for "son" asserts procreation, the literal word
  mis-reports the source. If that is right, compensation is not
  re-performance only; a compensating rendering can be the more faithful
  report for a reader with a different code. The WEA ruling, as quoted,
  splits the jobs: the familial word in the text, the correspondence in
  the paratext. The renderings are known only through Ross, the ruling
  only through a news report, and the translators' reader evidence is
  their own field reports (a reader who took Luke 2:48 as "proof that the
  Bible has been corrupted"), so this half is documented as an argument,
  not as reception.
- **What would close the gap.** The reader test the search report
  sketches, which could serve as items for the discriminating study
  ([CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md)). Take the ramen and hamburger line, the yakitori sentence
  in its literal and its localized form, and the mask scene. Ask matched
  English readers either a report question ("What would this character
  have been eating in Japan?") or a re-performance question ("Is this as
  ordinary here as it was there?"), and compare with Japanese players of
  the source. The claim predicts a crossing: the localized version wins on
  re-performance and loses on report. A null, with the same verdicts under
  both tasks, is the claim's defeat condition met.
