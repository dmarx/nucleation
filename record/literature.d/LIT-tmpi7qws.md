# LIT-tmpi7qws: Representational Theories of Consciousness (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

William Lycan (2000; substantive revision 2023), "Representational Theories of Consciousness", *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (ed. Edward N. Zalta)* — <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-representational/>

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmpxf5do](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpxf5do.md). The SEP has no page numbers; citations
are by section.*

- **Qualities as represented properties.** Strong representationalism holds
  that "sensory qualities are actually intentional contents, represented
  properties of represented objects" (§1). A hallucinated or after-image
  green is the green of a nonactual object.
- **Representation is not enough.** Pure representationalism is held by no
  one: "representation alone is cheap and ubiquitous" (§2.1). Strong
  representationalism (Dretske, Tye, Lycan) adds a sensory or functional
  condition: "systemic", "poised", or functional role (§2).
- **Wide or narrow, unsettled.** Dretske, Tye and Lycan hold qualities
  wide, so molecular duplicates could differ; Shoemaker, Kriegel, Levine
  and Chalmers hold them narrow. Against Block's Inverted Earth memory
  argument, Lycan replies that memory contents are wide too, so "memory is
  not after all a reliable check on the sensory qualities". The verdict:
  "no one should now find it uncontestable either that sensory qualities
  are narrow or that they are wide" (§4.6).
- **Unconscious representation answered by a split.** The objection that
  representation "can occur unconsciously" refutes only pure
  representationalism. A quality can occur unnoticed, as for the driver
  "on autopilot", and then there is nothing it is like to have it (§4.2).
- **"What it's like" is a further property.** It is "itself a property of
  the quality", ineffable where the quality is describable, and it
  "requires awareness" (§5, §5.1). "As always, the representational theory
  addresses only sense (3)" (§4.5.1), the quality.
- **The reverse programme.** Phenomenal intentionality makes phenomenology
  "the fundamental kind of intentionality", which "poses a general threat
  of circularity" when joined to representationalism (§4.3).
- **Illusionism** denies "what it's like" properties; Kammerer argues that
  "no previously existing account can explain the strength of the
  illusion" (§5.3).

## Why this is here

The owner's exchange of 2026-10-21, "Experience and interpretation", asked whether experience is constituted by interpretation or produced by it. The exchange took strong representationalism (Tye, Dretske, Lycan) as the standing position nearest the constitutive thesis, and noted that the phenomenal intentionality programme (Kriegel, Mendelovici) runs the explanation the other way, from phenomenal character to content. This entry is the reference map of both. Bears on [CLAIM-tmpdga80](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpdga80.md) and [THEORY-095](../theory.d/THEORY-095.md).

**Correction from the reading.** The exchange's description holds only in
part. Strong representationalism is an identity claim, and it needs a
further organizational condition beyond representation, as the
constitutive thesis does. But it is a theory of the character of
experience, not of its occurrence. It holds that a sensory quality can
occur with nothing it is like to have it, and it leaves what makes a
state experienced to an account of awareness, the higher-order entry
([LIT-tmpx8g8s](LIT-tmpx8g8s.md)). Its named defenders also hold qualities wide, which the
constitutive thesis, as it reads [THEORY-095](../theory.d/THEORY-095.md), cannot. The exchange's
account of phenomenal intentionality is accurate. Filed from the reading:
[THEORY-tmptw1fe](../theory.d/THEORY-tmptw1fe.md), that representing a sensory quality does not make it
experienced.
