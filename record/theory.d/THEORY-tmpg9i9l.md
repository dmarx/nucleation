# THEORY-tmpg9i9l: Moral blackmail, on Keller's account, is a wrongful act that forces someone to do something by making her alternatives morally unacceptable; the obligation it creates is genuine though she ought not to have it, and legal and professional fiduciaries are especially exposed, because their roles generate obligations of partiality whose triggering circumstances others can control

Keller (2016; issue 2018), LIT-tmpgo0lt, read in NOTE-tmp0loqu. Page
numbers are the preprint's.

## The account

- **Definition** (p. 2): "To commit moral blackmail is to perform a
  wrongful act that forces someone to do something by making her
  alternatives morally unacceptable."
- **Genuine obligation** (p. 21): "you can have a genuine moral obligation
  because you have been treated wrongly." It works "by way of the victim's
  moral sensibility" (p. 22).
- **Exposure of fiduciaries** (pp. 22–25). A fiduciary role becomes a
  special moral relationship whose obligations of partiality exceed the
  role. Whoever can influence the relevant circumstances "will be able to
  influence the facts about what special obligations you have" (p. 22):
  the beneficiary, or a third party such as an employer (the law firm) or
  a government (the teachers before exams).
- **Consequence** (pp. 26–27): the exposed can reasonably expect
  compensation, and the exposure falls on caring roles, often poorly paid
  and more often held by women.

## What it does not show

- **That the targeted party commits a wrong.** In every case the victim
  is pressed to do what is right. The teachers' strike is their "right",
  and the publicity the official expects is a smear. So it carries nothing
  for CLAIM-tmpciy9q's true accusation of a real wrong.
- **Unintended reliance.** All of Keller's cases are deliberate.
- **Frequency.** The cases are "stylized" (p. 25).
