# NOTE-tmp8uvyb: Maitra — The Nature of Epistemic Injustice

## Contribution

It tests Fricker's two kinds of epistemic injustice against cases of the
author's own, and asks why some credibility failures are injustices and
others are not. It accepts Fricker's central cases and argues the account
must be complicated: too broad for testimonial injustice where the hearer
owes the speaker nothing, too narrow where bad luck or carelessly kept
stereotypes wrong a speaker, and wrong to tie each kind to one sort of
prejudice. For this record it is mainly a report of the book's
definitions, with page numbers.

## Key insight

An injustice is a wrong done to someone, so a credibility deficit is an
injustice only where the hearer owed the speaker a credibility judgement
matched to the evidence. The juror and the near relative owe it; the
newsreader and the commuter do not (draft pp. 4–6). What makes Fricker's
central cases injustices is not prejudice alone but prejudice in someone
under that obligation.

## Assumptions

- **Fricker's account is taken as Maitra reports it.** "All page
  references, except where otherwise indicated, are to Fricker (2007)"
  (draft p. 1, n. 1).
- **Intuitions about cases settle whether a wrong is done.** The Zara,
  commuter, police officer, Simon and Arthur, David and Paul cases are
  judged by intuition.
- **Identity prejudice is individual unless said otherwise.** Maitra
  stipulates this after n. 2 (draft p. 2).

## Key results

What Maitra reports of the book (book pages in parentheses):

- **The project.** "a distinctively epistemic kind of injustice", each
  kind consisting in "a wrong done to someone specifically in their
  capacity as a knower" (1) (draft p. 1).
- **Testimonial injustice.** A speaker suffers it when she suffers a
  credibility deficit due to an identity prejudice on the hearer's part.
  A credibility deficit is credibility below "the evidence that they are
  offering the truth" (19). An identity prejudice is a prejudice against
  or for people qua members of a social group (35). It may be individual
  or structural, and "on Fricker's view, however, it's individual identity
  prejudice that's implicated in testimonial injustice" (draft p. 2).
- **Central cases.** Tom Robinson's trial (23–26) is a central case
  because the prejudice is systematic, tracking its targets "across several
  aspects of their lives" (27–28), not incidental. Incidental prejudice
  also yields testimonial injustice, but the systematic kind matters most
  for social justice (draft p. 2). Marge Sherwood in *The Talented Mr.
  Ripley* is the second central case (14–15, 86–89; draft p. 3).
- **Identity-prejudicial stereotype.** Quoted: "a widely held disparaging
  association between a social group and one or more attributes, where
  this association embodies a generalization that displays some
  (typically, epistemically culpable) resistance to counter-evidence owing
  to an ethically bad affective investment" (35). Identity prejudices in
  general are judgements "which display some (typically, epistemically
  culpable) resistance to counter-evidence owing to some affective
  investment on the part of the subject" (35) (draft pp. 2, 10). And: "The
  idea of a prejudice is most basically that of a pre-judgement ... made
  or maintained without proper regard to the evidence" (33) (draft p. 11).
  The Solomon case is at 33–34.
- **Harms and the virtue.** Primary and secondary harms (43–59, 129–46).
  The virtue of testimonial justice neutralizes prejudice in credibility
  judgements (86–92, 92), "reliably enough" (98) (draft pp. 3–4).
- **Epistemic bad luck is not testimonial injustice.** A hearer relying on
  a reliable, non-prejudicial stereotype "has not put a foot wrong" (42).
  The three cases, the shy speaker (41), the honest used car salesman and
  the liar who cries fire, are continuous, all "innocent error" (42–43);
  counting them would make the injustice too easy to commit (42) (draft
  pp. 7–8). Fricker also holds there can be testimonial injustice without
  the hearer's epistemic culpability, in the Greenleaf case (98–108)
  (draft p. 9).
- **Hermeneutical injustice.** Carmita Wood (149–50, 150). Hermeneutical
  injustices are suffered "when some significant area of their social
  experiences is obscured from collective understanding by hermeneutical
  marginalization (158)". "A group is hermeneutically marginalized when
  their hermeneutical participation with respect to significant areas of
  social experience is unequal to that of other groups, and that
  inequality redounds to this group's disadvantage (153)." It is "a matter
  of structural identity prejudice (155)" (draft pp. 12–13).
- **Systematic or incidental.** "As was the case for testimonial
  injustices, hermeneutical injustices can also be systematic, or
  incidental." A central case is one where the marginalization is
  systematic, like Wood's (draft p. 13). Harms at 162–69; the virtue of
  hermeneutical justice ameliorates them.

Maitra's own arguments:

- **Too broad (§3, draft pp. 4–7).** Zara deletes a news item because the
  writer is a "committed tea-partier"; her stereotype is identity-
  prejudicial and her deficit real, yet intuitively she does him no
  injustice, as Maitra does none to a commuter with a Southern accent.
  Hearers have "no general obligation to match our credibility judgments
  to the evidence in every case". Fricker's account needs a condition on
  when the hearer has that obligation, from a role (the juror) or a
  relationship (Greenleaf and Marge), or from what is at stake. That
  condition also explains why the jurors wrong Robinson even by switching
  off. Fricker's own worry that the injustice must not be too easy to
  commit (42) supports it.
- **Too narrow: epistemic bad luck (§4, draft pp. 7–10).** A police officer
  who dismisses a rape victim for her shifty manner, on a reliable
  stereotype, seems to wrong her where a neighbour excusing a missed lunch
  does not. Simon, in a segregated world, rejects Arthur's claim to
  numeracy on a stereotype reliable there, and Arthur suffers every harm of
  the central cases. The continuity argument fails.
- **Too narrow: stereotypes (§5, draft pp. 10–12).** David avoids black
  neighbours out of dislike and so never meets the evidence that would
  change his mind; Paul generalizes about all women from a handful. Neither
  resists counter-evidence, so neither is identity-prejudiced in Fricker's
  official sense, yet each lacks "proper regard to the evidence" (33).
  Failing to gather easy counter-evidence and generalizing from a tiny
  sample should count.
- **Individual and structural prejudice (§7, draft pp. 14–15).** Whether
  the stereotypes behind Robinson's and Marge's cases are available and
  powerful is a structural matter, and a derisive colleague could add
  individual prejudice to Wood's hermeneutical injustice. Token injustices
  of either kind mix the two in degrees, so the Simon and Arthur case,
  with structural prejudice and no individual prejudice, can be a
  testimonial injustice after all.

## Concepts

- **Credibility deficit** — credibility below the evidence that the speaker
  is offering the truth (book p. 19, as reported).
- **Identity prejudice** — a judgement for or against people qua members of
  a social group, showing resistance to counter-evidence owing to an
  affective investment (book p. 35, as reported); individual or structural.
- **Systematic against incidental** — prejudice or marginalization that
  tracks its target across several spheres of life, against prejudice that
  does not (book p. 27, as reported).
- **Hermeneutical marginalization** — unequal participation of a group in
  the practices that generate social meanings, to its disadvantage (book
  p. 153, as reported).
- **Hearer's obligation** — Maitra's condition: a duty, from a role, a
  relationship or what is at stake, to match one's credibility judgement
  to the evidence.

## Bearing on the record

- **THEORY-317, partly, and against its definitions as stated, at second
  hand.** Its promote_when asks whether the book requires identity
  prejudice for testimonial injustice, and whether hermeneutical injustice
  requires a lacuna owed to a group's powerlessness or admits local gaps;
  it is refuted as a statement of Fricker if the book differs from the
  précis on either point. On the first, Maitra reports that the book
  requires identity prejudice, individual, defined at p. 35, and excludes
  innocent error at p. 42, so THEORY-317's "prejudice" should narrow to
  identity prejudice, as its own caveat anticipates. On the second, she
  reports a generic definition through hermeneutical marginalization
  (p. 158) that is systematic or incidental, with the systematic case only
  as the central one: that differs from the précis, as THEORY-326 says.
  Marginalization is still defined for groups (p. 153), so local or
  institutional gaps that fall on no group are not shown to be admitted.
  The promote_when says secondary summaries cannot settle it, so this is
  evidence for where the book's definitions are (pp. 28, 35, 42, 153, 155,
  158), not a ruling.
- **THEORY-326**, the rival, is corroborated at second hand: a second
  source reports the book's definition in its terms.
- **QUESTION-072**, part 2, asks whether a project memory's failure to file
  a contributor's move could be a wrong in Fricker's sense. Maitra's
  condition adds a question: does whoever files owe the contributor a
  matched credibility judgement? A role, such as reviewer or curator, would
  supply one on her account.

## Limitations

- **A draft, read in place of the published text.** The published version
  was not seen; wording, pages and arguments may differ, and the draft asks
  not to be cited.
- **Second hand.** Every claim about the book rests on Maitra's paraphrases
  and quotations, which were not checked.
- **Intuition-driven.** Each of Maitra's own claims rests on judgements
  about her cases, which Fricker or a reader may not share.
- **Hermeneutical injustice gets one section,** as Maitra says the book's
  treatment is "much briefer" than its treatment of testimonial injustice
  (draft p. 1).
