# NOTE-tmp5fzuc: The Emerging Field of Emotion Regulation: An Integrative Review

## Contribution

The paper makes emotion regulation a field with one definition and one map.
Before it, the same phenomena were spread across defense, coping, mood repair,
self-control and developmental "self-soothing". It (a) fixes a definition, (b)
separates regulation from its neighbours, and (c) gives a process model that
classifies regulatory acts by the stage of emotion generation they act on. It
then surveys what seven subfields of psychology contribute, and closes with
three open problems.

## Key insight

If emotion is a sequence (situation, attention, meaning, response
tendencies, response), then regulation can be classified by which link of
the sequence it intervenes on, and interventions early in the sequence
should behave differently from those late in it. That is the whole model:
"emotion regulatory acts may be seen as having their primary impact at
different points in the emotion generative process" (p. 281). The
antecedent/response split is the version of it that experiments had tested.

## Assumptions

- **Emotion as response tendencies** (pp. 272–273), after James: "adaptive
  behavioral and physiological response tendencies that are called forth
  directly by evolutionarily significant situations", which "may be
  modulated". Figure 1 gives the "consensual process model": emotional cues,
  then evaluation, then response tendencies (behavioural, experiential,
  physiological), then modulation, then responses. Footnote 1 calls it "a
  distillation of major points of convergence" across appraisal and
  basic-emotion theorists, Lazarus (1991a) and Frijda's citations among them.
- **Emotion versus mood**: emotions are shorter, have objects, and carry
  specific behavioural tendencies; moods are diffuse and "bias cognition more
  than they bias action" (p. 273). The paper regulates emotion, not mood.
- **The nervous system as "multiple, partially independent information
  processing subsystems"** with bidirectional limbic–cortical links (p. 275).
  This is what allows regulation to act on one response channel and not
  another.
- **Regulation in self only** (p. 275). Influencing others' emotions is
  excluded, reversing Gross's earlier usage.

## Key results

The paper reports no data. Its results are definitions, distinctions and a
classification, and it cites the empirical findings below.

- **Definition** (p. 275). "Emotion regulation refers to the processes by
  which individuals influence which emotions they have, when they have them,
  and how they experience and express these emotions." Five glosses follow:
  it covers increase, maintenance and decrease of negative and positive
  emotion; regulation may differ by emotion; it is regulation of self; it
  runs on a continuum from conscious and effortful to unconscious and
  automatic; and "I make no a priori assumptions as to whether emotion
  regulation is good or bad".
- **Neighbouring constructs** (p. 276, Figure 2). Affect regulation is the
  superordinate term, over coping, emotion regulation, mood regulation and
  defense. Coping differs in its focus on decreasing negative experience and
  in taking the whole episode as its unit. Defenses are usually unconscious,
  aimed at anxiety, and studied as stable individual differences.
- **The five families** (pp. 282–285, Figure 4):
  - *situation selection*: approaching or avoiding people, places or
    objects. Avoidance can trade short-term relief for long-term cost, as
    when a shy person avoids social situations "at the cost of longer term
    social isolation" (p. 283);
  - *situation modification*: problem-focused coping in Lazarus and
    Folkman's sense, where the boundary with selection is unclear;
  - *attentional deployment*: distraction, concentration, and rumination.
    Rumination on depressive affect "leads to longer and more severe
    depressive symptoms", and worry to "long-lasting anxiety" (p. 284);
  - *cognitive change*: choosing among a situation's possible meanings,
    reappraisal included. Reappraisal of negative films lowered negative
    experience. For disgust films physiology did not always fall, which the
    paper explains by how little cognitive processing such images need
    (p. 285);
  - *response modulation*: acting directly on physiology, experience or
    expression, through drugs, exercise, relaxation, alcohol or food, and
    suppression of expression. Suppression lowered reported experience of
    some emotions (pride, amusement) but not others (disgust, sadness), and
    raised sympathetic responding (p. 285).
  The first four are antecedent-focused and the last response-focused
  (p. 282).
- **Seven stated limits of the model** (pp. 282–283): situations, aspects and
  meanings resist definition; modulation is richer than up and down; control
  may be asymmetric across channels; "situations" include internal ones;
  responses feed back into situations, giving iterated S-O-R cycles;
  regulation runs in parallel at several points; and the model is silent on
  mechanisms, relations among strategies, relative efficacy, and the
  interdependence of channels.
- **Clinical reach** (p. 280). "Emotion dysregulation is implicated in over
  half of the DSM-IV Axis I disorders and in all of the Axis II disorders"
  (citing APA 1994, Gross & Levenson 1997, Thoits 1985). Therapies are sorted
  by the same families: changing the situation (Lewinsohn et al.), its
  construal (Ellis), or the response (Deffenbacher, Novaco).
- **Three open challenges** (pp. 285–287):
  - whether regulation can be distinguished from generation. "Some theorists
    argue that emotion regulation is best seen as part and parcel of emotion
    (Frijda, 1986)". Inferring regulation needs a counterfactual unregulated
    response, so "thresholds for inferring emotion regulation should be
    high";
  - what people's regulatory goals are. They are not simply hedonic, since
    people also up-regulate negative emotion, as bill collectors do with
    anger;
  - how emotion regulation relates to other self-regulation, such as delay
    of gratification and appetite.

## Concepts

- **emotion**: a short-lived, multicomponent response tendency
  (behavioural, experiential, autonomic, neuroendocrine), called forth by an
  evaluated situation and open to modulation (pp. 272–273).
- **affect**: the superordinate category for valenced states: emotions,
  emotion episodes, moods, dispositions and traits, following Scherer (p. 273).
- **emotion episode**: the extended scene, with its protagonists, instigator
  and sequence of responses (p. 273).
- **antecedent-focused / response-focused regulation**: before versus after
  response tendencies are generated (p. 282).
- **reappraisal**: "cognitively transforming the situation so as to alter its
  emotional impact" (p. 284).
- **emotion dynamics**: the latency, rise time, magnitude, duration and
  offset of responses, which regulation changes (p. 275, after Thompson).

  later they take the side of Gross's first open challenge that Gross did not.
  On their account an emotion's own action readiness, triggered by appraising
  the consequences of acting on another, does much of the regulating, so
  "there is no clear distinction between processes of emotion and processes
  of emotion regulation". That is the position Gross attributes to Frijda
  (1986) and sets aside.

  Her 2017 account rejects the stimulus→response framing that Figure 1
  encodes. She keeps reappraisal as a phenomenon and recasts it as
  modification of predictions (her Figure 4C).

## Bearing on the record

- The reading supports a THEORY the record does not yet hold, proposed to the
  owner in this batch's report: *how* an emotion is regulated matters as
  well as *whether*, because antecedent-focused strategies such as reappraisal
  change experience at lower physiological cost than suppression. This paper
  states the distinction and cites the evidence. A THEORY should be sourced
  from the experiments and the later meta-analyses, not from this review
  alone, so it is Proposed at most on this source.
- It is a natural anchor for the batch's `psychopathology-and-treatment`
  works on transdiagnostic processes. Rumination, worry, avoidance and
  suppression all appear here as regulation strategies with costs.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice. Nothing here belongs in the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- **No data.** Every empirical claim is cited, and the experimental base for
  the antecedent/response contrast in 1998 was a handful of laboratory
  studies, mostly with films.
- **The definition of emotion is assumed, not defended.** The model needs
  emotions with a "true", unregulated course against which regulation can be
  measured. The paper itself admits that course is hard to know (p. 286).
- **The families overlap.** The paper concedes permeable boundaries at
  several points: modifying a situation can create a new one, and distraction
  can amount to selecting an internal situation.
- **The clinical figure (C5) is unsourced as a count.** "Over half" of Axis I
  disorders is not derived in the paper.

## Open questions

- Can regulation be measured independently of generation? The paper's own
  test is a reliable unregulated baseline. Constructionist and
  action-readiness accounts both deny that the baseline exists in the needed
  sense.
- Do the families differ in mechanism, or only in timing? The model is
  "silent" on mechanism (p. 283).
- What are people's regulatory goals, and are they hedonic?
