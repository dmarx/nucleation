# NOTE-tmp05o0u: Biological Embedding of Early-Life Adversity and a Scoping Review of the Evidence for Intergenerational Epigenetic Transmission of Stress and Trauma in Humans

## Contribution

A map of the human literature to mid-2023, coded by exposed parent,
timing (before conception, in pregnancy, after birth) and tissue. It is
what a search for paternal-line and preconception designs finds, in one
table.

## Key results

- **Scope** (3.1): "We identified 22 publications ... These publications
  come from 19 independent studies". 82% from North America or Europe;
  only two had more than 201 participants.
- **Timing and parent** (3.1): "In the majority of studies (n = 14), the
  exposures were assessed prior to conception." "The majority of the
  studies looked at only maternal transmission, but four considered both
  maternal and paternal ... and two focused only on fathers".
- **Tissue** (3.1): "all studies measured DNA methylation, which was
  assessed predominantly in the offspring's blood". "One study measured
  DNA methylation in the father's sperm" (Mehta et al. 2019: 38 Vietnam
  veterans, 16 with PTSD; 3 CpG sites; Table 1).
- **Yehuda et al. 2016, at second hand** (1.4.3): 32 survivors (mean age
  78), 22 offspring (mean age 46), "eight matched unexposed parent–offspring
  dyads". Methylation at "the same transcriptionally active site in both
  generations, although interestingly in opposing directions", with
  parents "10% higher" and offspring "7.7% lower". The analysis "controlled
  for current PTSD (51.6% of the Holocaust-exposed parents), childhood
  trauma in the offspring, or genetic variation in the gene." The
  reviewers add: "Potential epigenetic effects in maternal or paternal
  gametes could not, however, be investigated."
- **Yehuda et al. 2014 and Bierer et al. 2020** (Table 1): offspring
  "conceived after trauma" of Holocaust survivors. In 2014, NR3C1
  methylation was higher with paternal-only PTSD and lower with both
  parents'. In 2020, FKBP5 methylation was lower with parental exposure in
  childhood and not significant with exposure in adulthood.
- **The authors' verdict** (4.2): "The inability to detangle
  intergenerational transmission of environmental exposure via epigenetic
  modifications from other sources of parent–offspring transmission
  (including shared genetics, environment, and behaviors) will continue to
  be problematic and is something that cannot easily be resolved".

## Assumptions

- That terms used in titles and abstracts ("intergenerational",
  "transgenerational") capture the relevant studies. The authors grant this
  misses studies not using them (4.2), for example a grandmaternal
  violence study.
- Their definitions put preconception exposures under
  "intergenerational", G0 to G1, and reserve "transgenerational" for
  non-genetic transmission without direct exposure, G0 to G2.

## Concepts

- **Preconception versus pregnancy exposure.** It is the axis that
  decides whether a mother's exposure could reach the child through the
  womb.
- **Paternal-only design.** It removes the womb, and leaves rearing and
  culture.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-297](../questions.d/QUESTION-297.md), the routes** (evidence on the literature). The field's
  own survey finds no design that separates germline transmission from
  rearing or culture. Its authors say so. Paternal-only designs remove the
  womb, are two in number, and measure blood or sperm, not transmission.
- **[CLAIM-644](../claims.d/CLAIM-644.md)** (corroboration). An independent statement of the scope
  the claim takes from Horsthemke, by authors sympathetic to the
  epigenetic route.
- **[LIT-1692](../literature.d/LIT-1692.md)** (second-hand content). The FKBP5 study's controls for
  current PTSD, offspring childhood trauma and genotype are not in the
  record's account from Youssef et al. ([NOTE-1310](NOTE-1310.md)). This adds them, at
  second hand, with Zhou and Ryan's remark that gametes "could not ... be
  investigated".
- **[CLAIM-tmp3qsdg](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp3qsdg.md)** (ground). The survey bounds the search: none of the
  22 publications is described in its table as using donor gametes or
  adoption. Whether the exposed father raised the child is not coded, so
  that design is neither found nor excluded here.

## Limitations

- A scoping review by search terms, not a systematic one. Studies not
  using the two words are missing.
- Table 1 gives Yehuda et al. 2016's offspring mean age as 77, against 46
  in the text, where 77–78 is the parents' age; the table appears to give
  the wrong generation's age.
- No assessment of study quality or design beyond listing.
