# NOTE-1704: Goligher et al. — Avoiding insufficient and excessive effort

## Contribution

It turns the evidence that ventilation harms the lung and the diaphragm
into a single bedside approach: monitor the patient's respiratory drive and
effort, and adjust ventilator settings and sedation to keep effort inside a
range that protects both organs. It adds no new data.

## Key insight

The diaphragm is harmed by too much help and possibly by too little. In
the abstract's words, "the diaphragm develops atrophy as a consequence of
low respiratory effort and injury in case of excessive effort." So the aim
of support is not the most unloading but a moderate share of the work left
to the patient: "a respiratory effort level similar to that of resting
quiet breathing is probably optimal for both lung and diaphragm
protection" (principles of diaphragm-protective ventilation).

## Assumptions

- **Effort can be measured and set.** Oesophageal pressure, occlusion
  pressures, P0.1 and ultrasound thickening fraction index effort; Table 1
  gives targets, for instance an oesophageal pressure swing of 3–15 cmH2O
  as diaphragm-protective.
- **Atrophy and injury matter to outcome**, resting on the authors' cited
  cohort (Goligher et al. 2018), not shown here.
- **One target serves both organs** where possible; where lung protection
  and diaphragm protection conflict, the lung is weighed carefully.

## Key results

- **Ventilation as the source of harm (introduction).** Ventilation
  "delivered as the predominant breathing source can also lead to
  diaphragm atrophy and injury with a substantial deleterious impact on
  patient outcome", citing Goligher et al. 2018.
- **Prevalence (introduction).** Weakness at weaning "in up to 80% of
  patients with weaning difficulties" (Dres et al. 2017). The sentence
  before it says "after 24 h of mechanical ventilation, 64% of patients
  exhibit diaphragm weakness", but the study cited (ref. 9, Demoule et al.
  2013) is of dysfunction on admission to the ICU. It is weakness present
  on arrival, not loss caused by ventilation, and is not used as such here.
- **Levine's place (introduction).** "In a landmark study, Levine et al.
  demonstrated the development of diaphragm disuse atrophy in brain dead
  patients on controlled mechanical ventilation", and ultrasound studies
  tie low effort to time-dependent atrophy.
- **The injury side is unconfirmed (introduction).** Load-induced injury is
  suggested by biopsies and ultrasound, and "this hypothesis requires
  further confirmation".
- **Over-assistance (inspiratory settings).** "Excessive assist ... may
  almost abolish the patient’s the inspiratory effort, and as such promote
  diaphragmatic atrophy" (sic).
- **Support that follows effort (inspiratory settings).** "diaphragm
  inactivity due to over-assistance is unlikely in NAVA, as low diaphragm
  activity will immediately reduce inspiratory assist".
- **Sedation (sedation strategies).** "Complete suppression of respiratory
  drive and effort with sedation can also contribute to diaphragm disuse
  atrophy."
- **Not yet tested (summary).** Trials of ventilation and sedation
  strategies aimed at effort "are required to confirm the benefit of the
  lung and diaphragm-protective approach".

## Concepts

- **Lung- and diaphragm-protective ventilation**: ventilation set to limit
  lung stress and strain while keeping diaphragm effort in a physiological
  range.
- **Over-assistance**: support so high that the patient's own inspiratory
  effort nearly stops.
- **NAVA (neurally adjusted ventilatory assist)**: assist delivered in
  proportion to the diaphragm's electrical activity, so it falls when the
  patient's own drive falls.

## Connections

It cites Levine et al. (2008), [LIT-2212](../literature.d/LIT-2212.md), as the landmark study and
Jaber et al. (2011, AJRCCM) and Hooijman et al. (2015) as confirming
time-dependent atrophy; of Levine's results it restates only that disuse
atrophy developed under controlled ventilation, with no numbers, so it
stands in for the frame that grew from the study, not for the study. Its
two-sided account extends Jaber, Jung, Matecki & Petrof (2011),
[LIT-2211](../literature.d/LIT-2211.md) ([THEORY-1511](../theory.d/THEORY-1511.md)), who left the optimal effort open.

## Limitations

- A consensus-style narrative review with no new data.
- The excessive-effort side is a hypothesis; the optimal band is argued
  from physiology, "probably optimal", not from trials.
- The outcome link rests on observational cohorts.
- One prevalence figure (64% after 24 h) is cited to a study of a
  different question.

## Open questions

- Does titrating to a target effort reduce atrophy and improve weaning?
  A randomized trial of effort-targeted ventilation against usual care
  would tell.
- Does high effort injure the diaphragm in patients? Serial biopsy or
  imaging under measured high effort would tell.
