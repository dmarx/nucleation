# NOTE-tmp61nj5: Gollwitzer & Sheeran — Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis

## Contribution

Before this chapter the effect of if-then planning had been reviewed only
narratively or in small quantitative reviews (p. 70). It gives the first
large meta-analytic estimate (94 tests), sorts the tests by four problems
of goal striving (getting started, getting derailed, not calling a halt,
overextending oneself), and separately pools the tests of the two
postulated processes: heightened accessibility of the planned cue and
automatic running of the planned response.

## Key insight

A goal intention says what one wants; an implementation intention says in
advance which situation will trigger which response. Forming one
"involves a strategic abdication of effortful action control" (p. 84):
"forming an implementation intention means deciding these issues in
advance, thereby delegating the control of goal-directed behavior to
specified situational cues" (p. 100). The authors' own phrase for the
result is "instant habits" (p. 84). The plan is a pre-decided mapping from
cue to response, and its benefit is that nothing has to be deliberated in
the situation.

## Assumptions

- **The comparison is plan plus goal against goal alone.** d is the
  difference between participants who formed a goal intention and an
  implementation intention and those who formed only the goal intention
  (pp. 92, 98).
- **Inclusion.** A plan specifying a goal-directed response on an internal
  or external cue, and a retrievable statistic; searches January 1990 to
  December 2003, with unpublished studies requested from authors (p. 87).
  Eleven of the 94 tests were correlational (Table II, p. 93).
- **Pooling.** Hedges and Olkin's bias-corrected d, inverse-variance
  weighted; non-independent samples averaged and given the smallest N
  (p. 92). Fixed-effect weighting with Q tests of homogeneity; overall
  Q(93) = 173.46, p < .001 (p. 93).
- **The sample.** Of 94 tests, 79 were with university students; 38 were
  laboratory tasks and 23 health goals (Tables II and III, pp. 93, 95).

## Key results

- **Overall (p. 92).** d = .65, 95% CI [.60, .70], k = 94, N = 8,461.
- **Moderators (pp. 93–94, Table II).** No difference between
  correlational and experimental designs (.70 and .65), self-report and
  objective outcomes (.63 and .67), or published and unpublished tests (.65
  and .67). Three tests with people with psychological problems
  (schizophrenia, frontal-lobe injury, heroin withdrawal) gave d = 1.10
  against .66 for the rest, Q(1) = 4.54, p < .04.
- **Goal domains (Table III, p. 95).** Medium or large in all eight, from
  consumer (.41, k = 2) to environmental (1.12, k = 3).
- **Self-regulatory problems (Table IV, p. 96).** Getting started d = .61
  (k = 52); shielding from unwanted influences .77 (k = 21); disengaging
  from failing goals .47 (k = 3); conserving capability for a later task
  1.28 (k = 3), meaning no ego-depletion or stereotype rebound after the
  planned first task (p. 97).
- **Component processes (Table V, p. 97).** If-component (cue detection,
  discrimination, accessibility, attention, memory) d = .80, k = 6, three
  of the five measures from a single test each. Then-component: immediacy
  .77 (k = 7), efficiency under cognitive load .85 (k = 7), no need of
  conscious intent .72 (k = 3).
- **Not a motivational effect (pp. 100–101).** Studies measuring goal
  intention and self-efficacy before and after planning found no increase;
  planning still worked when both were near ceiling before it.
- **Who benefits most (p. 101).** "forming implementation intentions is
  most likely to benefit goal achievement when regulating the behavior is
  difficult or people have chronic difficulties in regulating their
  behavior."
- **Rigidity (pp. 103–104).** The authors take up whether plans make goal
  striving rigid and answer no on three counts, from narrative evidence,
  not pooled tests: plan effects appear only when the goal intention is
  strong and activated, so "action control by implementation intentions
  does not involve a mechanistic elicitation of action in the presence of
  environmental cues but rather respects the presence versus absence of
  activated strong goal intentions" (p. 103); speeding one aspect of
  performance did not cost accuracy or variety; and planners are argued,
  not shown, to stay sensitive to alternative opportunities. A fourth
  question is left open: what people do when a plan fails. "We do not know
  yet what happens" then (p. 104).
- **Plans can be bad (p. 102).** They help only if precise, viable and
  instrumental, and "How often" everyday plans meet these conditions "is
  an empirical issue".
- **Mediation (p. 106).** Only two studies had tested mediation: cue
  accessibility (Aarts et al. 1999), and cue accessibility with the
  strength of the cue–response link (Webb & Sheeran). Mediation by
  immediacy, efficiency and lack of intent was untested.
- **Repetition makes habits (p. 108).** Acting repeatedly on a plan
  produced more reported features of habit than acting on a goal intention
  (Orbell & Verplanken 2005, cited).

## Concepts

- **Goal intention**: "I intend to reach Z!"; the end of deliberation
  about what to achieve (p. 70).
- **Implementation intention**: "If situation Y is encountered, then I
  will initiate goal-directed behavior X" (p. 69). The then-part may also
  be suppressing a response or ignoring a stimulus (p. 83).
- **Intention–behaviour gap**: intenders who do not act; the median
  share was 47% in Sheeran's 2002 review (pp. 72–73).
- **Model of action phases**: predecisional, preactional, actional and
  postactional phases, after Heckhausen and Gollwitzer (pp. 74–75).
- **Instant habit**: the authors' term for the cue-triggered, automatic
  responding a plan creates without repetition (p. 84).

## Connections

It rests on Gollwitzer's theory of implementation intentions (1993, 1999)
and the model of action phases, and on Bargh's features of automaticity.
Its successor is Sheeran, Listrom & Gollwitzer's meta-analysis of 642
tests ([LIT-tmpks817](../literature.d/LIT-tmpks817.md), read in [NOTE-tmpauwdm](NOTE-tmpauwdm.md)), which finds substantial
publication bias and a much smaller corrected effect. The "if...then"
profiles of Mischel and Shoda (1995) are named as personality's own
situation–behaviour links, which a plan could be tailored to (p. 107).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-tmp0qupq](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp0qupq.md) (a learned prior over which processes govern).** The
  claim cites this work as showing that implementation intentions are
  pre-arranged if–then links of situation to response. That is right, and
  the reading sharpens what kind of link it is.
  - **It is a prior over actions, in the claim's terms.** The plan fixes
    the response to a cue in advance; its benefit is that the person no
    longer "has to deliberate in situ about what goal-directed response to
    undertake" (p. 100). That is the cached action policy the claim
    contrasts with a learned relation of influence, not an example of the
    latter. Filed as [THEORY-tmpfp8dy](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpfp8dy.md).
  - **Its gating is goal-dependent, which the claim does not mention.**
    Plan effects appear only when the goal intention behind them is active
    and strong (p. 103). So the cached mapping is not unconditional: a
    higher-level state still decides whether it fires. That is a third
    arrangement beside the claim's two (cue-to-action, and recruitment of a
    process that chooses): a cue-to-action mapping licensed by a goal.
    Filed as [THEORY-tmp8y1fr](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp8y1fr.md).
  - **The claim's test is the authors' open question.** The claim
    distinguishes the two priors by what happens "when the appropriate
    action changes". The authors raise exactly this case, a plan that
    "failed to lead to goal attainment (or even produced negative
    outcomes)", and say it has not been studied (p. 104). Twenty years
    later the 2024 review does not report it either.
  - **Some evidence of saving, not only persistence.** The claim says
    persistence is not amortization unless earlier work replaces later
    computation. The efficiency tests (planned responses unaffected by a
    concurrent load, k = 7) and the conserved-capability tests (no
    depletion or rebound afterwards, k = 3) are measured savings of later
    effort, not just stability (pp. 85, 96–97). They are small sets, and
    they show saving for a cached action, not for a learned allocation of
    authority.
- **[CLAIM-358](../claims.d/CLAIM-358.md) (learned accommodation reduces amortized cost).** The same
  efficiency and capability results are the nearest the record holds to a
  measured amortization in people: a decision taken once, in advance,
  costs less at each later encounter. They do not involve negotiation
  among processes.
- **[CLAIM-349](../claims.d/CLAIM-349.md) (access, authority and action are separable).** The ignore
  responses (p. 83; Appendix I) cut a stimulus's access to action by plan;
  the goal-dependence result keeps the plan's own authority conditional.
  A loose parallel only.
- **[CLAIM-277](../claims.d/CLAIM-277.md) (durable authority from endorsement, and its entrenchment).**
  Repetition of planned actions produced reported habit (p. 108), the
  route by which a planned mapping could entrench. Cited, not tested here.
- **[QUESTION-095](../questions.d/QUESTION-095.md) (caching computation).** The plan is a cached decision
  in the plain sense; this chapter's efficiency results bear on it.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Publication bias.** Checked only by a published/unpublished split
  (p. 94), the basis for the authors' view that the review is unlikely to suffer
  from a file-drawer problem (p. 98). No funnel plot or trim and
  fill. The 2024 review of 642 tests finds Egger's b = 1.06, "substantial",
  and a robust Bayesian estimate of d = .15 ([NOTE-tmpauwdm](NOTE-tmpauwdm.md),
  [THEORY-tmpk5znk](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpk5znk.md)).
- **Fixed-effect pooling with heterogeneous effects** (Q(93) = 173.46). The
  confidence interval [.60, .70] is narrower than a random-effects one
  would be.
- **Mostly students and laboratory tasks**; 11 correlational tests in the
  pool.
- **The process evidence is thin.** Several component estimates rest on
  one test each; mediation was tested in two studies, for accessibility and
  link strength only.
- **The rigidity defence is narrative**, and the failing-plan case is
  untested by the authors' own account.
- **Many tests are by the authors' own groups** (Table I), which the
  chapter does not discuss.
- **OCR copy.** Numbers were read from an OCR of a scan; a few were
  checked against table alignment by eye.

## Open questions

- What happens to a planned mapping when the planned response becomes the
  wrong one: is it dropped, revised or kept? A design that trains a plan,
  then changes which response is correct, would answer the authors'
  question and test [CLAIM-tmp0qupq](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp0qupq.md)'s distinction at once.
- Do immediacy, efficiency and lack of intent mediate goal attainment, as
  accessibility does?
- Does goal-dependence survive repetition, or does a repeated plan become
  a habit that fires without the goal?
