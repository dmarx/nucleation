# NOTE-tmpour9o: Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility

## Contribution

The paper makes two moves. It separates two things the principle of
alternate possibilities (PAP) runs together: what makes an action
unavoidable, and what brings it about. With the counterfactual-intervener
case, it shows they can come apart. It then argues that only the second
bears on responsibility, and proposes a replacement for PAP that keeps
coercion and compulsion as excuses without making unavoidability an excuse
in itself.

## Key insight

An excuse works by explaining. "I could not have done otherwise" excuses
when it tells us why the agent acted. When the factor that made the action
unavoidable did no work, as with Black, it explains nothing, and "it seems
quite gratuitous to assign it any weight in the assessment of his moral
responsibility" (p. 837). Responsibility is therefore settled by the
actual sequence that produced the action, not by the alternatives.

## Assumptions

- **No analysis of either concept.** The two concepts PAP employs,
  "morally responsible" and "could have done otherwise", are left
  unanalysed: "my Jolly Roger is now unfurled" (n. 1). The case is meant
  to work on any account of "could have done otherwise": Black is to use
  "whatever measures" the reader thinks sufficient (p. 835).
- **Moral intuitions about cases are evidence.** The examples "tend to
  disconfirm" PAP (p. 830); the argument rests on the judgement that
  Jones4 is responsible.
- **Black can predict the decision.** This is secured by a sign, a twitch
  that precedes deciding on A and never deciding on B. It assumes a
  causal relation between the twitch and later states but "does not
  require that these earlier phases be deterministically related to still
  earlier events" (n. 3).
- **A responsibility-relevant fact must figure in explaining the action**
  (§V). This is the premise the dilemma defence later presses.

## Key results

- **§I The target (pp. 829–830).** PAP: "a person is morally responsible
  for what he has done only if he could have done otherwise". Some call it
  "an a priori truth"; "practically no one … seems inclined to deny or
  even to question" it. "But the principle of alternate possibilities is
  false." Usual illustrations are cases in which "the same circumstances
  both bring it about that a person does something and make it impossible
  for him to avoid doing it" (coercion, hypnosis, inner compulsion). The
  paper looks for circumstances that make the action unavoidable "without
  them playing any role, indeed, in bringing it about" (p. 830).
- **§§II–III Coercion (pp. 830–834).** The doctrine that coercion
  excludes responsibility "is not correctly understood … as a particularized
  version" of PAP (p. 831). Three versions of a threatened Jones who had
  already decided to do what is demanded:
  - Jones1, unmoved by threats, is not coerced and keeps his
    responsibility; no counterexample to anything (pp. 831–832).
  - Jones2, stampeded, acts "entirely because he was terrified"; his
    earlier decision "played no role", so he is not responsible for the
    action (p. 832).
  - Jones3 would have submitted to the threat, but acted "solely by the
    considerations that had originally commended the action to him"; "his
    moral responsibility for what he did [is] unaffected by the threat even
    though … he could not have avoided doing what he did" (pp. 832–833).
  Either Jones3 was not coerced, so coercion requires acting because of the
  threat, or he was, and "being coerced does not exclude being morally
  responsible". Either way, "When we excuse a person who has been coerced,
  we do not excuse him because he was unable to do otherwise" (pp.
  833–834).
- **§IV The intervener (pp. 834–836).** The objection that Jones3 could
  still have defied the threat is met by changing the example. Black waits
  until Jones4 "is about to make up his mind"; if Jones4 is going to decide
  otherwise, Black "takes effective steps", by threat, potion, hypnosis or
  manipulation of "the minute processes of Jones4's brain", so "Whatever
  Jones4's initial preferences and inclinations, then, Black will have his
  way" (p. 835). Jones4 decides "for reasons of his own" to do what Black
  wants; Black never shows his hand. "Jones4 will bear precisely the same
  moral responsibility … as he would have borne if Black had not been
  ready"; it would be "quite unreasonable to excuse Jones4 for his action,
  or to withhold the praise to which it would normally entitle him" (p.
  836). "What action he performs is not up to him", though "it is in a way
  up to him whether he acts on his own or as a result of Black's
  intervention" (p. 836). Black may be replaced by a machine, or by
  "natural forces involving no will or design at all" (n. 4).
- **§V Why PAP fails (pp. 836–838).** Unavoidability is "a sufficient
  condition of his having done it" but "may play no role whatever in the
  explanation of why he did it" (p. 836). Then the circumstances "could
  have been subtracted from the situation without affecting what happened
  or why it happened in any way" (p. 837), and they give no ground for
  excuse. We accept "I could not have done otherwise" as an excuse
  "because we assume that what they tell us serves to explain why they did
  what they did" (pp. 837–838).
- **§V The replacement (pp. 838–839).** The first revision, not
  responsible "if he did it because he could not have done otherwise", is
  rejected: given the incompatibilist reading of "could have done
  otherwise" it entails that "a person is not morally responsible for what
  he has done if it was causally determined that he do it" (p. 838). The
  proposal: "a person is not morally responsible for what he has done if
  he did it only because he could not have done otherwise", that is, when
  "it was not because that was what he really wanted to do" (p. 838). It
  "does not appear to conflict with the view that moral responsibility is
  compatible with determinism" (pp. 838–839). An agent who acted because
  he could not do otherwise, but also because "it was what he really
  wanted to do", may be responsible (p. 839).

## Concepts

- **The principle of alternate possibilities (PAP).** "A person is morally
  responsible for what he has done only if he could have done otherwise"
  (p. 829).
- **Bringing about vs. making unavoidable.** Circumstances that "constitute
  sufficient conditions for a certain action" may still not "actually impel
  the person to act or in any way produce his action" (p. 830). This is
  the distinction Frankfurt-style cases trade on.
- **Coercion.** A threat coerces only if it "is what actually accounts for
  his doing it" (p. 833).
- **Acting "only because" one could not do otherwise.** Acting because of
  the constraint and not because "that was what he really wanted to do"
  (p. 838).

## Limitations

- **One case, one intuition.** C1 rests on the judgement that Jones4 is
  responsible. No argument is given for it beyond that "everything
  happened just as it would have happened without Black's presence" (p.
  836).
- **The prior sign opens the dilemma.** The twitch precedes the decision
  and is causally related to it (n. 3). Whether it is a deterministic sign
  is left open, which is exactly what the later dilemma defence exploits
  ([LIT-294](../literature.d/LIT-294.md), §4.3); the paper does not anticipate the defence.
- **Flickers are not addressed.** Jones4 still could have shown the sign
  and acted on Black's intervention rather than "on his own", which the
  paper concedes is "in a way up to him" (p. 836) but does not treat as an
  alternative that might matter.
- **The replacement is offered "briefly and without argument"** (p. 830).
  "What he really wanted to do" is not analysed, and the claim that it is
  compatible with determinism is hedged ("does not appear to conflict").
- **The text read is an OCR of a scan.** All quotations above were checked
  against the page image or the reprint; the subscripts on Jones are the
  reprint's.

## Open questions

- Does the case survive the dilemma defence: if the twitch determines the
  decision, is Jones4 responsible on libertarian terms; if it does not,
  does Jones4 have a robust alternative ([LIT-294](../literature.d/LIT-294.md), §4.3)?
- What exactly is "what he really wanted to do"? The 1971 paper's answer is
  the will he wants ([LIT-tmpuutyy](../literature.d/LIT-tmpuutyy.md), p. 19); whether that is the condition
  this paper intends is not said here.
- Is the result symmetric for blame and praise, as p. 836 says, against
  Wolf's asymmetry?
