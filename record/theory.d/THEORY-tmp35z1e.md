# THEORY-tmp35z1e: Gardiner argues that climate change joins a global commons problem to an intergenerational one that institutions and reciprocity cannot solve, because the generations do not coexist, and that these, with our theories' weakness, leave each generation open to moral corruption that hides its exploitation of its temporal position, even from itself

## The account

Three characteristics, dispersion of causes and effects, fragmentation of
agency and institutional inadequacy, read spatially give a tragedy of the
commons between states and read temporally give an intergenerational storm
(pp. 399, 402). Carbon dioxide's long tail in the atmosphere makes climate
change resilient, backloaded and deferred (pp. 402–403).

In the Pure Intergenerational Problem every generation prefers that all
restrict pollution, and each does better by overpolluting whatever the
others do. It is worse than the Prisoner's Dilemma: the first generation's
defection has a domino effect, and "the parties do not coexist" (p. 405), so
neither coercion mutually agreed upon nor reciprocity is available.
Temporally scattered agents "cannot actually become unified, and so may at
best only act as if they were a single agent" (p. 404).

Together with a theoretical storm, our theories' difficulties with
uncertainty, intergenerational equity and contingent persons (p. 407), the
storm "makes us extremely vulnerable to moral corruption" (p. 398). A
generation can herald weak accords while "simply exploiting its temporal
position", without "the unpleasantness of admitting it – either to others,
or, perhaps more importantly, to itself" (pp. 408–409).

## Evidence

An argument, not a study. The global and intergenerational models are stated
rather than derived here, generations are treated as unitary and
non-overlapping, and the climate figures are of 2005 ([NOTE-1252](../notes.d/NOTE-1252.md),
Limitations). That the intergenerational problem dominates is "in my view"
(p. 405); that theory itself may be corrupted is offered as "a very
unpleasant thought" (p. 408).
