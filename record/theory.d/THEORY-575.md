# THEORY-575: 'Social group' names two kinds of thing: organized social groups, such as teams, courts and families, are structured wholes whose people occupy the nodes of an internal social structure, while feature social groups, such as women, racial groups and classes, are social kinds, nodes in external social structures, and are not concrete wholes

## Source

Katherine Ritchie (2020, online 20 September 2018), "Social Structures and
the Ontology of Social Groups", [LIT-486](../literature.d/LIT-486.md), read in full in [NOTE-389](../notes.d/NOTE-389.md). Page
numbers are the journal's. The organized-group half is carried over from
Ritchie (2013), [LIT-479](../literature.d/LIT-479.md) ([NOTE-390](../notes.d/NOTE-390.md); [THEORY-549](THEORY-549.md)).

## What was actually shown

**Social structure (p. 407).** "A social structure is a structure that is
constitutively dependent on social factors." Constitutive dependence is a
disjunction: defining S needs social factors, or they are metaphysically
necessary for S, or they ground S. Merely causal dependence (lactose
tolerance and dairy farming) is not enough.

**Three distinctions (pp. 408–410).** Overt or covert; intentionally or
unintentionally created, and instantiated; internal or external relative to
a realization. A structure is internal if every node is occupied by
elements of the system and every element occupies a node. The American
League structure is internal to the league and external to each team
(p. 410).

**Organized social groups (pp. 410–414).** Structured wholes. Their
structures are social, their node occupiers are people, social creatures
or structured groups of them (p. 413), and they are often overt and
intentional (p. 414). Coincident clubs with the same members are distinct
(p. 412). Groups can be members of groups: Congress with the House and
Senate (n. 24). People in an X shape, women and African Americans are not
organized groups (p. 414).

**Feature social groups (pp. 414–420).** Groups whose members share a
feature broadly construed. They are social kinds (p. 415) and, on the
proposal, nodes in social structures (p. 416). Haslanger's and MacKinnon's
accounts of gender read as identifying *women* with a node related to *men*
by subordination, with the bodily marker as a further requirement
(pp. 409, 416–417). Ásta's conferralism reads as a node whose occupation
requires a conferred status (p. 417). Intersectional groups are nodes in
distinct, more complex structures, not sums of single-axis ones
(pp. 417–419).

**Goldilocks (p. 420).** No actual social structure has a node for people
whose left ear is larger than their right, or for people who have visited
Boise.

**What could have come out otherwise.** A standard account of gender could
have resisted being read as a node. Three did not. The paper names one view
that would: gender fixed by chromosomes alone (p. 417; [NOTE-389](../notes.d/NOTE-389.md) corrects
the paper's "could be accommodated" to "could not be").

## What this does not say

- **That every feature group is a node.** The paper shows the framework
  accommodates three views of gender. It does not argue against rivals,
  and its author says "more would need to be done" (p. 414) and
  "significantly more would need to be said" about intersectionality
  (p. 419).
- **That structuralism keeps arbitrary collections out.** The restriction
  to structures that "(a) intuitively exist or (b) figure in causal or other
  explanations" (p. 410) does that work. It is not derived from the
  framework, and any ontology could adopt it.
- **That covert, unintended structures cause oppression.** Offered as a
  possibility, "it might be" (p. 420).
- **That "social structure" is one relation.** Constitutive dependence is
  three relations (definitional, modal, grounding) joined by "or".
- **Anything about agency or minds.** The words do not occur. What follows
  is the record's application ([NOTE-389](../notes.d/NOTE-389.md), [LIT-486](../literature.d/LIT-486.md) Standing): only organized
  groups are candidates for agency or consciousness, since a kind does not
  act. Every group agent in List's examples ([NOTE-340](../notes.d/NOTE-340.md)) is an organized
  group. Talk of what "women" want is about an organization formed from
  members (n. 25) or is not literal. Schwitzgebel's United States, with
  artefacts among its parts ([NOTE-381](../notes.d/NOTE-381.md)), fails condition (ii) and falls
  among the wider social entities of p. 411.
