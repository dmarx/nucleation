# THEORY-tmpjlg7h: In groups that build a new communication system, larger groups make it more systematic, and the greater variety of input they face drives the effect

## Source

Limor Raviv, Antje Meyer and Shiri Lev-Ari (2019), *Proc. R. Soc. B*
286:20191262, LIT-825; read in NOTE-628 (main text; the supplementary
models were not read). What the experiment showed is THEORY-164, which
this entry extends.

## The claim

When people who share no language build one by communicating, the size of
the group they communicate in shapes the result. With everyone able to
talk to everyone and the same total amount of interaction, members of
larger groups end with languages whose forms map more systematically onto
meanings, reach that structure sooner, and differ less from one group to
the next. Members of small groups can sustain idiosyncratic, holistic
labels, because with few partners and much shared history they can
memorise them. The cause the paper argues for is input variability: more
partners means more competing variants to cope with, which favours forms
that are predictable from their meaning. The authors argue that the result
should scale to real communities and supports community size as a driver
of language evolution.

The experiment had two sizes, four and eight, with 12 groups each, of
Dutch adults, over a written channel, in a meaning space designed to
reward compositional structure. Input variability was measured, not
varied, and less shared history per pair travels with group size.

## What would settle it

The promote_when: a replication by other hands or with another meaning
space and channel, and a study that manipulates input variability at a
fixed group size. Correlations across natural languages cannot settle it,
since they carry the confounds of network density and adult
second-language learners that the experiment removed.
