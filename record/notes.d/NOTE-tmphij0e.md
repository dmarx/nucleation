# NOTE-tmphij0e: Bechara, Damasio, Tranel & Damasio — Deciding advantageously before knowing the advantageous strategy

## Contribution

Earlier work had shown that ventromedial patients fail the gambling task and
lack anticipatory SCRs (Bechara et al. 1994, 1996). This report adds a third
measure taken in parallel: what participants can say about the game, asked
repeatedly during play. That lets the authors place choice, bodily signal and
reportable knowledge on one timeline per group, and so ask whether advantageous
choice waits for knowledge.

## Key insight

Knowledge is neither necessary nor sufficient for good choice in this task.
Not sufficient, because patients who could state the correct strategy still
chose badly. Not necessary, the authors argue, because controls produced
anticipatory SCRs, and began to favour the good decks, before they could
say what was going on. Their reading is two parallel chains from the
situation: one through ventromedial "dispositional knowledge" of past
emotional experience to autonomic and neurotransmitter nuclei, whose
nonconscious signals bias evaluation; the other through overt recall of facts
and reasoning over them. The biases "would not decide per se, but rather
facilitate" the reasoning (p. 1294).

## Assumptions

- **Participants.** 10 normal controls; 6 patients with bilateral
  ventromedial prefrontal lesions and real-life decision-making defects
  (3 women aged 53, 63, 64; 3 men aged 51, 52, 65; education 13 ± 2 years,
  verbal IQ 111 ± 8, performance IQ 102 ± 8, mean ± SEM; note 3). Control
  demographics are not given; note 4 says their results resemble earlier
  controls'.
- **Task.** Four decks; A and B pay $100 a card, C and D $50; unpredictable
  penalties, large in A and B and small in C and D, so A and B lose overall.
  $2000 facsimile loan; stopped after 100 selections, which players are not
  told.
- **Awareness measure.** After card 20, and every 10 cards after, play is
  interrupted and two questions asked: "Tell me all you know about what is
  going on in this game" and "Tell me how you feel about this game." Answers
  are audiotaped and coded into periods. Nothing before card 20 is probed.
- **Periods** (Fig. 1 caption). *Pre-punishment:* before the first loss
  (about card 10). *Pre-hunch:* "no notion of what was happening", controls
  roughly cards 10–50, patients 9–80. *Hunch:* "liking" or "disliking"
  decks and guessing which were risky, unsure; controls roughly cards 50–80;
  never reached by patients. *Conceptual:* can say which decks are good or
  bad and why; after about card 80; reached by 7 of 10 controls and 3 of 6
  patients.
- **SCR as index.** An anticipatory SCR is one generated in the window
  before turning a card, "during the time the subject ponders" (Fig. 1
  caption). It is taken to index a nonconscious somatic signal.

## Key results

- **Narrative (p. 1293).** Before losses, everyone prefers A and B and shows
  no significant anticipatory SCRs. After a few losses (usually by card 10),
  controls begin anticipatory SCRs "to decks A and B", yet at card 20 all
  say they have no clue. By about card 50 all controls voice a hunch and
  generate anticipatory SCRs before A or B. The three controls who never
  reached the conceptual period "still made advantageous choices". No
  patient produced anticipatory SCRs or a hunch.
- **SCR statistics (note 8).** Three-way ANOVA (group × period
  [pre-punishment, pre-hunch] × deck [bad, good]). The reported key effect is
  group × period, F(1,14) = 16.24, P < 0.001. Newman-Keuls: no group or deck
  differences at baseline; SCRs rose from baseline to pre-hunch only in
  controls (P < 0.01), and controls' pre-hunch SCRs exceeded patients' in
  both periods (P < 0.01). No deck effect or deck interaction is reported
  for these periods. All controls and no patients generated anticipatory
  SCRs: Fisher's exact test, one-sided P < 0.001. In the hunch and
  conceptual periods, bad-deck SCRs were "generally higher" than good-deck
  ones but "the difference did not reach statistical significance"; bad-deck
  (not good-deck) SCRs in those periods exceeded baseline (P < 0.01).
- **Choice statistics (note 9).** ANOVA on cards selected: group × period ×
  deck, F(1,14) = 6.9, P < 0.02. Pre-hunch: patients chose significantly
  more bad than good cards (P < 0.01); controls chose more good than bad,
  "not statistically significant". Hunch and conceptual: controls chose
  significantly more good cards (P < 0.01). Conceptual: patients still chose
  more bad cards, not significantly.
- **Model (p. 1294, Fig. 2).** Two interacting chains, as in the key insight.
  The ventromedial cortex holds the dispositional link; its activation drives
  autonomic and neurotransmitter (e.g. dopamine) nuclei; the signals bias
  cognitive evaluation. Damage "acts by precluding access to a particular
  kind of record of previous and related individual experience". Note 7
  places the bias mechanism alongside response inhibition, working memory
  and selective attention: "an addition ... rather than an alternative".

## Concepts

- **Anticipatory SCR.** A skin-conductance response in the interval before a
  card is chosen, while the participant ponders; contrasted with SCRs to the
  reward or penalty itself.
- **Pre-hunch, hunch, conceptual.** Periods defined from the verbal
  reports (see Assumptions). A hunch is liking or disliking decks and
  guessing which are risky, without certainty.
- **Covert bias.** A nonconscious signal, from autonomic and
  neurotransmitter activity, that facilitates rather than makes a decision.
- **Dispositional knowledge.** Nondeclarative knowledge related to previous
  emotional experience of similar situations, held, the authors suspect, in
  ventromedial cortex (p. 1294). The term is from Damasio's convergence-zone
  work.

## Bearing on the record

- **No THEORY bears on it.** If the record later wants an account of affect
  in decision, this paper supports the group difference in anticipatory
  SCRs and the insufficiency of stated knowledge in three patients. It does
  not by itself support "nonconscious biases come first"; that needs the
  deck-specific pre-hunch tests this paper does not report, and a reading of
  the awareness critique.
- **Topic.** Tagged `cognition` first: the paper is about how a decision is
  reached and what the decider knows. `consciousness` for conscious versus
  nonconscious knowledge, `emotion-and-affect` for the bodily and emotional
  signal, `neuroscience` for the lesion and autonomic method. Nothing else is
  left unsaid.
- **No ML instruction.** Nothing here belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Sample.** 10 controls and 6 patients; control demographics unstated;
  the conceptual-period patient result rests on 3 people.
- **Awareness probe.** Two open questions, from card 20, every 10 cards. A
  participant who knows something but does not volunteer it is coded as not
  knowing it, and nothing is asked during cards 10–20, when the SCRs are said
  to begin.
- **Abstract versus notes.** The abstract's "began to choose advantageously
  before they realized which strategy worked best" and "whenever they
  pondered a choice that turned out to be risky, before they knew" outrun
  notes 8 and 9, as set out in the claims table.
- **Correlation.** The SCRs and the choices are measured together; nothing
  here shows that the SCR, or what it indexes, causes the choice.
- **Mechanism.** The two-chain model and the ventromedial role in it are
  stated, not tested.

## Open questions

- Is there a bad-versus-good deck SCR difference in controls during the
  pre-hunch period? The analysis in note 8 does not report one.
- Do controls in the pre-hunch period know more than the two questions
  elicit? This is the question the later critique takes up.
- Does blocking or reducing the peripheral signal change choices, which would
  bear on whether the SCR is a cause or a by-product?
