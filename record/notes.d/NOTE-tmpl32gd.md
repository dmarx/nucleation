summary: >-
  A review of the evidence that hosts import their own proteins into
  bacterial endosymbionts, which has long been taken to mark an organelle
  off from an endosymbiont. Import of hundreds of nucleus-encoded proteins
  is established, by proteomics of isolated endosymbionts, in two systems
  only: the chromatophore of Paulinella and the nitroplast of
  Braarudosphaera. In both, the imported proteins come mostly from the
  host's own genes and from bacteria other than the endosymbiont, and the
  authors say that gene transfer from the endosymbiont "is not a
  precondition" for genetic integration. Elsewhere, in a trypanosomatid,
  a diatom and four insects, there are one to seven imported or targeted
  proteins per system, each found by antibodies or proteomics. The
  authors propose an order, effector proteins first and dependence on an
  imported redundant protein second, which they call plausible on
  limited data. The import mechanisms are almost entirely unknown.

# NOTE-tmpl32gd: Sørensen, Stiller, Kröninger & Nowack — Protein import into bacterial endosymbionts and evolving organelles

## Contribution

It gathers, system by system, the direct evidence that host-encoded
proteins reach the inside of bacterial endosymbionts. Before it, the
textbook line was that endosymbionts are "genetically autonomous": they
take metabolites from the host but not proteins. The review shows two
cases of import at scale and a scatter of single proteins in others. It
also separates two things the record had run together: gene transfer
from the endosymbiont to the nucleus, and genetic integration by import.
The second happens without the first.

## Key insight

An organelle, on the authors' definition, is an endosymbiont "in which
essential endosymbiont genes have been functionally replaced by nuclear
genes with products that are imported into the endosymbiont". The
replacing genes need not come from the endosymbiont. In *Paulinella* the
hundreds of imported proteins "derive from EGTs and HGTs, but mostly from
the host itself". In the nitroplast, "Evidence for EGT has not been
found", yet more than 350 nucleus-encoded proteins are imported. Hence:
"It is important to note that EGT is not a precondition for the genetic
integration of an endosymbiont." What makes the organelle is a host
protein that reaches inside and a symbiont gene that is then lost.

## Assumptions

- **Proteomics of isolated endosymbionts detects import.** A host protein
  enriched in an endosymbiont fraction is taken as imported. For the
  diatom, the host membrane is lost on isolation, so enrichment shows
  targeting "at least across this outermost host membrane", not
  translocation into the bacterium.
- **Antibody localization shows import.** Single-protein cases (RlpA4 in
  *Buchnera*, the GOT enzymes in *Nardonella*, ColA in *Sodalis*, MurF in
  *Moranella*) rest on immunofluorescence or immunogold microscopy. The
  weevil case is flagged: "Further experiments are needed to confirm the
  import of these proteins".
- **Absence of evidence is weak.** Proteome coverage is partial: 6.5% of
  predicted nucleus-encoded proteins in the diatom, and 1940 of 34 616
  predicted aphid proteins (5.6%). Systems with few imported proteins may
  have more.
- **A metabolically integrated endosymbiont "should, in theory, be viable
  outside the host when supplemented with the right metabolites".** This
  is the test that separates the two kinds of integration. It is stated,
  not applied to any system.

## Key results

- **Mitochondria and plastids.** Their genomes encode "no more than 66
  and 240 proteins". Their import channels are of mixed origin: Tom40
  has no identified bacterial precursor, Toc75 is related to bacterial
  BamA, and the Tim17/22/23 family's ancestor "remains obscure". Bacteria
  do not have import systems to start from, since export systems "cannot
  be simply operated in reverse". So organellogenesis needed "a major
  restructuring of the bacterial envelope".
- **Envelope loss in reduced symbionts (Figure 2A, KEGG
  presence/absence).** Some endosymbionts lost phospholipid, PG or LPS
  synthesis, and Sec, Tat and BAM. In genomes under 0.2 Mbp "often all of
  the above-mentioned envelope-related functions are missing", so they
  "completely depend on host-derived lipids and presumably protein
  complexes". Only the caption of Figure 2 was read.
- **The chromatophore of *Paulinella*.**
  - Acquired "~ 90–140 million years ago" from a cyanobacterium of the
    *Synechococcus*/*Prochlorococcus* clade.
  - Genomes of ~1 Mbp with "878–860 protein‐coding genes" lost "around
    2/3 of their original coding capacity".
  - About 60 EGT-derived and 170 HGT-derived nuclear genes add to the
    complementarity.
  - Radiolabelling with ribosome-specific inhibitors showed the
    EGT-derived photosystem I subunits PsaE and PsaK made on host
    ribosomes and assembled into chromatophore photosystem I. PsaE was
    also seen in the Golgi.
  - Proteomics found "hundreds" of imported proteins in two classes:
    short ones (under 90 aa) without obvious signals, and long ones
    (over 250 aa) with a ~200 aa bipartite "chromatophore transit
    peptide" whose first ~50 aa are cleaved and the rest retained.
  - About 20% of long imported proteins have genetic-information roles
    (DNA polymerase I, helicases, a ligase, transcription factors), and a
    family of ~20 octotrico peptide repeat proteins suggests control of
    chromatophore gene expression. "In sum, the chromatophore is a new
    genetically integrated photosynthetic organelle."
  - The route runs through the secretory pathway, "very different" from
    TIC/TOC and TIM/TOM. The outer chromatophore membrane is
    "interpreted" from micrographs as host-derived.
- **The nitroplast (UCYN-A in *Braarudosphaera bigelowii*).**
  - About 91 million years of coevolution. Genome ~1.4 Mbp, without
    photosynthesis or carbon fixation, with all *nif* genes.
  - One UCYN-A per host cell, dividing with it and vertically transmitted.
    But "when nitrogen compounds are externally provided in culture, B.
    bigelowii can lose UCYN‐A".
  - No EGT found. More than 350 nucleus-encoded proteins imported, many
    filling pathway gaps. They include a methionine-tRNA formyltransferase
    that "likely replaces" the lost *fmt* gene.
  - 62% of enriched host proteins carry a C-terminal extension, the
    "UCYN-A transit peptide", presumably cleaved on import. Its C-terminal
    position suggests proteins are folded before recognition.
  - "the nitroplast shows a similar integration level as the chromatophore".
- **Spheroid bodies in *Epithemia*.** 12–35 million years old, genomes
  2.5–3.0 Mbp. Vertically transmitted, two to 16 per cell. Only six host
  proteins enriched, none of bacterial origin. They "apparently have not
  reached the same level of genetic integration".
- ***Kinetoplastibacterium* in *Angomonas*.** Genome 0.8 Mbp, one
  symbiont per host, division synchronized. Seven targeted host proteins
  found with fluorescent fusions. Two localize at the endosymbiont
  division site: a predicted PG hydrolase and a dynamin-like protein,
  which suggests "nuclear control over endosymbiont division". One EGT
  gene is known, and its product goes to glycosomes, not the symbiont.
- **Insects.**
  - *Buchnera* (0.64 Mbp): no host protein "reliably enriched" in the
    symbiont by proteomics. One protein, RlpA4, was localized to *Buchnera*'s
    cytoplasm with antibodies. It is encoded by a gene horizontally
    transferred from a bacterium other than *Buchnera*. "no functional EGT
    occurred in the pea aphid". Bacteriocyte-specific cysteine-rich
    peptides (BCRs) kill *E. coli* in vitro. Whether they enter *Buchnera*
    has "yet to be demonstrated".
  - *Nardonella* in the red palm weevil (0.20–0.23 Mbp, 196–231 genes):
    two host transaminases complete its one remaining job, tyrosine
    synthesis, and were seen in its cytoplasm. Import is not yet confirmed.
  - *Sodalis* in the cereal weevil (4.51 Mbp, replacing *Nardonella* 20
    million years ago): the host antimicrobial peptide ColA enters the
    symbiont's cytoplasm and regulates its growth. "relatively recently
    captured endosymbionts can already be targeted by host effector
    proteins".
  - The mealybug: the imported protein is MurF, found in the cytoplasm of
    *Moranella* (0.54 Mbp), the inner symbiont, not *Tremblaya* (0.14
    Mbp). Its gene came by HGT. It must cross five membranes. One protein
    doing so makes it "in our opinion likely" that a general mechanism
    exists.
- **Rhizobia.** *Medicago* targets about 700 NCR peptides to its
  bacteroids, which control division, endoreduplication and translation.
  Yet "we would not consider rhizobia as genetically integrated
  organelles". They are not vertically transmitted, keep their soil life,
  and no endosymbiont function has been replaced by a nuclear gene.
  Effector manipulation is "an effective alternative to the genetic
  integration that is always linked to a permanent host/endosymbiont
  association with vertical transmission".
- **A scenario.** "A scenario for progressing organellogenesis that
  appears plausible in the light of the available, though limited,
  data":
  1. The host first targets effector proteins into the endosymbiont.
  2. A nuclear protein redundant with a symbiont function gains import.
     Mutations in the symbiont copy can then fix.
  3. If metabolite import cannot replace the process, the endosymbiont
     becomes dependent.
  4. A general import mechanism with a targeting signal "likely spreads
     quickly", bringing hundreds of proteins.

  Whether EGT contributes depends on how many genes are replaced, the
  host's access to HGT, and the rate of nuclear integration of symbiont
  DNA.

## Concepts

- **Genetic integration**: essential endosymbiont genes "functionally
  replaced by nuclear genes with products that are imported into the
  endosymbiont". The authors' mark of an organelle.
- **Metabolic integration**: the endosymbiont receives only metabolites,
  though host effectors may still act on it. In theory such a symbiont is
  viable outside the host with the right metabolites.
- **EGT**: endosymbiotic gene transfer, from the endosymbiont to the
  nucleus. Distinguished from HGT, transfer from other bacteria.
- **ETP**: endosymbiont-targeted protein.
- **crTP and uTP**: the chromatophore transit peptide (N-terminal,
  bipartite, partly retained) and the UCYN-A transit peptide
  (C-terminal, cleaved).
- **Effector**: a host protein that manipulates the endosymbiont without
  replacing a lost function (NCRs, ColA, perhaps BCRs).

## Connections

It is the review that McCutcheon et al. (LIT-1571, read in NOTE-1213)
point to for import. The two agree that the smallest symbionts lack
envelope genes. This review sharpens the import evidence that
McCutcheon et al. cite. The aphid protein is RlpA4 in *Buchnera*, and the
mealybug protein is MurF in *Moranella*. Both symbionts are above 0.5
Mbp, well above McCutcheon's 200 kb tipping point. The *Paulinella*
primary studies (LIT-1557, LIT-1621) and the nitroplast paper (LIT-1562)
are cited here and are unread in this record. The mealybug HGT genes are
LIT-1569's subject.

## Bearing on the record

- **QUESTION-277, the gene-transfer-and-import route.** The question
  calls the route "Genes move to the host nucleus and their products are
  imported back". This reading corrects that. Import is the route.
  Transfer from the endosymbiont is optional and was minor in
  *Paulinella* and absent in the nitroplast. The replacing genes are
  mostly the host's own, or come from third-party bacteria. So the route
  moves C (control by import) and G (functions now on the host genome),
  and the G move does not need the symbiont's genes to travel. The C move
  includes control of the symbiont's DNA replication, expression and
  division. The inference that this is one route with two variants is
  mine.
- **QUESTION-277, the order of dimensions.** The authors' scenario puts
  C first, as effectors, and D after, when a redundant imported protein
  lets the symbiont copy decay. Their rhizobia argument says genetic
  integration is "always linked" to vertical transmission (R). So on
  their account R is a condition for the C-then-D sequence to end in an
  organelle, and C without R (rhizobia) stops at manipulation. This is an
  order to test, not one shown. The mapping to the question's letters is
  mine.
- **QUESTION-277, directional dependence.** *B. bigelowii* can lose
  UCYN-A when given fixed nitrogen. The organelle-level integration of
  the symbiont therefore coexists with a host whose dependence is
  conditional on its environment. D is asymmetric even here. The
  inference is mine.
- **CASE-174 (*Paulinella*).** The case's figures are from its unread
  primary sources. This review gives a later age (90–140 million years,
  against the case's "about 60"), about 60 EGT and 170 HGT genes
  (against "more than thirty"), and names two imported photosystem I
  subunits, PsaE and PsaK (the case says three). It says EGT played "only
  a circumstantial role". The case title's "genes moved to the host
  nucleus and their proteins imported back" overstates transfer.
- **CASE-167 (the nitroplast).** Confirmed: coordinated division, vertical
  transmission, more than 350 imported proteins. Added: no EGT, a
  C-terminal targeting signal on 62% of imported proteins, and a host
  that can lose the symbiont in culture.
- **CASE-171 and CASE-173.** The mealybug protein is MurF, in
  *Moranella*, not *Tremblaya*. The aphid protein is RlpA4, from a
  horizontally transferred gene, and proteomics found no host protein
  reliably enriched in *Buchnera*. Both cases' notes from NOTE-1213 should
  say so.
- **THEORY-1049.** Consistent with its envelope claim. But the single
  imported proteins it cites as evidence of host supply are in symbionts
  above both its thresholds. The best case near 200 kb is *Nardonella*,
  whose import is unconfirmed. So import into a tipping-point
  endosymbiont is still not shown.
- Filed as THEORY-tmp74xnn and THEORY-tmpkvjih. No instruction for
  machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **A review by one group,** one of whose authors holds a fellowship
  named for chromatophores (Acknowledgements). The two systems with
  import at scale are its strongest evidence, and the others are cited
  from primary studies not read here.
- **Single-protein evidence is antibody-based.** Cross-reactivity is a
  general risk. The review reads ColA antibodies binding *Nardonella* as
  a sign of ColA's wide reach; that it could also be cross-reaction is my
  reading, not the paper's. No single-protein case has a mechanism.
- **"More common than generally realized" is a belief,** stated as such,
  from partial proteomes. No system has been shown to lack import.
- **The scenario is a narrative.** No system has been followed through
  its steps, and the order of effector targeting and dependence is not
  observed anywhere.
- **Figures were not seen.** Figure 2A's gene matrix and Figure 3's
  membrane counts were read only from captions and text.
- **The *Paulinella* gene count is printed "878–860"**, and the genome
  fraction lost is "around 2/3", where the record's case says 26% kept.
  The two use different reference genomes, presumably. The review does
  not say which.

## Open questions

- Is any protein imported into a symbiont under 200 kb? *Tremblaya* and
  *Nardonella* are the candidates.
- How does a protein cross the bacterial inner membrane in any of these
  systems? Only signals are known.
- Do effectors in fact come first? A system caught with effectors but
  no replaced function, then followed, would show it. *Angomonas*, with
  genetic tools and seven ETPs, is the authors' suggested model.
- Can a metabolically integrated symbiont be grown outside its host with
  supplements? That is the authors' own criterion, untested.
- Does genetic integration ever arise without vertical transmission?
  The rhizobia contrast says no, but it is one contrast.
