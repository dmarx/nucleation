# CASE-tmpq8ynx: Labov's recorded sounding sessions: ritual insult turning personal, with the same exchange shape and a different act

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for: a documented case in
which the speaker's intention is held fixed, or nearly, and the act verdicts
of informed participants change with a relation among the parties
([QUESTION-tmpcs4qg](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpcs4qg.md)). Two research reports read it, and they read its structure
differently. This entry reconciles them.

## The case

Labov, Cohen, Robins and Lewis recorded group sessions of "sounding", ritual
insult, among the Thunderbirds and the Jets in Harlem. The report is
*A Study of the Non-Standard English of Negro and Puerto Rican Speakers in
New York City*, Vol. II (1968), §4.2.3. Pages below are the report's own, read
in the OCR text of the ERIC scan. The quotations were checked against that
text on 2026-10-10.

**The rule.** A sound and a personal insult can have the same form, a
pejorative statement about the hearer's relative. Members tell them apart by
uptake: "a personal insult is answered by a denial, excuse or mitigation,
whereas a sound or ritual insult is answered by another sound" (p. 103). Rule
(1) makes a remark a sound when B believes that A believes P is not true, and
B believes that A believes B knows it. Labov sums this up (p. 107) as "it is
an AB event that P is not true", made before an audience C. Then: "If the
proposition P were deleted, the ritual insult would become a personal insult.
*Your family is poor!* is not a ritual insult, but a personal one" (p. 107).

**Boot and David (Thunderbirds), pp. 100–102.** David answers Boot's sounds
with a taunt about Boot's stepfather's stutter. Labov: "The fact that this is a
personal insult and not a ritual insult is shown by the fact that Boot answers
it." Boot replies about David's father's grey hair, and Labov says the reply
"is related to A-5 in exactly the way that one sound is related to another"
(p. 101). Both are taken as personal. David denies ("Because he old, he's old,
that's why!"), is brought to tears, and Ricky objects: "Aw man, cut it out."
Labov adds (p. 107): "Boot's stepfather does stutter; David's father is old and
has gray hair—and all the Thunderbirds know this." Then Boot "now returns to
ritual sounding: his next insults are not intended as allegations of fact, but
David continues to take them as personal" (p. 101), and "group support" ebbs
from him (p. 102).

**The Jets' chair, p. 111.** "When a sound becomes too ordinary — too possible
— we can then observe a sudden switch in the pattern of response to that
appropriate for a personal insult. This can happen by accident". A: "I went in
Junior house 'n' sat in a chair that caved in." B: "You's a damn liar". Labov:
it is "the only instance in the Jet sounding session where a statement is
denied".

**The hypothetical question, pp. 98–99.** Clarence Robins, of the research
team, asks Money: "What would you say if Boot said 'Your father look like
Punjie'?" The group laughs, and Boot says: "He's sounding on you, Money!"
Robins: "No, no, if Boot said it…". Labov: "other staff members join in and
try to make it clear that we are only asking what Money would say if". His
gloss is that the question "is quickly transformed into actual sounding."
Here the speaker's side states its intention in the transcript, and the group
takes the remark as a sound anyway.

**Standing decides it among the Crow and Hidatsa.** A companion datum, from
a kin institution. Lowie (1912, p. 204) on the Crow *i'watkucé*, the
joking-relative: one who had done wrong might be twitted "and publicly
disgrace[d]", "yet such action must not be resented". He gives the
non-verbal form of the test too. A man finding his wagon wheels reversed
"would resent such action, but as soon as he finds out who has played the
joke he cannot get angry". Lowie (1917, p. 42) on the Hidatsa: "it was not the
function of his fellow-clansmen but of his maku'tsati to reprove him or make
fun of him". The same reproof is licensed or an offence by who gives it.
[CASE-tmp15jvz](CASE-tmp15jvz.md) files the institution for another purpose.

### Two readings of its structure, reconciled

One report reads the case as "different structure, different act": sounds
form chains (S-1 e S-2 e S-3), personal insults form insult–denial dyads, and
rule (1) adds a relation that personal insults lack. The other reads it as
"same structure, different act", on Labov's own words: Boot's reply relates to
David's "in exactly the way that one sound is related to another".

Both are right, about different relations.

- **The exchange relations** are the same. Each turn answers the last in kind,
  targets the other's father, and is made before the group. Labov states that
  correspondence himself.
- **One further relation** differs: whether the untruth of P is shared
  knowledge, an AB-event, before the audience. Under it the two exchanges are
  not isomorphic. Stepfathers who stutter and chairs that cave in are possible.
  Mothers who look like Howdy Doody are not.

So the case does not show that no structure fixes the act. It shows that the
structure shared by the turns does not, and that a specified further relation
does. Labov specified it from outside the exchange, from what the group
knows.

## What it can show

**For [CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md).** The documented, non-institutional case the claim's
version 2 asks for. Whether a remark is a sound or a personal insult follows
a relation among A, B and the audience C. It does not follow the words, which
can be the same, or only the speaker's state. In the Boot exchange Labov
attributes a ritual aim to Boot throughout the turn that David takes as
personal. In the chair exchange Labov calls the switch an accident. In the
Robins exchange the speaker's side states its aim, and the group's verdict
differs. Labov states the act condition itself in relational terms, over three
parties. This is the "frame the parties sustain together" that
[QUESTION-tmpcs4qg](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpcs4qg.md) asks about, in a field linguist's recorded data. The Crow
and Hidatsa norm adds the acts with standing conditions, reproof among them,
that the claim's defeat condition names. There, who may say it decides between
licensed reproof and offence.

**For [CLAIM-138](../claims.d/CLAIM-138.md).** Its point at the level of acts, on documented
material. Take the exchange relations alone, and Boot's and David's turns
correspond as one sound to another while the acts differ. Add the
independently specified relation, and structure and act line up. So
relational structure fixes the act only once the relations that count are
chosen, which is the remedy [CLAIM-138](../claims.d/CLAIM-138.md) says concedes non-structural input.
The chosen relation is epistemic, shared knowledge of untruth. It is not
extensional. That is the reading on which [NOTE-038](../notes.d/NOTE-038.md)'s C4 says the ontic
structural realist escapes Newman. So the case shows both what the objection
costs and the way out. It does not settle which way [CLAIM-136](../claims.d/CLAIM-136.md)'s owner should
go.

**For [CLAIM-tmpc25sn](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpc25sn.md).** The first case the record holds that can be read
against the intention-based rival on documented data, not on [CASE-018](CASE-018.md)'s
invented variation.

## What it cannot show

**The condition met only partly is that the intention is held fixed and
documented independently.** In the Boot and chair exchanges the ritual aim is
Labov's attribution ("not intended as allegations of fact", "by accident"),
inferred from the sequence. It is not the speaker's avowal. Boot's one
apparent avowal, "Yeah, I only be playin', I only be playin'" (p. 102), comes
as mitigation of a charge of David's, and what it refers to is unclear in the
OCR text. Only the Robins exchange has the speaker's side stating its
intention, and there "He's sounding on you" is ambiguous between Robins and
the hypothetical Boot. It is also a use/mention case: a question about a sound,
taken as a sound.

**The strongest reply.** Rule (1) is stated in nested beliefs about beliefs,
which is Gricean in shape. The intention-based theorist reads it as the
condition under which a speaker can rationally intend to sound, and a hearer
recognise that intention. On that reading Boot did sound, and David misread
him: the act was a sound and the denial is mistaken uptake. The Robins
exchange gets the same answer, that the group misclassified a question. The
chair case resists the reply best. The group raises no contradiction, and
Labov treats the outcome as a personal insult, not as a misread sound. But the
speaker's view is not recorded there.

**The category is contested.** Kochman (1983) argues that personal insults lie
inside sounding, so Labov's boundary is one analyst's (abstract only read).
That cuts both ways: competent analysts disagree about the act category of
the same exchanges.

**What would close the gap.** An elicited test on these transcripts. Hold the
speaker's stated intention fixed and vary only whether P's untruth is shared
knowledge, as a change of situation, do(S), not an instruction to the rater,
do(I) ([CLAIM-130](../claims.d/CLAIM-130.md)). For instance, one group of raters takes the audience role
with the group's history in hand: they have heard, in the session, that
Boot's stepfather stutters. Another meets the line cold. Labov predicts
outsiders "would have to suspend judgment" (p. 107). Use the chair line and a
matched impossible line ("sat in a chair made of Dog Yummies"), Boot's
stutter turn and a matched ritual turn. These could serve as items for the
discriminating study, [CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md), as an arm with no rendering in which
only the audience's relation changes. Raters who are told the speaker meant
it ritually and still call it personal would meet the rival. Raters who follow
the stated intention would support it.

**Unverified.** The 1972 book chapter, the canonical revised text, was not
read (lending only). Its wording and pages are unverified, and the
quotations stand on the 1968 report.
