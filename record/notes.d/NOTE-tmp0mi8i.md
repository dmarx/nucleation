# NOTE-tmp0mi8i: Morrison, Madden, Odum, Friedel & Twohig — Altering impulsive decision making

## Contribution

The first test of an acceptance-based intervention with delay discounting
as the target. One session of ACT-derived exercises and values work
lowered measured discounting a week later in students selected for steep
discounting, relative to a waitlist. The process measures the training
was meant to move, psychological flexibility and distress tolerance, did
not change relative to control.

## Key insight

If choosing the sooner reward is partly escape from the discomfort of
waiting, then teaching people to have that discomfort without acting on
it should make them wait more. Here the choices moved toward waiting. The
questionnaire measure of the willingness that was supposed to explain the
move did not.

## Assumptions

- **Choosing the SSR as avoidance.** "A mechanism of delay discounting is
  that selection of the SSR represents avoidance of the aversive
  properties of the delay to the LLR" (Introduction). The abstaining
  smoker's withdrawal and craving are the example.
- **Distress intolerance and psychological inflexibility are related
  constructs** that acceptance-based treatment targets (Introduction).
- **Hypothetical money stands for real choice.** The discounting task
  (after Rachlin et al. 1991) uses a hypothetical $1000 at seven delays
  from 1 week to 25 years, with test–retest r = .91 at one week as cited,
  and r = .953 in this sample (Measures; Discussion).
- **The AAQ-II measures the process.** Seven items, "a 7-item
  Likert-scale measure of experiential avoidance and psychological
  inflexibility"; scores above 24–28 go with clinical distress (Measures).

## Key results

- **Sample (Participants).** Utah State undergraduates screened online.
  Those with AUC < 0.4 and systematic data were invited. 34 were
  randomized, 17 per group. Four withdrew, one because "the training
  elicited distress". 30 completed (ages 18–41, mean 21.5, half women,
  90% without meditation experience). Two per analysis were excluded for
  nonsystematic discounting data.
- **Training (Acceptance-Based Training Session).** One 60–90 minute
  session with a graduate student under a licensed psychologist's
  supervision, on "acceptance and values". It included a leaky-pipe
  exercise on the futility of control, the Polygraph and Chocolate Cake
  exercises, the Unwanted Party Guest metaphor, and carrying a card with
  an unwanted thought on it all day. Participants who reacted with fear
  were helped to view "the thought as a thought (i.e., private behavior
  without literal meaning)" by holding an object in an open hand, which
  "illustrate[s] the distinction between the person holding the thought
  and the thought itself". Then came a values bull's-eye, a fork-in-the-road
  variant of Passengers on the Bus, and a week of goal-tracking. The
  training opened by telling participants that "impulsive decision making
  may cause problems for you".
- **Time 1 to Time 2, trained against waitlist (Results, Table 1).** AUC
  went from 0.26 (SD 0.16) to 0.44 (0.22) in the trained group and from
  0.22 (0.13) to 0.25 (0.17) on the waitlist. Group × time F(1, 26) =
  4.658, p = .04, partial η² = .152. Time F(1, 26) = 10.725, p = .003.
  Group F(1, 26) = 4.098, p = .053.
- **Process measures did not move (Results).** AAQ-II: time p = .202,
  training p = .281, interaction p = .377. The means were 23.27 to 21.47
  trained and 19.07 to 18.73 waitlist (lower is more flexible). DTS:
  time p = .092, training p = .447, interaction p = .491.
- **The waitlist after its own training (Results).** DTS rose,
  t(14) = −2.49, p = .026. AUC did not change (p = .267) and the AAQ-II
  did not either (p = .132).
- **Pooled pre–post (Results, Table 2).** AUC 0.27 to 0.39, t(25) =
  −2.911, p = .007, d = .57. DTS 3.46 to 3.66, t(29) = −2.665, p = .012,
  d = .49. AAQ-II 21.00 to 19.50, t(29) = 1.972, p = .058, d = .36.
- **The authors' reading (Discussion).** "One potential interpretation is
  that distress tolerance plays a larger role than psychological
  flexibility in delay discounting." They grant that the pooled analysis
  has no control group.

## Concepts

- **AUC** — the area under the curve of indifference points, from 0 (always
  the sooner reward) to 1 (no discounting).
- **Psychological inflexibility, experiential avoidance** — what the
  AAQ-II measures, after Bond et al. 2011.
- **Distress tolerance** — the DTS's beliefs about tolerating, appraising,
  being absorbed by and regulating distress (Simons & Gaher 2005).

## Connections

It applies the experiential-avoidance account (Hayes et al. 1996) to
Ainslie's impulsive choice. Madden and Odum's laboratory supplies the
discounting methods (AUC; nonsystematic-data criteria of Johnson and
Bickel 2008). Rung and Madden's meta-analysis (LIT-tmp364v1, NOTE-tmpjiwb7)
includes it as one of two mindfulness-based studies, with g = 0.94. That
review describes the between-group difference as trend-level (p < .06).
The paper reports the group × time interaction at p = .04, and it is the
group main effect that is p = .053. The review's figure is an effect size
it computed, and the difference is in which test is meant.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-156: against it in part, without meeting its defeat
  condition.** The claim says mindfulness integrates by suspending the
  step from impulse to action, a step "that needs no change in the
  discount curves". Here an acceptance session, with a "thought as a
  thought" exercise for some participants, lowered measured
  discounting. So some of its effect was on the valuations, which is
  the flow route the claim's body says Dixon et al. (LIT-906) would
  point to. The study does not meet the defeat condition. It measures
  only discounting, not self-control or choice under temptation apart
  from it, so it cannot show the effect fully mediated by discounting.
  It is also mostly acceptance and values, not mindfulness as the claim
  describes it. And it is one pilot, whose waitlist arm did not
  replicate the change after its own training.
- **THEORY-050: against its process claim, weakly, and outside its
  `promote_when`.** The outcome changed and the AAQ-II, ACT's measure of
  inflexibility, did not change relative to control. It only approached
  significance when pooled without a control. So nothing here shows
  inflexibility changing first or carrying the effect. The authors
  themselves suggest distress tolerance may matter more. But the AAQ-II
  is the global self-report `promote_when` sets aside, the sample is
  non-clinical (mean AAQ-II 19 to 23, near or below the clinical range),
  and n = 30. This is weak evidence against inflexibility as the process
  here, not a test of the account.
- **THEORY-051.** The training's object-in-hand exercise separates "the
  person holding the thought" from "the thought itself", a self-as-context
  move. It was given only to some participants and not measured
  separately, so it bears on nothing the account waits on.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

The authors list six (Discussion):

- A Hawthorne effect, since participants were told the study's rationale
  before training but not during the waitlist.
- A small pilot sample.
- A homogeneous sample: mostly White undergraduates.
- Mostly subclinical problems.
- No adherence coding of the session.
- No long-term follow-up.

Three more:

- The waitlist group's post-training discounting change was not
  significant (p = .267). The pooled pre–post effect leans on the trained
  group.
- Discounting was measured with hypothetical money, and the one
  additional discounting measure that was given is "not reported in this
  paper" (Measures).
- The intervention was tailored to each participant's "particular
  struggles", so the manipulation is not uniform.

## Open questions

- Against an active or sham control, and with real or non-monetary
  rewards, does the effect on discounting survive?
- Does anything measured, whether distress tolerance, the AAQ-II or a
  behavioural measure of avoidance, change before discounting and
  mediate it?
- Does the training change self-control in choices under temptation by
  more than its change in discounting carries? That is the test CLAIM-156
  asks for.
