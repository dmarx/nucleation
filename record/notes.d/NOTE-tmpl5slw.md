# NOTE-tmpl5slw: Adams & Aizawa — Defending the Bounds of Cognition

## Contribution

The second round of Adams and Aizawa's exchange with Clark, after
[LIT-438](../literature.d/LIT-438.md) and Clark's replies. It adds one new thing: a name and a
pattern for the objection that coupling is not constitution, which
[LIT-438](../literature.d/LIT-438.md) argued without naming. The rest restates and clarifies the
2001 marks of the cognitive against Clark's criticisms. The authors say as
much: they are "disappointed that we were unable to convey our objections
clearly enough to forestall Clark's criticisms".

## Key insight

The opening joke carries it. "Question: Why did the pencil think that 2 + 2
= 4? Clark's Answer: Because it was coupled to the mathematician." If being
coupled to a cognitive agent in the right way is what makes a thing
cognitive, then the thing's own nature does no work, and that is the
mistake. What makes X part of a cognitive process has to be something about
X: a mark of the cognitive.

## Assumptions

- **The extended-mind case runs on coupling.** The draft reads Clark as
  holding that "the nature of the processes internal to a pencil ...
  has nothing to do with whether that thing carries out cognitive
  processing", and grants that Clark "does shy away from this from time to
  time" (n. 1).
- **The 2001 marks.** Cognition involves non-derived content, and is a kind
  of causal processing that, as a contingent empirical fact, happens only
  in the brain. Neither mark is argued afresh here; both are taken from
  [LIT-438](../literature.d/LIT-438.md).
- **Naturalistic content for mental representations.** Mental
  representations of trees and rocks mean what they do "in virtue of
  satisfying some naturalistic conditions on meaning", for which Fodor's
  asymmetric causal dependence is offered as one candidate. No theory is
  defended.
- **Scientific orthodoxy as evidence.** The authors "side with what is by
  all accounts scientific orthodoxy", and take that to give prima facie
  support.

## Key results

- **The coupling-constitution fallacy (opening section).**
  - Pattern: draw attention to cases where an object or process is coupled
    to a cognitive agent; "slide to the conclusion that the object or
    process constitutes part of the agent's cognitive apparatus or cognitive
    processing".
  - Counter-case: the neurons into a neuromuscular junction are coupled to
    the muscle, but not part of it; neurotransmitter release is coupled to
    contraction, but not part of that process.
  - Called "the most common mistake that extended mind theorists make",
    charged to Van Gelder and Port (1995), Clark and Chalmers (1998), Clark
    (2001), Gibbs (2001) and Haugeland (1998) (n. 3).
  - A fuller treatment is deferred to "Adams & Aizawa (in progress).
    Problems for process externalism".
- **Intrinsic content (§ "The Intrinsic Content Condition").**
  - The condition has "calculated openness": cognitive states must involve
    non-derived content, not consist of it. A Turing machine's
    non-representational head states are offered as a reason (n. 6).
  - Clark is said to attack both a too-strong reading (all content
    intrinsic), which they "explicitly rejected", and a too-weak one
    (vacuous). Their reading: a process with *no* intrinsic content is
    non-cognitive, as Tetris blocks on a screen "are not representations of
    blocks to be rotated, they are the blocks to be rotated".
  - Euler circles: the convention that gives meaning to circles on paper is
    "logically separate" from what makes a mental image of them mean what it
    does. The dependence on a prior convention is historical, like a mental
    image of a car depending on the car's invention.
  - Against convention fixing mental content: a convention needs access to
    "the would-be syntactic item" it gives meaning to, and "we have no way
    to identify particular tokens of brain states qua syntactic items".
- **Causal processing (§ "The Causal Processing Condition").**
  - First flaw (armchair): the authors deny doing armchair philosophy. That
    a brain-tool science is possible does not touch a claim about what the
    evidence shows. Clark offers no regularity they missed.
  - Even granting information-processing regularities across the skull:
    DVD players, radios and phones process information differently from
    brains, which is why brains do language and face recognition and they
    do not. What Clark needs is "a cognitive level of description", not
    just a unified level such as physics.
  - Tools "do not constitute a natural kind", so a brain-tool science would
    have to cover "an open disjunction", "a motley", not "a multiplicity".
  - Second flaw (cognition may itself be a motley): intracranial processes
    may be many, but genome and development bound them; the brain is "at
    least in the running to be a natural kind". And even a motley
    intracranial cognition, if distinct from extracranial processes, would
    still make cognition intracranial.

## Concepts

- **Coupling-constitution fallacy.** Inferring that X is part of a
  cognitive process or system from X's being causally coupled to it.
- **Mark of the cognitive.** A criterion that says what makes a process
  cognitive rather than non-cognitive; the authors' is non-derived content
  plus a distinctive causal process.
- **Non-derived (intrinsic) content.** Content a state has not by
  convention or by an interpreter's say-so.
- **Multiplicity vs. motley.** Many distinct principles in a bounded domain,
  against an open-ended collection with nothing in common. Only the second
  defeats a science.
- **Intra-, extra- and transcranialism.** Cognition inside the brain,
  outside it, or spanning the boundary.

## Bearing on the record

- **[NOTE-180](NOTE-180.md)** found [LIT-097](../literature.d/LIT-097.md)'s step from coupling to parthood
  unargued. This text gives that finding its standard name, and the
  objection a counter-example. It does not add an argument that coupling
  *never* suffices, only that it does not *entail* parthood.
- **Groups.** The motley argument (C6) is about artifacts. It is not
  applied to groups of people, and organisms are not artifacts, so it does
  not by itself count against a nation as a cognitive system
  ([LIT-159](../literature.d/LIT-159.md)).

## Limitations

- A draft. The published chapter may differ, and was not seen.
- The fallacy is shown by a single analogy, and the attributions to five
  authors are not supported in this text.
- The processing contrast rests on everyday examples of devices, not on
  cognitive science.
- The marks of the cognitive are restated, not argued; the text's force is
  as a clarification of [LIT-438](../literature.d/LIT-438.md).
