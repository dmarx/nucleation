# CASE-tmpxnd3y: Hafez's "Shirazi Turk" in English: one ungendered beloved rendered as a maid, as God and as a boy

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for: documented cases of a
change of cover, for [CLAIM-tmp1rlu5](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp1rlu5.md), whose own illustrations are invented
(tu and vous in the abstract) or re-used ([CASE-010](CASE-010.md)).

## The case

Persian has one third-person pronoun, *u*, and its possessive clitic *-ash*
has no gender either. The source, Hafez, *Divan*, ghazal 3, verse 1
(Ganjoor, https://ganjoor.net/hafez/ghazal/sh3, fetched 2026-10-10):

> اگر آن ترک شیرازی به دست آرد دل ما را / به خال هندویش بخشم سمرقند و بخارا را

Gloss: "If that Shirazi Turk takes our heart in hand, for his/her Hindu
[= dark] mole I'd give Samarkand and Bukhara". *hendu-yash*, "his/her
Hindu", is ungendered, and so is "Turk". Ganjoor's own prose paraphrase
keeps the open pronoun (خال سیاه او, "his/her black mole"). Verse 6
turns to the second person, still ungendered: اگر دشنام فرمایی, "if you
deign to curse [me]". Whether the beloved is a boy, a woman or God (the
"true Beloved" of the Sufi reading) is disputed in the scholarship.

English must choose at the first possessive, "his" or "her", and a
capital ("His") can choose a divine addressee as well. Four renderings
choose three ways.

- **Jones (1771): a woman.** William Jones, *A Grammar of the Persian
  Language* (London: W. and J. Richardson, 1771), read in the archive.org
  scan `agrammarpersian00jonegoog`, whose title page is the 1771 first
  edition. His literal prose, near the end of the grammar (about p. 135):
  "If that lovely maid of Shiraz would accept my heart, I would give for
  the mole on her cheek the cities of Samarcand and Bokhara." His verse
  "A Persian Song" (about p. 137): "Sweet maid, if thou wouldst charm my
  sight, / And bid these arms thy neck infold; / That rosy cheek, that
  lily hand / Would give thy poet more delight / Than all Bocara's vaunted
  gold, / Than all the gems of Samarcand." The decision is made even in
  his grammatical example on verbs: "If that fair damsel of Shiraz would
  accept my heart", although he transliterates the line on the same
  scan as "Egher ... turki Shirazi". The verse also turns the third person
  into direct address.
- **Clarke (1891): God.** H. Wilberforce Clarke, *The Dīvān-i-Hāfiz*,
  vol. 1 (Calcutta, 1891), p. 40, ode 8 in his numbering (archive.org
  `thedivan01hafiuoft`): "If that Bold One (the true Beloved) of Shiraz
  gain our heart, / For His dark mole, I will give Samarkand and Bukhara
  (both worlds)." His gloss on the page lists the alternatives:
  "Turk-i-Shirazi signifies:— (a) Hāfiz's beloved (Shakh-i-nabat). (b) The
  true Beloved (God)." Shakh-i-nabat is glossed on the same page as "A
  lovely woman". On p. 43 Clarke reprints Jones's "Sweet maid" version,
  saying it gives "neither the metre, nor the rhyme, nor the sense". One
  book prints the divine reading and the female one side by side.
- **Bell (1897): a woman, addressed.** Gertrude Lowthian Bell, *Poems from
  the Divan of Hafiz* (London: Heinemann, 1897), pp. 70–71 (archive.org
  `poemsfromdivanof00hafiiala`): "Oh Turkish maid of Shiraz! in thy hand /
  If thou'lt take my heart, for the mole on thy cheek / I would barter
  Bokhara and Samarkand." (The OCR reads "On"; "Oh" is the evident
  reading.) Like Jones, she also turns the third person into the second.
  Her notes (pp. 127–129) reject the alternative in print: "The whole poem
  has received a mystical interpretation which seems to me to add but
  little to its value or to its intelligibility". They tell the Timur
  anecdote with "the black mole on the cheek of thy mistress".
- **Davis (2012): a boy.** Dick Davis, *Faces of Love: Hafez and the Poets
  of Shiraz* (Mage, 2012; Penguin Classics, 2013), pp. 128–129 by the
  book's own notes, read in archive.org
  `facesoflovehafezandthepoetsofshiraz`: "If that Shirazi Turk would take /
  My heart within his loving hand / I'd give for his dark mole the towns /
  Of Bokhara and Samarqand;". His note on the line tells the same Timur
  anecdote as "some pretty boy's mole".

Davis states the forced choice in his Introduction (page numbers not
legible in the scan): "Persian pronouns have no gender distinctions, so
that the same word may be translated as 'he,' 'she,' or 'it.' ... there is
usually no way of telling whether a boy or a girl is being talked about."
And: "Unfortunately a translator of Persian lyric poetry into English
usually has to make a decision as to the gender of the person being
addressed in a given poem ... the reader should be aware that this is in
most cases an arbitrary decision, so that he or she is perfectly welcome
to change most instances of 'he' to 'she' and 'she' to 'he'". He names
the history too: "some Victorian commentators and translators tended to
bowdlerize these poems by making them always about girls, a blanket
insistence that they are always about boys seems to me to be equally
tendentious."

What is held fixed: the mole, the two cities, the wine and the
cup-bearer. The renderings differ on which questions they decide, not on
those.

## What it can show

It is a documented instance of the half of a change of cover that
[CLAIM-tmp1rlu5](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp1rlu5.md) calls "the target decides what the source left open". It
shows it on the question the claim needs: who is addressed, and in what
relationship. Take the question "Is the beloved a woman, a boy, or God?".
Expert readers of the Persian disagree on it, as Clarke's two-way gloss,
Davis's "no way of telling" and the scholarship's dispute attest. A reader
of Jones or Bell answers "a woman", a reader of Davis "a boy" and a reader
of Clarke "God", each on the text's word. The answer settles what kind of
act the poem is: a gallant courtship lyric, a love lyric in the pederastic
convention, or devotion to God. That is a change in which contexts exist
for which readers, with no other question's answers needing to move,
which is the claim's form. Davis's printed licence to swap "he" and "she"
shows a translator treating the question as undecidable in the source and
decided only by the rendering.

Davis's licence also bears, as a dispute and not a resolution, on
[CLAIM-tmpfe37n](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpfe37n.md)'s search for renderings that keep the act while changing a
proposition. Davis holds that the gender swap keeps the poem's act ("the
real subject is longing and desire, polymorphous and overwhelming"). Bell
and Clarke treat the parallel choice, human or divine, as changing it.
The proposition is also one the source never asserted, so strictly it is
added, not changed.

## What it cannot show

- **It meets only one half of the definition.** [CLAIM-tmp1rlu5](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp1rlu5.md) defines a
  change of cover as a question determinate for source readers becoming
  indeterminate for target readers, *or the reverse*. This case shows only
  the second of those, a question open in the source that the rendering
  decides. It shows no question the source decides that a rendering makes
  undecidable, the claim's "the source decides what the target cannot". A candidate in this poem is the honorific
  *farmāyī* ("deign") in verse 6, which marks the lover's deference; no
  critic was found saying a rendering loses it, so it is not claimed.
- **It measures no reader agreement.** The claim's operational test is
  agreement among readers above a threshold fixed in advance. The evidence
  here is a handful of translators and scholars, who are readers but not a
  sample. Their disagreement shows that experts differ, not that ordinary
  readers of the Persian answer below a threshold. That the English
  readers answer determinately is inferred from the explicit pronouns, not
  measured.
- **The strongest reply.** A defender of the view that this is no change
  of cover would say the Persian is not open for its competent readers.
  The genre convention makes the beloved male by default (Davis: "scholars
  have generally assumed" the pederastic relationship, and when gender is
  shown "it's usually clear that a boy rather than a girl is being referred
  to"). The Sufi reading is a second layer of interpretation, not an
  indeterminacy in the first. On that reply, a Persian reader answers
  "a boy" determinately, and Jones, Bell and Clarke change the answer to a
  question both sides can decide. That would be distortion within a shared
  context, which [CLAIM-tmpx3m7e](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpx3m7e.md) measures apart from a change of cover. It
  would not be the cover changing. Against the reply stand Davis's own
  "no way of telling", Clarke's two-way gloss, and Lewisohn's view (known
  here only through Wikipedia, unverified) that the beloved is "nearly
  always female". The reply cannot be settled from the texts.
- **What would close the gap.** The reader test the search report sketches
  could serve as items for the discriminating study ([CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md)). Put
  the question "Is the beloved a woman, a boy, God, or does the poem not
  say?" to readers of the Persian. Put it to matched English readers of
  Davis's version as printed and with "his" changed to "her", and of
  Clarke's capitalised version. Compare agreement against a threshold
  fixed in advance. The case supports the claim if the Persian readers
  fall below the threshold and each English group is above it on a
  different answer. It is the strongest reply's case if the Persian
  readers agree on "a boy".
- **It is not contextuality,** as [CLAIM-tmp1rlu5](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp1rlu5.md) itself says of a change
  of cover. Nor does it show the change at small local distortion in any
  measured sense; it shows that such changes are made.

**Leads for the reverse half, unverified.** The half this case lacks needs
a source that marks what the target cannot. The search's companion report
on compensation names Pushkin's «Ты и вы» (1828), a poem whose event is a
woman's slip from вы into ты. Its English versions handle it differently:
Givental and Wilson-Egolf use "thou" (https://math.berkeley.edu/~giventh/verse/thou.pdf),
and Bullock moves the pronouns into French (https://oxfordsong.org/song/tu-et-vous).
Whether any English version leaves the slip undecidable for its readers,
and whether any critic says so, has not been checked. Dolly's ты in
[CASE-tmpmsyg7](CASE-tmpmsyg7.md) is the same contrast in prose, filed for compensation, where
the translators work to keep the question decidable. Failing a documented
case, the search report proposes a stipulated one: a T/V-marked source
dialogue whose switch of pronoun is the event, rendered into English, with
"Did the relationship change at line n?" put to both groups of readers.
