# THEORY-555: In London in 2018, Airbnb listings that were possibly breaking the 90-night short-let cap, defined as entire homes offered for more than 180 days a year by hosts with two or more listings, were about 0.16% of the housing stock (about 5,300 homes) but 3.5–7% in a few central small areas, and across small areas they correlate weakly with flats, private renting and low dwelling-type diversity

## Source

Sarah Shabrina, Elsa Arcaute and Michael Batty (2021), "Airbnb and its
potential impact on the London housing market", *Urban Studies*,
[LIT-094](../literature.d/LIT-094.md), read in full in [NOTE-071](../notes.d/NOTE-071.md). Figures came through as captions only,
so map values are known only where the text states them.

## What was actually shown

**Supply.** Airbnb listings were 0.75% of London's stock (about 25,000) in
2015 and 2.38% (about 80,000) in 2019 (Fig. 2). In 2018, 82% of LSOAs
(3,982 of 4,835) had at least one active listing.

**The proxy.** "Possible misuse" is an entire-home listing available more
than 180 days a year whose host has two or more listings. The threshold
assumes 50% occupancy, taken from Visit Britain hotel bed-space
occupancy of 53–55%, so that 180 days offered means more than 90 nights
booked, the legal cap under the Deregulation Act 2015, s. 44 (note 1).

**Extent, 2018 (Fig. 3).** Entire homes available more than 180 days:
0.25% of stock (about 8,200). Possible misuse: 0.16% (about 5,300). In
some LSOAs in Camden, Westminster, the City and Hackney possible misuse
reaches 3.5–7%; by borough it is at most 1.5% (City of London).

**Where it concentrates (LSOA correlations, Figs. 4–5).** Purpose-built
flats r = +0.28, privately rented +0.33, dwelling-type entropy −0.14,
owned −0.31, detached houses −0.25. No significance tests or spatial
adjustment are reported, and r² is at most 0.11.

**What could have come out otherwise.** Possible misuse could have been
spread evenly, or tracked owner occupation. It is small citywide and
concentrated in flat-dominated, privately rented central areas.

## What this does not say

- **That 0.16% of homes breached the cap.** The proxy counts availability,
  not bookings. It misses single-listing hosts who exceed 90 nights and
  includes multi-listing hosts who do not. The occupancy assumption is not
  tested on Airbnb data.
- **That more than 2% of London's homes are misused.** That is the
  abstract's wording; the body's 2% is the share listed at all.
- **That Airbnb raises rents, or by how much.** The borough regression
  (n = 33, four predictors, R² 0.62) finds logged 2015 misuse associated
  with a min–max-normalised 2015–17 asking-rent change (β 0.084, p 0.001).
  It is a cross-section with no control for prior rent, tourism or trend
  and no spatial model. Its translation, "a 100% increase ... up to 8%"
  rent, about £90 a year, mixes a log unit with a doubling and a
  normalised unit with a percentage ([NOTE-071](../notes.d/NOTE-071.md)'s analysis). The panel and
  instrumental studies it cites are the better sources for an effect size;
  the record has not read them.
- **That the correlations are causal or robust.** They are pairwise, small
  and untested.

## Connections

- The definition follows Schäfer and Braun (2016) on Berlin, whose
  possible misuse was 0.30% of stock (5,555 units). The record does not
  hold it.
