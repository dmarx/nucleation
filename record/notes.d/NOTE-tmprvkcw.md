# NOTE-tmprvkcw: Merton — The self-fulfilling prophecy

## Contribution

The essay takes W. I. Thomas's theorem, "If men define situations as
real, they are real in their consequences", and names one consequence of
it: a public definition of a situation becomes part of the situation and
can make itself true. Merton coins the phrase "self-fulfilling prophecy"
for this, gives it a definition, and argues that it goes far toward
explaining ethnic and racial conflict in the America of 1948. It is an
essay for a general magazine, not a research report. Its evidence is a
parable, everyday examples, and figures from Myrdal, the census and
Merton's own housing study, none of them cited to a source.

## Key insight

A prediction about people is not outside the system it predicts. "This
is peculiar to human affairs": forecasts of Halley's comet do not change
its orbit, but a rumour that a solvent bank is insolvent empties it. The
false belief then looks true, because "the prophet will cite the actual
course of events as proof that he was right from the very beginning".
Merton calls this "a reign of error".

## Assumptions

- **The definition is false at first.** The bank was solvent. The
  definition says the prophecy is "in the beginning, a false definition
  of the situation". A true prediction that comes true is not the case
  in view.
- **The definition is public and acted on.** "Public definitions of a
  situation (prophecies or predictions) become an integral part of the
  situation". The mechanism runs through behaviour that the definition
  evokes: depositors queue, unions exclude, school boards withhold funds.
- **The counter-case is set aside.** Footnote 1 names the "suicidal
  prophecy", which so changes behaviour that it fails to come true, and
  says "This important type is not considered here".

## Key results

The essay is argument, not finding. Its claims, by section:

- **§I. The definition.** Three examples: the Last National Bank (a
  fictional parable set in 1932), the student whose fear of failing
  makes him fail, and two nations that think war is "inevitable" and
  arm until it is. The definition and the "reign of error" follow.
- **§II. Exclusion makes the strikebreaker.** The white unionist excludes
  Black workers as strikebreakers, and exclusion leaves strikebreaking
  as their only way into the work. "History creates its own test": Black
  workers stopped being strikebreakers where they were admitted to
  unions. Education alone will not break the circle; the teachers share
  the prejudice, and "false ideas [do not] quietly vanish when confronted
  with the truth".
- **§III. "Damned if you do and damned if you don't."** Measured against
  the virtues of Lincoln, the in-group's hero, the out-group is damned in
  two ways. Where it lacks the virtues (Black Americans, given
  one-fifth as many college graduates in proportion and denied the
  money for schools), the lack is the prophecy's product. Where it has
  them (Jews, Japanese Americans), "moral alchemy" renames them: "I am
  firm, Thou art obstinate, He is pigheaded". The in-group's hard work
  is the out-group's "sweatshop mentality". Condemnation "continues
  largely irrespective of what he does". The Trobriand chiefs' resentment
  of a commoner's "too much" success is the comparison, and Merton
  insists it is not calculated.
- **§IV. The out-groups' responses.** Both responses answer the in-group's
  definition, and both are turned against them. Accused of inferiority,
  one group magnifies its achievements; accused of excess, the other
  minimizes its own (the Anti-Defamation League's "only ten" of the 200
  largest non-banking corporations with a Jewish head). "Self-assertion
  and self-effacement" have the same function, and the in-group watches
  both "with mingled derision and contempt".
- **§V. Institutions break the circle.** Bank suspensions averaged 635 a
  year in the 1920s and 2,276 a year around the Crash, and 28 a year in
  the twelve years after the FDIC. Admission to unions ended Black
  strikebreaking. In Hilltown, a Pittsburgh housing project half Black
  and half white that Merton was studying with Patricia J. Salter, one
  white resident in 25 had expected race relations to go smoothly and
  five times as many expected serious trouble, and three in four of the
  most apprehensive later found that the races "get along fairly well".
  "The self-fulfilling prophecy … operates only in the absence of
  deliberate institutional controls."

## Concepts

- **Thomas theorem**: "If men define situations as real, they are real
  in their consequences."
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy**: "a false definition of the situation
  evoking a new behavior which makes the originally false conception
  come true".
- **Suicidal prophecy**: one that so changes behaviour that it fails to
  come true (footnote 1); named, not treated.
- **Moral alchemy**: "the same behavior must be differently evaluated
  according to the person who exhibits it"; the means by which
  "in-group virtues become out-group vices" (after Donald Young).
- **In-group, out-group**: Sumner's terms; the lines between them shift
  with the situation (Joe Louis is out-group by race and in-group by
  nation).

## Connections

The theorem is W. I. Thomas's. Merton places Bossuet, Mandeville, Marx,
Freud and Sumner before him, and Myrdal's *An American Dilemma* is the
essay's main empirical ground for race. MacIver's *The More Perfect
Union* (1948) gives examples of both types of prophecy, and Merton's own
"The Unanticipated Consequences of Purposive Social Action" (1936) is
cited for the general statement. Soros's reflexivity ([LIT-1397](../literature.d/LIT-1397.md),
[NOTE-1362](NOTE-1362.md)) generalizes the loop to run both ways, giving the
self-defeating direction the equal place that Merton's footnote sets
aside. Espeland and Sauder ([LIT-1330](../literature.d/LIT-1330.md)) count it among the mechanisms of
reactivity to rankings. Land's hyperstition ([LIT-1396](../literature.d/LIT-1396.md)) is the loop made a
programme.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-141](../cases.d/CASE-141.md), the label that confirms itself.** The case is Merton's
  mechanism, and the essay supports reading it as working through others'
  treatment rather than through the labelled person's belief. In §II the
  Black worker becomes a strikebreaker because the unions exclude him,
  not because he comes to see himself as one. The CASE's "registered
  unread" can now read "read" (NOTE-tmprvkcw).
- **[CASE-142](../cases.d/CASE-142.md), the label confirmed by resistance.** The LIT registered the
  essay to check whether Merton already covers this route. In part he
  does. §IV describes out-groups that reject the in-group's definition
  and answer it, by self-assertion or self-effacement. The in-group
  takes both answers with derision, and §III's moral alchemy says why:
  the condemnation does not depend on what the out-group does. That is
  the CASE's "the rejection is what confirms it", at the scale of
  groups. But it is moral alchemy, not the self-fulfilling prophecy. The
  conduct that confirms the judgement is not produced by the judgement;
  it is renamed. In [CASE-142](../cases.d/CASE-142.md) the anger is produced by the accusation,
  so the CASE combines the two routes: the accusation provokes the
  conduct, and the conduct is read as proof whatever it is. Merton names
  both routes and does not join them.
- **[LIT-1396](../literature.d/LIT-1396.md) (hyperstition).** The LIT contrasts the two: "Merton's
  prophecy begins as a false definition of a present situation; a
  hyperstition is typically a fiction or a forecast". The definition does
  speak of a "false definition of the situation", but two of Merton's own
  three opening examples are forecasts: the student "convinced that he is
  destined to fail", and war believed "inevitable". The contrast holds
  for the definition and for the bank, not for the essay's examples.
- **[TERM-075](../terms.d/TERM-075.md), [CLAIM-402](../claims.d/CLAIM-402.md), [CLAIM-426](../claims.d/CLAIM-426.md).** They cite the essay as the source
  of the self-fulfilling prophecy. It is, and it adds the suicidal
  prophecy (footnote 1) as its named and untreated counterpart.

## Limitations

- **An essay, not a study.** Its figures carry no citations. Hilltown is
  reported in four numbers, and Merton says "This is not the place to
  report the findings … in detail".
- **The definition requires falsity at the start**, and the essay never
  says how one would know the first definition was false. The bank is
  stipulated solvent. For the racial cases the argument is that the
  definition is false because its evidence is its own product, which is
  the conclusion.
- **The suicidal prophecy is set aside**, so the essay says nothing about
  when a prophecy fulfils itself rather than defeats itself.
- **Moral alchemy is not distinguished from the prophecy** in the
  essay's own terms. Both fall under the Thomas theorem; only the first
  is defined.
- **Errors and slips.** None of substance found. Kronecker as "the
  creator of the modern theory of numbers" is an overstatement. The
  Tocqueville line is called a "112-year-old apothegm", which dates it
  to about 1836; its source is not given and was not checked.

## Open questions

- When does a public definition fulfil itself and when does it defeat
  itself? The essay names both and treats one.
- Can the self-fulfilling route and the moral-alchemy route be told
  apart in a case like [CASE-142](../cases.d/CASE-142.md), where the accusation both provokes the
  answer and fixes how it is read?
