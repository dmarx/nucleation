# THEORY-460: Every leading reason to deny consciousness to current large language models except biology names a missing feature, recurrent processing, a global workspace, unified agency, world and self models or senses and embodiment, that engineering could add, so the case against current models is much stronger than the case against their extended successors; the one possibly permanent objection, biology, is the one the argument sets aside

## Source

Chalmers (2023), "Could a Large Language Model be Conscious?",
arXiv:2303.07103 (v3, the *Boston Review* text), from a NeurIPS 2022
invited talk, [LIT-411](../literature.d/LIT-411.md), read in full in [NOTE-345](../notes.d/NOTE-345.md), with v1 compared for its
conclusions.

## What was actually shown

**The form (§§2–3).** A claim for or against consciousness in a language
model should name a feature X, with reasons that models have or lack X and
reasons that having or lacking it makes consciousness probable or
improbable.

**Evidence for (§2).** Self-report is fragile: a one-word prompt change
reverses it, and the model "has learned to imitate those claims" from a
corpus of people talking about consciousness, so "the evidence is much
weaker". Seeming conscious is cheap, as ELIZA showed. Conversation matters
only as a sign of general intelligence, which gives "some limited reason
to take the hypothesis seriously".

**Evidence against (§3).** Six features: biology, senses and embodiment,
world and self models, recurrent processing, a global workspace and
unified agency. Transformers are "almost entirely feedforward"; models are
"chameleons" without stable goals; self models are "especially limited".
The strongest are recurrence, workspace and agency. "For all of these
objections except perhaps biology … the objection is temporary rather
than permanent": each names a research programme, with simple systems
that already have the feature (LSTMs and external memory, Perceiver-style
modular interfaces, agent models trained on one individual).

**The arithmetic (§4).** Give at least 1/3 credence to each of the six
being required. If independent, a system lacking all six has under a 1/10
chance of consciousness, since (2/3)⁶ ≈ 0.088; dependence pushes it up and
unknown requirements down, giving "somewhere under 10 percent". Over 50%
that sophisticated extended models with these features exist within a
decade, times at least 50% that they would be conscious, gives "25 percent
or more". The figures are "for illustrative purposes", with "specious
precision" conceded.

**What could have come out otherwise.** An objection other than biology
could have turned out permanent: a feature no engineering programme could
supply, or one whose simple instances clearly lack what the objection
needs. The paper finds none, by surveying the features one at a time.

## What this does not say

- **That current models are conscious, or that their successors will be.**
  It says the case against the successors is weaker. The second credence
  is the product of two stipulated 50% figures.
- **That biology has been answered.** It is set aside: Chalmers has "argued
  that these views involve a sort of biological chauvinism" and "I'll set
  this issue aside" (C7, asserted here). It enters the numbers at 1/3, so
  the credences are not conditional on functionalism alone, but they are
  credences under assumed theories, which [THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md) says cannot settle the
  question.
- **That the features are sufficient.** The twelfth challenge, "If that's
  not enough for conscious AI: What's missing?", frames the burden as a
  functionalist would. For a biological naturalist the missing feature is
  being alive, which is not an engineering item ([THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md)).
- **That the survey figures are credences.** The theory-balanced approach
  (fn 30) reads acceptance of, or finding promising, global workspace,
  recurrence and higher-order theories in an expert survey as collective
  credence, and leaves the conversion to "further work".
- **That the empirical claims are current.** They are dated 2022–23; the
  July 2023 Afterword already says GPT-4 shortens the timelines. v1 gave
  different weights (25% and 50%) and reached "at most a 5% or so chance"
  before adjustment ([NOTE-345](../notes.d/NOTE-345.md), Corrections).

## Connections

- **[THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md)** (current evidence cannot settle AI consciousness). This
  paper makes that account's behaviour argument independently, in 2022,
  and is an instance of its architecture half: the obstacles that do the
  work are theory-derived, and building them counts as progress only for a
  functionalist. It adds a fifth position on the prior. Its tenth challenge,
  a model that describes features of consciousness it was not trained on,
  is a proposal for the training-immune marker [THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md) names as a
  refuter, not an instance of one.
- **Butlin et al.** ([LIT-056](../literature.d/LIT-056.md)) build their indicator method on this paper's
  theory-balanced approach ([NOTE-052](../notes.d/NOTE-052.md)).
- **Shanahan** ([THEORY-606](THEORY-606.md)) names this paper as the evidential approach
  and rejects the frame: for a disembodied chat agent, the question is
  answered by the grammar of the word, not by features.
- **Unified agency.** The suggestion that one model "can support an
  ecosystem of multiple agents" is offered as a reply to the disunity
  objection, not argued. It meets [THEORY-490](THEORY-490.md) (Shanahan on what a chat
  agent's "I" refers to) and [THEORY-043](THEORY-043.md)'s remark that functionalism lets one
  system host two minds.
