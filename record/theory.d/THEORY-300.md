# THEORY-300: Acting on a coercive instruction reduces the implicit sense of agency over the outcome, compared with freely choosing the same action

## Source

Haggard (2017), [LIT-1009](../literature.d/LIT-1009.md), "Society, agency and responsibility" (manuscript
pp. 16–17) and Fig. 5, read in full in [NOTE-827](../notes.d/NOTE-827.md). The experiment is
Caspar et al. (2016), *Current Biology*, cited as ref. 92 and not read here.

## What was actually shown

Pairs of participants took turns as agent and victim, so each had felt
the shocks. The agent pressed one of two keys. One delivered a visibly
painful shock to the victim, synchronous with a tone, and earned the
agent money. The other did neither. In the coercive condition the
experimenter said which key to press before each trial. In the free
condition the agent chose. The agent estimated the interval between
keypress and tone, an implicit measure in which voluntary actions yield
shorter estimates than passive movements.

- **Interval estimates** were "significantly greater" under coercion than
  under free choice. Under the measure's interpretation, that is a reduced
  sense of agency.
- **The auditory N1** evoked by the tone was smaller under coercion, "both
  when the action lead to a painful shock, and when it did not" (Fig. 5).
  This is the part of the result that is not a report.

The review's reading: "obeying instructions reduces the sense of agency,
producing an experience closer to passive movement than to voluntary
action". It ties this to the finding that more alternatives to choose
from increase the sense of agency, and suggests that "social coercion
effectively constrains an individual's free choice" (p. 17).

Both measures could have come out the other way: equal interval estimates
and equal N1 across conditions would have meant the instruction changed
nothing in the experience.

## What this does not say

- **Not that the coerced agent is less responsible.** "The finding does
  not, of course, legitimate the defense of 'only obeying orders', but it
  does explain how acting under coercion can lead individuals to
  experience a psychological distance" (p. 17).
- **Not that the agent did not act.** She pressed the key herself. The
  claim is about her experience of the act and of its outcome.
- **Not about manipulation.** The instruction was overt, and the agent
  knew where it came from. [THEORY-235](THEORY-235.md)'s harm of covert influence runs
  through the agent's not knowing, and [THEORY-040](THEORY-040.md)'s manipulation acts on
  her psychological structure. Neither is tested here.
- **"Coercive" is the review's word for an instruction from an
  experimenter.** No threat was made beyond the setting itself. Whether
  the effect needs authority, or comes from the loss of choice alone, is
  not separated in what is reported.
- **One experiment, reported.** Samples, effect sizes and replications are
  not given in the review.
