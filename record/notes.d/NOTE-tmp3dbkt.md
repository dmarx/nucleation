# NOTE-xxxx: Transgenerational Epigenetic Inheritance: Myths and Mechanisms

## Contribution

A mechanistic review of how epigenetic states could pass between
generations, and of the reprogramming that stops them. It sets out what
is well shown (plants, nematodes, some fruit-fly cases), what is rare
(mammalian epialleles at transposons), and what is not shown
(environmentally induced, adaptive inheritance in mammals and humans). It
also fixes the definition the later debate uses: an effect is
transgenerational only in a generation never exposed to the trigger.

## Key insight

Epigenetic inheritance and germline reprogramming are "two sides of the
same coin" (Introduction). Reprogramming exists so that each embryo starts
from the genome, not from its parents' lives. In mammals it happens twice,
in the germline and after fertilization, and "these two rounds of
epigenetic erasure leave little chance for inheritance of epigenetic
marks, whether programmed, accidental or environmentally induced". Where
marks do escape, they are mostly at transposons, which the cell silences
for its own defence. So inheritance, where it occurs, looks like a
by-product of genome defence, not a channel for a parent's experience.

## Assumptions

- **A conservative definition of epigenetics** (Box 1): the perpetuation of
  gene expression and function across cell divisions without a change in
  DNA sequence. Waddington's developmental sense and Bird's 2007
  "structural adaptation" sense are noted and set aside.
- **Sequence first.** A heritable difference counts as epigenetic only
  "when individuals that are truly genetically identical" show it
  (Epigenetic variation). Where the gene is unknown, a mutation can
  hardly be ruled out.
- **Exposure defines generations** (Box 1, Figure 1). Any generation whose
  cells were present during the exposure is excluded from the test.

## Key results

- **Definitions** (Box 1, Figure 1).
  - Intergenerational (parental) effects: the exposed parent, and any
    embryo or germ cells exposed with it.
  - Transgenerational effects: "found in generations that were not
    exposed to the initial signal".
  - An exposed pregnant female exposes F1 (the fetus) and F2 (the fetus's
    germ line). "Only F3 individuals can be considered as true
    trans-generational inheritance."
  - An exposed male exposes himself (F0) and his germ line (the future
    F1). F1 is intergenerational; only F2 and later count.
- **Mammalian reprogramming** (Germline reprogramming and imprinting; Box
  2; Figure 2A). Two waves: in the zygote and early cleavage stages
  (imprints survive it) and in primordial germ cells (imprints are erased
  and the inactive X reactivated). Mouse genome-wide profiling found 4,730
  loci escaping germline demethylation (more than 40% methylated), mostly
  repeat elements, and 233 single-copy loci, which the authors call
  "prime candidates" for transgenerational inheritance in mammals.
- **Plants reprogram less** (Box 2; Figure 2B). More than 80% of methylated
  cytosines are kept in pollen sperm cells; only asymmetric CHH
  methylation is reset. Epialleles can persist "for literally hundreds of
  years". In Arabidopsis epiRILs, hypomethylated segments were inherited
  for eight or more generations and account for up to 90% of the
  heritability of flowering time and root length.
- **Worms and flies.** Small-RNA silencing in C. elegans lasts more than 20
  generations, and an olfactory imprint more than 40. A piRNA-based
  trans-silencing effect in Drosophila lasts more than 50.
- **Mammalian cases are few and short.**
  - Agouti viable yellow and Axin fused: bona fide transgenerational
    epialleles, both at retrotransposon (IAP) insertions; Agouti is
    inherited through the female germ line only.
  - A diet of methyl donors changes Agouti offspring coat colour, but
    "this effect is only transmitted over two generations and is lost by
    the third".
  - The Kit paramutation-like effect disappeared by F4.
  - Human familial epimutations (MLH1, DAPK1) turned out to depend on
    DNA sequence polymorphisms: the silencing is re-established each
    generation and erased in the germ line.
- **Rodent exposure studies** (Epigenetic variation; Implications for
  human health; Box 3).
  - Vinclozolin's multigenerational effect on male fertility was seen in
    outbred rats, not in an inbred strain, so genetic variation may
    explain it.
  - Paternal diet effects in mice: transmitted, probably via sperm, by an
    unknown agent. No global change in sperm methylation was found, and
    most paternal RNA and histones are replaced after fertilization.
  - Paternal transmission of social-defeat phenotypes was traced to
    maternal provisioning, not epigenetic inheritance (Dietz et al.).
- **Human evidence** (Implications for human health). Discussed only for
  nutrition.
  - Dutch famine: those exposed in gestation had poorer glucose
    tolerance; offspring of prenatally undernourished fathers, not
    mothers, were heavier. "No evidence of transgenerational effects of
    grandmaternal under-nutrition during gestation" was found.
  - Most such studies are "inter-generational (parental or
    grand-parental exposure) rather than truly trans-generational", and
    cannot rule out postnatal nutrition or missing covariates.
  - Bona fide transgenerational inheritance over several generations
    "seems unlikely given the robust reprogramming found in the mammalian
    germ line".
- **Not discussed.** Överkalix, Holocaust offspring and FKBP5 are not
  mentioned. Stress and behaviour appear only in Box 3, as a
  "very seductive but highly controversial topic".

## Concepts

- **intergenerational (parental) effect**: an effect on the exposed parent
  or on offspring whose cells were exposed with it, in utero or as germ
  cells.
- **transgenerational effect**: an effect in a generation never exposed to
  the trigger.
- **germline reprogramming**: erasure and resetting of DNA methylation,
  histone marks and small RNA in germ cells, and again after
  fertilization, so that the embryo is totipotent.
- **epiallele**: a heritable variant in a gene's epigenetic state without a
  change in sequence.
- **paramutation**: one allele heritably silences another in trans, via
  small RNA.

## Connections

- **The other two sceptics.** Horsthemke (LIT-tmpmsnfq) takes the F2/F3
  rule from here and adds the secondary epimutation. Bird (LIT-tmpaf6cr)
  cites this review for directionless plant epialleles. Bird is thanked

- **The mouse fear study** (Dias and Ressler, LIT-tmpk0wlg) appeared too
  late to be discussed; Bohacek and Mansuy (LIT-tmph5rq7) is the record's
  review on the other side.
- **Inherited state in bacteria** (THEORY-179). E. coli cells pass a
  physiological state to descendants for about four generations, then
  lose it. Box 3 lists exactly this kind of route, a self-sustaining
  feedback loop or a metabolite, as epigenetic inheritance without
  chromatin, and doubts it could last through development in complex
  organisms. Bacteria have no germline to reset; mammals do. The two
  together are a contrast, not a parallel: the same short, decaying
  persistence (Agouti diet: two generations; Kit: gone by F4) is what
  survives in mammals.
- **Cultural persistence** (THEORY-889). The authors' last word, that human
  culture is Lamarckian, is the route THEORY-889 documents for attitudes.

## Bearing on the record

- **The question (QUESTION-tmp8bttd).** On the germline route this review
  is a strong negative for mammals in general and a weak one for humans in
  particular. It examines only nutritional human studies, and finds them
  intergenerational. It never addresses trauma in humans.
- **A consequence for the question's framing (an inference).** By Figure
  1's rule, a parent traumatized before conception exposes only their own
  germ cells, so their children are F1, intergenerational. The
  grandchildren are the first generation that could show germline
  transmission. Any study of survivors' children, whatever it finds, can
  test only the intergenerational effect. The question's "germline" route
  should be split: a parent's exposed gametes (intergenerational, not
  excluded by this review) and inheritance through untouched generations
  (what reprogramming makes unlikely).
- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** Indirect. If marks are reset
  each generation, what reaches grandchildren who never met the trauma
  must travel by care, behaviour or story. That leaves more of
  "intergenerational trauma", past the first generation, to the social
  routes, and the identity route is one of them. The review does not
  choose among those routes, and it treats culture as transmitted
  teaching and habit, not as identity. No note on the claim is proposed.

## Limitations

- **Humans get one section, on nutrition.** Stress, trauma and behaviour
  are confined to a paragraph in Box 3.
- **The Dutch famine passage is unclear.** It first reports "increased
  neonatal adiposity among the grandchildren of women who had been
  undernourished during pregnancy", then says no transgenerational effect
  of grandmaternal undernutrition was found. By the review's own Figure 1,
  those grandchildren are F2 of an exposed pregnant woman, so any effect
  in them would be intergenerational anyway: the passage uses
  "transgenerational" in the loose sense Box 1 warns against.
- **The mammalian negative is argued from mechanism and from absence.** The
  233 single-copy loci that escape reprogramming are left as candidates.
- **Figures not seen.** Only their legends are in the copy.
- **Typos** in the manuscript.
  - "penotype" (Paramutation section); "bone fide" for "bona fide"
    (twice); "specfically"; "extrambryonic"; "quantititative";
    "inlcuding"; "sterotypical"; "Single nucleotie polymorphisms";
    "descendents" in the abstract.
  - "IAPTR1" for what is probably IAPLTR1.
  - "This mechanism highly reminiscent" lacks "is".
  - "No evidence ... were found".
  - The same NIH grant number, GM067014, is listed twice.
  - The text names SET-24 and SET-25 for C. elegans; the Figure 4 legend
    names SET-25 and SET-32.

## Open questions

- Do any of the 233 single-copy loci that escape germline demethylation
  carry an environmentally induced state to F2 or F3?
- Does the F2/F3 rule hold for small RNA in sperm, whose fate after
  fertilization the review calls unclear?
- Human trauma is not examined here. Do the human studies the question
  cites (LIT-tmpmxksg, LIT-tmp4sa73) meet even the intergenerational
  standard, or only the loose one?
