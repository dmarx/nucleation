# NOTE-tmpjth7b: Scanlon — Contractualism and intuitionism

## Contribution

A first-hand, current statement of what Scanlon now thinks his 1998
formulation got wrong (the strictly bilateral interpretation, the
Transmitter Room, conventions in aggregation cases), of the Principle of
Established Practices with a new wrong of not helping to establish a needed
norm, and of the concession that sufficiency and relevance judgments are
substantive normative judgments. Footnote 1: the paper "began as a lecture
presented to a conference on contractualism in Zurich in 2022."

## Key results

- **The old formulation** (p. 1): "The particular formulation that I gave
  to the contractualist idea also seems to me now to have some serious
  problems, but I don't regret offering it." Its grounds lie "in normative
  facts about the reasons that individuals have to accept or reject
  principles", not in agreement.
- **Sufficiency** (pp. 6–7): the facts of morality "are facts about the
  sufficiency of various reasons for rejecting a principle when various
  opposing reasons are taken into account"; justification is interactive,
  depending on whether one position's reasons "are sufficient reasons for
  rejecting a principle given the reasons those in other positions have".
- **Established practices** (p. 21): "if some practice is needed to avoid a
  result that we each have strong reason not to want, and if some practice
  of this kind that is not itself unnecessarily burdensome or unfair is
  widely accepted, then it is wrong not to comply with that practice."
- **Needed norms** (p. 22): "Cases in which such norms are needed but have
  not been established are importantly different. What is wrong in these
  cases is not, or not just, failing to do what a norm would require but
  also failing to do one's part in getting such a norm established." The
  duty "does not specify what needs to be done or at what cost" (n. 41),
  and the argument "does not "legislate" which norm to follow" (p. 23).
  Rawls's natural duty is the parallel: it requires us "to support and to
  comply with just institutions that exist" and "to further just
  arrangements not yet established, at least when this can be done without
  too much cost to ourselves" (n. 41). "compliance and non-compliance are
  not the only alternatives" (p. 24).
- **Rights** (p. 31): "the response, "But he has a right to do that" needs
  further explanation of what it is to have a right and why that matters."
- **Aggregation retracted** (p. 41): "The line of thinking about
  interpersonal aggregation that I set out in What We Owe to Each Other now
  seems to me mistaken in several respects." Kept (p. 47): "Reasons against
  rejecting a principle add up but reasons for rejecting a principle do
  not."
- **Relevance** (pp. 53–54): "the question of whether interests of a
  certain kind are relevant to a certain decision is broadly speaking a
  moral question", and "The moral nature of this idea of relevance weakens
  the explanatory power of a reductive view."

## Assumptions

Reasons are basic ("the bedrock (or perhaps I should say the shifting
sand)", p. 5); only individuals' personal reasons count for rejection;
moral judgments may lack definite truth values; the morality concerned is
among rational beings.

## Concepts

The questions of content and of acceptance; reductive and interactive
justification; schematic principles; assurance; intrapersonal aggregation;
the Principle of Established Practices; the strictly bilateral
interpretation; counterbalancing; relevance and the disabling of reasons.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpes6ef.** Scanlon separates the wrong of not complying with an
  established, justified practice (p. 21) from the wrong of failing to do
  one's part in establishing a needed one (p. 22), and cites Rawls's duty,
  which separates supporting and complying with existing just institutions
  from furthering those "not yet established", with a cost bound. The
  third duty is imperfect and does not say which norm, which fits the
  claim's bound without stating its terms of reachability. A102's "The
  latter may itself be morally problematic" understates him: he calls it
  wrong.
- **QUESTION-tmpk7u3e.** Sufficiency and relevance depend on "the relation
  we stand to one another and the decision that is at issue" (p. 54): a
  place where relations do work in Scanlon's own account. Powers and
  authority are not discussed.
- **QUESTION-tmpkqvxv.** In the pond case, less burdensome arrangements
  that could be established (a fence, a lifeguard) make reasonable the
  rejection of a principle permitting no steps toward them (p. 24): what
  could be established enters rejectability. Mistaken beliefs about
  feasibility are not discussed.
- **CLAIM-tmpyn6oi.** Left open; nothing on baselines narrowed by the
  governing party.
- **LIT-tmp06l3b.** First-hand evidence of what Scanlon now retracts from
  the 1998 book.
- The exchange's A100 and A102 are faithful; "significant problems" is A's
  for Scanlon's "some serious problems".
