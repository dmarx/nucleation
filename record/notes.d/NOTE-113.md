# NOTE-113: Status distrust of scientific experts

## Contribution

Distrust of scientific experts often persists despite supporting evidence, demonstrated competence and displays of goodwill, and it is usually put down to irrationality. Desmond instead proposes a logic of "status distrust". Because low-status individuals are objectively exposed to the outcomes of collective decision-making, they can be justified in distrusting high-status experts when they lack confidence that those experts care about their interests. On this view social status indicates who will be distrusted. The paper spells out the implications for work on trust in science and on expert communication.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1 (p. 2) opens with HPV-vaccine refusal in a rural community. It notes that distrust of scientists is a minority attitude but a puzzling one, since scientists remain the most trusted profession (Ipsos 2019).
- §§2–4 (pp. 6–12) contrast two sociological views of what status indicates. They argue that scientific experts are in fact high-status, and that status can itself be an indicator of distrustworthiness when service-for-prestige norms are thought to be violated.
- §5 (p. 12) is a case study of data sharing in precision medicine.
- §6 "The logic of status distrust" (p. 15) reconstructs the reasoning as an explicit argument. It reads Larson's account of vaccine hesitancy (feeling "ignored", "herded like sheep") as a perception that one's values are not represented in deliberation.
- §7 (p. 20) connects this to conspiracy thinking and irrationality. §8 (pp. 21–22) concludes that status differentials are unavoidable, and that what grounds distrust is the perception that high-status people disregard low-status people's values, which can happen even when experts mean well.

## Open questions

- It is a rationalising (non-debunking) account of science denial. Check whether §6's premises are argued to be true in real cases or only shown to be valid; the text says it establishes only that valid reasoning can underlie such distrust.
- It is relevant to the social epistemology of expertise and to AI-governance debates about trust in technical elites.
- Check the precision-medicine case study (§5) for whether any empirical evidence connects status to distrust or whether it is illustrative only.
