# CLAIM-423: In a political collective, components that change at different speeds (attention, coalitions, elected terms, administrative careers, law and treaties, generational memory) are not only nested by timescale: under a given institutional arrangement their different temporal horizons affect which forms of influence each can accumulate, exercise and keep, and so which of them holds authority over the others, so a state can stay continuous as an actor while the organization producing its positions changes under it

## The claim

The owner, at U28, quoting "regime ecology": "I think a domain where this
idea becomes extremely concrete is the intersection between domestic
politics and international politics. I'm thinking about the internal
power struggle of political parties vying for control, the external
struggles of states negotiating amongst themselves while simultaneously
undergoing their own internal microstruggles, and how separation of
timescales in elections, durations of tenure in elected and appointed
positions, lifespan, generational memory, etc. all interact to create
amorphous collectives of varying orders and degrees of coupling."

A28 tabulated the horizons, "illustrative rather than universal": public
attention in hours to weeks, coalition negotiation in weeks to years,
elections in years, civil-service careers in years to decades, courts,
legislation and treaties in years to generations, demographic change in
decades, collective memory in generations. "These are not simply
progressively larger boxes." Then: "The system's components not only
change at different speeds; their different speeds help determine which
components acquire authority over the others. A long-serving institution
possesses memory and operational competence that a newly elected
government may lack. An elected government may possess authority to
change the law that constitutes or constrains that institution. A party
faction may possess short-term leverage because the government needs its
votes immediately."

[CLAIM-331](CLAIM-331.md) says a level of organization has a temporal facet:
higher-level variables are characteristically slower. This claim rests on
that and adds that the speeds do not settle the order of authority. Fast
components can hold authority over slow ones (an election over an
administration's law), and slow ones over fast (a treaty over a
government). Which holds when depends partly on the speeds themselves:
memory and competence accrue to the slow, leverage to whoever the others
need now.

The record already holds two pieces A28 did not cite. [CLAIM-408](CLAIM-408.md) (Active)
is a formal model of two parties' shares of authority under
self-reinforcement and a common constraint, in which weakening the
constraint makes unequal allocations stable: the owner's "internal power
struggle of political parties" at one timescale. [CLAIM-327](CLAIM-327.md) holds that a
slow collective variable its members read and regenerate is part of the
collective's dynamics, which is A28's policy that "is helping produce the
landscape within which future choices will be made". This claim adds the
coupling between such pieces running at different speeds.

The consequence A40 drew for states: "Our proposed integration adds a
distinction between the state's formal continuity as an actor and the
changing organization of perspectives through which its policies are
generated and maintained. An agreement negotiated by one coalition might
be approved by another and implemented by a third. No one coalition owns
its entire history. Yet the continuing state can remain legally
responsible for its obligations." This is [CLAIM-417](CLAIM-417.md)'s carrier kept
apart from its perspective, for a state.

## What it does not say

- **Not that timescale is a source of authority.** Speed confers nothing
  by itself. A long-lived bureaucracy may accumulate information, networks
  and capacity to implement without any lawful authority to decide policy,
  and an election changes a coalition's authority quickly because
  constitutional rules give that event its consequence, not because
  elections are fast. The claim is that temporal horizons affect the
  accumulation, exercise and durability of particular forms of influence
  under specified institutional arrangements. (Made explicit in the title
  the same day after a review the owner relayed.)
- **Not new as political science.** A28 named Pierson's path dependence
  ([LIT-1372](../literature.d/LIT-1372.md)) and Putnam's two-level games ([LIT-1375](../literature.d/LIT-1375.md)), with
  Allison's bureaucratic politics and Tsebelis's veto players, as
  explaining much of it, and proposed "putting Putnam, Allison, Tsebelis,
  Pierson, and the existing Nucleation lines on authority and
  organizational continuity into direct conversation". What it claims as
  the record's is the link between timescale and authority, stated as an
  organizational relation of the kind [CLAIM-027](CLAIM-027.md) names.
- **Not the owner's U29 list as cases.** The "super stable" perspectives
  the owner named next (religious fundamentalism, anti-vax, fascism,
  progressive social liberalism, the Southern strategy, party lineages,
  Mormon control of Utah) are not filed as cases from the replies'
  summaries. A33 itself warned against "an abstract equivalence between
  traditions with radically different aims and moral significance". Party
  realignment is filed with its source ([CASE-133](../cases.d/CASE-133.md)).
- **Not legitimacy.** A40: "the conditions that make a commitment durable
  need not make it legitimate", and future generations are bound by
  commitments they could not help authorize. "A present government is not
  simply the present psychological state of one immortal national person."

## Note of 2026-10-22: the defeater, checked in part

The claim falls if Pierson or Putnam already state it.

- **Pierson does not, on the 1997 text** ([NOTE-1076](../notes.d/NOTE-1076.md); the 2000 article
  was not reached). He treats time horizons as a cause of which paths are
  taken and kept: "Because the decisions of voters, which determine
  political success, are taken in the short-run, elected officials employ
  a high discount rate" (p. 32). He does not ask which component of a
  polity gains authority over another, and calls horizons open: "Yet we
  know relatively little about the time horizons of different political
  actors" (p. 32).
- **Putnam** ([LIT-1375](../literature.d/LIT-1375.md)) was not reached, so his half is untested.
- **Two further accounts to answer.** Abbott's precursor ([NOTE-1077](../notes.d/NOTE-1077.md))
  makes tempo an explanatory variable in one case: the political arena's
  urgency against medicine's settle-once horizon decided who the doctors'
  allies were. Sewell ([LIT-1383](../literature.d/LIT-1383.md)) offers a rival to timescale: depth.
  Deep political structures last because they are taken for granted, and
  his "single constitution" under five party systems (p. 24) is the
  claim's continuous state under changing organization, stated in 1992.
  Neither links speed to authority, so neither defeats the claim.

## Note of 2026-10-30: what holds it back

Its defeat condition is only half checked. The claim falls if Putnam's
two-level games already state it, and Putnam ([LIT-1375](../literature.d/LIT-1375.md)) is Unavailable and
unread. Pierson's 2000 article was not reached; [THEORY-885](../theory.d/THEORY-885.md) reads only the
1997 text, which leaves the authority half open. No case in the record
shows a component's temporal horizon, rather than the formal rule that
gives an event its consequence, deciding which component holds
authority.
