# LIT-2664: Collision Between Vehicle Controlled by Developmental Automated Driving System and Pedestrian, Tempe, Arizona, March 18, 2018

National Transportation Safety Board (2019). *Collision Between Vehicle Controlled by Developmental Automated Driving System and Pedestrian, Tempe, Arizona, March 18, 2018*. NTSB Highway Accident Report NTSB/HAR-19/03 (PB2019-101402). https://www.ntsb.gov/investigations/AccidentReports/Reports/HAR1903.pdf.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading of 2026-11-17; the reading of 2026-11-16 is below.*

- **Probable cause** (p. v): the operator's failure to monitor, "because
  she was visually distracted throughout the trip by her personal cell
  phone".
- **Contributing**: Uber ATG's "(1) inadequate safety risk assessment
  procedures, (2) ineffective oversight of vehicle operators, and (3) lack
  of adequate mechanisms for addressing operators' automation
  complacency—all a consequence of its inadequate safety culture"; further,
  the impaired pedestrian's crossing and "the Arizona Department of
  Transportation's insufficient oversight of automated vehicle testing".
- **The design** "relied instead on the operator's intervention" to brake
  (p. v); removing the second operator "increased the task demands on the
  sole operator" (p. 45); the culture contributed "specifically to the
  vehicle operator's extended distraction" (p. 46).

## Reading of 2026-11-16

Read from the NTSB's PDF: the executive summary, human factors, company operations,
safety-culture analysis, probable cause and Vice Chairman Landsberg's concurrence. The report
names both the frontline failure and the organizational conditions. The design relied "on the
operator’s intervention to avoid a collision or mitigate an impact" (p. v), and ATG's culture
"created conditions ... that contributed ... specifically to the vehicle operator’s extended
distraction" (p. viii). The operator could still have avoided the crash had she been attentive
(p. vii). The report "does not assign fault or blame", so it is evidence of causes, not of how
blame was distributed. A17 §4 calls the operator's distraction a contributing cause; the NTSB
made it the probable cause. A17 also omits the pedestrian's impaired crossing, a contributing
factor. Elish ([LIT-2665](LIT-2665.md)) discussed the case before this report, from the preliminary report.

## Reading of 2026-11-17

Read a second time on 2026-11-17 by the continuation's readings and reconciled with the reading above on 2026-11-18: [NOTE-2034](../notes.d/NOTE-2034.md) now carries both readings and [THEORY-1932](../theory.d/THEORY-1932.md) both accounts. What that reading recorded here:

**The exchange's characterization: faithful in content, soft on the
ranking.** Every failure A17 lists is in the probable-cause statement.
A17 leaves out that the NTSB made the operator's distraction *the*
probable cause, with Uber's failures contributing (A's "contributed
directly" understates it), and that it also named the impaired
pedestrian's crossing. A17's "the operator really did fail to perform part
of their assigned role" and "the organization had not adequately managed
the known difficulties associated with that task" are supported (pp. 42,
44). The report does not show the operator becoming a moral crumple zone:
the NTSB itself carries cause upstream.
