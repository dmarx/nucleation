# THEORY-1598: Reimer, Schmid, Hewstone and Al Ramiah present self-categorization as placing oneself in a social category, hierarchical in level and salient by accessibility and fit, and social identification as a separate, multidimensional relation to the group whose components need not covary, and conclude that these identity processes alone do not explain negative intergroup attitudes

## The account

Self-categorization is "placing oneself in a social category" (p. 3).
It can come apart from membership: a German citizen may not
self-categorize as German. It is hierarchical (personal, social, human)
and situational, with salience set by accessibility and fit, and it
leads to depersonalization. Social identification is the further
relation to the category: value, importance, attachment, embeddedness,
behaviour and meaning, Ashmore et al.'s seven components, which "need
not converge or correlate" (pp. 5–7). Social identity theory explains
minimal-group bias by positive distinctiveness. Multiple-categorization
models (crossed categories, a common in-group, dual identity, in-group
projection, identity complexity) predict when bias falls. The chapter
concludes that self-categorization and identification "are not
sufficient to explain negative intergroup attitudes and behaviour"
(pp. 20–21).

## Evidence

Secondary throughout ([NOTE-1251](../notes.d/NOTE-1251.md)). The multidimensionality of
identification rests on measurement reviews (Ashmore et al. 2004; Leach
et al. 2008). The chapter says the self-esteem hypothesis "has received
extensive empirical support", which Rubin and Hewstone's review, read
in [NOTE-1278](../notes.d/NOTE-1278.md), does not bear out. No effect sizes or replication status
are given.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-300](../questions.d/QUESTION-300.md) asks for "a criterion of the self's extent that can
come apart from caring", and names "salient self-categorization" among
the candidates. The chapter holds that self-categorization and the
evaluative and affective components of identification are distinct and
need not covary, so it states the separation the question needs as a
premise of the field, without testing it against motivation.
