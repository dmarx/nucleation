# NOTE-407: Baumard, André & Sperber 2013, mutualistic morality

## Contribution

- A mutualistic (+/+) rather than altruistic account of the evolution of
  morality, based on partner choice instead of partner control (sect.
  2.1).
- An argument that partner choice, unlike partner control, pins down the
  division of gains: fairness as equal marginal benefit per unit invested
  (sect. 2.1.4).
- A two-step account of a non-instrumental moral sense: calculated
  fairness first, then intrinsic fairness, selected because it makes a
  more reliable partner (sect. 2.2.1).
- Mutualistic readings of mutual aid and punishment, and of a body of
  economic-game results (sects. 2.2–3).

## Key insight

The contract that contractualist philosophers describe is not a myth to be
explained away. It is the equilibrium of a market for cooperation
partners. Where everyone can walk away to someone else, every deal must
give each side at least its outside option, and with equal options that
means proportionality. A moral sense that tracks this is what partner
choice selects.

## Assumptions

- **Partner choice.** Individuals can leave a partner and find another;
  hunter-gatherer mobility and reputation-based choice are cited as
  evidence (sect. 2.1.3).
- **Equal outside options.** The fairness result requires that partners
  have the same opportunities outside the interaction (p. 63).
- **Morality = fairness, by definition.** "Here, we use morality in a sense
  that implies fairness" (note 2). Kin altruism and friendship are
  cooperation, but "not intrinsically moral" (p. 60).
- **Proximate and ultimate kept apart.** Selection is for reputation; the
  psychology is a genuine moral sense (sect. 2.2.1; R3).

## Key results

- **Formal claim (sect. 2.1.4).** With freely chosen partners, biased
  splits are not evolutionarily stable; payoff should be proportional to
  invested resources and to talent. The pairwise result is cited from
  André and Baumard 2011a; for effort and talent "a formal proof will
  require further modeling" (p. 67).
- **Entitlement in dictator games (sect. 3.1).** When dictators earned the
  money, 79% (of $10 earners) and 70% (of $40 earners) gave nothing,
  against 19% and 15% without earning (Cherry et al. 2002). When recipients
  earned it, modal offers matched what they had earned above the $10
  floor (Oxoby & Spraggon 2008).
- **Merit (sect. 3.2).** Modal dictator allocations matched contribution
  (30 of 73, Frohlich et al. 2004). In Cappelen et al. 2007, about 80%
  found it fair to reward effort and about 70% to reward talent.
- **Mutual aid (sect. 3.3).** Framing a game as Maasai *osotua* (need-based
  mutual help) lowered transfers and returns (Cronk 2007); Samburu
  hospitality framing lowered offers to about the share customary for
  real sharing (Lesorogol 2007).
- **Punishment (sect. 3.4).** In random-income games, people paid to
  reduce above-average and raise below-average incomes (Dawes et al.
  2007); antisocial punishment in public-goods games is read as
  retaliation for punishment felt to be unfair (Herrmann et al. 2008).
- **Commentary themes.**
  - *Purity, disgust, harm and the scope of morality.* Graham; Ramlakhan
    & Brook; Rochat & Robbins; Machery & Stich; Atran; Sachdeva, Iliev &
    Medin.
  - *Relationships and need.* Clark & Boothby; Warneken; Dunfield &
    Kuhlmeier.
  - *Market and bargaining dynamics.* DeScioli; Fessler & Holbrook;
    Guala; Alvard; Binmore.
  - *Altruism and group selection.* Gintis; Gill, Packer & Van Bavel;
    Roberts; Rachlin, Locey & Safin.
  - *Mechanism.* Ainslie; Ainsworth & Baumeister; Bshary & Raihani; Cova,
    Deonna & Sander; Tummolini, Scorolli & Borghi; Kirkby, Hinzen &
    Mikhail; Shaw & Knobe; Cappelen & Tungodden; Bonnefon et al.;
    Iran-Nejad & Bordbar.
- **Response (R4).** Two positions. *Broad* (André, Baumard): kin care,
  coalitions, hierarchy and purity are moralized only where fairness
  regulates them; sexual restraint is fairness within monogamy (R4.4,
  after Weeden et al. 2008). *Narrow* (Sperber): many such norms rest on
  other evolved dispositions and are not moral proper.

## Concepts

- **mutualism** — behaviour beneficial to both actor and recipient (+/+),
  following Hamilton's classification (p. 60).
- **partner control / partner choice** — stabilizing cooperation by
  retaliating against a fixed partner versus by leaving for another (pp.
  61–62).
- **outside options** — what each party could get outside the interaction;
  they bound acceptable splits (p. 62).
- **social selection** — selection by the choices of conspecifics, of
  which partner choice is a case (p. 61).
- **fairness** — impartiality, or mutual advantage: each party gains
  equally given what it invested (pp. 63, 77).

## Limitations

- The evidence is reinterpretation. None of the games was designed to
  separate mutualistic fairness from altruism or from other accounts; the
  article says experiments that could falsify it "can and should be
  devised" (p. 77).
- The core formal result is in another paper; the extension to effort and
  talent is unproven by the authors' own statement.
- Defining morality as fairness makes the theory unable to fail on purity,
  loyalty or hierarchy: what does not fit is not moral (Machery & Stich;
  Ramlakhan & Brook).
- The commentators' heterogeneity point stands: in controlled games with
  shared information, people still split into egalitarians, meritocrats
  and libertarians (Cappelen & Tungodden), which one shared sense of
  fairness does not predict without extra assumptions.

## Open questions

- Are fairness judgments sensitive to the cost of switching partners, the
  model's most direct prediction (DeScioli)?
- Does the broad or the narrow position fit purity better? [LIT-101](../literature.d/LIT-101.md)'s
  vignette mediation results bear on the broad one; a test that separated
  contamination from puritanical purity would bear on both.
- Why is fairness rare in [LIT-047](../literature.d/LIT-047.md)'s ethnographic record if it is the moral
  sense? A recoding with sharing norms included would answer it.
