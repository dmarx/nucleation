# NOTE-tmpqf55c: Martela and Ryan (2021) — If giving money to the Red Cross increases well-being, does taking money from the Red Cross increase ill-being?

## Contribution

An experimental, not correlational, test of the frustration criterion for
beneficence. The same "dosage" of impact on others, positive or negative, is
randomised in an otherwise identical task. It adds an asymmetry finding:
"the same 'dosage' of impact, which when positive is capable of increasing
participants well-being is not enough, when negative, to increase
participants ill-being to a similar degree" (p. 9).

## Key results

- **Hypotheses (p. 2).** Positive impact raises well-being and negative
  impact raises ill-being, each without necessarily moving the other side.
- **Study 1 (p. 3).** Negative affect was higher in the negative-impact than
  the neutral condition on Tukey's test (p = .035), but the main effect
  fell to p = .068 once gender was controlled. Its sample was planned for
  a large effect (Cohen's f = 0.50).
- **Study 2 (pp. 5–6).** "we didn't find any parallel support for the
  hypothesis that having an antisocial impact would increase people's
  ill-being"; negative-impact participants reported more meaning than
  neutral ones.
- **Study 3 (pp. 7–8).** No ill-being differences; the negative-impact group
  rated "I helped scientific research" higher than the neutral group
  (p ≈ .05).
- **Meta-analysis (§4, p. 8).** Negative versus neutral: beneficence
  frustration d = 0.84 [0.67, 1.01], negative affect 0.07 [−0.02, 0.16],
  anxiety 0.09 [−0.08, 0.25], depression 0.06 [−0.09, 0.20]. "Thus
  meta-analysis showed no effect of the negative impact condition on
  ill-being indicators. Instead, being in the negative impact condition
  seemed to increase positive affect, vitality, and meaningfulness as well
  as both beneficence satisfaction and beneficence frustration."
- **Exclusions (p. 6).** A minority of "resisters" pressed less in the
  negative condition and were excluded by an earnings cut that was not
  preregistered; the authors report that reanalysis with them left the
  results unchanged.
- **Limitations stated (p. 9).** One online panel; and the possibility that
  participants see positive impact as their own responsibility and
  negative impact "as the responsibility of the instructor".

## Assumptions

- Felt antisocial impact can be induced by a small, instructed loss to a
  charity, comparable in "dosage" to an equal gain.
- Self-reported mood immediately after the task is the outcome on which
  ill-being would show.

## Concepts

Beneficence satisfaction and frustration; prosocial versus antisocial
impact; dosage; compensation; asymmetry of enhancement and harm.

## Limitations

- One US online panel; self-report; trivial stakes (15c) and instructed
  harm, so responsibility can be shifted to the experimenter, as the authors
  say. A null at that dose does not show beneficence frustration harmless at
  real doses.
- The asymmetry compares a gain and a loss assumed equal in felt size. The
  manipulation checks move satisfaction more (d 1.26) than frustration
  (d 0.84), so the "same dosage" is asserted, not shown.
- The compensation account rests on one single-item measure at p ≈ .05.
- The situational depression scale is weakly reliable (α .52–.61), so its
  null is weak.
