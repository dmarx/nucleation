# NOTE-tmpliz3l: Barandiaran — Organizational Accounts of Malfunction

## Contribution

A critique of the most cited organizational account of malfunction and a
replacement for it. The dual-order account, which the paper calls SMM after
Saborido, Moreno and Mossio, needs two orders of norms and a regulated
switch between regimes before anything can malfunction. Barandiaran shows it
excludes canonical cases and proposes a single, graded standard drawn from
the organism's viability space. It splits dysfunction into three kinds and
sketches how a failure can be attributed to a part.

## Key insight

If norms are all-or-nothing (alive or dead), nothing can malfunction while
the organism lives, and a second order of norms has to be invented to make
room for failure. If instead the organization defines, at each state from
which it is heading toward death, how fast some parameter must change to
turn back, then a trait can be judged by whether it pushes the right way
and fast enough. Graded norms come from the first order alone.

## Assumptions

- **The organizational account of function.** Norms arise from dynamic
  presuppositions among the traits of a recursively self-maintaining
  organization: "The normative function of a trait or process A is to
  operate according to the dynamic presupposition of other traits (B, C,
  ...) within a recursively self-maintaining organization."
- **Essential variables and a viability space**, after Ashby (1952), not
  Aubin. Viability theory in this paper means bifurcation analysis of a
  parametrized dynamical system. Aubin is not cited.
- **A choice of parameter.** "The normative field is defined by the
  “intrinsic” dynamics of the system without the parameter P (or for a
  fixed parametric value)." The field is relative to which parameter is
  under evaluation; another choice moves P into the intrinsic dynamics.
- **Continuity.** The definition is given for the continuous case, and
  the discontinuous one is said to follow.
- **Ceteris paribus** throughout the model; the environment is held fixed.

## Key results

- **The dual-order account (§3.1).** Moreno & Mossio (2015, p. 85):
  "malfunctions are a subset of functions that fit first-order norms (of
  the first-order ongoing organization in which they match functional
  presuppositions), but not second-order ones (since they do not obey to
  second-order regulatory functions, and prevent the shift to another
  first-order organization)." Reconstructed: T1 malfunctions iff a
  regulatory trait T2 switches the system from regime Ra to Rb and T1
  fails to match the range Rb requires. The canonical case is a coronary
  artery that does not widen when adrenaline moves the body from rest to
  stress.
- **Too narrow (§3.2).** (1) It cannot count a malfunction within one
  regime: a stiffening artery at rest does not malfunction by it. (2) It
  cannot count a malfunction of the highest regulation: the autonomic
  nervous system that fails to trigger the stress response does not
  malfunction by it. And it cannot tell whether T1 failed to match the new
  range or T2 failed to shift it.
- **Two assumptions behind it (§3.2).** "First order (constitutive) norms
  are all or nothing, gradual norms are to be found somewhere else. 2.
  Regulations occur (only) as switches between regimes of
  self-maintenance." Against the first: bradycardia, dyspnea and hypoxia
  name functions fulfilled at the wrong pace. Against the second:
  enzymatic, hormonal and cardiac regulation are mostly continuous
  modulation of rates.
- **Regions of the viability space (§4.1, §4.3).** A viable region, where
  with the parameter held constant the system stays alive; a "precarious
  region where the system is (still) alive but will inevitably die if no
  parametric change occurs"; and a terminal region, where "the system is
  till alive but it will eventually die. No matter how much we increase
  [F], it is already too late to recover". A viable region need not exist:
  with an upper boundary (bursting) low enough, the system must oscillate
  inside the precarious region.
- **The normative field (§4.2).** "A normative field can be defined as the
  minimum constant parametric change, for each point of the precarious
  region, necessary to avoid the irreversible disintegration of the
  system". It is stated negatively, as avoiding death, because a safe
  region may not exist. It is a minimum: more may help, only the minimum
  is required. It is a rate, so that no normative judgment has to wait for
  the last instant before death.
- **Function (abstract, §4.2).** "A trait operates normatively when its
  effects on the viability space correlate positively with the normative
  field."
- **Three kinds of dysfunction (§4.4).** "Subfunctional operations are
  those that positively correlate with the normative field but
  quantitatively fail to match the required speed. Malfunctional
  operations are those that do not positively correlate with the normative
  field, either because they negatively correlate with it or because they
  don’t correlate at all". Nonfunctional traits do not operate, or operate
  with null effect. "It is important to note that not all nonfunctioning
  traits are dysfunctional, they are only dysfunctional when they have to
  operate and they don’t". Most of what SMM call malfunction is here
  subfunction or nonfunction.
- **Attribution (§4.5).** Identifying which norm is unmet is not
  attributing the failure to a part. Proposed procedure: vary each trait
  within its mechanistic and systemic feasibility range to see whether
  changing it alone would meet the field, and recurse until a feasible
  intervention is found. The locus of dysfunction (uptake U) can differ
  from where responsibility lies (a missing enzyme S, note 21).
- **Compensation does not hide dysfunction (§4.5).** "compensations have a
  cost on the viability of the system". A remaining kidney's
  hyperfiltration and the muscles that stabilise a torn ACL keep the
  function going and wear the compensating parts, and the organism's
  presuppositions (two kidneys, a given joint geometry) let the damaged
  part be called dysfunctional without evolutionary history.
- **Norms without orders (§5.1).** Regulations can malfunction, and regime
  switches are added dimensions of the viability space. "in biological
  systems the basic order is always “broken”, there is nothing like a
  constitutive closure that is intrinsically stable." "However, it is still
  possible to be a dualist on organizational orders (acknowledging genuine
  ontological differences between first and second order closure) but a
  monist regarding norms."
- **Nested autonomies (note 23).** "Conflict between normative levels can
  indeed happen but they demand the emergence of new levels of autonomy
  (multicellular, sensorimotor, social, etc.)".
- **Illness as a shrunken viability space (§5.2).** "some maladies and
  illnesses might be better conceived as reducing the viability space (and
  the consequent autonomy of the organism) than as a direct failure to
  contribute to self-maintenance", after Canguilhem.
- **The nature of norms (§5.4).** Against reading norms as causes in
  conflict, they are conditions of possibility of the organism's existence:
  "Functions are explanatory without being efficiently causal." The horizon
  of normative judgment is "the autonomy of each living organization".

## Concepts

- **Dynamic presupposition.** B and C presuppose A when their operation
  and existence would be compromised were A to operate differently, in a
  recursively self-maintaining organization.
- **Viability space.** The space of essential variables.
- **Viable, precarious and terminal regions.** Safe with parameters fixed;
  alive but dying unless a parameter changes; alive and beyond recovery.
- **Normative field.** The minimum constant parametric change, at each
  precarious state, needed to avoid disintegration.
- **Subfunction, malfunction, nonfunction.** Right direction too slow;
  wrong or no direction; no operation or no effect where operation is
  required.
- **Dysfunctional attribution.** Locating which part's failure accounts
  for an unmet norm, distinct from identifying the unmet norm.

## Connections

Builds on Barandiaran & Egbert (2014) for the normative field and model,
Christensen & Bickhard (2002) for the organizational account, and Di Paolo
(2005, [LIT-1017](../literature.d/LIT-1017.md)) for the need to grade norms; Di Paolo's adaptivity and this
paper's precarious region describe the same zone. It targets Moreno &
Mossio (2015, [LIT-536](../literature.d/LIT-536.md)) and Saborido (2011). Mossio, Saborido & Moreno
(2009, [LIT-570](../literature.d/LIT-570.md)) is cited as part of the standard approach, but the
dual-order definitions quoted come from the 2015 book; the 2009 paper's
dysfunction is a trait produced by the organization that fails to
contribute to it ([NOTE-444](NOTE-444.md)). Barandiaran keeps constraint closure
([LIT-582](../literature.d/LIT-582.md)) as compatible and leaves it out of the model (note 6). It
relates the account to McShea's field theory of teleology and to
dispositional accounts.

## Bearing on the record

**On [QUESTION-tmpiyoc5](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpiyoc5.md): a partial yes, with the standard named.** The
question asks whether a self-reproducing authority structure can be
pathological by a standard the organization itself supplies. This paper
supplies one: the normative field of the whole organization, which no one
outside assigns. Three of its results bear directly.

- Regulatory traits can malfunction (§5.1). An authority structure is a
  regulation of other processes, so it can be judged by whether its
  operations correlate with the organization's field. The dual-order
  account could not judge the highest regulation at all.
- Some illness is a shrinking of the viability space, not a failure to
  contribute (§5.2). [TERM-020](../terms.d/TERM-020.md)'s fifth step, continued activation that
  "further reduces the practical capacity to leave or revise it", is that
  kind of harm in the record's terms. The mapping is the record's, not the
  paper's.
- Compensation costs viability (§4.5). An entrenched authority whose
  dominance others work around would show up as wear on the compensating
  processes.

What it does not give. The standard is the whole's viability. An authority
that perpetuates itself while keeping the organization as viable as
before, with no shrinkage and no cost, is not dysfunctional on this
account, however it came to rule. So the paper does not tell merited trust
from entrenched advantage ([CLAIM-tmpf1063](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpf1063.md)) where both keep the organization
alive equally well. Note 23 adds the harder case. If the authority
structure has become an autonomous level with its own normative field, its
norms and the organization's can conflict, and the paper gives no rule for
which prevails. That is Di Paolo's addiction case ([NOTE-719](NOTE-719.md)) for
organizations.

**On [CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md).** Agrees. The field's norms are norms for the organism,
relative to its persistence. Nothing in the paper says anything ought to
persist.

**On [THEORY-134](../theory.d/THEORY-134.md) and [CLAIM-079](../claims.d/CLAIM-079.md).** The terminal region, where the system
is alive and no admissible change of the parameter saves it, is the
complement of the viability kernel for the controlled system. The
precarious region lies inside that kernel but is not invariant at the
current parameter. Barandiaran's "viable region" is not Aubin's viable
set: it is where the system stays alive with the parameter held constant.
The paper does not draw these correspondences; they are the record's. Like
viability theory, it takes the essential variables as given, so the
question [THEORY-134](../theory.d/THEORY-134.md) leaves open, which constraints count as required, is
open here too.

**On [QUESTION-tmpgjngk](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpgjngk.md).** Indirectly. The attribution procedure, varying
one trait within its feasible range and asking whether that alone meets the
field, is an intervention design of the kind the question asks for. It
locates where a norm fails, not whether the norm is the system's own.

## Limitations

- The model has one variable and one parameter. Nothing is shown about
  computing a normative field for a real organism; the author says it "is
  extraordinarily complex and may not be feasible".
- The field depends on which parameter is evaluated and on how the system
  is partitioned; the author notes the partition "is not trivial".
- "Correlate positively" is not given a measure.
- The essential variables and the boundaries of viability are assumed, not
  derived from the organization.
- The critique is of the definitions as quoted from Moreno & Mossio (2015).
  Whether their wider account answers it is not examined.
- §5.4 on transcendental norms is a sketch, and note 29 says so.

## Open questions

- Can a normative field be computed for a model with several interacting
  regulatory traits, and does it sort subfunction from malfunction there?
- How should the field be defined when a regulatory subsystem has its own
  autonomy (note 23)? Whose viability space sets the norm?
- Is a shrinking viability space a fourth kind of dysfunction, or a
  property of the field? The paper raises it and leaves it open.
