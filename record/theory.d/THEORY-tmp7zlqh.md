# THEORY-tmp7zlqh: Spears and Postmes state the SIDE model: anonymity and immersion in a group do not remove the self but make the salient identity more salient, so behaviour follows the group's norms more closely under a salient group identity and moves away from them under a salient personal one, and anonymity to a powerful out-group frees in-group-normative behaviour it would punish

## The account

Deindividuation theory held that anonymity and immersion cut
self-awareness and release antinormative behaviour. SIDE rejects it:
"Whereas deindividuation implies a loss of self in the group,
depersonalization refers to the emergence of group in the self: the
tendency to see others but also oneself in group terms" (p. 27), and
"behavior becomes more social or socially regulated, not less so"
(p. 26). What anonymity amplifies depends on which self is salient. A
strategic dimension adds that anonymity to a powerful out-group, and
in-group support, free behaviour normative for the in-group that the
out-group would punish.

## Evidence

From NOTE-1280: Postmes and Spears's meta-analysis; Spears et al.
(1990), anonymous groups polarizing toward the norm under group
salience and away from it under personal salience; Reicher and Levine
(1994) and Spears et al. (2002) on the strategic dimension; four studies
cited in one sentence that anonymity does not lower personal
self-awareness; and Chan's donors, whose giving rose with salience
"particularly among low identifiers" (p. 39).

## What it bears on

QUESTION-300 lists "salient self-categorization" as a candidate
"criterion of the self's extent that can come apart from caring". SIDE
experiments fix the self's level by instruction, not by what
participants value, and that level predicts the direction of influence.
The model also denies that the crowd member is selfless, while treating
the change as a switch of self rather than an enlargement.
