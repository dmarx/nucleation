# THEORY-442: Where the non-epistemic consequences of error are foreseeable, sound scientific reasoning requires weighing them in the internal choices that carry inductive risk, and non-epistemic values then enter legitimately only indirectly, through the costs of error, never as reasons to think a claim true

## Source

Douglas (2000), [LIT-139](../literature.d/LIT-139.md) (*Philosophy of Science* 67(4), 559–579), read in
full in [NOTE-145](../notes.d/NOTE-145.md).

## What was actually shown

**The premise from the cases.** Inductive risk arises at the choice of
methodology, the characterisation of data and the interpretation of results,
as well as at acceptance ([THEORY-639](THEORY-639.md)).

**The argument (§3, pp. 563–565).** At each internal stage "one can make a
wrong (i.e., epistemically incorrect) choice, with consequences following
from that choice". When those consequences fall outside science and can be
foreseen, deciding which error to risk more needs a valuation of them, and
that is a non-epistemic value judgement. Whatever threshold or classification
rule is adopted answers it implicitly. So "non-epistemic values are required
for good reasoning" (p. 565), and for such science "value-free science is
inadequate science" (p. 559).

**Two roles (§3, §5, §7).** Longino's uncontroversial roles of values
(choosing problems, using results, ethical limits on methods) are direct and
external. Inductive risk gives an indirect, internal role: values weigh the
consequences of possible error, together with how likely error is. Values
alone never decide the choice (p. 565). A direct role inside science stays
illegitimate. Blinding is endorsed to prevent "the seeing of something
because one wants to see it" (p. 572), and "I want X to be true, therefore X
is true" remains a bad argument (p. 578).

**Against leaving values to the users (§2, p. 563).** Jeffrey and McMullin
would have scientists report and users apply utilities. Douglas replies that
scientists' claims are authoritative in policy, and that many risks are taken
before acceptance, where decision-makers cannot see them.

**What could have come out otherwise.** This is a normative argument. It could
fail at its unargued premise, or at the reply to the division of labour.

## What this does not say

- **Why scientists bear that responsibility.** The premise is explicitly
  "beyond the scope of this paper" (p. 563), and "required" inherits it.
- **That reporting uncertainty cannot replace acceptance.** The argument does
  not engage that rejoinder ([NOTE-145](../notes.d/NOTE-145.md), Limitations). The reply to Jeffrey and
  McMullin is one paragraph.
- **How to tell the indirect role from the direct one in practice.** A rule for
  borderline slides chosen for its known regulatory effect may slide from one
  to the other. The paper does not address this; Douglas's later book does.
- **Who should supply the valuations.** The paper argues only that scientists
  cannot avoid them.
- **That most research is affected.** That research without foreseeable
  outside consequences is "rapidly becoming a minority" is asserted (§7).
- **What this means for objectivity.** That is deferred to "future work"
  (p. 578).

## Connections

- Reiss ([LIT-118](../literature.d/LIT-118.md); [THEORY-537](THEORY-537.md)) builds the same indirect role into a theory
  of evidence: higher stakes lower the bar for rejecting an alternative. He
  does not cite Douglas.
- Longino's critical contextual empiricism ([THEORY-151](THEORY-151.md)) also denies that values
  stay outside science. Douglas's argument is normative where Longino's is
  descriptive (fn. 8).
