# THEORY-tmpefpin: Groupware that requires extra work from members who do not perceive a direct benefit from it is resisted or goes unused, and the applications that succeed in groups give each user who does the work a benefit of their own

## Source

Grudin (1994), "Groupware and social dynamics: eight challenges for
developers", [LIT-757](../literature.d/LIT-757.md), read in [NOTE-tmpdnj1q](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpdnj1q.md): Table 1 (p. 97), the
section "A Disparity: Work vs. Benefit" (pp. 96–97), the sections on
intuition (pp. 100–101) and on email (pp. 102–103), and the closing list
(p. 104).

## What was actually shown

**The claim, in Grudin's words.** "Groupware applications often require
additional work from individuals who do not perceive a direct benefit from
the use of the application" (Table 1, challenge 1). "Most groupware
requires some people to do additional work to enter or process
information required or produced by the application" (p. 96).

**The cases.**

- Automatic meeting scheduling helps the convener and needs everyone's
  on-line calendar. Calendars were found to be kept by managers, not by
  individual contributors, so "this groupware feature is rarely used".
- Voice annotation is easier for the speaker and harder for the listener.
- Project management helps the manager and needs others to enter what they
  do not otherwise keep on-line. A naval system built over ten years was
  replaced partly for that reason.
- Decision makers favour applications that benefit managers and
  underestimate "the unwelcome extra work that an application will require
  of other users, resulting in neglect or resistance" (p. 101).

**The success case.** In email "the primary beneficiary typically does a
little more work" (p. 103): the sender, who wants the message read, types
it.

**What could have come out otherwise.** The success cases could have
included applications that asked work of non-beneficiaries and were used
anyway. Grudin reports one, a voice-messaging system that succeeded only
after management removed the alternative, and treats it as a case of
forced critical mass, not as a counterexample.

## What this does not say

- **Not a measurement.** No study here relates the size of the disparity
  to the rate of use. The cases are chosen to illustrate, some come from
  the author's experience, and there is no base rate of failures.
- **Not sufficient for success.** Grudin says "even a net benefit with
  equal costs and benefits for all users will not guarantee groupware
  success" (pp. 96–97), because of critical mass and freeloading, and
  lists six further challenges. Removing the disparity removes one cause
  of failure, not all of them.
- **Not about time.** The disparity is between roles at one time:
  convener and invitee, manager and subordinate. It is not, as the
  design-rationale literature often puts it, between an author now and a
  reader later.
- **Not about mandated systems.** Where an organization makes the extra
  work someone's job, the problem is "much less likely" (p. 96).

  benefits the record rather than the reviewer, is where neglect will
  appear.

  already in use (p. 100) agrees with capture inside work already being
  done. It says nothing about when to capture.
