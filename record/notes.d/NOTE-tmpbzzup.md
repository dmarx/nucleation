# NOTE-tmpbzzup: Searle — Minds, brains, and programs

## Contribution

A thought experiment that puts a mind inside the implementation of a
program, so that one can ask the implementer whether running the program
produced understanding. It turned the strong-AI claim from a slogan into a
target with a fixed shape, and its catalogue of replies, labelled by the
universities that offered them, organised the debate that followed.

## Key insight

If a program were sufficient for understanding, then anyone who ran it
would understand. A person can run it by hand and not understand. So the
understanding, where there is any, comes from something other than the
program: for Searle, from the causal powers of the stuff that runs it.

## Assumptions

- **Proposition 1** (abstract): intentionality is a product of causal
  features of the brain, "an empirical fact".
- **The man is a full implementation.** Following the rules by hand, he
  is "simply an instantiation of the computer program". That his slowness
  and the scale of the task do not matter is assumed, not argued.
- **First-person authority settles the case.** "It seems to me quite
  obvious in the example that I do not understand a word of the Chinese
  stories." The verdict on the system is read off the verdict the man
  gives about himself.
- **Clear cases suffice.** Degrees and kinds of understanding are granted
  and set aside: "there are clear cases in which 'understanding' literally
  applies and clear cases in which it does not".
- **Programs are purely formal** and can be "carved off" from any
  particular causal realisation.

## Key results

- **The two-part conclusion** (§§1–2 of the argument): the program is not
  sufficient for understanding; and "not the slightest reason has been
  given to suppose" it is necessary or contributory.
- **Six replies** and Searle's answers:
  1. *Systems* (Berkeley): the man internalises rules and data; "there
     isn't anything in the system that isn't in him", so the system
     doesn't understand. A Chinese subsystem would be "simply a part of
     the English subsystem". The reply's only motivation is passing the
     Turing test, and the adequacy of that test is the point at issue.
     Accepting it makes stomach, heart and liver "understanding
     subsystems".
  2. *Robot* (Yale): adds causal relations to the world, and so concedes
     that formal symbol manipulation is not enough; but the man inside the
     robot still understands nothing.
  3. *Brain simulator* (Berkeley and M.I.T.): an odd reply for a
     functionalist; water pipes simulating the synapses do not understand,
     and the man can internalise the pipes.
  4. *Combination* (Berkeley and Stanford): we would rationally ascribe
     intentionality to a humanlike robot "as long as we knew nothing more
     about it", and withdraw it on learning it runs a formal program.
  5. *Other minds* (Yale): the question is what is attributed, not how it
     is known; "it is no answer to this argument to feign anesthesia".
  6. *Many mansions* (Berkeley): concedes the thesis by redefining AI as
     whatever produces cognition artificially.
- **What could think.** "We are precisely such machines"; an artifact
  with a nervous system "sufficiently like ours" could; a digital computer
  could, in the sense that everything instantiates some program; but
  nothing thinks "solely in virtue of being a computer with the right sort
  of program".
- **Three breaks in "mind is to brain as program is to hardware".** Programs
  have absurd realisations (toilet paper and stones, water pipes, wind
  machines); intentional states are defined by content, not form; mental
  states are a product of the brain, a program is not a product of the
  computer.
- **Simulation is not duplication.** Simulated fires do not burn, and "to
  confuse simulation with duplication is the same mistake, whether it is
  pain, love, cognition, fires, or rainstorms".
- **Diagnosis.** "Information processing" equivocates; AI keeps a residual
  behaviourism; and strong AI presupposes a dualism in which the brain
  does not matter.

## Concepts

- **Strong AI** — the claim that the appropriately programmed computer
  literally has cognitive states and that programs explain human
  cognition. **Weak AI** — the computer as a tool for studying the mind.
- **Intentionality** — "that feature of certain mental states by which
  they are directed at or about objects and states of affairs in the
  world" (n. 3). Beliefs, desires and intentions have it; undirected
  anxiety does not.
- **Understanding** — as used here, possession of the intentional states,
  not their truth (n. 2).
- **Formal** — identifiable "entirely by their shapes".
- **Causal powers** — the brain's capacity to produce intentional states;
  what a simulation of "the formal structure of the sequence of neuron
  firings" leaves out.

## Connections

- **Turing** ([LIT-tmp265e0](../literature.d/LIT-tmp265e0.md), read in [NOTE-tmpjsht2](NOTE-tmpjsht2.md)). Searle's "other minds
  reply" is Turing's answer to Jefferson, and Searle gives it "only a
  short reply": the issue is what is attributed, not how it is known.
  Turing's paper does not claim understanding for a passing machine; it
  claims the word "think" will come to be used of it.
- **Block** ([LIT-tmpc6np5](../literature.d/LIT-tmpc6np5.md), read in [NOTE-tmpxt8ou](NOTE-tmpxt8ou.md)). The China brain and
  the Chinese room both build a functional duplicate out of people. Block
  doubts its qualia and grants a Psychofunctional homunculi-head beliefs;
  Searle denies even the intentional states. Block's elementary-particle
  case is the mirror of Searle's internalisation: there the tiny people are
  parts of *you*, and Block says your pains survive.
- **Nagel** ([LIT-096](../literature.d/LIT-096.md)). Searle's reference list has Nagel's bat only as a
  commentator's citation; the target article argues about intentionality
  and does not use the bat.
- **Seth** ([LIT-135](../literature.d/LIT-135.md), [NOTE-169](NOTE-169.md)) separates his biological naturalism from
  Searle's later formulation. This paper is the earlier, causal-powers
  version, and it already leaves open whether another chemistry would do.
- **Chalmers.** His implementation paper, being filed now by other
  readers, answers Searle by finding two computational structures, and so
  two minds, in the room.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-tmp31lxe](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp31lxe.md) (Proposed): extends it with a third route.** The
  account finds two ways to accept a mind of mindless parts while refusing
  a mind of minded parts: anti-nesting, and architecture. Searle refuses
  the downward move itself, and on neither ground: by causal powers that a
  formal organisation cannot carry. His argument would equally refuse a
  society of mind, a nation, or a single brain described as a program, so
  it does not separate Minsky from Schwitzgebel at all; it rejects both
  functionalist moves. The account should name this as the position that
  sits outside its dispute rather than as a way across it.
- **[THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md) (Proposed): consistent.** C5 shows that for Searle
  behaviour does license ascription until architectural knowledge defeats
  it. That is [THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md)'s two-level structure, with Searle taking the
  biological side of the architectural dispute.
- **[NOTE-176](NOTE-176.md):** Šekrst's use of the Chinese room for the claim that
  current models lack understanding is within the paper's scope, but the
  paper's conclusion is that no program *suffices*, not that a given
  system lacks understanding. [NOTE-176](NOTE-176.md) records that Šekrst later disavows
  the argument as one against the possibility of machine understanding,
  which matches what Searle himself grants.

## Limitations

- **The decisive step is asserted.** The internalisation answer to the
  systems reply assumes that whatever the man runs in his head is a part
  of him whose understanding he could report. That is the question.
- **Introspective access is assumed** to be authoritative about all
  understanding the man's head might host.
- **Scale and speed are idealised away.** No estimate is given of how long
  the man would take, or whether that bears on the verdict.
- **Consciousness is not argued.** The paper mentions producing
  "consciousness, intentionality, and all the rest of it", but its
  argument concerns intentionality and understanding.
- **Which causal powers matter is left open** ("an empirical question").
- **Only the target article was read.** The commentaries,
  including Dennett's and Hofstadter's, and Searle's response were not.

## Open questions

- What test, if any, would show a system has the brain's relevant causal
  powers, since by C5 behaviour cannot and the program cannot?
- Does the internalisation reply survive if the internalised system's
  states are not accessible to the man's introspection, as the subsystem
  version of the systems reply supposes?
