# NOTE-1389: Gioia, Schultz and Corley — Organizational identity, image, and adaptive instability

## Contribution

It takes Albert and Whetten's (1985) definition of organizational
identity, "central, enduring, and distinctive", and argues the
"enduring" pillar away. Identity is constructed in interaction with
outsiders, image in its several forms feeds back on it, and the result
is an identity that changes in meaning over short periods while keeping
the same labels. The authors argue that this instability is useful to
the organization, not a threat to it.

## Key insight

The durability is in the labels, not in their meaning. "We stand for
service!" or the "H-P Way" persist while what they mean is
reinterpreted, so an identity can have continuity without being
enduring. The paper separates the two: "An identity with a sense of
continuity ... is one that shifts in its interpretation and meaning
while retaining labels for 'core' beliefs and values that extend over
time and context" (p. 64).

## Assumptions

- Identity is "an organization's members' collective understanding of
  the features presumed to be central and relatively permanent" (p. 64),
  socially constructed, at the organizational level by the same
  processes as at the individual (Cooley, Gergen, Weick).
- Members are also outsiders (customers, audiences of the media), so
  they are sensitive to how others see the organization.
- The first, process-model account rests on realist assumptions
  (identity has some substantive basis and changes incrementally); the
  revisionist-history and postmodern accounts that follow are
  nominalist (p. 63–64).

## Key results

The results are conceptual.

- **Forms of image (Table 1).** Construed external image (members' view
  of how outsiders see the organization), projected image, desired
  future image, corporate identity, transient impression and
  reputation, "relatively stable, long-term, collective judgments by
  outsiders".
- **Process model (Figure 1, pp. 67–70).** A triggering external
  feedback or event prompts members to compare identity with construed
  external image ("Who do we think we are?" against "Who do they think
  we are?"). No discrepancy affirms identity; a discrepancy may be
  ignored, or acted on by changing identity (helped by a projected
  desired future image) or by changing outsiders' impressions
  (communicating identity more clearly, impression management, or
  false images). Projected images return in "refracted" form and the
  comparison recurs. Shell's Brent Spar (1991–1999) runs through every
  branch.
- **Revisionist history (pp. 71–72).** Members reinterpret the past to
  fit present and desired images: "The 'facts' of the past might not be
  in doubt, but their meaning always is." This "makes identity appear
  stable to perceivers, even as it changes."
- **Postmodern lens and its limits (pp. 72–74).** Baudrillard's
  "everything ends up as image" is set against three limits: the
  environment's demand for recognizable, stable partners; evidence that
  members keep identity apart from image even when image is flattering
  (Elsbach and Kramer's business-school rankings); and multiple
  identities that do not shift together and so damp wholesale change.
  Identity "is inevitably influenced by image but does not necessarily
  become image".
- **Adaptive instability (pp. 74–75).** Instability "allows better
  adaptation", reactively (IBM's response to its image of a ponderous
  mainframe maker) or proactively (top managers introducing
  "ambiguity-by-design" and a desired future image, in Gioia and
  Chittipeddi's and Gioia and Thomas's university).
- **For research (pp. 75–77).** Cross-sectional studies may treat
  identity as stable; longitudinal ones may not. The authors ask how to
  account for ephemeral organizations, "hollow corporations", and "the
  Florida Marlins baseball team, which won the 1997 World Series and then
  was decimated in the space of months by the trading of key players".

## Concepts

- **Organizational identity**: members' collective understanding of
  central, distinctive features presumed relatively permanent.
- **Continuity versus endurance**: continuity keeps labels while
  meanings shift; endurance would keep both.
- **Construed external image**: members' beliefs about how outsiders
  see the organization (Dutton et al. 1994's relabelling).
- **Reputation**: outsiders' "relatively stable, long-term, collective
  judgments".
- **Adaptive instability**: identity's instability, through its
  interplay with image, as an aid to change.

## Connections

It builds on Albert and Whetten's definition, Dutton and Dukerich's Port
Authority study and Gagliardi's "change to remain what they have always
been". Cloutier and Ravasi's identity trajectories ([LIT-1141](../literature.d/LIT-1141.md), not
reached) is a later longitudinal study of the continuity the paper asks
researchers to study. Feldman and Pentland ([LIT-1124](../literature.d/LIT-1124.md), [NOTE-1386](NOTE-1386.md)) make
a parallel move for routines: the ostensive aspect persists as a gloss
while performances change what it means, as identity labels do here.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-103](../cases.d/CASE-103.md).** The case's split of a fanbase's reputation into members'
  behaviour (P1), others' belief (P2) and the belief's effect on the
  fanbase (P3) is close to the paper's split between identity, reputation
  and construed external image, and the paper's process model is A5's
  insider-identification path: members compare who they think they are
  with who they think outsiders think they are, and change one or the
  other. The paper also makes reputation the slow variable ("relatively
  stable, long-term"), against an identity that can shift quickly, which
  fits the case's reputation that outlasts the behaviour that earned it.
  It does not study a case where the reputation is wrong, and it does
  not consider the case's selection path, a reputation that attracts or
  repels members.
- **[CLAIM-329](../claims.d/CLAIM-329.md).** The paper's continuity of labels with changing meanings
  is a challenge to any persistence criterion that requires identity
  content to stay fixed, as A6 expected. It does not supply the claim's
  causal-transmission criterion: its continuity is members' interpretive
  work and outsiders' demand for recognizable partners, and its examples
  (the Marlins' gutted roster among them) are posed as puzzles, not
  answered.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- No data. The illustrations are chosen successes of deliberate change
  (IBM, Oticon, British Airways, an unnamed Pittsburgh firm told in "a
  personal conversation"); the paper does not show that instability is
  adaptive on average or say when it is not, beyond warning of "the
  unintended substitution of faddish image for key values".
- "Adaptive" is not defined against a measure of fitness or survival.
- **Slips.** Bourdieu is "Bourdieau" in the text and the reference list;
  "Wnen" for "When" on p. 68; Shell's "ca series" on p. 67. The Brent
  Spar account says Greenpeace's misinformation about the platform's
  contents was "eventually refuted"; the paper gives no source for it.

## Open questions

- When does a change of meaning under the same labels stop being the
  same identity continuing? The paper's criterion is members' felt
  continuity, which it also says revisionist history manufactures.
- Is adaptive instability more common than maladaptive instability, and
  how would one count?
