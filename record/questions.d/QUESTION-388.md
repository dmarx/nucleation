# QUESTION-388: What distinguishes a programmable system from one that governs its own reconfiguration?

## Why it is a question

A, Part 5: a reconfigurable structure computes y = f_z(x), with z its
configuration. "If an external robot or engineer selects the next
configuration, then the mechanical structure is programmable without
necessarily governing its own programmability." With internal feedback,
y_t = f_{z_t}(x_t) and z_{t+1} = g(z_t, x_t, y_t). A sets out a
progression: fixed geometry, reconfigurable geometry, trainable response,
and, still open, self-maintaining organization.

The readings fill the first three steps and show that internal feedback
alone does not reach the fourth.

- **Reconfigurable, set from outside.** Hu et al. ([NOTE-1608](../notes.d/NOTE-1608.md)), in the
  reading's words: "The arrangement is the program, and the mechanism that changes it is
  separate from both the program and the computation." Chen, Tan and Yan
  ([NOTE-1559](../notes.d/NOTE-1559.md)): a robot programs the array, and in the reading's words
  "Programming is external: the function is set by a magnet layout
  designed outside the device, which the robot only executes."
- **A fixed map, learning elsewhere.** Liu et al. ([NOTE-1600](../notes.d/NOTE-1600.md)): the
  multistable structure under a fixed actuation rule is a fixed map, and a
  linear readout, in the reading's words "trained in software, does the
  learning".
- **Trainable, gated from outside.** Stern et al. ([NOTE-1615](../notes.d/NOTE-1615.md)): "if the
  response is the desired one, as determined by a ‘supervisor’, folded
  creases are allowed to soften in proportion to their folding strain." The
  sheet's own material carries g, so z_{t+1} depends on z_t and on the
  sheet's response. But the verdict that drives g comes from outside. In
  the reading's words, "the material changes itself under a supervisor's
  signal".

So z_{t+1} = g(z_t, x_t, y_t) can hold while the criterion g applies is
another's. The record says as much about the readings: in [CLAIM-025](../claims.d/CLAIM-025.md)'s
note of 2026-11-05, "In all of them the capacity to transform comes from
outside, a robot, rig or supervisor".

The record has the target without the criterion. [CLAIM-545](../claims.d/CLAIM-545.md) identifies
freedom of will, operationally, with an agent's governance of its own
governability. [QUESTION-090](QUESTION-090.md) asks when a population develops a
self-maintaining governance structure. [QUESTION-396](QUESTION-396.md) asks when stored
history helps control. [CLAIM-566](../claims.d/CLAIM-566.md) separates persisting from
regenerating. None says what, in a reconfigurable system, makes the
reconfiguring its own.

## What would answer it

A criterion, stated on the update g and on what supplies its inputs, that
classes the four readings correctly and says what a fifth step would need:
for instance, that the criterion g applies be computed from the system's
own states and not delivered from outside; that g itself be revisable by
the system; that the constraints realizing g be produced by the system, in
[TERM-070](../terms.d/TERM-070.md)'s sense. Then an organizational case on each side of the line:
one reorganized by a consultant's verdict through its own procedures, one
whose procedures produce the verdict.

## What would not answer it

Internal feedback alone, which Stern et al.'s sheet already has; or a
system that reconfigures itself with no account of where the criterion
for its reconfiguration comes from.

## Note of 2026-11-05: the resource-bounded realizability readings

The pasted synthesis on resource-bounded realizability first asks whether constraints are constitutive of the object or can themselves be reconfigured by computation, which would point to "computation that constructs, modifies, and navigates spaces of constraints"; A then finds the filing already answers yes. [TERM-114](../terms.d/TERM-114.md) gives a frame in which this question can be put: changes of architecture are costed moves of their own, beside moves within one architecture. In that frame a merely programmable system is one whose architectural moves are chosen from outside, and the question is what makes the choosing the system's own. The frame states the moves and does not answer that. The reading is the filer's. No status changes.

## Note of 2026-11-07: a ladder of self-modification in software, from "Individuation, Possibility, and Disposition"

A, at A32 §8 of the exchange "Individuation, Possibility, and Disposition" (2026-11-06 to 07) ("The hard question: can the software maintain its own transformation machinery?"), gives a ladder: a service that updates on an external schedule; one that repairs its deployment and selects configurations; one that diagnoses failures in its own updating mechanisms and builds replacements. "But even this is not automatically self-governance. The capacity to modify a rule is different from maintaining a reliable process for determining *when, why, and under what constraints* the rule should change." That extends this question's distinction into software, and places the line where the question does. A does not cite it. Open.

## Note of 2026-11-12: Kiczales et al., read

Kiczales, Lamping, Mendhekar, Maeda, Lopes, Loingtier and Irwin's
"Aspect-oriented programming" ([LIT-2334](../literature.d/LIT-2334.md), skimmed in [NOTE-1716](../notes.d/NOTE-1716.md);
[THEORY-1637](../theory.d/THEORY-1637.md)), read as a stand-in for Part I of The Art of the Metaobject
Protocol. It bears on this question by
analogy at one point. A reflective system gives "a base language and (one
or more) meta-languages that provide control over the base language's
semantics and implementation" (§8.2), and the authors found that "this kind
of reflective access is so powerful that it can be dangerous or difficult
to use" (§6.2), so they replaced it with a narrower aspect language of
their own design. The machinery of change is inside the system; what it may
change is fixed by designers outside it, the split this question finds in
Stern et al.'s sheet. The paper does not ask who should govern
reconfiguration. Open.

Chapter 5 of the book itself, skimmed the same day ([NOTE-1718](../notes.d/NOTE-1718.md);
[THEORY-1636](../theory.d/THEORY-1636.md)) once the owner ruled the copy usable, says the same thing
more exactly. Users change CLOS by specializing its metaobjects, and the
change propagates to dependents inside the system; but "This balance
between user extensibility and implementor freedom is mediated by placing
explicit restrictions on each", restrictions its designers wrote, and the
authors call protocol specification "inherently about specifying leeway".
Who fixes the leeway is an ingredient this question's criterion may need.
Open.

## Note of 2026-11-12: a research agenda for recursive improvement, and a test of causal uptake, from "luria experiment design"

A41 of the exchange "luria experiment design" (2026-11-10, https://chatgpt.com/share/6af35bfc-35f8-83e9-a63d-d594703eabda) takes this question's note of 2026-11-07 as "almost a
research agenda for Luria-mediated RSI": "An agent that changes luria.yaml
when told to do so has demonstrated configurability. An agent that
recognizes a representational defect and proposes a repair has
demonstrated adaptive use of that configurability. A team that discovers
that its schema-review process is failing, then proposes and successfully
evaluates a repair to that process, demonstrates something stronger", and
"even that does not establish independent self-governance". The owner's
U17 is filed as [QUESTION-tmp7a7l4](QUESTION-tmp7a7l4.md).

A50 §12 adds a test this question needs: a governing rule's change must
alter behaviour under controlled later tasks. "A repository that stores
governance rules without influencing operations is not sufficient evidence
of operational self-governance." A43: such a study "does not establish
philosophically independent self-governance, because the experimenter
still defines the ultimate comparison". Open.
