# NOTE-tmpdp35k: Balganesh — The Immanent Rationality of Copyright Law

## Contribution

It reconstructs the method of Drassinower's book as Weinrib's "immanent
rationality" applied to copyright: identify the institution's essential
features, then show they form a coherent whole (pp. 1054–1055). It places
the book in the dispute between welfarist empiricism and rationalist theory
in copyright, and defends the book's standing as reasoned rather than
faith-based (Part II). It sets out three methodological puzzles the book
leaves open (Part III).

## Key insight

Drassinower's theory succeeds at coherence and has not earned
essentiality. An immanent account must first say which features are
essential and only then show that they cohere. If coherence is allowed to
decide what counts as essential, the sequence is reversed, and the account
explains whatever it chose to include. The review asks, for instance, "why
isn’t “fixation” an essential part of the ensemble that Drassinower
identifies?" (p. 1056). The same gap reappears when the book criticizes
existing law: to call a rule unjustified because it does not cohere, the
book needs a reason why autonomy, or coherence itself, is worth having
(pp. 1062–1064).

## Assumptions

- **Anglo-American copyright.** The review's frame is US doctrine and
  scholarship; the book also draws on Canadian law (p. 1066).
- **The book's thesis is reported fairly.** The review's account is close
  and page-referenced but second-hand for this record.
- **The reviewer's own theory.** The private-law objection rests on
  Balganesh's view that the author's control over whether to sue is central
  to copyright, and he says so (p. 1065 nn. 85–86).

## Key results

### Drassinower's thesis, as the review reports it (Part I)

- **The aim.** A normatively coherent account of copyright's "fundamental
  features" as a "coherent whole" (p. 1048; book pp. 7–8), against the
  welfarist account, which explains the institution only by its
  consequences.
- **The work as a communicative act.** "[c]opyright law protects the
  integrity of the work as a communicative act" (p. 1051; book p. 8).
  Copyright is not a mere property right but a "right inhering in persons
  as speaking beings" (p. 1051; book p. 56). The Kantian source is Kant's
  1785 essay on the unauthorized publication of books (p. 1054 n. 30).
- **Independent creation as anchor.** Originality and authorship are
  about "speaking in one’s own words" (p. 1051; book p. 62). That is why
  originality ignores the content or merit of the speech and asks only
  whether it is the author's own. In claiming protection an author submits
  "to every other person’s equal right" (p. 1052; book p. 63), so an
  identical work made independently does not infringe and may be protected
  in its turn.
- **Derived doctrines.** The idea/expression dichotomy follows from the
  equal dignity of authors: owning an idea would make others' expression of
  it liable (book p. 69). Transformative use is exempt because the
  plaintiff is always "an author among authors" (p. 1052; book p. 76). In
  *Baker v. Selden* the defendant's use was not "communicative", so it did
  not touch the work as communication (p. 1053; book pp. 92–95). Some uses
  are therefore "non-use" rather than fair use (book pp. 108–109).
- **What is wrong with copying.** Infringement, "the communicative
  reproduction of a work", is wrong because it is "compelled speech" (book
  p. 111), "ventriloquism practiced on an unwilling subject" (book p. 113).
  "By communicating an author’s speech without his or her consent, a
  defendant purports to speak for the author." (p. 1053). It takes from the
  author the choice of what, when, how and whether to speak (book pp.
  119–120). This is why attribution is no defence, and why the law draws no
  principled line between published and unpublished works (book pp.
  120–121).
- **The public domain.** It has an "inherent dignity" (book pp. 158–159)
  and is not a utilitarian residue. What does not perform the
  communicative function is outside copyright from the start. An author
  protects the work as communication "and never beyond", so the author's
  "insistence to be respected as an autonomous speaker thus constitutes
  itself as a gift to the public" (p. 1054; book p. 182).
- **Limitations and exceptions.** Limitations are internal to copyright's
  juridical order and bound its subject matter and scope. Exceptions are
  external: special-interest carve-outs, or the meeting of copyright with
  other juridical interests such as free speech (p. 1054; book pp.
  187–216).
- **Consequences.** All noncommunicative uses fall outside liability (p.
  1061; book pp. 8–9), including copying for personal use (p. 1063 n. 79,
  quoting book p. 9). Protecting computer programs against
  noncommunicative uses is suspect (p. 1063; book p. 221).

The review does not say that Drassinower limits the right by the structure
of dialogue, and the word dialogue does not occur in it. What it reports is the equality
of authors as speakers, the plaintiff as "an author among authors", the
public domain as the unprotected part of a communication, and limitations
as internal to the right. A reading of the book should check whether
dialogue is its own term.

### The review's case for rationalism (Part II)

- Copyright scholarship has married utilitarianism to empiricism, and
  treats empirical verifiability as "the litmus test of a theory’s
  legitimacy" (p. 1058).
- Lemley's essay calls untestable theories faith-based. Balganesh replies
  that "demonstrability is not the same as empirical verifiability", with
  mathematical proof as the example (p. 1059). A rationalist theory invites
  disagreement with its premises and steps; it does not ask for trust.
- Welfarism's own premise, that maximizing welfare is the goal, "is just as
  incapable of being tested through evidence as is Drassinower’s emphasis
  on individual autonomy" (p. 1060).
- Drassinower's theory is not status quo apologetics: it would exempt all
  noncommunicative uses (p. 1061). Its attention to the equality of authors
  as a collective distinguishes it from deontic theories of the atomistic
  author (p. 1060).

### The review's objections (Part I end and Part III)

1. **Essential or merely coherent (pp. 1055–1056).** The book gives no
   definition of "fundamental". It seems to take as essential what one
   cannot imagine copyright without, a mix of the "fixed" and the sine qua
   non. Fixation, which Congress called a fundamental criterion, is absent.
   The risk is that the account "emphasizes coherence ahead of
   essentiality" (p. 1056).
2. **Explanation and justification (pp. 1062–1064).** Drassinower says his
   account is "neither descriptive nor normative" (book p. 7). Following
   Coleman, an explanation need not defend the value it finds; a
   justification must. The book gives "no independent defense of autonomy"
   (p. 1063), yet switches into justification when it calls rules at odds
   with its coherence unjustified (private copying, programs). It then
   owes a defence either of coherence or of autonomy "as more than just a
   coherence-inducing ideal" (p. 1063). It also lacks a priori criteria for
   the fundamental.
3. **Private or public (pp. 1064–1066).** Keep every substantive rule and
   remove the civil action, leaving only criminal enforcement at a
   prosecutor's discretion. Drassinower's entitlement would survive almost
   unchanged. What makes copyright private law is that the author is "the
   first arbiter of the harm" (p. 1065) and decides whether an unauthorized
   publication is compelled speech worth suing over. That choice is itself
   an exercise of the autonomy the book prizes, and the book does not
   account for it, nor for remedies. If liability's structure were
   fundamental, the account would need corrective justice.
4. **Static and pervasive (pp. 1066–1068).** Courts reason in terms of
   balance, incentives, market harm and the public interest. Either judges
   conceal their reasons or they apply a lesser, modern copyright; both
   answers are unsatisfying. The book assumes its rationality is static
   across eras and pervasive from doctrine to system. Relax the first and a
   term like originality could have begun deontic and become
   consequentialist by "radical semantic evolution" (p. 1068). Relax the
   second and the doctrines could be deontic while the system serves
   welfare, as some immanent theorists allow (Weinrib on deterrence). The
   book admits other values only as external juridical orders (book pp.
   208–214).

The conclusion calls the book "a significant and enduring contribution to
copyright theorizing" (p. 1069).

## Concepts

- **Immanent rationality (immanent intelligibility).** Weinrib's method:
  understand a legal institution from its own essential features and their
  coherence, without external goals.
- **Communicative act.** Drassinower's term for what a work is in
  copyright: the author's speech, not an object.
- **Compelled speech.** Drassinower's account of infringement: the
  defendant makes the author speak without consent.
- **Independent creation.** The rule that a work made without copying is
  original and does not infringe, even if identical to an earlier work.
- **Non-use.** A use of a work that is not communicative, outside
  copyright altogether rather than excused as fair use.
- **Limitations and exceptions.** Internal bounds of the right, against
  external constraints on it.
- **Explanation and justification.** Coleman's distinction: saying what a
  thing is, against defending it.

## Connections

The review draws on Weinrib (*Legal Formalism*, *The Idea of Private Law*)
for method, Coleman and Leiter on explanation and justification, Lemley's
*Faith-Based Intellectual Property* as the target of Part II, and Kraus on
semantic evolution in common-law concepts. It cites Lessig's *The Future
of Ideas* among theorists of the public domain (p. 1053 n. 29). It cites
Balganesh's own papers on copying, copyright trolls and the obligatory
structure of copyright for the private-law objection. Drassinower's book,
*What's Wrong with Copying?* (Harvard University Press, 2015), is being
filed in this record separately.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md) (sharing a kind versus being a rendering).** The sharpest
  point of contact. Drassinower's anchor, as reported, is independent
  creation: a work identical to an earlier one but made without copying
  does not infringe and may be protected itself (p. 1052). That is
  [CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md)'s distinction in legal form: two works can share everything
  that would be preserved and still differ in whether one is a rendering of
  the other, and the law's wrong attaches only to the rendering.
  Drassinower gives a reason the distinction matters normatively: the
  independent maker speaks in their own words, the copier speaks the
  author's.
- **[CLAIM-061](../claims.d/CLAIM-061.md) (footing and force).** [CLAIM-061](../claims.d/CLAIM-061.md) says renderings that share
  content can differ in footing. Drassinower's account of infringement is a
  claim about footing: an unauthorized reproduction is wrong because it
  presents the author as speaking, so that "a defendant purports to speak
  for the author" (p. 1053). On his account the wrong is not in what is
  said but in who is made to say it. The record could use this as a case
  where a change of footing, not of content, is what a community treats as
  the harm. The review does not use the word footing.
- **[CLAIM-002](../claims.d/CLAIM-002.md) (identity across retellings).** Copyright's copying
  requirement weights continuity of transmission: similarity without
  derivation is no wrong. Drassinower's version adds that what is protected
  is the work as communication, not the artifact as object, which puts
  weight on the third of [CLAIM-002](../claims.d/CLAIM-002.md)'s relations, similarity of communicative
  function, as the thing whose unauthorized transmission wrongs the author.
  This is the book's view as reported, and the review does not discuss
  retellings or translation.
- **[QUESTION-002](../questions.d/QUESTION-002.md) and [CLAIM-220](../claims.d/CLAIM-220.md) (what survives a rendering).** No direct
  bearing. The review never asks what a reproduction or a derivative work
  preserves. The communicative/noncommunicative line (*Baker*) is a line
  about uses, not about what survives a rendering. Whether the book treats
  translation as compelled speech is a question for the book.
- **[NOTE-tmptp3k0](NOTE-tmptp3k0.md) (Lessig).** A real contrast. Lessig complains that
  copyright regulates every copy, so ordinary digital use needs permission.
  On Drassinower's account as reported, copying for personal use is not
  within an author's copyright at all and noncommunicative uses are
  non-uses, so much of the copy trigger Lessig describes would fall away
  by principle, not by reform. Remix that makes "new expression, meaning,
  or message" (p. 1052, quoting *Campbell*) is, for Drassinower, the
  remixer's own speech as an author among authors. Lessig argues the same
  outcomes from culture and economics; Drassinower, as reported, from the
  structure of the right.
- **[CASE-100](../cases.d/CASE-100.md), [CLAIM-332](../claims.d/CLAIM-332.md), [QUESTION-119](../questions.d/QUESTION-119.md).** No bearing.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice, and not held by the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- **Second-hand on the book.** This record's account of Drassinower comes
  through the review until the book is read.
- **The objections are posed, not pressed.** Each Part III puzzle is a
  question the book "would benefit" from answering; the review does not
  show that the book's answers would fail. The dynamic and layered
  alternatives are sketched in a page.
- **Part II is partly about Lemley.** The defence of rationalism answers
  an essay that does not discuss Drassinower, and the point that welfarism
  also rests on an untestable premise is asserted in a paragraph.
- **The private-law objection is the reviewer's own theory.** He flags
  this and sets it aside, but it carries the objection.
- **Slips.** The book is once called *What's Wrong with Copyright?*
  (p. 1060), and a footnote quotes "idea/expression of dichotomy" (p. 1054
  n. 31).

## Open questions

- Does Drassinower's book state the limits of the right in terms of
  dialogue, and how does it treat translation and adaptation: as the
  translator's own speech, or as the author made to speak in another
  language?
- If an unauthorized reproduction wrongs the author by its footing, does
  an attributed paraphrase wrong them less than a verbatim copy, and does
  the book say?
- For the record: is the legal line between independent creation and
  copying evidence that communities treat the rendering relation of
  [CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md) as normatively significant apart from what is preserved?
