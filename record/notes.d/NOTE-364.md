# NOTE-364: The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity

## Contribution

The essay gives the self a definite ontological category. It is neither a
thing in the brain nor nothing. It is an abstractum of the kind physics
already uses, a centre of gravity, posited because interpretation is easier
with it. To that it adds the specific model of the posit: a fictional
character, with a fictional character's indeterminacy, authored by a
process that need not itself be a self. The label "center of narrative
gravity" is the essay's.

## Key insight

The question "what is the self, really?" has the same answer as "what is
the centre of gravity of this chair, really?": nothing further. The self is
the point a person's story, told by others and by the person's own brain,
coheres around. It is as real as the coherence, as determinate as the
story, and as many as the points the story needs.

## Assumptions

- **The abstracta/illata distinction (after Reichenbach).** Centres of
  gravity are abstracta, "theorist's fictions"; atoms and neutrinos are
  illata, inferred entities with facts of the matter. The essay assumes the
  distinction and that abstracta are legitimate in "serious, sober, echt
  physical science".
- **A mindless machine could write a passable novel.** Stipulated for
  Gilbert. The reader who doubts it is challenged "to show why you think
  this must be so", not argued with.
- **Brains are disunified.** Gazzaniga's view that "the normal mind is not
  beautifully unified" is adopted ("If this is true (and I think it is)").
- **Split-brain cross-cueing generalises.** That the operation "reveals—but
  does not create" a talent normal people have is called the hypothesis
  "most likely" worth investigating, after Gazzaniga.

## Key results

There are no formal results. The essay's moves, in order:

- **Centre of gravity.** No mass, no colour, "no physical properties at
  all, except for spatio-temporal location", yet it figures in explanations
  that "appear to be causal" ("Why doesn't the lamp tip over?"). It can be
  manipulated, and it can move discontinuously. If its trajectory matched
  one iron atom's, identifying the two "would be a category mistake".
- **Fictional characters.** Readers learn more about Ishmael than Melville
  says, by implication and extrapolation. Beyond that "fictional worlds are
  simply indeterminate": Holmes's three nostrils get a no, his shoulder-blade
  mole gets "no fact of the matter" (example credited to Lewis, "Truth and
  Fiction", 1978). Bivalence fails for fictional objects and holds for
  historical persons.
- **Gilbert.** A novel-writing computer prints "Call me Gilbert". Gilbert
  "is a product of a design or invention process in which there aren't any
  selves at all". Given wheels and a camera, the robot's story turns out to
  have "a truth-preserving interpretation" in the world: hit the robot and
  Gilbert is hit; lock it in the closet and it says "Help me!" The robot
  "doesn't know what it's doing", and neither does your brain. The robot is
  also "a self-interpreter".
- **Location.** Selves have only grossly defined location (three on a park
  bench), and finer brain localisation will not end at "that cell … that's
  the self!"
- **Updike.** Readers could ask Updike for a new novel about the young
  Rabbit Angstrom. That would not uncover facts already fixed. It would make
  the character more determinate by a creative step. "That is the way we
  treat each other; that is the way we are." Self-interpretation can
  rewrite one's past in the same way. That would be "utterly mysterious" if
  the self were anything but an abstractum.
- **Contradiction and bifurcation.** An inconsistent sequel gives "no true
  story", and interpreters resolve the conflict by splitting the character.
  Multiple personality cases are treated so. Dennett rejects as "overly
  skeptical" the view that therapists create all the selves, and grants that
  in some cases two to four selves "had already begun laying down a
  biography" before therapy.
- **Why talk to oneself (after Jaynes).** Speakers who asked aloud for help
  sometimes answered their own question. That opened a route via the ears
  between brain parts with "an accessibility problem". Shortened, the loop
  became subvocalisation and then "conscious, verbal thought". Ryle's
  "self-schooling" in *On Thinking* is read the same way. The essay labels
  the account speculative.
- **Split brain (Gazzaniga).** A right hemisphere that feels a pencil in a
  bag can press the point into the palm. Pain goes to both hemispheres, so
  the left can start guessing, cued by smiles. Unity in normal people is the
  same resourcefulness, "enhanced by an illusion of greater unity".
- **Conclusion.** "The chief fictional character at the center of that
  autobiography is one's self." Two selves in one body need only that the
  story "coheres much better" around two points than one. It closes on
  Hume's failure to find a self by introspection.

## Concepts

- **abstractum / illatum** (after Reichenbach) — a posit of a theory with
  only the properties the theory gives it, versus an inferred entity with
  facts of the matter beyond the theory.
- **people-physics** — the essay's name for the interpretive science of
  persons, "more soberly known as a phenomenology or hermeneutics, or
  soul-science (Geisteswissenschaft)".
- **centre of narrative gravity** — the self: the point around which the
  interpretation of a person's behaviour and talk coheres.
- **auto-hermeneutics** — self-interpretation, including re-telling one's
  past, which changes the character one is.

## Connections

- **"Real Patterns"** ([LIT-220](../literature.d/LIT-220.md), [NOTE-190](NOTE-190.md)). There, centres of gravity are
  the model of a "good abstract object", judged on the scientific path, not
  the metaphysical one. This essay applies the same model to the self. Both
  turn on the idea that a posit can be real without being one of the
  physical parts.
- **Minsky, *The Emotion Machine*** ([LIT-432](../literature.d/LIT-432.md), [NOTE-348](NOTE-348.md)). Minsky's
  draft calls the Single-Self a useful fiction and says that to think of
  one's Self is to switch among "a network of models" (its C10). This essay
  and the draft agree that there is no central self and that the idea of one
  is useful. They differ in kind. Minsky's selves are models inside the
  mind, many of them and used by it. Dennett's self is one point posited by
  an interpreter, which may be the brain itself, and it multiplies only when
  the story fails to cohere. Minsky also starts from Gazzaniga's
  disunified-modules picture in spirit: his resources are switched on and
  off and cannot see one another directly. The society of mind itself
  ([LIT-429](../literature.d/LIT-429.md)) is not cited.
- **Hofstadter, *I Am a Strange Loop*** ([LIT-423](../literature.d/LIT-423.md), unread, so this is
  from its description and contents only). The book asks whether "our 'I'"
  is "merely a convenient fiction". Its answer, by its description, is a
  strange loop through which symbols "push particles around". This essay's
  answer is fiction, but of a kind that appears in explanations "that appear
  to be causal". So the essay offers a third reading between "mere fiction"
  and "downward causation": an abstractum that explains without being a
  part. Which reading the book takes, and whether it engages this essay, a
  reading of the book should check. Dennett also reviewed that book and
  *The Emotion Machine* together in 2007; the review is not held here.
- **The extended self** ([LIT-097](../literature.d/LIT-097.md), [NOTE-180](NOTE-180.md)). Clark and Chalmers infer in
  one paragraph that the self extends with the mind (their C14). This essay
  gives the self no boundary of the kind that could extend or not. Its
  location is "only grossly defined" and set by interpretation.
- **Ryle.** The essay cites Ryle's *On Thinking* (1979), edited by Kolenda,
  for "What is Le Penseur doing?" It does not cite *The Concept of Mind*
  ([LIT-318](../literature.d/LIT-318.md)).
- **Dennett's other work.** The robot that writes its own story recurs in
  "Where Am I?" ([LIT-405](../literature.d/LIT-405.md)), where the narrator's point of view, not his
  brain, fixes where he is. Dennett's intentional-stance and consciousness
  papers, being filed in parallel, are where the interpretive method this
  essay applies to the self is set out for beliefs and experience. That is
  the same move as [LIT-220](../literature.d/LIT-220.md)'s.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) (the bridge): decomposition side, and a counting rule.**
  The essay's self is produced by parts that know nothing of it: the robot's
  computer, a brain that "doesn't know what it's doing", modules with
  accessibility problems. So it belongs with Selfridge ([LIT-427](../literature.d/LIT-427.md)) and
  Minsky on the downward side. What it adds is a rule for counting selves:
  as many as the points the best story coheres around. That rule is
  interpretive, not architectural, and says nothing about nesting. Applied
  to a group, it would count a group self wherever the best interpretation
  of the group coheres around one point. The essay does not discuss groups,
  so the application is mine. It is not an argument about experience, which
  is where [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) locates the dispute.
- **[THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md) (AI consciousness): bears, without settling.** Gilbert is a
  machine whose self-narration is true of it, and the essay says such a
  machine has a self in the only sense anyone has one. That is a claim about
  selves, not about consciousness, and the essay stipulates that Gilbert is
  "not a conscious machine". So it does not answer [THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md)'s question.
  It does say that a self-model or self-narrative is no evidence of
  experience on its own.
- **No ML instruction.** The novel-writing machine is a 1980s thought
  experiment, not a claim about language models, though readers now meet it
  as one. Nothing here belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- **The analogy is the argument.** The essay names the features a self
  shares with a centre of gravity and a fictional character, but does not
  show that they are the features that matter. A reader who thinks selves
  have properties neither analogue has (a first-person point of view, a
  stake in their future) is not answered.
- **Gilbert rests on a stipulation.** Everything turns on a mindless
  machine writing a passable, true autobiography. The essay invites
  objection rather than defending it.
- **The evidence is cited.** The split-brain and multiple personality
  material is reported from Gazzaniga and the clinical literature, and its
  reading is Gazzaniga's.
- **The evolutionary story is speculation.** The essay says so, and leans on
  Jaynes.
- **Not the published text.** This reading used a copy-edited
  transcription. Before a quotation from it is relied on as Dennett's
  wording, it should be checked against the Erlbaum printing or Dennett's
  own posted copy.

## Open questions

- Does "the self is an abstractum" answer the question of what a person is
  owed or what survives, or only what a self is for an interpreter? The
  essay does not take up personal identity in the persistence sense.
- Who interprets when the interpreter is the brain itself? The robot is
  "also, of course, an interpreter", but the essay does not say what makes
  self-interpretation succeed.
