## Summary

Two cross-sectional online surveys of European football fans recruited
through fan clubs of 395 clubs (Study 1, N = 172, a few weeks after the
April 2021 European Super League announcement; Study 2, N = 184, about six
months later; ~90% men). Each respondent rated commitment, word-of-mouth
and behavioural loyalty for their club now ("actual") and then at once
"If my club were participating in a Super League…" ("if"). The difference
is the "change". Identity is measured in three dimensions after Wang
(2017): cognitive (self-image overlaps the club's), affective
(attachment, belonging) and evaluative ("I am an important member of my
club", "Others respect me for being associated with my club", "I consider
myself a valuable partner of my club"). All three predict a smaller
hypothetical commitment decline, cognitive most (β −0.22 / −0.27);
commitment change predicts word-of-mouth and loyalty change (β
0.63–0.73); commitment mediates evaluative identity (both studies) and
affective (Study 1 only), not cognitive. A single item, opposition to the
club joining a Super League, moderates all three identity–commitment
links.

## Contribution

Applies the tripartite (cognitive, affective, evaluative) reading of
Tajfel (1982) to fan responses to an off-field, club-wide controversy, and
adds opposition intensity as a moderator. The authors say the tripartite
framework is "a widely accepted interpretive extension" that "Tajfel
(1982) did not explicitly formulate" (p. 3).

## Key results

- **Main effects (p. 9, Fig. 2).** "Fans' cognitive identity (H1a; β =
  –0.22 ... β = –0.27), affective identity (H1b; β = –0.19 ... –0.18), and
  evaluative identity (H1c; β = –0.19 ... –0.23) were all negatively
  related to commitment change." R² = .26/.36 for commitment change,
  .53/.44 for word-of-mouth change, .52 for behavioural loyalty change.
- **Mediation (p. 9, Table 3).** Evaluative identity → commitment change →
  loyalty, both studies (e.g. −0.121 [−0.244, −0.005]); affective only in
  Study 1; "No significant mediation effects were found for cognitive
  identity."
- **Moderation (p. 10, Fig. 3).** Interaction terms all negative and
  significant (e.g. cognitive × opposition β = −0.145 in both studies).
  Simple slopes: cognitive identity–commitment change "significantly
  positive under low opposition (Study 1: β = 0.25 ...; Study 2: β =
  0.37)", ~0 at medium, "significantly negative in Study 1 (β = –0.19)"
  under high opposition. Affective and evaluative show the same order.
- **Interpretation (p. 11).** "stronger fan opposition to a club's decision
  reduces the protective effect of all identity dimensions on commitment
  change"; "When perceived moral violations are severe, even strong
  identification cannot fully prevent commitment erosion" (p. 12).
- **Correlations (Table 2, p. 9).** Opposition correlates −0.52 to −0.71
  with the three identity dimensions and +0.72 to +0.77 with commitment
  change: strong identifiers oppose the move less.
- **Robustness (p. 9).** Fans of ESL-invited and non-invited clubs: paths
  equal in a multigroup SEM. Common-method bias checked with a marker item
  ("I sit in the same spot every time during football matches").
- **Limitations stated (pp. 14–15).** Single-item moderator; hypothetical
  scenario ("hypothetical contexts may elicit idealized responses");
  recall bias; "consistency bias or anchoring effects" from answering
  near-identical items twice; ~90% men; English-language survey.

## Assumptions

- A within-respondent difference between "actual" and "if" answers in one
  sitting measures change in commitment and loyalty.
- The three identity dimensions are distinct (Fornell–Larcker passed;
  inter-correlations .48–.67) and causally prior to commitment change.
- Opposition to the decision is a moderator of identity's effect, not one
  of its consequences.

## Verdict on the source's characterization

The LIT's summary ("Two cross-sectional studies of reactions to a
hypothetical European Super League entry: cognitive identity buffers
commitment best, and strong opposition to the decision weakens every
buffer") matches the abstract and the authors' reading, and A6's caution
("the hypothetical design limits what it shows about loyalty") is
confirmed by the authors' own limitations. Two corrections:
- "Cognitive identity buffers commitment best" holds for the direct effect
  on commitment change only. For loyalty, commitment mediates evaluative
  identity, not cognitive (p. 9); the authors' claim that cognitive
  identity sustains loyalty "directly ... bypassing" commitment (p. 11) is
  asserted, not shown (no direct path to loyalty is reported).
- "Strong opposition weakens every buffer" is the authors' reading, but
  their own reported signs do not plainly carry it (see Limitations).
- The citation's pages 263–282 are the issue's; this copy is online-first.

## Limitations (including gaps in the argument)

- **Change is not observed.** Commitment "change" is a same-sitting
  difference between real and imagined answers; the authors grant
  anchoring and hypothetical bias (p. 15). Nothing here shows what fans
  did.
- **The moderation's sign is not reconciled with its reading.**
  Commitment change is the decline (actual minus if; means 1.43–2.10),
  and the main effects of identity on it are negative (buffering). The
  interactions are negative too, and the simple slopes run from positive
  at low opposition (β 0.25–0.37: stronger identity, larger decline) to
  negative at high opposition (β −0.19, −0.22: stronger identity, smaller
  decline). Read with the coding the paper states, that is buffering that
  *strengthens* with opposition, the reverse of the abstract's "strong
  opposition ... significantly weakens the buffering effect" and of
  p. 15's "under strong opposition the same identity ties are associated
  with sharper declines". The paper does not say whether the PROCESS
  outcome was recoded. A filer should not rely on the moderation's
  direction without the data (available "upon request", p. 15).
- **Identity and opposition are confounded.** They correlate up to −0.71
  (Table 2); low-opposition respondents are mostly high identifiers, so
  the simple slopes at the 16th percentile of opposition rest on few low
  identifiers. Opposition may be a consequence of identification (or of
  the club's being invited), not an independent moderator.
- **Mediation in cross-section.** Identity → commitment change → loyalty
  change is estimated from one sitting; the order is asserted.
- **Single-item moderator; fan-club recruitment; 90% men; English.**
- **"Cognitive identity bypasses commitment"** (p. 11) is offered to
  explain a null mediation, without a reported direct path.
