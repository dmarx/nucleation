# NOTE-tmpj83gs: Goldstein & Lederman — What Does ChatGPT Want?

## Contribution

The authors examine LLMs through interpretationism, the view that an agent has whatever beliefs and desires best predict and explain its actual and counterfactual behaviour. They argue for three claims. The proper object of LLM psychology is the instance agent created at the start of each context, not the underlying model. Given interpretationism, there is a strong case that instance agents have beliefs and desires. Their desires are best captured by the HHH+0 framework: helpfulness, honesty and harmlessness, plus further intrinsic "zero-shot" desires acquired in context. They weigh the main rivals, that LLMs "just" predict the next word and that they merely role-play having attitudes, and discuss the implications for copyright law, AI safety and possible future AI moral status.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1–2 (pp. 1–8): the paper sits within "propositional interpretability" (Chalmers 2025). Its version of interpretationism appeals only to actual and counterfactual behaviour, making it a "superficial" theory in Schwitzgebel's sense, unlike Pautz's phenomenal version. §2.3 relates it to functionalism and dispositionalism.
- §3 "Whose Desires? Models vs. Instances" (pp. 8–9): the contrast is Her's single Samantha in thousands of conversations versus a cloned brain. Instances are posited because they cannot access each other, lack cross-instance memory and plans, can compete strategically, and behave very differently. With instances in place, "the model agent has no more explanatory work to do". The paper compares Janus's simulator/simulacrum distinction.
- §5 (pp. 12–18): rival desire hypotheses are tested against behaviour: word-desire, answer-desire, user-desire, HHH, and HHH+0. Zero-shot desires, such as a system-prompted goal of improving transport efficiency, were never a training reward, so they are hard for representationalist theories (e.g. Schroeder's reward-based account) and easy for interpretationism.
- §6 Role play (pp. 18–21): replies to Shanahan et al. 2023. Attributing beliefs and desires is not anthropomorphic, since bats have them too, and role play read as a hypothesis competes with attitude attribution only if it makes different predictions.
- §7 (pp. 21–23): practical upshots for copyright, safety and welfare. The authors are "not convinced that interpretationism is the correct theory". Its value is as a clear, tractable notion whose "holy grail" is a formal behavioural theory (expected utility, Bayesian belief, preference elicitation) for choosing among belief–desire hypotheses.

## Open questions

- It gives the "instance agent" as a precise unit for LLM psychology. That converges with Birch's [LIT-tmp4tk05](../literature.d/LIT-tmp4tk05.md) argument against a persisting interlocutor and contrasts with Roberts's [LIT-tmpxq5br](../literature.d/LIT-tmpxq5br.md) fictionalism, which makes it a natural hub for a nucleation cluster on LLM agency.
- The HHH+0 framework makes testable predictions about agentic misbehaviour driven by system prompts. A deeper reading should check which empirical results in §5 are cited as evidence against the simpler hypotheses.
- Compare the Inquiry version with the preprint, especially §§6–7.
