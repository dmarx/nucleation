# LIT-tmpnn8ma: Macrocognition: A Theory of Distributed Minds and Collective Intentionality

Bryce Huebner (2014), *Oxford University Press (hardback ISBN 9780199926275; published 24 December 2013, 2014 imprint)* — DOI-10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199926275.001.0001

`Deferred` because no lawful full text was reachable, so it is not read
and no NOTE is filed. What follows is only what the publisher and the
chapter metadata say, and what two read works quote from it.

**What the publisher says.** The book "develops a novel approach to
distributed cognition and collective intentionality". It argues that
"genuine cognition requires the capacity to engage in flexible
goal-directed behavior, and that this requires specialized
representational systems that are integrated in a way that yields fluid
and skillful coping with environmental contingencies". So "collective
mentality should be posited where and only where specialized subroutines
are integrated to yields goal-directed behavior that is sensitive to the
concerns that are relevant to a group as such" (sic). It claims "there are
many kinds of collective minds: some groups have cognitive capacities
that are more like those that we find in honeybees or cats than they are
like those that we find in people", and that "groups are unlikely to be
'believers' in the fullest sense of the term".

  conscious; but it is just as hard to imagine that a mass of neurons,

**Where it sits on the seam.** On the composition side, with a criterion
that is a decomposition criterion. Its test for a collective mind is the
one Minsky's society of mind meets by construction: specialised
subroutines, integrated so that the system copes flexibly with its
environment. So it is the work most likely to say that whatever makes a
society of mindless agents a mind also makes some groups minds. It also
grades collective minds, from honeybee-like to cat-like, which is
Schwitzgebel's dumbing-down move (his squidbits) applied to real groups.
On Schwitzgebel's claims as [NOTE-131](../notes.d/NOTE-131.md) numbers them:

- **C3:** on cognition, the description supports composition. On
  phenomenal consciousness, the one passage seen (ch. 5, p. 120) only
  disarms the intuition against it. It does not assert it. So its stance
  is unknown, and plausibly neutral-to-sympathetic.
- **C4 and C5:** unknown. Nothing seen mentions Putnam, IIT or
  anti-nesting.

Chapters 6–7, on the "explanatory superfluity" of collective mentality,
appear from their titles to take up the objection Kammerer
([LIT-tmpdpmaj](LIT-tmpdpmaj.md)) builds on: that the members' states already explain the
group's behaviour. A reading should check whether Huebner answers it in a
form that would touch Kammerer's principle.

Neighbours. Pilgrim et al. ([LIT-046](LIT-046.md)) is a computational framework for
collective intelligence. Huebner's "specialized subroutines … integrated"
names the same kind of structure, as a criterion for a mind rather than a
measure of performance. *Biological Individuals* ([LIT-168](LIT-168.md); [NOTE-114](../notes.d/NOTE-114.md))
treats honey-bee colonies as Darwinian individuals and as living agents
only via "the cognitive metaphor". Huebner's claim that some human
groups have literal, if honeybee-grade, cognition is the kind of claim
that would turn that metaphor literal; whether the book treats insect
colonies themselves is unchecked. Dennett's
"Real Patterns" ([LIT-220](LIT-220.md)) is the usual warrant for taking group-level
cognitive descriptions as real; whether Huebner leans on it is unchecked.
Hofstadter's "Ant Fugue" colony ([LIT-tmpsm6ia](LIT-tmpsm6ia.md)) is the same idea in
fiction.

**Topic.** `cognition` is primary: the book's subject is distributed
cognition. `agency` holds collective intentionality and goal-directed
group behaviour. `mereology` holds the question of which collectives are
minds. `consciousness` is filed on the evidence of chapter 5 as quoted by
List, which addresses the intuition against collective phenomenal
consciousness; a reading should confirm it. `social-science` holds the
book's groups (parties, uprisings, corporations, in the description).

No instruction for machine-learning practice appears in what was seen, so
nothing here points to the Anthology of the SOTA.

**What a reading should check.**

- The exact integration criterion of chapters 3–4, and whether it is
  Minsky-compatible: are the "specialized subroutines" required to be
  mindless, or can they be persons?
- What chapter 5 concludes about collective phenomenal consciousness, and
  whether chapter 9's taxonomy assigns any group experience.
- Whether chapters 6–7 answer the explanatory-superfluity objection in a
  way that bears on anti-nesting principles (Putnam's, Kammerer's).
