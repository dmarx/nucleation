# THEORY-tmpjz519: Greaves and MacAskill argue that in the most important decisions facing agents today every near-best option is near-best for the far future and delivers much larger benefits there than in the near future, on expected-value reasoning that they concede needs a zero pure rate of time preference and may fail through fanaticism

## The account

Axiological strong longtermism: in the most important decision situations,
every option that is near-best overall is near-best for the far future, and
delivers much larger benefits there than in the near future (p. 3). Expected
value is driven by the largest scenario given any credence, so a small
credence in space settlement or digital minds sets the expected size of the
future, and effects that change which persistent state the world enters do
not wash out (§4.1).

A stakes-sensitivity argument carries this to deontic strong longtermism:
when the stakes are very high and no serious side-constraint applies, one
ought to choose a near-best option (§9).

The concessions: a zero pure rate of time preference is "near-essential",
and "With even a modest positive rate of pure time preference ... the
argument would not go through" (p. 18). Fanaticism is "One of the most
plausible ways in which the argument for strong longtermism might fail" (p.
25).

## Evidence

- Benchmark: bednets save 0.025 lives per $100 (p. 5).
- Expected future beings: main estimate 10^24, low 10^18, restricted 10^14
  (p. 9).
- Per $100, AI safety buys one trillion, one million or 100 lives on the
  three estimates, the only example that beats bednets on the restricted one
  (pp. 11–15).

The decisive AI numbers have "no hard quantitative evidence" (p. 14);
robustness to person-affecting views is shown on that least quantified
example; individual-level fanaticism is conceded (p. 26) ([NOTE-1302](../notes.d/NOTE-1302.md),
Limitations).
