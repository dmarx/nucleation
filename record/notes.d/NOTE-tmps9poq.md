# NOTE-tmps9poq: Intergenerational Justice

## Contribution

A map of the philosophical debate over what the living owe future and
past people, and an argument through it. It sets out why relations
across generations differ from relations among contemporaries. It
answers the doubts that future people can have rights. Its main work is
to give a notion of harm that survives the non-identity problem, and to
specify it. It ends by saying what a rights-based account cannot
explain.

## Key insight

We can wrong a future person whose existence depends on our choice, but
only by a standard that does not compare her with how she would
otherwise have been. On a threshold notion of harm, a person is harmed
if she is brought into existence, or kept, below a level she is owed;
she is then "worse off than she ought to be" (§3.1). That needs no
counterfactual self, so the non-identity problem does not touch it. It
also has a cost the entry states plainly. A person-affecting account of
this kind "may specify no reason for preferring a future with people all
of whom have lives far above the level of a sufficiency threshold to a
future with no people" (§4.6).

## Assumptions

- **The Interest Theory of rights**, not the Will Theory (§1). On the
  Will Theory, future people cannot exercise rights against us and so
  have none.
- **A weak person-affecting view** (§1). What matters is what is done to
  a particular person who exists at some time. "People who never exist
  and people who potentially exist do not count". A strong
  person-affecting view would require the same person to be worse off
  than she would otherwise have been; impersonal views count states of
  affairs regardless of whose they are.
- **Parfit's genetic identity view** frames the contingency (§1): who
  exists depends on which gametes meet, so most long-term policies
  change who will live. The European Commission's estimate that the
  Erasmus programme produced more than a million babies is the entry's
  illustration.
- **The threshold is normative**, not found (§4, §5).

## Key results

- **How intergenerational relations differ** (§1).
  - No direct reciprocity. The entry notes accounts of indirect and
    transgenerational reciprocity, Scheffler 2013 among them.
  - A permanent asymmetry of power.
  - Future people's existence, number and identity depend on our
    choices.
  - We cannot know future people as individuals.
  - Remote future people cannot affect our well-being, but they may set
    back the interests of present or past people "insofar as the latter
    have, or had, interests with respect to posthumous future states of
    affairs".
  - Motivation is raised and set aside: compliance with duties to
    remote future people "is likely to be less reliable" (citing
    Scheffler 2025, 439–442). So is Gardiner's "tyranny of the
    contemporary".
- **Doubts about future rights, answered** (§2.1). That future people do
  not exist now shows they have no rights now, not that we cannot
  violate their future rights. No one has a right to exist, so we need
  not ascribe one. Not knowing future people as individuals does not
  block welfare rights such as subsistence, which rest on their being
  human (note 1).
- **The procreation asymmetry** (§2.2). No obligation to procreate for
  the sake of possible children; an obligation not to beget children
  whose lives would be miserable. It goes back to Narveson 1967 and 1973.
  Meyer defends it on the weak person-affecting view with a threshold:
  the reason "that persons can be worse off than they ought to be"
  speaks against creating a sub-threshold person, but not for creating
  an above-threshold one. Impersonal defences are noted: negative
  consequentialism, or a weak, overridable duty to create happy people.
- **Four notions of harm** (§§3.1–3.2).
  - (I) *Diachronic*: worse off at t2 than before t1.
  - (II) *Subjunctive-historical*: worse off at t2 than if the agent had
    not acted with respect to her at all.
  - (III) *Threshold*: caused to exist, or to be, in a sub-threshold
    state, avoidably and not minimized.
  - (IV) *Disjunctive*: (II) or (III).

  (I) and (II) cannot reach people whose existence depends on the act.
  (III) can, but is under-inclusive: stealing a rich man's car would not
  harm him. (IV) fixes that.
- **Four responses to the non-identity problem** (§3.1): deny that such
  people have rights; rights without harm (Kumar); limit the problem to
  acts that are necessary conditions of existence; or the threshold
  conception, which Meyer holds.
- **Parfit's No-Difference View** (§3.2). Parfit's two medical programs
  (pregnancy testing, which affects actual people, and preconception
  testing, which affects possible people). The single threshold view is
  compatible with the No-Difference View. The disjunctive notion,
  plausibly read, makes cancelling pregnancy testing worse, because two
  harms apply there and one in the other case.
- **Specifying the threshold** (§§4.1–4.3).
  - *Egalitarian*: implies levelling down, and if pegged to the present
    generation, that it is in one respect worse for successors to be
    better off.
  - *Prioritarian*: excessive demands, given how many future people
    there may be, and no plausible cap on population.
  - *Sufficientarian*: the Positive Thesis (weighty reasons to secure
    enough) and the Shift Thesis (a discontinuity in the weight of
    reasons once enough is secured). Weak sufficientarianism, giving
    priority but not absolute priority below the threshold, is more
    plausible across generations. Below the threshold, a priority view.
- **The currency** (§4.4): basic needs or central capabilities. Not
  preferences, which future people may not share, and not resources,
  which track well-being badly.
- **Rawls's just savings** (§4.5). Rawls never discusses the
  non-identity problem and mostly assumes a constant population. Save
  until just institutions are secure (the accumulation stage), then
  maintain them (the steady state). *A Theory of Justice* had the
  contractors care for their descendants; *Political Liberalism* drops
  that assumption for mutual disinterest plus the condition that they
  must want all previous generations to have followed the principle.
  Sidgwick, Ramsey, Rawls and Parfit all reject pure time discounting.
- **Where rights run out** (§4.6).
  - The person-affecting account entails the procreation asymmetry and
    gives no right to be created.
  - Above-threshold concerns are, at most, weaker justice claims:
    environmental conditions, cultural goods, ways of life. Hence the
    admission quoted under Key insight.
  - So "widely shared concerns about the continuation of human life on
    earth at a high level of well-being" need another ground. Meyer cites
    McKinnon (2025) on Scheffler's "afterlife conjecture": the prospect
    of extinction "undermines the conditions under which we value,
    because many of our projects, evaluative commitments, and affective
    attachments presuppose the continued existence of future people".
    Such concerns reflect "our participation in an ongoing chain of
    generations" and our "evaluative, emotional, and practical
    dependence on them".
  - Meyer's own proposal (after Baier 1981 and Thompson 2009) is an
    obligation with no correlative right. The living owe respect to
    valuable goods their predecessors bequeathed, and to their
    contemporaries' future-oriented projects. So they must not wilfully
    destroy those goods, or the social practices on which pursuing such
    projects depends. "While future people belong to the beneficiaries,
    the obligation is owed to both present and past people."
  - Recent relational and environmental accounts are listed as
    reinforcing this, among them Casal's "Conservationist Sufficiency".
- **Historical injustice** (§5). The threshold and disjunctive notions
  carry over. Past injustice can set what counts as sub-threshold for
  people who would not have existed without it (Waligore). Sher's
  subsequent wrongs and De-Shalit's transgenerational communities give
  further routes.

## Concepts

- **non-identity problem**: if an act is a condition of a person's
  existence, she cannot be worse off because of it than she would
  otherwise have been.
- **weak / strong person-affecting view**: only people who exist at some
  time count / an act harms only if the same person is worse off than
  she would otherwise have been.
- **impersonal view**: the value of states of affairs is not reducible
  to their effects on particular people.
- **threshold notion of harm**: being caused to exist, or to be, below a
  normatively set level of well-being.
- **disjunctive notion of harm**: harm on the threshold or on the
  subjunctive-historical notion.
- **No-Difference View** (Parfit): it makes no difference to what we
  should do whether the size and composition of future generations
  depend on our choice.
- **Shift Thesis**: once enough is secured, the weight of reasons to
  benefit someone further changes discontinuously.
- **afterlife conjecture** (Scheffler, as glossed here): much of what we
  value presupposes that people will live after us.
- **non-correlative obligation**: a duty with no matching right in
  anyone.

## Bearing on the record

- **What the living owe the far future (QUESTION-tmpmx9ha).**
  The entry fills in the question's second ground, the good of future
  people, and shows it is not as empty as the question's summary says.
  - A weak person-affecting view can state harms to actual future
    people despite the non-identity problem, by a threshold.
  - But it owes nothing to merely possible people, and by its own
    account gives no reason to prefer a flourishing future to an empty
    one.
  - It meets the question's third ground, what the living value, at
    exactly that point. Meyer reaches for Scheffler, and for an
    obligation owed to present and past people.
  - So, on the person-affecting side, concern for the far future's
    existence rests on what the living and the dead value, not on future
    people's claims. That is the entry's structure, not an inference.
- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** These are
  inferences; the entry does not discuss the self.
  - *Neither an extended self nor an impersonal valuation is the
    entry's ground, but the gap it leaves is where the thesis would sit.*
    A future that is merely good is no one's claim on this view. What
    makes it matter is that the living's projects and attachments
    presuppose it. If those projects count as part of an extended self,
    the thesis has a way to read the strongest concern for the far
    future as self-interest. If they do not, Scheffler's case stands
    against it.
  - *Meyer's obligation is owed to the living and the dead, with future
    people only as beneficiaries.* That fits the thesis's shape: concern
    whose object is the future, whose ground is people who exist or
    existed.
  - *Rawls's first device was the thesis's shape, and he dropped it.*
    In *A Theory of Justice*, savings rested on the contractors' care for
    their descendants, a self extended along a family line. In
    *Political Liberalism* he replaced it with mutual disinterest plus a
    consistency condition. A theory of justice that once rested on
    extended concern now avoids relying on it.
