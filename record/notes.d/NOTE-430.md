# NOTE-430: Relational Frame Theory: Some Implications for Understanding and Treating Human Psychopathology

## Contribution

A compact statement of Relational Frame Theory by two of its founders, aimed
at clinicians. It adds an argument for why the theory changes behaviour
therapy, and a first RFT reading of three ACT processes. It reports no new
data. Its contribution is the mapping from RFT's basic concepts to clinical
phenomena (trauma talk, anticipatory fear, metaphor, rules, the self), with
worked examples for each.

## Key insight

Animals readily learn that a neutral event predicts an important one, but
not the reverse. Humans learn both directions, and then derive relations
they were never taught. Once a word stands in a learned, bidirectional,
derivable relation to an event, the word can carry the event's
psychological functions, and those functions can travel along relations
(sameness, comparison, opposition) to events never encountered. So most
human suffering is not only conditioned but verbal. Treatment has to work
on the relations and the contexts that control them, not only on direct
contingencies.

## Assumptions

- **Relating is operant.** Derived relational responding is "a type of
  generalized operant behavior that has been brought under the control of
  contextual cues (e.g., the word 'is') through a process of differential
  reinforcement" (p. 361). The paper argues for this from training studies
  it cites; it is the theory's central empirical claim.
- **Continuity has limits.** The "continuity assumption", that principles
  found with rats and pigeons apply to humans, "has served its time" (p.
  359). A human-specific research agenda is needed.
- **Functional, not syndromal, classification.** Behaviour analysts prefer
  dimensions such as emotional avoidance that "directly guide assessment and
  treatment" to DSM syndromes (p. 356, citing [LIT-551](../literature.d/LIT-551.md)).

## Key results

These are conceptual claims illustrated by examples and supported by cited
studies, not results the paper establishes.

- **Bidirectionality (pp. 359–360).** A dog learns "walkies" → exercise but
  not exercise → "walkies"; a child told after play that her toy is "Nimbo"
  is excited later by "Nimbo". So a human's report of a shock can acquire
  the shock's aversive functions, and a pigeon's report cannot.
- **Networks and derived relations (pp. 360–362).** Taught "Nimbo" = toy and
  "Daxy" = "Nimbo", a child relates "Daxy" to the toy (equivalence).
  Multiple-exemplar training establishes the generalized operant. The same
  holds for more/less, different, opposite: a one-euro coin is "worth more"
  than a physically larger fifty-cent coin.
- **Frames and their properties (pp. 362–363).** Frames of coordination,
  comparison, distinction, opposition and hierarchy. Each has mutual
  entailment (A same as B → B same as A), combinatorial entailment (A > B,
  B > C → A > C and C < A; A > B and A > C leave B–C unspecified) and
  transformation of function, all under contextual control ("Think of"
  versus "Think of the smell of").
- **Clinical transformations (pp. 363–364).** A child conditioned to fear
  "doctor", told dentists are like doctors, fears the dentist without an
  aversive dental history. An agoraphobic man ranks Macy's above the store
  where he panicked, through comparative frames ("larger than", "busier
  than").
- **Relating relations (pp. 364–365).** "Struggling with anxiety is like
  struggling in quicksand" relates two relational networks. The same
  process can make a person who feels "trapped" in a relationship sensitive
  to enclosed spaces. Skinner's extended tact "fails to specify" how this
  develops.
- **Rules (pp. 365–367).** A rule is a relational network; following it is
  regulated by coordination between that network and the network describing
  what is happening. Rules understood may still not be followed, for four
  reasons: missing repertoire, speaker credibility, the speaker's control of
  reinforcement, and the rule's plausibility.
- **The self (pp. 367–369).** Verbal self-knowledge makes a passed-over
  promotion something one "may literally fail everyday". Three selves: the
  conceptualized self (content of verbal relations), the knowing self
  (ongoing process) and the transcendent self (context). Decisions taken
  from self as context contact both content and process without either
  having "absolute control".
- **ACT (pp. 369–371).** Acceptance: a rule predicting an aversive event
  (going to the mall → panic) that then coheres with experience; relational
  coherence is reinforced, so acceptance strategies persist. Defusion:
  exemplar training that shifts thoughts from I HERE NOW to I THERE THEN.
  Values: the top of a hierarchical relational network.

## Concepts

- **derived relational responding / arbitrarily applicable relational
  responding**: relating events under the control of contextual cues set
  by the verbal community, not by the events' formal properties (p. 362).
- **relational frame**: a pattern of derived relational responding;
  coordination, comparison, distinction, opposition, hierarchy (p. 362).
- **mutual entailment, combinatorial entailment, transformation of
  function**: the three defining properties of a frame (p. 363).
- **relating relations**: relating one relational network to another; the
  RFT account of analogy and metaphor (p. 364).
- **rule-governed behavior**: "a frame of coordination between two
  relational networks serves as a source of control over behavior" (p. 366).
- **conceptualized, knowing and transcendent self**: self as content,
  process and context of verbal relations (p. 368).
- **I HERE NOW / I THERE THEN**: the deictic positions defusion moves a
  thought between (p. 370).

## Connections

- **The RFT book ([LIT-549](../literature.d/LIT-549.md)), unread.** Every concept here is cited to
  its chapters. This paper is a précis of it with a clinical slant, and the
  `extends` relation on the LIT says so.
- **Experiential avoidance ([LIT-551](../literature.d/LIT-551.md)), unread.** Cited for the
  functional-dimensional alternative to syndromal diagnosis. This paper
  gives the mechanism the 1996 paper's dimension needs: if words carry the
  functions of what they describe, private events can be as aversive as
  the events themselves, and avoiding them becomes a general strategy.
- **The ACT model ([LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md)), read.** The 2006 review condenses the same
  account into four implications and a model of "psychological
  inflexibility". This paper's acceptance, defusion and values are three of
  that review's six processes. Its self as context is a fourth.
- **Bandura ([LIT-545](../literature.d/LIT-545.md)), unread.** Cited as one of the cognitive–
  behavioural integrations that kept behaviour therapy going after Chomsky
  without changing its basic science (p. 358).
- **Emotion regulation ([LIT-554](../literature.d/LIT-554.md)).** Gross's process model treats
  regulation as acting on an emotion's generation. This paper locates much
  of the trouble in the verbal relations that give private events their
  functions, which is a different place to intervene. This paper does not
  cite Gross.

## Limitations

- **No data of its own.** Every empirical claim is a citation. The
  clinical cases are constructed examples.
- **Part 3 is labelled heuristic** by the authors (p. 369). The acceptance
  account, which rests on reinforcement of relational coherence, is not
  tested here.
- **A partisan statement.** The authors are the theory's originators, and
  the paper does not engage the critics; their reply (2003) does.
- **The DSM contrast is brief.** The paper dismisses syndromal
  classification in a paragraph, citing its own tradition.

## Open questions

- Is derived relational responding learned, as RFT claims, or does
  multiple-exemplar training only reveal a capacity it does not create? The
  paper asserts the first and cites training studies. The question is the
  centre of the commentaries the editors answered in 2003.
- Does relational coherence explain why acceptance-based instructions work,
  or would a simpler expectancy account do as well?
- Can the transformations of function the paper describes be measured in
  clinical populations, not only in the laboratory?
