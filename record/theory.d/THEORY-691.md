# THEORY-691: A self is an abstractum like a centre of gravity, posited by interpreting a person's behaviour and talk, the brain's own autobiography included; it is as determinate as that story, it can be authored by a process with no self in it, and a body holds as many selves as the points the best story coheres around

## Source

Dennett (1992), "The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity", in Kessel,
Cole and Johnson (eds.), *Self and Consciousness: Multiple Perspectives*,
[LIT-442](../literature.d/LIT-442.md), read in full in [NOTE-364](../notes.d/NOTE-364.md). The text read was a copy-edited
transcription, not the Erlbaum printing, so no pages are given and the
quotations below should be checked against Dennett's own wording before
they are relied on.

## What was actually shown

**The centre of gravity.** It has "no mass; it has no color; it has no
physical properties at all, except for spatio-temporal location", yet it
figures in explanations that "appear to be causal" ("Why doesn't the lamp
tip over?"). If its path matched one iron atom's, identifying the two
"would be a category mistake". The essay assumes Reichenbach's line between
abstracta, theorists' fictions, and illata, inferred entities.

**The fictional character.** Readers learn more about a character than the
author states, by implication. Beyond that, fictional worlds are
indeterminate: of Holmes's mole on his left shoulder blade, "There is
simply no fact of the matter." Bivalence fails for fictional objects and
holds for historical persons.

**Gilbert.** A novel-writing computer prints "Call me Gilbert", and Gilbert
"is a product of a design or invention process in which there aren't any
selves at all". Given wheels and a camera, the story turns out to have "a
truth-preserving interpretation": hit the robot and Gilbert is hit. The
robot "doesn't know what it's doing", and neither does your brain. That a
mindless machine could write a passable novel is stipulated; the doubter is
asked "to show why you think this must be so".

**Determinacy and revision.** A new Updike novel about the young Rabbit
would make the character more determinate by a creative step, and "That is
the way we treat each other; that is the way we are." Self-interpretation
can rewrite one's past in the same way.

**Counting.** After Gazzaniga, the normal mind is a bundle of partly
autonomous systems that make themselves cohere in one story, "enhanced by
an illusion of greater unity". An inconsistent sequel is resolved by
splitting the character. Two selves in one body need only that the story
"coheres much better" around two points than one. "The chief fictional
character at the center of that autobiography is one's self."

**What could have come out otherwise.** A search of the brain could have
ended at "that cell … that's the self!" The essay predicts it will not,
since selves have only grossly defined location. That is a prediction from
the account, not a finding the essay reports.

## What this does not say

- **That the self is an illusion or nothing.** It is a theorist's fiction
  of the respectable kind, like a centre of gravity. Shanahan ([LIT-466](../literature.d/LIT-466.md))
  cites the essay for a no-self conclusion; the filing readings call that a
  reading, not Dennett's view.
- **That the analogy has been shown to hold.** The essay names the shared
  features and does not show they are the ones that matter. A reader who
  thinks a self has a first-person point of view or a stake in its future is
  not answered.
- **Anything about consciousness.** Gilbert is stipulated "not a conscious
  machine". On the essay's own terms a self-narrative is no evidence of
  experience, so it does not bear on [THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md)'s question.
- **What a person is owed or what makes one persist.** The essay does not
  take up persistence, which is the question Locke, Parfit and Lewis answer
  ([THEORY-417](THEORY-417.md) separates them).
- **That self-talk evolved as the essay says.** The account, after Jaynes,
  is labelled speculative.
- **That the clinical cases are evidence here.** The split-brain and
  multiple-personality material is reported from Gazzaniga and the clinical
  literature, with their readings.
- **Anything about groups.** Counting a group self wherever the best
  interpretation of a group coheres around one point is [NOTE-364](../notes.d/NOTE-364.md)'s
  extension, not the essay's.

## Connections

- **Strawson** ([THEORY-684](THEORY-684.md)) places Dennett at "[+D +F +S +R]",
  committed to falsifying revision. [NOTE-383](../notes.d/NOTE-383.md) and [NOTE-364](../notes.d/NOTE-364.md) read the essay
  otherwise: the Updike case makes the self more determinate, it does not
  falsify facts. The two accounts disagree about whether people in general
  narrate themselves, and about what follows if they do not.
- **Gallagher** ([THEORY-458](THEORY-458.md)) objects that a single point cannot hold
  conflict and self-deception, and proposes the self as "the sum total of
  its narratives". Dennett meets the same cases by splitting the character
  into two selves; Gallagher keeps one self with the conflict inside it.
- **Hume** ([THEORY-681](THEORY-681.md)). The essay closes on Hume's failure to find a
  self. The fiction Hume blames on the imagination is here the brain's
  autobiography.
- **Shanahan** ([THEORY-490](THEORY-490.md)). A role-played simulacrum has a fictional
  character's structure: indeterminate beyond the conversation, made more
  determinate as it goes on. Dennett would count such a coherent character a
  self; the role-play paper counts it as "no-one at home". The pairing is
  [NOTE-370](../notes.d/NOTE-370.md)'s.
- **"Real Patterns"** ([LIT-220](../literature.d/LIT-220.md), [NOTE-190](../notes.d/NOTE-190.md)) uses the centre of gravity as the
  model of a good abstract object; this essay applies it to the self.
  Minsky's Single-Self ([LIT-432](../literature.d/LIT-432.md), [NOTE-348](../notes.d/NOTE-348.md)) is also a useful fiction, but
  many models switched among rather than one posited point.
- **[THEORY-043](THEORY-043.md).** The essay's selves are produced by parts that know
  nothing of them, so it sits on the decomposition side, and its counting
  rule is interpretive, not architectural.
