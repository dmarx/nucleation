# NOTE-1047: Barnehl and Schumacher — Media capture, captured

## Contribution

It replaces the annual, country-level expert score of media freedom with a
content-based score for each outlet at high frequency. Each nominally
independent outlet is placed on a scale whose ends are fixed by outlets of
known allegiance: regime-owned outlets mark full capture, and opposition
outlets in exile or online mark full independence. It is validated on one
country by five checks the authors set in advance.

## Key insight

A regime's editorial preferences are not published, but the outlets it owns
reveal them, and outlets it cannot reach reveal what reporting free of it
looks like. Capture can then be measured as relative position, with no
need to know the "true" agenda: an outlet is as captured as its coverage is
closer to the regime's own outlets than to the uncoercible ones. The
measure is a distance ratio. What makes a movement toward the regime pole
capture, rather than a change of mind, is not in the measure.

## Assumptions

- **Definition.** Media capture is "a situation in which news media fail to
  provide the public with scrutiny of government performance", narrowed by
  "Another condition for media capture is therefore that an outlet has to
  be coerced by an external actor to alter its editorial choices in his or
  her favour."
- **Regime-owned outlets express the regime's preferences.** "Being under
  complete control of the regime, these outlets can be assumed to adhere
  to its editorial preferences." The regime's preference is assumed
  coherent.
- **Opposition outlets mark full independence**, and their changes in
  coverage reflect the changing importance of issues.
- **Agenda and tone are the levers.** Agenda bias is grounded in
  agenda-setting research, and tone bias in effects of tone on attitudes.
  Framing is left out deliberately, for want of an agreed definition and
  measure.
- **Topics relevant to legitimacy are chosen in advance**: welfare,
  security, democratic procedure, and, for Nicaragua, COVID-19, the 2018
  protests, the OAS dispute, elections, repression and censorship, and the
  revolution.

## Key results

- **Topic independence score (Eq. 1).**
  i = [(d(P_N, P_Reg) − d(P_N, P_Opp)) / d(P_Opp, P_Reg) + 1] / 2, with 1
  for an outlet that covers a topic like the opposition and 0 for one that
  covers it like the regime. By the triangle inequality the numerator's
  difference lies within ±d(P_Opp, P_Reg), so the score lies in [0, 1]
  (checked here). It is undefined when the poles coincide.
- **Outlet agenda score (Eqs. 2–3).** A weighted sum of topic scores, with
  weights proportional to how far apart the poles are on each topic. The
  paper's example, distances 0.2, 0.1 and 0.05 giving weights 0.57, 0.28
  and 0.14, was rechecked.
- **Tone independence score (Eq. 4).** The same ratio on mean sentence
  polarity (−1, 0, 1 from pysentimiento) over sentences mentioning Ortega.
- **Data.** Twelve of 27 national outlets with an active web presence, and
  144,700 articles. Six are regime-owned, three nominally independent
  (Radio Corporación, Canal 10, Canal 14) and two opposition (Confidencial,
  100% Noticias). A 30-topic STM with outlet and date as covariates.
- **Validity checks I–V, all reported passed.**
  - I: topics track known events (COVID-19 cases near 6% prevalence in
    2020; elections in 2016–2017 and 2021).
  - II: opposition outlets cover the OAS, COVID-19, protests and
    human-rights topics more than regime outlets, and regime outlets cover
    welfare and infrastructure more.
  - III: opposition tone averages about −.4 and regime tone about .2.
  - IV: all three independent outlets' agenda scores fall over 2018–2022.
  - V: agenda and tone agree that Canal 14 moved most and Radio Corporación
    least. Canal 14's tone score fell from about .8 to below .5 by early
    2022. Canal 10 had too few mentions of Ortega for a tone score.
- **Against expert indices.** RSF and V-Dem series show a general decline
  since 2018 and cannot show that the outlets responded differently.

## Concepts

- **Media capture (regime-driven)**: an outlet's editorial choices
  "forcibly substituted by the regime's editorial preferences in terms of
  tone when addressing the regime leadership, and media agenda".
- **Nominally independent outlet**: not overtly owned by the regime and
  still operating legally in the country.
- **Agenda independence score, tone independence score**: an outlet's
  relative position between the regime and opposition poles, 0 to 1.
- **Agenda bias, tone bias**: unequal attention to topics, and evaluative
  slant toward an actor.

## Connections

It grounds agenda bias in agenda-setting (McCombs and Shaw,
[LIT-1335](../literature.d/LIT-1335.md), through the Luo et al. meta-analysis) and takes "capture" from
Stigler's regulatory capture. It cites Guriev and Treisman on informational
autocracy and Glasius on authoritarian practices. It does not cite Besley
and Prat or Szeidl and Szucs. Its method is close in spirit to Szeidl and
Szucs's use of a critical reference outlet to net out the neutral level of
coverage, with two poles where they have one.

## Bearing on the record

- **[TERM-066](../terms.d/TERM-066.md).** The paper's definition adds a condition the term
  lacks and the term adds one the paper lacks. The paper requires
  coercion by an external actor, so an outlet that agrees with the regime
  is not captured. The term requires a standard set apart from the
  process judged, and the paper supplies exactly such a standard, made
  operational: the uncoercible outlets' coverage. A25's caution holds for
  this paper's own measure: "Agenda and tone are observable indicators of
  possible capture. They are not themselves a complete causal
  identification of capture." The paper agrees in its validity section.
  Its observable signature is one of the three things the term says to
  keep apart, and the paper shows how to measure it, not how to tell
  coerced from agreed alignment.
- **[CLAIM-404](../claims.d/CLAIM-404.md).** The measure detects influence on an evaluator's
  output without access to its decisions or procedures, which fits the
  claim's starting point that control need not issue decisions. It does
  not tell the evidence route from the criteria route: a falling agenda
  score could be either.
- **[TERM-064](../terms.d/TERM-064.md), the direction "institution legible to society".** The
  method is a way to make an outlet's governance legible to outsiders
  from its output alone, with the caution the term already carries:
  whoever chooses the poles and the topic list controls what counts as
  captured. The topics here are chosen by the researchers as relevant to
  the regime's legitimacy.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **The poles are assumed, not measured.** If opposition outlets change
  their niche, for example becoming more critical after being driven into
  exile, a stable independent outlet loses score. The authors raise this
  and say there is "no obvious better gold standard".
- **Coercion is not observed.** The score measures alignment. The authors
  propose correlating score drops with data on attacks on journalists and
  have not done it.
- **One country, three outlets.** Canal 10 has no tone score.
- **No uncertainty.** The main text reports no statistical tests or
  intervals for the scores or the pole differences, though it calls the
  differences significant. The classifier's validation is in an annex not
  read here.
- **Requires a fully captured outlet**, which limits it to regimes already
  far along. Tone uses untargeted sentence sentiment.

## Open questions

- Do score drops follow specific regime measures, such as the cybercrime
  law, and acts of harassment? That would be the causal test the paper
  proposes.
- Does the measure agree with Szeidl and Szucs's single-reference slant in
  a case where both can be computed, such as Hungary, which the authors
  name as the next application?
