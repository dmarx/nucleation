# NOTE-tmpxsjeo: Baird, McKenzie & Özler — cash transfers and adult labour market outcomes

## Contribution

It sets out, against the textbook labour-leisure prediction, the channels
through which cash can raise or lower adults' work in poor countries
(missing markets, conditionality, scarring, general equilibrium), grades
each by how much evidence it has (Table 2), and summarises the typical
labour impact of each program type (Table 1): government conditional and
unconditional transfers, pensions, charitable and humanitarian cash,
remittances, job-search subsidies, business grants and graduation bundles.
Its conclusion is that the presumption that transfers undermine work
"seems largely unfounded" (p. 20) for the transfers studied.

## Key insight

The income effect that should make recipients work less is real but seldom
dominant among the poor, because cash also relaxes binding liquidity and
risk constraints, and relaxing them makes work pay. It shows most "when
transfers are large and/or prolonged (as with lottery winnings and
pensions)" (p. 19), that is, where nothing else binds. The one clear
reduction, among pensioners, is in a group "for whom more leisure" is
hardly "viewed as being a social bad" (p. 19).

## Assumptions

- **Studies taken at face value.** The review is "not ... a formal
  systematic literature review/meta-analysis" but "a structured overview
  of studies we view as well-identified" (pp. 2–3). It includes
  quasi-experiments (pension age, altitude, exchange-rate shocks), and does
  no pooling, quality scoring or publication-bias check.
- **"Labour" is market and own-account work.** Domestic and care work
  enter only as substitutes for children's work.
- **The base is mostly temporary, small or one-time transfers.** The
  conclusion rests on those; the authors name dependable, permanent,
  announced transfers as the case that may differ (p. 20).
- **Constraints bind for the poor.** The liquidity and insurance
  explanation is inferred from the pattern of results, not measured.

## Key results

1. **The prediction** (p. 3): a recipient of a windfall "should work less
   and earn less".
2. **Government transfers** (p. 7): in Banerjee et al.'s reanalysis of
   seven conditional-transfer RCTs in six countries, the authors "do not
   observe a significant effect either individually or pooled on
   employment or hours of work"; PROGRESA's effect on adult beneficiaries
   "is small at best". No effect sizes or intervals are given.
3. **Unconditional transfers in Malawi and Zambia** (p. 8):
   labour-constrained households "substitute away from being wage
   laborers for others" toward own farm work. The work changes kind; it
   does not stop.
4. **Pensions** (pp. 8–9): pension eligibility "causes large declines in
   labor force participation among the elderly themselves". For prime-age
   co-residents the estimates conflict: Bertrand, Mullainathan and Miller
   find "sharp decreases in the labor supply of prime-aged individuals
   living with elderly", while Ardington, Case and Hosegood find the pension
   "increases employment among prime-aged adults, by easing credit
   constraints for migration and job search".
5. **Children of recipient households, later** (pp. 9–10): mixed. In
   Nicaragua, boys aged 9–12 at baseline show "0.236 SD effect on a LFP
   index" ten years on; in Zomba, Malawi, two years after transfers ended,
   "no impact of either the conditional or the unconditional cash transfer
   intervention" on wage work, wages, hours or labour income.
6. **Charitable cash, GiveDirectly in Kenya** (p. 10): about nine months
   on, "there were no effects on labor supply or occupational choice".
7. **The reduction case, Syrian refugees in Lebanon** (p. 11), identified
   by an altitude discontinuity: winter cash "reduced adult labor supply
   during the past four weeks by 13%", which the authors call "consistent
   with the standard economic model of labor supply".
8. **Remittances** (p. 13), from quasi-experiments only: "the more
   reliable studies in the literature do not support the view that
   remittances make recipients lazy and work less".
9. **Job search** (p. 14): in Ethiopia "a 6.7 percentage point increase in
   employment", significant only at the 10 percent level; outcomes are
   measured over short horizons.
10. **Business grants** (p. 15): in Uganda, conditional start-up grants
    gave "a 17% increase in work hours and 38% increase in earnings"; in
    Kenya, small unconditional grants faded: "the cash impacts quickly
    dissipate".
11. **Graduation bundles** (p. 17): in Bangladesh, "four years after the
    initial transfer (or two years after the cessation of all support)",
    hours and days worked rose 17% and 22%, "indicating idle work
    capacity"; in six other countries labour rose "about 0.05 standard
    deviations (or about 6% over control households)" one year after
    support ended; quantile effects "at least an order of magnitude larger
    at the top of the asset/consumption distribution".
12. **Conclusion** (pp. 19–20): "prime age adults tend to see very little
    change in either the amount they work, or the amount they earn"; but
    "the impacts are likely to be different when transfers are dependable
    and permanent rather than temporary and/or surprises, when the
    eligibility criteria are known in advance".
13. **Table 2's grades** (p. 27): scarring "Appears plausible, but no
    evidence for this channel in existing literature"; means-test
    deterrence has "little evidence of this channel for most low- and
    middle-income country cash transfers"; insurance "Applies most when
    transfers are reliable and repeated".

## Concepts

- **Income effect, or labour-leisure trade-off**: the textbook prediction
  that a windfall buys leisure.
- **Missing-market channels**: health productivity, liquidity for
  self-employment, insurance, and investment in search.
- **Conditionality price effects**: work conditions, means-test
  deterrence, and conditions that take up time.
- **Human capital, or scarring, effect**: time out of work erodes skill,
  so a known-temporary transfer is predicted to keep people working.
  Graded as without evidence.
- **General equilibrium effects**: wages rising for non-recipients, or the
  value of leisure rising when others rest.
- **Idle work capacity**: the authors' reading of the Bangladesh results,
  under-employment by constraint, not by preference.

## Bearing on the record

Level first: every finding is about households and individuals. It does
not test the record's state-level mechanisms (tax learning, the fiscal
contract, upkeep of donor-funded assets), and the record should not use
household intuitions, that assured help makes people slack, in their support.
What it removes is that household analogy; what it supports is the
distinction between support that relaxes a constraint while the recipient
does the work and support that replaces the work.

- **[CLAIM-tmpc3x0c](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpc3x0c.md) (safe withdrawal; disuse, slack effort, selection).**
  Contradicts slack effort as a general household-level route: assured cash
  did not lower prime-age effort under government transfers, charitable
  cash or remittances (results 2, 6, 8, 12). Supports its conditions that
  support start a process and that the party do the work: graduation
  bundles raised work and kept it raised two years after all support ended
  (result 11), while small unconditional grants faded (result 10). Supports
  showing persistence after withdrawal, since impacts at exit overstated in
  Kenya and Malawi (results 5, 10).
- **[CLAIM-tmpqa0qu](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpqa0qu.md) (Black Queen loss; Samaritan's logic).** Contradicts it
  as a prediction for poor households: where constraints bind, cash
  complements effort. Fits it only where nothing binds, the pensioners
  (result 4), whom the authors judge harmless. The loss-of-capability
  channel itself, scarring, is ungraded for want of evidence (result 13).
- **[CLAIM-tmpoh07b](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpoh07b.md) (supply that does the work crowds out capacity).**
  Qualifies, consistent in its terms: cash does not do the household's
  work, it covers what the household cannot yet do, the claim's benign
  case, and work rises. It does not test the harmful case.
- **[CLAIM-tmpw4ai2](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpw4ai2.md) (thresholds).** Weak and indirect: graduation gains
  concentrate at the top of the distribution (result 11), consistent with
  gains needing a starting stock but not a threshold estimate; note 1
  reports Kraay and McKenzie finding poverty-trap evidence weak.
- **[CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md) (supplied repair decays on withdrawal).** None directly; the
  graduation persistence after cessation is a counter-pattern where support
  built a self-run asset, consistent with the claim's exclusion of systems
  that make their own parts.
- **[CLAIM-558](../claims.d/CLAIM-558.md) (disuse decay).** The review reports no evidence among
  workers for the disuse channel (result 13); absence of evidence, not a
  test.
- **[THEORY-tmph35ra](../theory.d/THEORY-tmph35ra.md) (Ostrom et al.).** Contradicts its behavioural premise
  carried down to households; leaves its own domain, donor-funded
  infrastructure upkeep, untested. The business grants that worked were
  conditional on investing, loosely like its ownership conditions.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md) (when a partial organization becomes independent).** The
  best household evidence on post-withdrawal persistence is the graduation
  result: gains held one and two years after support ended, concentrated
  where households started better off; small grants show the gains
  vanishing.

## Limitations

- The working paper was read, not the journal text; revisions after April
  2018 are unknown.
- A narrative review with no pooled estimates and few intervals or sample
  sizes; effect sizes are as the review reports them, unchecked against
  the primary studies.
- Selection is the authors' judgement, with no search protocol.
- The evidence is thin exactly where the record's claims sit: permanent,
  dependable transfers; announced eligibility; scarring or disuse.
- Short horizons: many follow-ups run 9 months to 5 years, and few measure
  after transfers stop.
- Baird and Özler review their own Malawi trial and McKenzie his own grant
  studies.
- The remittance evidence is non-randomized, and the refugee reduction is a
  single discontinuity study.
