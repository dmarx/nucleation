# NOTE-tmpqoajh: Comparing the Major Theories of Consciousness

## Contribution

A handbook chapter that compares the three frameworks neuroscientists take
most seriously, and sets aside quantum, panpsychist and representationist
theories. Its comparison uses one criterion, whether a theory recognises
the explanatory gap, and argues that only the biological theory does. It
also separates an "ambitious" from a "modest" higher-order theory and
argues against the first.

## Key insight

A theory that analyses consciousness in functional or cognitive terms
cannot have the explanatory gap. It has instead its own question, why
*this* function or *this* combination of thought and representation should
be conscious, and that question would arise for any realisation of the
function. The real gap, why this neural basis is the basis of this
experience, presupposes only that experience has a brain basis. So a
theory's fit with the gap is a test of whether it is a theory of
experience at all.

## Assumptions

- **Phenomenal consciousness** is "what it is like" (Nagel), and the gap is
  real at least for current neuroscience.
- **All scientific accounts** accept that consciousness is based in the
  brain; that alone generates the gap.
- **Representation** is used in a less liberal sense than mere correlation:
  an MT+ activation is a representation only if strong enough to be
  harnessed in choice, at minimum in priming.
- **The consciousness-of relation** is granted for the sake of argument:
  a conscious state is one one is conscious of being in.

## Key results

- **Three distinctions (pp. 1112–1113).** A non-representational MT+
  activation, a genuine representation of motion, and a representation that
  is a key part of a phenomenally conscious one. In workspace terms: a
  minimal sensory activation, a dominant peripheral coalition, and a global
  activation.
- **What consciousness does versus is (p. 1113).** On the biological view,
  broadcasting and higher-order thought are functions of consciousness, not
  its nature.
- **HOT and the gap (p. 1114).** HOT's ingredients (thought, aboutness,
  representation) each exist unconsciously, so neuroscience could explain
  each without finding "some magic glow" when they combine. The puzzle is
  HOT's own, as a "prime order thought" theory would have its own puzzle
  about prime embeddings. HOT is "a kind of 'no consciousness' theory of
  consciousness".
- **Gap and dualism (pp. 1114–1115).** As a pre-Socratic could not see how
  heat is motion, we lack the objective concept that would show how a
  subjective concept picks out the same thing. New Wave physicalism:
  subjectivity and objectivity are properties of concepts. Papineau's
  attempt to dissolve the gap this way fails, since two concepts of orange
  produce no gap.
- **Consciousness-of (pp. 1115–1116).** The deflationary (Sosa) and same-order
  (Brentano, Burge, Kriegel) accounts explain why a conscious state is one
  we are conscious of, without HOT.
- **Against ambitious HOT (pp. 1116–1118).** Rosenthal's conditions
  (non-inferential, mental states only, present tense) are added to meet
  counterexamples. The mismatch problem: what is experienced if a HOT
  about toe pain accompanies a visual red, or nothing? Infants: auditory
  synaptic density peaks around 3 months, frontal association cortex
  around 15 months; children to 3–4 have trouble reporting the source of
  their knowledge (Gopnik and Graf).
- **The self (pp. 1118–1119).** Visual-field relations make an experience a
  subject's own even if he cannot know of it, as might be the case for
  extinction patient GK. Goldberg, Harel and Malach found an "intrinsic"
  self-related system active in introspection and inhibited in fast
  categorisation, which "disconfirms" Rosenthal's view that a visual
  experience consists in a perception causing a thought that I have it.
- **Machines (p. 1119).** Workspace and HOT allow machine consciousness;
  the biological theory may require the electrical–chemical–electrical
  transfer of neural coding.

## Concepts

- **biological theory**: consciousness is a biological state of the brain
  (Place, Smart, Feigl; Block 1978; Lamme).
- **ambitious vs modest HOT**: an analysis of phenomenal consciousness in
  terms of higher-order thought, versus the claim that there is also a
  higher-order kind of consciousness.
- **explanatory gap**: we do not understand why the neural basis of an
  experience is the basis of that experience rather than another or none.
- **New Wave physicalism**: the gap is a dualism of concepts, not of
  properties.
- **mismatch problem**: a HOT whose content does not match the sensory state
  it is about, or that is about no sensory state.

## Limitations

- **A handbook overview.** The empirical points are brief and second-hand.
- **The comparison is by one criterion**, the gap, which a deflationist
  rejects at the outset; the chapter grants that this makes HOT attractive
  to those who deny the gap.
- **The biological theory is left unspecified** beyond the MT+ example and
  the thalamic-switch background, which the chapter says "is in flux".

## Open questions

- What would make a biological property, rather than the function it
  realises, the basis of consciousness, and how could that be tested?
  Block (2002) argues we have no conception of how.
