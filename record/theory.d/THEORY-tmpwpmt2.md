# THEORY-tmpwpmt2: In US law schools under the U.S. News ranking, administrators and faculty reported, and public sources corroborated, that schools changed what they did in order to move their rank: they moved money toward the ranking's inputs, redefined admissions and career-services work around its indicators, and gamed its figures, so the ranking altered the things it measured

## Source

Espeland, W. N. & Sauder, M. (2007), [LIT-tmpngpsy](../literature.d/LIT-tmpngpsy.md), read in [NOTE-tmpxq56d](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpxq56d.md).

## What was actually shown

The ranking weighs reputation (40%), selectivity (25%), placement (20%)
and faculty resources (15%), and covers every accredited school. The
authors ran two waves of open-ended interviews (66 and 70, about 45
minutes each), with deans, other administrators, faculty and staff from
70 schools across the four tiers. They added short interviews at an
admissions fair, 30 business-school interviews, school materials, chat
rooms and public data. Responses were coded for themes, "consequently,
the frequency of particular responses is only an approximate indication
of their importance."

What they found, as reported:

- **Reallocation.** Merit scholarships to raise median LSAT scores, in
  some schools at the expense of need-based aid (27 administrators
  reported increases in merit scholarships). Brochures to the
  reputation-survey raters, with many schools reported to spend over
  $100,000 a year and "no evidence that it works".
- **Redefinition of work.** All 16 career-services respondents described
  more effort spent tracking graduates' employment status, at the expense
  of counselling. Admissions decisions moved toward ranking criteria.
- **Gaming**, "efforts to improve ranking factors without improving the
  characteristics the factors are designed to measure". This was checked
  against public sources: in 1995, 29 of 177 schools reported higher
  median LSATs to USN than to the ABA, and 13 the next year. Other
  practices were reported: low scorers classed as part-time or
  probationary, students moved into night programs, faculty pushed to take
  leave in the spring.

The study could have found otherwise. Respondents could have described the
ranking as an outside report their schools ignored, which is what most did
at first, before "angry students and alumni" taught them otherwise.

## What this does not say

- **Not how much.** The study has no effect sizes and no comparison with
  schools or years without the ranking. That the changes were caused by the
  ranking is the respondents' account and the authors' inference.
- **Not that the ranking raised its own validity.** That is the paper's
  self-fulfilling-prophecy argument. Its quantitative links are cited, not
  shown, and it is held separately in [THEORY-tmpnfh8u](THEORY-tmpnfh8u.md), Proposed.
- **Not other measures or fields.** The authors chose the case because USN
  has no rival in law and ranks every school. Business schools, which face
  several rankings, are said to be affected differently.
- **Not that reactivity is harmful in general.** The authors note what the
  few supporters praised (more information for consumers, more disclosure)
  and call their own account one of "mostly harmful effects" in this case.
- **Not a test of any account of kinds.** [CLAIM-tmpeftzb](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpeftzb.md) can cite this as an
  instance of a measure acted on by the measured. The case does not show
  that the ranking tracks nothing independent of its use.
