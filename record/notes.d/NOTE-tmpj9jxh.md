# NOTE-tmpj9jxh: Feldman & Lynch — self-generated validity

## Contribution

Before it, the reactivity of measurement was treated as a methodological
nuisance: social desirability, evaluation apprehension, sensitization. The
paper gives it a substantive theory in the terms of social cognition, as
the authors say physics does with Heisenberg: "Our goal is to develop a
similar linkage in psychology, relating theories of the generation of
judgment and behavior to the effects of measurement" (p. 421). It names
self-generated validity: when a measured construct does not exist before
the question, "the act of measurement changes the phenomenon under study,
producing the thought processes predicted by the theory being tested and
quite possibly influencing behavior" (p. 422). It then gives a three-factor
account of when one answer becomes the input to a later one, and proposes
designs to tell retrieved from computed answers.

## Key insight

An answer is either retrieved or computed on the spot. A computed answer
is then stored, and is retrieved in its turn: "Once the answer has been
computed, it can be retrieved to answer subsequent questions" (p. 422). So
a question does two things, at two times. It can create a cognition that
did not exist, and the cognition it creates becomes one of the inputs to
later judgements and to behaviour. Which input a later judgement uses is
decided by accessibility and perceived diagnosticity, not by a fixed
hierarchy from belief to attitude to intention to behaviour. People with
judgements already formed and rehearsed, through expertise, direct
experience or strong involvement, retrieve those and are little moved.

## Assumptions

- **A partly constructionist view, set against four traditional
  assumptions** (p. 422): that measures "serve only to indicate what is
  already present and highly robust"; that behaviour is under the control
  of intention; that the constructs influence each other in a fixed causal
  hierarchy; and that they are context independent.
- **Cognitive economy.** "Resources are not used to develop judgments,
  beliefs, or plans of action unless some motive exists" (p. 422), so only
  some of what is measured already exists in memory.
- **Search stops at sufficiency.** Following Wyer and Srull, working memory
  is searched first and search stops when a sufficient basis is found
  (pp. 427, 429).
- **Scope.** Survey and laboratory measures of belief, attitude, intention
  and behaviour, in organizational, consumer and social research. The
  target is observed correlations among measures, not mean levels alone.
- **Evidence.** The paper reports no study of its own. "Our discussion has
  necessarily been speculative because little of the research that has
  provided the theoretical and empirical base for this article has been
  concerned with effects of measurement operations per se" (p. 432).

## Key results

The results are propositions supported by cited studies. The numbers are
the cited studies', as the paper reports them.

- **Measurement creates attitudes (Fazio, Lenn & Effrein 1984, p. 423).**
  Subjects who filled in a paper-and-pencil attitude measure after playing
  with novel puzzles answered later yes/no evaluative questions faster than
  subjects who had not. "This was interpreted as evidence that neither
  group had precomputed attitudes prior to questioning, but that the paper
  and pencil measures had stimulated the formation of retrievable
  attitudes." Groups told to expect an interview or a choice were as fast:
  attitudes form spontaneously "but only if it is functional to do so".
  No effect sizes are given.
- **The created state is carried forward (p. 423).** A survey "can function
  to bring normally automatic behavior under conscious control". Questioning
  "serves as the basis for attitude formation, which then serves as the
  most salient basis for the development of an intention", and "the
  antecedent cognitive states were not ones that would have occurred in the
  absence of measurement". Sherman (1980): stating an intention to perform
  a behaviour increases the likelihood of performing it.
- **Question-order effects as diagnosticity (Schuman & Ludwig 1983,
  p. 425).** Allowing American reporters into a Communist country: 90% yes
  when asked first, 66% when asked second. Allowing Communist reporters into
  the United States: 36% yes when asked first, 73% when asked second.
  Lawyers mercenary (72%) against doctors (50%): no order effect. Schuman
  and Ludwig read this as a norm of even-handedness; the paper reads it as
  the first answer being diagnostic for the second.
- **A prior evaluation replaces retrieval of its basis (Lichtenstein &
  Srull 1985, pp. 425–426).** Subjects who evaluated a product and
  evaluated it again an hour later recalled no more of the advertisement
  two days later than controls with no intervening task, while those who
  had judged style and then judged the product recalled as much as those
  told to recall. Only subjects "possessing a relevant, diagnostic prior
  judgment retrieved it for later use".
- **Three factors (pp. 424–430).** An earlier response i is used for a
  later one if (a) it is perceived as diagnostic for the later one, which
  is asymmetric and itself a judgement; (b) it is accessible, which falls
  with time and similar intervening material and rises with elaboration and
  rehearsal; and (c) more diagnostic alternative inputs are not accessible.
  Equation (1), p. 426, writes the probability of basing response i + n on
  response i as a function of the items' similarity and of n (the scan
  garbles the expression).
- **Lability (p. 424).** "any of 16 possible causal flows" among belief,
  attitude, intention and behaviour can be shown, especially for measures
  taken in immediate proximity. So an order-invariant correlation does not
  show that measurement did not inflate it.
- **A sequential test (p. 425).** For pairs of questions with pretested
  diagnosticity, the theory predicts the correlation is unaffected by order
  when A and B are equally diagnostic for each other, and higher in the
  order A, B when A is more diagnostic for B than B is for A.
- **Output interference (pp. 428–429).** Eliciting some cognitions
  suppresses the retrieval of others (Hoch 1984: generating reasons for an
  event raises its judged probability, mediated by the accessibility of the
  reasons). Tesser: "time spent thinking about an object or an issue
  renders schema (category)-consistent beliefs more accessible" (p. 429),
  which polarizes the attitude.
- **Moderators (pp. 424, 430).** Direct experience: "persons with direct
  experience have already formed and rehearsed overall attitude judgments
  prior to attitude measurement" (p. 424). Expertise (Bettman & Sujan):
  "Expert photographers choosing between two cameras showed no priming
  effects", but were as sensitive as novices choosing between a camera and
  a computer: "These subjects had no retrievable criteria for comparing
  such dissimilar alternatives" (p. 430). Trait involvement: "Because
  respondents high in trait involvement have accessible, affectively
  polarized categories, the operation of framing, priming, and context
  effects of measurement is mitigated" (p. 430). These value systems become
  articulated where the environment mediates "strong, frequent, positive or
  negative outcomes" (p. 430).
- **Scope claimed (p. 430).** "We believe, however, that situations
  conducive to self-generated validity effects are the rule, not the
  exception."
- **Not peculiar to surveys (pp. 422, 431).** "these processes are not
  peculiar to responses generated because a researcher has asked the
  subject to fill out a questionnaire"; conversation, reading and seeing a
  product do the same. "all real-world experiences with an object have the
  potential to shape subsequent responses to it" (p. 431).

## Concepts

- **Self-generated validity.** The state of affairs in which a measured
  construct does not exist, or is not spontaneously generated, without the
  researcher's query, so that measuring it produces the thought processes
  the tested theory predicts (p. 422).
- **Retrieval against computation.** An answer read from memory against one
  computed on the spot from other inputs; a computed answer can be
  retrieved later (p. 422).
- **Perceived diagnosticity.** The degree to which the respondent
  perceives that the answer to the first question correctly identifies how
  the second should be answered (p. 424, paraphrased closely; the scan runs
  words together). Asymmetric, response-dependent, and itself a judgement.
- **Elicitation context.** "externally provided and internally generated
  retrieval cues", which can change both the inputs to a judgement and the
  process acting on them (p. 422).
- **Trait involvement.** Chronic personal relevance, with affectively
  polarized, frequently rehearsed and context-independent categories
  (p. 430).

## Connections

It draws on the accessibility literature of Fazio, Wyer and Srull, on
Lichtenstein and Srull's separation of memory-based from computed
judgements, on Hoch's output interference, and on Fischhoff, Slovic and
Lichtenstein's labile values (1980), whose line Slovic summarizes
([LIT-tmpetez2](../literature.d/LIT-tmpetez2.md), read in [NOTE-tmp38ooy](NOTE-tmp38ooy.md)). The mere-measurement work of Morwitz,
Johnson and Schmittlein ([LIT-tmpj2lkz](../literature.d/LIT-tmpj2lkz.md), not read) tests its prediction that
measured intentions change behaviour.

**Measurement context and the interpreter's state ([TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md), [CLAIM-127](../claims.d/CLAIM-127.md)).**
[TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md) notes that A129's model gives the elicitation protocol C an arrow
into the judgement Y only, not into the update of the interpreter's state
R, and leaves the other route open. This paper takes the other route, and
names both. Its first way is an arrow from C into R: the question creates a
cognition that is then stored, "If the researcher asks a question about
attitude (or belief, intention, etc.), and no such cognition already
exists, the measure creates one" (p. 431), and the Fazio et al. reaction
times are evidence of the stored state, not of the response. Its second way
is the one [TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md) calls direct influence: when the cognition already
exists, order and format decide which input the answer is computed from,
and "Both retrieved and computed responses can be modified by the
elicitation context" (p. 422). Even this second way runs partly through
state, because the earlier answer is retrieved from memory. Its sequential
test (p. 425) is the design [TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md) and [CLAIM-127](../claims.d/CLAIM-127.md) say would separate the
routes. Its warning on p. 424 bears on reading such designs: an
order-invariant correlation does not rule out an effect of measurement.

**[CLAIM-tmpq9do8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpq9do8.md), the diachronic form.** The paper supports it directly,
and from outside the record. Its central proposition is the diachronic
form: "The respondent may use retrieved answers to earlier survey
questions as inputs to response generation to later questions" (p. 421).
Lichtenstein and Srull's result shows a judgement changing the observer
in a measurable way: those who had already judged did not re-retrieve the
original information and so later remembered less of it (p. 426). That is
a judgement changing what the judge will later have available, with no
action on the world. The mechanisms are all classical: retrieval,
accessibility, rehearsal, output interference. The paper invokes Heisenberg
only as an analogy for a theory of the instrument (p. 421). Two points cut
against the claim's use of it. First, the paper's main concern is that the
created state shapes behaviour, "quite possibly influencing behavior"
(p. 422), and its examples run to purchase, turnover and helping; the loop
it describes closes through action as often as through later judgement.
Second, it treats measurement as one case of a general fact, that "all
real-world experiences with an object have the potential to shape
subsequent responses to it" (p. 431). Judgement is not singled out.

**[CLAIM-tmpt568z](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpt568z.md) and [QUESTION-tmpr5tck](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpr5tck.md), what damps the effect.** The
moderators are expertise, direct experience and trait involvement. On the
paper's account they work because the person already holds a formed,
rehearsed judgement that is retrieved instead of computed (pp. 424, 430).
That is not evidence arriving from outside the interpretation it tests,
which is what [CLAIM-tmpt568z](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpt568z.md) calls an anchor. It is the loop's own earlier
output, made context-independent by rehearsal. The case for reading it as
an anchor rests on where those judgements came from: "direct experience"
with the object (p. 424) and environments that mediate "strong, frequent,
positive or negative outcomes" (p. 430). The paper also notes that
expectancy theories hold only where "strong contingencies between behavior
(or performance) and outcomes" exist (p. 424). So, on this paper's
account, the damping is a stored product of contingency between behaviour
and outcome, which is what [CLAIM-tmpki5gp](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpki5gp.md) says the loop needs. [QUESTION-tmpr5tck](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpr5tck.md)
reads damping by prior experience as the shape candidate 1 (unselected
input) predicts. This paper supports at least as well a reading on which
the damping is rehearsed commitment (candidate 3) formed under action. The
expert who is unmoved choosing between cameras but moved choosing between
a camera and a computer (p. 430) shows that the protection does not
generalize beyond the judgements already formed.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-tmpq9do8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpq9do8.md)**: supports the diachronic form, with classical
  mechanisms, from a theoretical synthesis with no new data. It does not
  support the claim's exclusion of action: the paper's measured state feeds
  behaviour, and it gives judgement no special place among experiences.
- **[TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md) and [CLAIM-127](../claims.d/CLAIM-127.md)**: the paper is a source for the arrow from
  measurement context into the interpreter's state, and supplies a
  sequential, diagnosticity-based design to separate it from direct
  influence on the response.
- **[CLAIM-tmpt568z](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpt568z.md)**: sharpens it. The moderators the literature reports
  are prior, rehearsed judgements, not independent evidence; whether they
  count as an anchor depends on their origin, which the paper places in
  direct experience and outcome contingency.
- **Contextuality.** The Schuman and Ludwig order effects it reanalyses are
  the kind of question-order data the record's contextuality line uses.
  The paper explains them classically, by diagnosticity, and does not
  bear on whether they are contextual.
- It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

The paper is speculative by its own account (p. 432) and reports no data.
Its theory is stated qualitatively; equation (1) names a function without
form. Diagnosticity is defined as a judgement and admitted to be
task-specific, so its causes are left unspecified: the paper says further
work is needed to specify them (p. 426). It concerns correlations among survey
measures, so its evidence that a created state lasts beyond the session is
indirect: Lichtenstein and Srull's two days, and the cited behaviour
studies.

## Open questions

- Does the asymmetric-diagnosticity order effect on correlations (p. 425)
  hold when tested? That is the paper's own prediction, and a test of the
  state route that [TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md) asks for.
- How long does a measurement-created cognition persist, and does it
  change later judgements of new material, not only answers to the same
  question?
- Do judgements formed through direct experience protect against
  measurement effects because experience brought evidence the judgement did
  not select, or only because it rehearsed the judgement?
