# NOTE-847: Harland — Beyond the Moral Influence Theory?

## Contribution

A critique of Manuel Vargas's *Building Better Beings* (2013). Vargas
presents his agency cultivation model (ACM) as a rehabilitated moral
influence theory (MIT) that escapes MIT's objections by aiming praise and
blame at a valuable form of agency: responsiveness to moral
considerations. Harland argues two things. ACM is MIT under another name,
because the agency it cultivates is just a disposition to behave
morally. And Vargas's counterfactual test of free will is epistemically
redundant and inverts the relation between freedom and responsibility.

## Key insight

If what makes an agent "free" is defined by what a well-functioning
blaming practice needs, then freedom cannot be a reason to blame. To know
whether S satisfies the test, you must already know whether blaming
people like S is good policy. Once you know that, the test adds nothing.
In Harland's image, Vargas's free will is "the whistle on an engine":
"it is actually our blameworthiness that makes us free".

## Assumptions

- **Harland's stipulations, not Vargas's definitions.** "External
  motivation is left undefined but we might stipulate the following". He
  stipulates external motivation, intrinsic motivation and
  "Responsiveness to Moral Considerations: A person is responsive to some
  moral consideration M to the extent that he is intrinsically motivated
  to act in accordance with M" (pp. 407–408). The identification is
  supported by passages of Vargas (2013: 203, 208–209, 217), not quoted
  from him.
- **Vargas's own framing of the test.** The similarity and proportion
  parameters are fixed by "the standards an ideal, fully informed,
  rational, observer in the actual world would select as at least
  co-optimal for the cultivation of moral considerations-responsive
  agency" (Vargas 2013: 214, quoted p. 418), and responsibility is "a
  function of what the justified norms of moral influence say" (Vargas
  2013: 103, footnote 11).
- **Classes of agents already sorted by their response to blame.** The
  redundancy argument proceeds by classes: psychopaths, kleptomaniacs,
  boisterous boys, pupils with "rotten social backgrounds", religious
  bigots, alcoholics. It assumes we can know how a class responds to
  blame before applying the test.
- **Deontology as Nozick's side-constraint view**, and virtue as
  requiring "proper reasoning" (Timpe and Boyd), for the argument that
  non-consequentialists will not adopt ACM (§2.2).

## Key results

- **ACM's replies to MIT's objections are available to MIT (§1).**
  - Deontological side constraints can be added to either.
  - The objections from conflating being and holding responsible, from
    the phenomenology of the reactive attitudes, and from backward-looking
    blame (Klaus Barbie) get replies that "can just as easily be made by
    supporters of MIT".
  - Vargas's claim that a future-oriented justification is "all the
    license we can hope for" (2013: 194) is at odds with his opening
    claim that praise and blame make sense "partly because of" their
    effects (2013: 3–4).
- **The John and Fred cases (§2.1).** John internalises, through fear of
  hellfire and ostracism, an aversion to homosexuality he cannot justify.
  Fred internalises, through punishments, an aversion to racism he cannot
  justify ("They just should!"). Both end intrinsically motivated. Vargas
  must deny John enhanced agency, since his norm "carries no genuine moral
  force". But Fred came by his true norm the same way, so he "remains
  unworthy of the label moral agent": "There is an inverse relationship
  between moral agency and the susceptibility of one's moral convictions
  to external forces." Fred is "a moral drifter". Even so, "there are
  obviously good reasons for holding them accountable"; what fails is the
  claim that doing so "promotes their moral agency".
- **The dilemma for a richer notion (§2.1).** A Dworkinian notion of
  moral agency (scrutinising one's convictions against one's others, the
  facts and logic) would distinguish ACM from MIT, but it "requires mental
  fortitude, intelligence, and creativity", which praise and blame are not
  shown to enhance. "The richer the conception of agency employed, the
  less tenable ACM becomes."
- **ACM draws the line where MIT does (§2.2).** Vargas's example of
  moral responsiveness, perceiving "when a quiet friend is in need of
  consolation" (2013: 208), is shared by chimpanzees, bonobos, rats (as
  cited from Churchland) and infants of 18–30 months. So ACM, like MIT,
  counts some animals and toddlers as responsible agents and fit for
  "wholehearted resentment".
- **A defence of ACM (§2.2).** Against Elzein, ACM does not treat anyone
  merely as a means, because it justifies a system whose benefits reach
  everyone it blames, following Kramer on deterrence.
- **Why deontologists and virtue ethicists will not adopt ACM (§2.2).**
  It grounds desert in consequences (against Pereboom's basic desert and
  Moore's retributivism); it invites Hegel's "raising one's stick at a
  dog"; it cannot separate blame from milder brainwashing or pills; and it
  treats compliance with duties as an end to maximise, a "utilitarian of
  rights" in Nozick's phrase. "His account does not merely share MIT's
  shortcomings; it just is MIT."
- **Vargas's test (§3.1).** S is a responsible agent for considerations M
  in circumstances C if S has the basic agential capacities and (A) the
  capacity to detect M (S actually detects it, or does so "in a suitable
  proportion" of worlds relevantly similar to C) and (B) the capacity for
  self-governance with respect to M (S is motivated accordingly, or is so
  in a suitable proportion of relevantly similar worlds, where S detects
  M, becomes motivated by it and acts accordingly) (Vargas 2013: 213–214).
  Free will is (A) and (B) jointly. Relevance and proportion are fixed by
  what is "co-optimal" for "fostering agency that recogni[s]es and
  suitably responds to moral considerations" (2013: 219–221): "a
  Goldilocks notion of capacity" (Vargas 2018: 120).
- **Epistemic redundancy (§3.2).** "We cannot determine how Jones would
  have behaved in the relevant circumstances until we have decided
  whether it is good policy to blame similar people for similar
  wrongdoings … once the matter is settled, we will be left with no
  reason to engage in any counterfactual reasoning." "The work necessary
  to give the counterfactual test meaning makes that very test
  redundant."
- **Conceptual inversion (§§3.2–4).** "A wrongdoer's blameworthiness is
  logically prior to his free will." "If freedom simply means belonging to
  a group of people whom it is good policy to blame, it is not really
  freedom in any recognisable sense of the term." The scope is stated:
  "In short, if we embrace ACM, enquiries into an agent's counterfactual
  freedom will become superfluous when considering the ethics of holding
  him responsible."
- **The diagnosis (§3.2).** Vargas wants the test to do two jobs: pick out
  agents likely to be improved by blame, and preserve the folk intuition
  that responsibility requires the ability to do otherwise. His
  "philosophical conservation" principle is "entirely pragmatic", based on
  the costs of revising beliefs. The result is "a much more profound shift
  in folk thinking than he recognises".
- **A note on psychopaths (footnote 9).** On Vargas's capacious notion,
  psychopaths who adjust conduct to prudential concerns, and might gain an
  affective drive against wrongdoing through blame, should count as moral
  agents, though Vargas exempts them.

## Concepts

- **Moral influence theory (MIT).** Responsibility practices are
  vindicated by their tendency to strengthen dispositions to behave in
  morally desirable ways (Nowell-Smith, Smart, Schlick, Dennett, Arneson,
  McGeer).
- **Agency cultivation model (ACM).** Vargas: moralised praise and blame
  "are justified by their effects, that is, how they develop and sustain a
  valuable form of agency".
- **External and intrinsic motivation.** Motivation to comply with a norm
  that does, or does not, depend on expecting beneficial or adverse
  personal consequences. Harland's stipulations, after Sripada and Stich.
- **Moral consideration.** Vargas: "a consideration with moral
  significance such that, were one to deliberate about what to do, it
  ought to play a role in those deliberations"; it may be "largely or
  perhaps exclusively affective", and its detection "need not be
  conscious".
- **Moral drifter.** An agent whose moral convictions are the product of
  "unthinking capitulation to social pressures", whether or not they are
  true.
- **Counterfactual freedom.** Vargas's free will: conditions (A) and (B)
  above, circumstantial (relative to M and C) and scalar (footnote 8).

## Connections

The target is Vargas (2013) and his reply to critics (2015). The paper
sides with Elzein (2013) against ACM on desert but against her
mere-means objection, and engages McGeer (2015), Capes's review (2016),
Pereboom (2014) on basic desert, Fischer and Ravizza (1998), whose
epistemic and control conditions Vargas's two parts track, and
Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment". None of these is held here except
through other readings of the free-will line. Harland writes from the
Kramer school (acknowledgements; Kramer on rights and on capital
punishment).

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-068](../questions.d/QUESTION-068.md) (Deferred; no `answers:` relation).** It asks whether
  settling whose a will-organization is would settle accountability, or
  whether accountability needs conditions, such as normative competence
  and avoidability, that ownership does not supply. The paper bears on it
  in two ways, both indirectly.
  - *Avoidability, on an influence account.* Harland argues that once
    responsibility is justified by its effects, an avoidability
    condition calibrated to those effects is not an independent
    condition. It is fixed by the policy question. So on such accounts
    the question's second option, a separate avoidability condition,
    collapses into the ethics of holding responsible. This is the
    account filed as [THEORY-310](../theory.d/THEORY-310.md). It does not touch accounts on which
    avoidability is a metaphysical condition, as in [THEORY-118](../theory.d/THEORY-118.md) and
    [THEORY-119](../theory.d/THEORY-119.md).
  - *Ownership against moral agency.* Fred's norm is internalised and
    intrinsically motivating, yet Harland calls him "a mere receptacle of
    social mores" and still finds "good reasons for holding" him
    accountable. That is a candidate for the converse case "What would
    answer it" asks for: accountability where the will is arguably not
    the agent's own. But the reasons given are forward-looking, the
    verdict is asserted rather than argued, and Harland does not discuss
    ownership. It does not survive the dispute between [THEORY-118](../theory.d/THEORY-118.md) and
    [THEORY-119](../theory.d/THEORY-119.md), because it uses neither.
- **[LIT-991](../literature.d/LIT-991.md)'s filing note overstates in two places.**
  - It says the paper criticises a responsiveness condition "stated
    apart from whose the will is". The paper never raises ownership. Its
    complaint is that Vargas's responsiveness is too capacious to
    distinguish moral agency from a disposition to behave morally.
  - It says the paper argues "that freedom does not bear on holding
    someone responsible, which cuts against the question's ownership
    side". The abstract says so unconditionally, but the body restricts
    it: "if we embrace ACM, enquiries into an agent's counterfactual
    freedom will become superfluous". It is a claim about Vargas's
    freedom inside Vargas's model, and it says nothing about ownership,
    so it does not cut against the ownership side.
- **[THEORY-122](../theory.d/THEORY-122.md).** Harland's Dworkinian alternative, moral agency as
  reflective scrutiny of one's convictions, is a normative-competence
  condition of the kind [THEORY-122](../theory.d/THEORY-122.md) holds that identification does not
  supply. The paper offers it as a dilemma for Vargas, not as its own
  view.
- **[THEORY-147](../theory.d/THEORY-147.md).** Harland separates holding someone accountable (there
  are "good reasons" to hold John and Fred accountable) from their being

## Limitations

- **Second-hand Vargas.** Every claim about ACM rests on Harland's
  quotations and his stipulated reconstruction of responsiveness. Vargas
  2013 and the 2015 reply are not held here. Footnote 7 records that
  McGeer read Vargas the other way (capacities "ontologically prior to,
  and conceptually independent of" their role in the system), and that
  Vargas rejected McGeer's reading.
- **The redundancy argument assumes the classes.** It works class by
  class, with the class's response to blame already known. On the
  reader's view, not the paper's: if the counterfactual test is what
  sorts individuals into classes, or if the parameters are calibrated
  once for the whole system and then applied to individuals by facts
  about them, the test keeps work to do. Harland's teacher example
  calibrates per class ("boisterous boys"), which is the case most
  favourable to him.
- **Thin empirical support.** The animal and infant cases come from
  secondary sources (Churchland, Goodall, Bartal et al., Zahn-Waxler et
  al.). The reference for Bartal et al. gives Science volume 344; the
  article is in volume 334 (2011).
- **The John and Fred cases rest on intuition** about what moral agency
  requires, set against a Dworkinian standard that Harland does not
  defend.
- **The abstract overstates the conclusion**, as noted above.

## Open questions

- Does Vargas's 2015 reply, or later work, answer the redundancy
  argument, for instance by fixing the parameters once at the system
  level? Reading Vargas 2015 (Philosophical Studies 172:2659–2678) would
  settle what the test is meant to do.
- Is there a forward-looking account whose capacity condition is not
  calibrated to the system's aims, and so escapes the inversion?
