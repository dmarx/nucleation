# NOTE-tmpgwh6b: Choroszewicz — public sector practitioners as moral crumple zones

## Contribution

Extends Elish's concept from the attribution of an accident to the
ordinary allocation of responsibility before any failure, in early pilots
of generative-AI work tools, and names three opacities users face:
epistemic, interpretative and operational.

## Key results

- **The question** (p. 2): "how public sector practitioners are positioned
  as MCZ for the effective and ethical use of LLM-based tools".
- **Definition used** (§2, p. 3): "MCZ positioning of public sector
  practitioners is conceptualized as the disproportionate allocation of
  responsibility to LLM-based tools' users relative to their actual
  authority, control and capacity to influence these tools' design,
  functioning and quality of outputs."
- **Oversight codified as user burden** (§4.1, p. 6): "human oversight and
  responsibility as liability were explicitly codified as a user burden".
- **Faults reframed as user error** (§4.2, p. 6): "flawed and low-quality
  outputs tended to be portrayed as the result of poor user interaction
  rather than issues with the tool's design and development"; "It is
  always users' responsibility to correctly interpret outputs".
- **Persuasive fallibility** (§4.5, p. 9): "persuasiveness may mask
  fallibility, increasing the likelihood of undetected errors, especially
  for insufficiently experienced workers"; vigilance became "continuous
  cognitive and emotional labour".
- **Three roles** (§5, p. 9): users were placed "to act as testers, AI
  outputs' quality verifiers and interpreters", "despite the tools'
  technical immaturity and opacity", "creating a paradox where tools
  intended to save time instead generated additional workload".
- **Insulation** (§5.2, p. 10): "Codifying responsibility as a user burden
  insulates technologies and their developers from accountability".
- **Self-limitation** (§5.3, p. 10): the lens "may nevertheless
  oversimplify the complexity of varying levels of human involvement and
  control".

## Assumptions

- That the concept applies before any failure event: the "failures" here
  are poor outputs and failed pilots, not accidents.
- That innovators' observed rhetoric reflects how the organizations
  allocate responsibility.
- Finnish legal standards requiring human oversight and decision-making
  (§5.2).

## Concepts

- **MCZ positioning**: disproportionate responsibility relative to
  authority, control and capacity to influence.
- **Sparring buddy**: the immature tool marketed as a partner.
- **Persuasive fallibility** and the **burden of vigilance**.
- **Epistemic, interpretative and operational opacity**.

## Bearing on the record

Filed as THEORY-tmpmdej3.

- **CLAIM-tmpb47ul**, the control-and-duty part, empirically: responsibility
  allocated beyond "actual authority, control and capacity to influence".
  And the conscientiousness part in a mild form: vigilant, experienced
  users caught errors that made the tools look workable, at a cost of
  unrecognized labour (§4.5, §5). It does not show blame landing after a
  failure, nor who sets the standards beyond the innovators' framing.
  Corroborates.
- **CLAIM-tmp238zy** (scrutiny demanded of individuals while the
  institution stays unanswerable): verification duties placed on users
  while the tools and their developers are insulated (§5.2). Corroborates.
- **Elish (LIT-tmpnmwlk)**: the study applies her concept to the period
  before failure, which she did not treat.

## Limitations

- Early pilots, not deployed systems; one country; two organizations.
- The author says the lens may oversimplify.
- Users interviewed are a fraction of testers (about 43 of about 900
  Copilot testers).
- No incident in which a user was blamed for a harmful outcome is
  reported.
