# NOTE-tmp46uej: Pereboom — Living without Free Will

## Contribution

The pages read set out the book's position and its plan. The position is
named hard incompatibilism. Both determinism and the indeterminacy of
standard quantum mechanics are incompatible with the free will that moral
responsibility requires. Agent-causal libertarianism would secure that
free will, but we have no evidence for it. So we have no evidence that we
are morally responsible, and "morality, meaning, and value remain intact".
Chapter 1's opening defends one plank: the incompatibilist's core
condition concerns an action's causal history, not alternative
possibilities.

## Key insight

Frankfurt-style cases show that responsibility does not need the ability
to do otherwise. They do not show that responsibility survives an action's
being produced by a deterministic process that traces back to factors
beyond the agent's control. So incompatibilism can concede Frankfurt and
relocate. Its core is origination: the agent must be the source of her
decision, not a channel for factors she had no control over.

## Assumptions

- **Moral responsibility means basic desert.** For an agent to be morally
  responsible for an action is for it "to belong to the agent in such a
  way that she would deserve blame if the action were morally wrong". The
  desert is "basic": deserved "just by virtue of having performed the
  action, and not ... by way of consequentialist considerations" (p. xx).
- **Judgements of responsibility are factual.** They are not, at bottom,
  attitudes toward the person (pp. xx–xxi).
- **Responsibility attaches primarily to decisions.** Omissions and
  consequences are set aside, on the ground that responsibility is "a
  matter of control" exercised mainly over decisions (p. xxi).
- **Holding accountable is not the topic.** Determinism does not threaten
  holding people accountable for the sake of moral improvement. A
  compatibilism about that "avoids the issue" (pp. xxi–xxii).

## Key results

*Skim: Introduction pp. xiii–xxiii and Chapter 1 pp. 1–10, read in full.
Section titles for the rest come from the table of contents.*

- **The position (pp. xviii–xix).**
  - "Hard determinism" is rejected as a label, "since I do not claim that
    determinism is true", and whether quantum mechanics is deterministic
    "is currently an open question".
  - "Not only is determinism incompatible with moral responsibility, but
    so is the sort of indeterminacy specified by the standard
    interpretation of quantum mechanics, if that is the only sort of
    indeterminacy there is."
  - "We have no evidence for indeterminacy of the kind that would be
    required for agent-causal libertarianism, and ... therefore, we have
    no evidence that we are morally responsible."
  - Against "no-free-will-either-way" views: "there is a coherent
    indeterminist scenario in which we have this sort of free will ... and
    it may well be that agent-causal libertarianism is metaphysically
    possible."
  - In Strawson's table of nine positions, hard incompatibilism takes
    positions 1, 4 and 7.
- **The stronger claim, stated once (p. xiii).** "I shall argue that our
  best scientific theories indeed have the consequence that we are not
  morally responsible for our actions."
- **Plan of the book (pp. xv–xx).**
  - Chapter 1: Frankfurt-style arguments are "largely successful", and a
    new one is offered. They do not threaten "the core incompatibilist
    claim ... to have causal histories that make agents ultimate sources
    of their actions".
  - Chapter 2: event-causal libertarianism fails to make agents sources.
    Agent-causal libertarianism "might well satisfy this requirement".
  - Chapter 3: agent causation is "seriously challenged by empirical
    considerations".
  - Chapter 4: against P. F. Strawson, "determinism can indeed be relevant"
    to the reactive attitudes. Causal-integrationist compatibilisms are
    met with "counterexamples" and "a general argument that no relevant
    distinctions can be made between cases in which agents are determined
    in ways compatibilists think are consistent with moral responsibility
    and those in which agents are determined in ways that clearly
    undermine moral responsibility". The table of contents names it "the
    four-case argument" (§ "Haji's response to an earlier version of the
    four-case argument", p. 117).
  - Chapters 5–7: morality, criminal justice (no retributivism; preventive
    detention and rehabilitation can be justified) and meaning in life
    survive.
- **What it does not deny (pp. xxii–xxiii).** The hard incompatibilist does
  not deny "the ability to choose in accord with one's values, or the
  capacity to be responsive to the reasons there are for acting", or
  freedom from coercion. The position is "essentially a negative
  position".
- **Chapter 1, the two intuitions (pp. 1–6).**
  - Principles (1) and (2): free action requires that the agent could have
    done, or chosen, otherwise. Pereboom will argue that "any such
    requirement that is relevant to explaining why an agent is morally
    responsible for an action falls to counterexamples".
  - Fischer's Black/Jones case is quoted. With Fischer, Pereboom holds
    that it does not refute "the claim that moral responsibility requires
    that the actual causal history of the action not be deterministic".
  - Condition (3): "An action is free in the sense required for moral
    responsibility only if it is not produced by a deterministic process
    that traces back to causal factors beyond the agent's control."
  - Condition (O): "If an agent is morally responsible for her deciding to
    perform an action, then the production of this decision must be
    something over which the agent has control, and an agent is not
    morally responsible for the decision if it is produced by a source
    over which she has no control." This is "the most fundamental and
    plausible incompatibilist intuition", and it also explains why an
    uncaused decision would not be one the agent is responsible for.
  - Leeway against causal-history incompatibilism: Pereboom is the
    second.
  - "The more powerful, and indeed the best, type of challenge to
    compatibilism develops the claim that causal determination presents
    in principle no less of a threat to moral responsibility than does
    covert manipulation. We shall turn to that challenge in Chapter 4"
    (p. 6).
- **Chapter 1, robustness and Widerker (pp. 6–10).** A flicker of freedom
  must be "robust" to ground responsibility: securing it must be what
  would have got the agent off the hook. Blushing is not robust. The
  Kane/Widerker dilemma is stated. If the prior sign determines the
  action, the libertarian lacks the intuition of responsibility. If it
  does not, the agent could have done otherwise. Pereboom calls it "a
  test for the effectiveness of any Frankfurt-style argument". His answer,
  a scenario "without prior signs" and "a new Frankfurt-style scenario"
  (pp. 14–23 by the contents), falls outside the pages read.

## Concepts

- **Hard incompatibilism.** Moral responsibility is incompatible with
  determinism and with event-causal indeterminacy of the quantum kind, and
  we have no evidence for the agent-causal indeterminacy that would allow
  it. Not "hard determinism", since determinism is not asserted.
- **Basic desert.** Desert of blame or credit just for having performed
  the action, not for consequentialist reasons.
- **Leeway incompatibilism.** Responsibility needs an indeterministic
  causal history only because that secures alternative possibilities.
- **Causal-history incompatibilism.** The causal history matters to
  responsibility independently of alternative possibilities.
- **Origination (O).** The production of a decision must be something over
  which the agent has control.
- **Robust alternative.** An alternative whose securing would by itself
  have made the agent not responsible for what she did.

## Connections

The pages read engage Frankfurt and Fischer (Black/Jones), Kane's
ultimate-responsibility condition (UR), Honderich on origination,
Widerker's and Ginet's defences of alternative possibilities, McIntyre's
princess case (footnote 9), Galen Strawson's table and
"no-free-will-either-way" view (Double's term), and P. F. Strawson's
reactive attitudes. The acknowledgments say the book's general conception
comes from "Determinism Al Dente" (Noûs, 1995), and that part of Chapter
1 is "Alternative Possibilities and Causal Histories" (Philosophical
Perspectives, 2000).

  corrected. Its description of the four cases is unverified here and is

## Limitations

- Only the Introduction and ten pages of Chapter 1 were read. The

- The Introduction's summaries are the author's own and are not
  arguments.
