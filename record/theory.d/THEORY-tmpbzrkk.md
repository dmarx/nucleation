# THEORY-tmpbzrkk: Puritanical norms against harmless bodily pleasures are cooperative morality: people believe cooperation needs self-control, which indulgence erodes and discipline trains, and condemn indulgence as an indirect threat to cooperation

## Source

Fitouchi, André & Baumard (2021), [LIT-101](../literature.d/LIT-101.md) (*Behavioral and Brain
Sciences* 46), read in [NOTE-132](../notes.d/NOTE-132.md). The BBS commentaries and the authors'
response were not read.

## What was actually shown

**The account.** Puritanical morality condemns lust, gluttony, intoxicants,
immodesty and idleness, and praises temperance and piety. It "does target
cooperation, and is reducible to concerns for harm or fairness" (p. 9).
People believe that reciprocal and reputation-based cooperation requires
delaying gratification, that indulgence erodes the capacity and that
discipline trains it. Victimless pleasures are condemned as indirect,
probabilistic threats to cooperation.

**The direct evidence.** The authors' vignette studies (N > 1,100): perceived
change in self-control mediated 100% of the effect of indulgence versus
restraint on perceived cooperativeness. For alcohol, the same design gave
full mediation for cooperativeness and 71% for perceived moral character.

**Against disgust (§2.1).** Classic purity vignettes are pathogen-laden.
Pathogen-free "spiritual purity" violations do not produce disgust's facial
expression, nausea or "grossed-out" reports. A meta-analysis (Landy &
Goodwin 2015) and replications find disgust does not amplify condemnation.
Correlations between disgust sensitivity and condemnation vanish once
perceived harm is controlled (Schein et al. 2016).

**Supporting reviews.** Loyalty and Authority predict the moralization of
victimless excess about as strongly as Purity does (Mooijman et al. 2018,
N > 3,000). Across about 20,000 people in 56 countries, how much a society
values self-control predicts how restrictive its hygiene norms are better
than pathogen threat does (Eriksson et al. 2021). Stereotyping men as unable
to control sexual urges predicts moralizing immodesty, with reproductive
strategy, religiosity, conservatism and sexism controlled (Moon et al.
2021).

**What could have come out otherwise.** Perceived self-control could have
mediated little of the effect, leaving a direct path from indulgence to
judged cooperativeness. It did not.

## What this does not say

- **That the belief causes the norms.** The mediation evidence is
  third-person perception of hypothetical targets. It shows that people hold
  the belief, not that the belief produced puritanical norms historically.
- **That the norms work.** The account is agnostic about whether
  self-discipline makes anyone more cooperative (pp. 10, 26). That makes it
  robust and hard to test at the population level.
- **That all of purity is cooperative.** Contamination purity is excluded by
  definition (p. 5), so the case against disgust covers only part of the
  Sanctity domain. The abstract's "does not require adding mechanisms
  unrelated to cooperation" is narrower than it reads.
- **That puritanism declines because people come to trust each other.** That
  rests on cross-national correlations, and one key World Values Survey figure
  is uncited (p. 39).
- **That morality is morality-as-cooperation's seven domains.** It reduces
  puritanism to harm and fairness, which saves "morality is about
  cooperation" but not that list.
