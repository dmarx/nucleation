# NOTE-126: Worrall — Evidence in medicine and EBM

## Contribution

Medicine, like any rational activity, obviously has to rest on evidence; the interesting question is how general principles of evidence apply there. Worrall focuses on the Evidence-Based Medicine movement and raises difficulties for the rationales given for its evidence hierarchy, especially the privileged place of RCTs and meta-analyses of RCTs. He does not oppose a scientific approach to medical evidence. His aim is to show where philosophers of science can help implement that approach properly.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1: an unusual Compass piece, not a literature guide but a pitch for a new area where philosophers of science could have practical impact. Principles of evidence are held to be universal, with application depending on the discipline.
- §2 traces the birth of EBM. §3 says EBM rightly abandoned the crisp "only RCTs count" view but replaced it with an unclear one. It lists open issues: why only therapeutic claims need RCTs (3a), "face validity" (3b), amalgamating evidence types (3c), combining trials (3d), biased RCTs (3e), external validity (3f).
- §4 analyses the arguments for randomization's extra weight: (4a) Fisher's argument from significance-testing logic; (4b) randomization controls all confounders, known and unknown; (4c) selection bias; (4d) observational studies "known" to exaggerate effects; (4e) randomization and probabilistic causality.
- Conclusion: sound method reduces to "scientific commonsense" (Mill, Popper): telling evidence counts against plausible rivals. What matters is balance on known plausible confounders and a placebo control. Whether balance comes from randomization with post-hoc checks or from deliberate matching "seems epistemically unimportant". The advantage of randomization in a double-blind, balanced study is hard to see.

## Open questions

- A classic challenge to the "gold standard" status of RCTs, directly relevant to how the anthology weighs randomized ablations and A/B tests against observational comparisons.
- Check §4(b) in full: the unknown-confounders argument is the one most often used in ML experiment design (random seeds, splits).
- Read with Cartwright ([LIT-213](../literature.d/LIT-213.md)) and Reiss ([LIT-118](../literature.d/LIT-118.md)), which extend the anti-hierarchy line. Check later responses defending randomization (e.g. on selection bias and allocation concealment).
