# NOTE-1683: Collins and Shester — Urban renewal grants and city outcomes, read from the NBER working paper

## Contribution

The literature on urban renewal is, in the authors' words, "highly
critical but remarkably thin when it comes to providing econometric
evidence on the program’s effects" (p. 27). This is the first
well-identified estimate of what the program did to whole cities. It
reverses the received "dismal legacy" for city aggregates ("a far less
dismal legacy than is commonly portrayed", p. 4) while leaving the
distributional and neighbourhood verdict open, with the costs to the
displaced explicitly outside its scope.

## Key insight

Which cities could use the program, and how early, was set by when their
state passed the enabling legislation that let local agencies take
private land by eminent domain for private redevelopment. That timing
moved grants and, the authors argue, nothing else that mattered for city
outcomes. Cities with more renewal then did better on income, property
value and size, and the gain did not fade after the money stopped. The
authors read it as "consistent with a model in which local productivity
is enhanced" (abstract).

## Assumptions

- **Exclusion.** Enabling-legislation timing affects 1980 outcomes only
  through renewal. Defended by a falsification test on about 1,500 rural
  counties, added controls and overidentification tests.
- **Grants approved as the measure of renewal.** They correlate 0.99 with
  gross project cost in 1966; nominal sums are taken at face value, and
  grant supply is treated as elastic for eligible projects (fn. 6).
- **Census medians at city level.** No within-city or individual data, no
  land-versus-building split, nothing on the displaced.
- **Rosen-Roback capitalization** for any welfare reading through
  property values, which the authors flag as strong.

## Key results

- **Scale (p. 2).** "more than 2,100 distinct urban renewal projects with
  grants totaling approximately $53 billion (in 2009 dollars)", with new
  funding that "ended in 1974".
- **Displacement (p. 5).** To 30 June 1966 projects "cleared (or intended
  to clear) over 400,000 housing units, forcing the relocation of over
  300,000 families, just over half of whom were nonwhite"; footnote 7 gives
  "Approximately 54 percent".
- **First stage (pp. 14, 16).** "an additional year of eligibility for
  participation is associated with 9.71 additional dollars of grants per
  capita; the standard error is 2.67", 10.32 with controls; the
  first-stage F-statistic "is 13.8".
- **Falsification (p. 16).** In rural counties "the regression results show
  no evidence of a relationship between urban renewal enabling legislation
  and outcomes".
- **Headline (p. 17).** "a $100 per capita difference in grant funding is
  associated with a 2.4 percent difference in 1980 median income and a 6.9
  percent difference in 1980 median property value. The median city in our
  dataset received $122 per capita". Employment and poverty effects have
  the favourable sign and are imprecise (Table 3). "They are not estimates
  of the social returns on investment or local fiscal multipliers."
- **OLS below IV (p. 18).** The authors infer that "urban renewal funding
  was correlated with unobserved negative shocks or trends": declining
  cities took more renewal.
- **Robustness (pp. 18–22).** The estimates survive controls for public
  housing, Model Cities and poverty spending, 1950 bond ratings, state aid,
  the Goldwater vote, a Bartik labour-demand shift and January
  temperature, dropping each state's largest city, recoding the
  instrument, and a second instrument from state supreme court reversals
  (F = 32.2). The instrument's variation lies mostly outside the Northeast
  and Midwest, where estimates are similar but imprecise (p. 22).
- **Concentration (pp. 22–23).** County-level effects are weak, "suggesting
  a limited scope for positive geographic spillovers. Indeed, it is
  possible that economic activity was pulled to central cities at the
  expense of surrounding areas".
- **Timing (p. 23).** "no evidence that urban renewal had affected median
  property values in 1960", but for income and employment "strong evidence
  of a connection as early as 1960"; "the typical project took more than 10
  years to complete".
- **Not the displacement channel (pp. 24–25).** "there is no reduction in
  the black population" (the point estimate is positive) even though
  "black residents were disproportionately displaced from renewal areas
  within cities"; with 1980 composition controlled, "The coefficients on
  urban renewal are slightly larger in the augmented regressions" (Table
  6).
- **Growth (p. 25).** "$100 per capita increase in urban renewal funding is
  associated with a 9 to 11 percent increase in population and housing
  units", and a smaller share of pre-1940 housing.
- **After the money stopped (p. 26).** "the estimated urban renewal effects
  do not dissipate with time"; in 2000 the "IV estimates of β1 are larger
  than those found in 1980". Footnote 40 says this "might reflect the
  operation of agglomeration economies, in which a positive initial effect
  on local productivity reinforces itself with time", and: "Given the large
  standard errors, we hesitate to make strong inferences".
- **Welfare (pp. 27–28).** The authors are cautious given "the literature’s
  emphasis on the disproportionate costs borne by those displaced by the
  program, which would not be capitalized in local property values", and
  "the city-level results do not imply that the costs imposed on relatively
  poor residents and small businesses that were displaced by urban renewal
  were unimportant".

## Concepts

- **Rosen-Roback spatial equilibrium**: renewal as a productive amenity
  capitalized into incomes and property values.
- **Displacement channel against renewal-and-growth channel**: city
  medians could rise because the poor were pushed out, or because the city
  grew; the composition tests favour the second.
- **Clearance as coordination** (p. 7): after Hornbeck and Keniston, "one
  might think of slum clearance and urban renewal as the policy equivalent
  of large fires", removing the option of keeping old buildings and the
  holdout problem.
- **Multiple equilibria in upkeep** (p. 7): in Schall's model, from a
  low-quality equilibrium "targeted improvements have short-lived effects;
  however, the possibility of multiple equilibria in the model allows for a
  sustained, positive urban renewal effect."
- **The proponents' "virtuous circle"** (p. 6) and agglomeration
  self-reinforcement (fn. 40), offered and not established.
- **Learning by doing in the agencies** (p. 11): delays mattered partly
  because "there was learning-by-doing in formulating project proposals".

## Connections

It builds on Rosen and Roback for the model, on Hornbeck and Keniston for
rebuilding after fires, on Schall for multiple equilibria in upkeep, and
on the historical critics, Jacobs, Anderson, Gans and others (p. 3),
whom it does not test. LaVoice (2024), [NOTE-1680](NOTE-1680.md), read alongside as a
complement, follows the same program into the neighbourhoods cleared and
uses the same variation in state legislation to choose control cities.
In this batch, Kline and Moretti (2014) on the Tennessee Valley Authority
is the other big, time-limited, place-based programme with persistence
after it ended; Neumark and Simpson (2015) survey place-based policy;
Michaels et al. (2021) contrast greenfield sites and services with slum
upgrading in Tanzania. Juhász's blockade study, [THEORY-919](../theory.d/THEORY-919.md), has the same
shape: a temporary, legally timed, regionally varying exposure leaving
persistent local gains, with agglomeration as the offered mechanism and a
modest first stage. Schall's multiple equilibria is the urban analogue of
[LIT-2169](../literature.d/LIT-2169.md) on alternative states in restoration ecology. The Food for
Peace exit studies, [THEORY-1492](../theory.d/THEORY-1492.md) and [THEORY-1496](../theory.d/THEORY-1496.md), are the aid
batch's persistence-after-exit cases; Ostrom et al., [THEORY-1480](../theory.d/THEORY-1480.md),
supply the resident-ownership question this paper cannot reach.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md)** (when transient support is safe to withdraw):
  qualifies. Support that was large, one-off and substitutive, outside
  agencies doing the clearing and building, left a city-level effect that
  persisted through 2000 after funding ended in 1974. But the party that
  persisted is the city as a place, measured in aggregates; the
  population nominally supported was removed from the cleared areas. The
  claim's supported party needs a unit: here place and people come
  apart. Not a defeater, since the party that persisted is not the one
  the condition about doing the work concerns.
- **[CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md)** (ignition, collapse, hysteresis): weak support at
  most. Schall's multiple equilibria, the "virtuous circle" and fn. 40's
  self-reinforcing agglomeration are threshold-and-feedback stories, and
  the effect persisting without the supply fits a process kept going on an
  available flux (private investment, migration). Nothing tests a
  threshold, a dose-response nonlinearity or hysteresis.
- **[CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md)** (support that does the work lowers capacity): not
  addressed for residents, whose capacity is unmeasured. For the city
  agencies it points the other way: "learning-by-doing in formulating
  project proposals" built capacity by making the agency do the work.
- **[CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md)** (supplied upkeep never becomes optional): if renewal is
  read as upkeep, the persistence contradicts it; read as formation, with
  cities drawing on private investment and so not supplied every part from
  outside, the case is outside the claim's scope. The second reading fits.
- **[CLAIM-603](../claims.d/CLAIM-603.md)** (one-way against reciprocal supply): little bearing.
  The federal supply was one-way, with a local share (federal grants of
  "two-thirds (or in some cases three-fourths)" of net cost, p. 4) and
  private co-investment, and left no measured city-level dependence.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md)** (independence from the founding intervention): a case
  answering it at city scale, in part. The income effect is visible by
  1960 and persists to 2000 after the program ended, with clearance as the
  removal of a lock-in that lets investment coordinate.
- **[THEORY-919](../theory.d/THEORY-919.md)**: parallel in design and finding, as above.
- Filed: [THEORY-1470](../theory.d/THEORY-1470.md), the city-level account. No instruction for
  machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **City aggregates.** The design cannot see the displaced, the cleared
  neighbourhoods or social networks; whether renewal destroyed local
  self-organization is not answered by this work.
- **Where the variation is.** Mostly the South and West and late states;
  Northeast and Midwest estimates are imprecise.
- **Relocation, not creation, possibly.** Weak county effects suggest
  activity pulled into central cities from their surroundings.
- **2000 is imprecise.** That the effects grew is not established.
- **Grants as a proxy.** Private co-investment is unmeasured; the "over
  $5-to-$1" private-to-federal ratio is quoted from Slayton and not
  checked here.
- **No welfare accounting**, which the authors disclaim (fn. 5).
- **The working paper, not the AEJ text**, was read.
