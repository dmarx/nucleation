# NOTE-tmpxf5do: Lycan — Representational Theories of Consciousness (SEP)

## Contribution

An encyclopedia entry on theories that explain consciousness by
representation. It separates four things "conscious" can mean and treats
two of them. One is the sensory quality, such as the green of an
after-image. The other is "what it's like" to experience that quality.
It sends awareness of one's states and introspection to the companion
entry on higher-order theories (LIT-tmpx8g8s). The author is a party to
the debate he surveys. He is one of the three named defenders of strong
representationalism, and several of the replies the entry gives are his
own (Lycan 1996, 1998, 2001). The entry argues for some things. It argues
that pure representationalism fails and strong representationalism does
not fail for the same reason, and that quality and "what it's like" are
distinct. On wide against narrow content it ends undecided.

## Key insight

A representational theory of sensory qualities is a theory of what is
experienced, not of what makes it experienced. The quality is "what the
world is or seems like" (§5.1), a represented property of a represented
object. It can be present in a state the subject is wholly unaware of.
Whether there is anything it is like to have it is a further,
higher-order matter that needs awareness. "As always, the representational
theory addresses only sense (3)" (§4.5.1).

## Assumptions

- **Intentionality is representation** (introduction). Each theory
  "attempts to explain its target phenomenon in terms of intentionality,
  and assumes that intentionality is representation".
- **Four senses of "conscious"** (introduction): (1) states whose
  subjects are "directly, internally aware of them"; (2) introspection;
  (3) having a sensory state with "a distinctive qualitative property";
  (4) "what it's like" for the subject. The entry uses "qualitative" for
  (3) and "phenomenal" for (4), and notes that Block (1995) runs (3) and
  (4) together, and that Dretske and Tye use "what it's like" for (3).
- **Materialism is the usual motive** (§1, §2.4, §3.1), though
  representationalism "need not be reductive" (§2.4).
- **Nonactual objects are "everyone's problem"** (§4.1). The theory says
  only "that when you hallucinate a lemon, the yellowness you experience
  is that of the lemon", and is neutral on the metaphysics of
  nonexistence.

## Key results

- **The problem (§1).** Bertie sees a green after-image. There is no
  physical green thing outside his head and none inside it, so the green
  thing is not physical: "a valid deductive argument against materialism,
  and its premises are hard to deny".
- **The representationalist resolution (§1).** "sensory qualities are
  actually intentional contents, represented properties of represented
  objects". The after-image is an illusion of a green blob in the room.
  The "there is" in "there is a green thing that Bertie is experiencing"
  is the lenient one of "There is at least one mythical god that the
  Greeks worshipped", so no actual green entity follows. A consequence
  the entry calls "slightly surprising but harmless": qualities "are not
  themselves properties of the experiences that present them". The
  experiences have the property of representing them. Most hold the
  representation to be nonconceptual. Dretske and Tye supply
  teleologized indicator semantics.
- **Representation is not enough (§2).** Saying "red", semaphoring it or
  pointing to a chip represents redness without anything looking red. The
  representation must be "specifically a visual representation".
  Dretske's further factor is that it is sensory and "systemic". Tye's
  is that it is nonconceptual and "poised". Lycan's is functional role.
- **Grades (§2.1).** Pure representationalism, that representation alone
  suffices, is held by no one, "representation alone is cheap and
  ubiquitous". Strong representationalism (Dretske, Tye, Lycan):
  "representation of a certain kind suffices for a sensory quality",
  with the kind given in functionalist or other materialist terms.
  Block's "quasi-representationism" counts as strong. Weak
  representationalism, that qualitative states have representational
  content, is "fairly uncontroversial". It is now denied by Campbell,
  Travis, Noë, Brewer and Fish. Strong representationalism is "all the
  less defensible" for pains and itches. Dretske and Tye hold that
  qualities exhaust phenomenal character; Lycan (1998) denies it.
- **Wide and narrow (§2.2).** If qualities are represented environmental
  properties, they are wide, "and molecularly identical subjects could
  experience different qualities". Dretske and Lycan defend this
  "phenomenal externalism", which the entry says should be called
  "qualitative" externalism. Shoemaker, Horgan, Kriegel, Levine and
  Chalmers are narrow representationalists.
- **Contents and reduction (§§2.3–2.4).** Shoemaker takes a colour
  experience to represent a disposition to cause that type of experience.
  Chalmers argues that perceptual content is Fregean, which suits a narrow
  account. Chalmers's nonreductive representationalism identifies
  qualities with contents that "cannot be characterized without reference
  to sensory qualities".
- **Five arguments for (§3).**
  - Materialism (§3.1): the alternatives are sense-data, property dualism,
    type-identity (an error theory about greenness), adverbialism
    ("severely and tellingly criticized") and eliminativism. Dretske
    (1996) holds that nothing intrinsic to the brain distinguishes a red
    quality from a green one.
  - Veridicality (§3.2): the experience's veridicality condition "seems to
    exhaust not only its representational content but its qualitative
    content".
  - Transparency (§3.3), after Harman (1990): "Look at a tree and try to
    turn your attention to intrinsic features of your visual experience."
    This shows the weak thesis only. The strong one needs two premises
    more: that further mental properties would be introspectible, and
    that none is introspected. The counterexamples include mental paint,
    blur, moods, the sense modality and "what it's like" itself, and they
    "take a lot of overcoming". Representationalism as defined does not
    need transparency.
  - Seeming (§3.4), after Byrne (2001): no change in qualitative
    character without a change in how the world seems. The entry says the
    argument presupposes that Block's two senses of "looks" do not come
    apart.
  - Hallucination (§3.5), after Pautz: a relational view, then
    representationalism as the surviving relational view.
- **Awareness of qualities is not the theory's problem (§3.6).** The
  options are higher-order representation, self-representation,
  attention, acquaintance, or replication of content. "the problems of
  awareness of content are already with us, and do not afflict the
  representational theory of sensory qualities in particular".
- **The supervenience thesis (§4).** The theory entails "that there can be
  no difference in sensory qualities without a representational
  difference". Most objections are counterexamples to it.
- **Unconscious representation (§4.2).** Sturgeon, Kriegel and Chalmers:
  representation cannot suffice for a quality "because representation can
  occur unconsciously". The reply turns on the senses of "conscious". In
  senses (1) and (2) most representationalists already hold "that a
  sensory quality can occur without being noticed by its host": the
  driver "on autopilot" who saw the red light and its redness but was
  unaware of it. In sense (3) the assumption is tautologous and the
  objection begs the question. In sense (4) "so far as has been shown, a
  (first-order) quality can occur without there being anything it is like
  for the subject to experience that quality on that occasion". A state
  contains a quality "in virtue of representing that quality in a
  distinctive way": the functional facts that fix the modality, and,
  where experience implies awareness, "whatever is called for by one's
  account of awareness-of".
- **Phenomenal intentionality (§4.3).** Horgan and Tienson: an internal,
  narrow intentionality constituted by phenomenology, "the fundamental
  kind of intentionality: the narrow, phenomenal kind that is a
  prerequisite for wide content and wide truth conditions". It makes
  "what it's like" prior to intentionality, contrary to the spirit of
  representationalism, and "certainly it poses a general threat of
  circularity". Against it: unconscious intentional states. Kriegel
  (2011) replies that their intentionality is not "intrinsic" but
  "interpretivist," merely charitable interpretation à la Davidson and
  Dennett. Mendelovici and Bourget (2014) argue that naturalistic
  considerations favour it. It blocks any direct argument from
  transparency, which Kriegel uses for phenomenal intentionality.
- **Laws of appearance (§4.4).** Pautz (2017): nothing can appear both
  round and square, and these impossibilities have no parallel in belief.
  Speaks (2017) doubts that they are metaphysical.
- **Counterexamples (§4.5).**
  - Moods and sensations (§4.5.1): Rey's elation and anxiety "seem to be
    just overall states of oneself". Replies: Brentano's thesis that every
    mental state is intentional (Lycan, Tye); or, against transparency,
    Lycan's (1998) split of a pain into its sensory quality and its
    affective aspect. "The quality is what remains under morphine; what
    morphine blocks is the affective aspect". The affective part can then
    be treated as functional.
  - Same content, different quality (§4.5.2): Peacocke's trees, the
    Necker cube, the duck-rabbit, Nickel's squares seen "as prominent",
    attention raising apparent contrast (Block 2010, Carrasco), blur. In
    each, representationalists look for a difference in what is
    represented. Macpherson rebuts the replies, and Green (2016) defends
    them.
  - Inversions (§4.5.3). Shoemaker's inversion is only conceivable. In
    Levine's fish-heads the two eyes differ functionally, so a
    psychosemantics may give them different representata. On Block's
    Inverted Earth there are three replies: visual contents would not
    shift; qualities are a special narrow content (Rey, Chalmers's Fregean
    content); or qualities are wide, so molecular duplicates can differ.
    Dretske, Tye and Lycan take the third. The entry offers "two
    arguments, though not very strong arguments, for the claim that the
    qualities are wide". Biggs's Scrambler: qualities cut off
    functionally and causally from the environment represent nothing.
- **Against wide representationalism (§4.6).** The arguments are
  introspection, narrow content, modelling a shift, modes of presentation,
  memory and structural mismatch, each with a reply.
  - Memory (Block 1996): long-term memory "gives you good reason to think
    that the phenomenal character of the experience has not changed".
    The reply, after Lycan (1996) and Tye (1998): memory contents are
    wide too, so after the shift "your post-shift memories of good old
    Earth are false" and "memory is not after all a reliable check on the
    sensory qualities".
  - Structural mismatch (Pautz, after Hardin): blue resembles purple more
    than green, while the reflectances go the other way. So qualities
    match neural substrates, not representata.
  - The verdict: "Hardly anyone will accept all of the foregoing replies.
    But no one should now find it uncontestable either that sensory
    qualities are narrow or that they are wide."
- **"What it's like" is distinct (§5, §5.1).** The higher-order sense is
  "the property of what it is like to experience that quality". It cannot
  be identical with the quality, because it is "itself a property of the
  quality". Two reasons are given. First, the quality is describable
  ("it looks green"), and what it is like to experience it "seems to be
  ineffable". Second, qualities can occur unnoticed, and then "it would
  not be like anything for the subject to experience that quality". So
  the property "requires awareness and so is something distinct from the
  quality itself". Carruthers (2000) is credited with the distinction.
- **Anti-materialist arguments (§5.2).** The knowledge, conceivability and
  explanatory-gap arguments concern sense (4). The "perspectivalist"
  response is that Mary acquires "a new, introspective or first-person way
  of representing" a fact she already knew. It requires that the
  first-order state itself be represented, and so "encourages a
  representational theory of higher-order conscious awareness". Phenomenal
  concepts come in many accounts (recognitional, indexical, demonstrative,
  quotational or constitutive, unimodal). Chalmers's "Master Argument" is
  against all of them.
- **Illusionism (§5.3).** To deny WIL properties, with Dennett and Dretske,
  "is of course not to defend a representational theory or any other
  theory of them". Some explain belief in them as misrepresentation in
  introspection, which Frankish (2016) calls illusionism. On this view a
  sensory quality may be mistaken for a WIL property. Rey: introspection
  "mistakes the lack of detail it delivers for the accurate
  representation of a simple ineffable property". Kammerer (2018),
  sympathetic, argues that "no previously existing account can explain
  the strength of the illusion".

## Concepts

- **Sensory quality** (the strict, "first-order" sense of "quale", after
  C. I. Lewis and Goodman): "a qualitative property inhering in a sensory
  state", introspectible, apparently monadic, describable in words like
  "green" (§1).
- **Pure, strong, weak representationalism** (§2.1): representation alone
  suffices; representation of a certain kind suffices; qualitative states
  have representational content.
- **Wide and narrow** (§2.2): whether qualities supervene on what is in the
  head.
- **Transparency thesis** (§3.3): "not even the most determined
  introspection ever reveals any such additional properties", that is,
  properties beyond the represented ones.
- **Mental paint** (Harman, Block; §3.3): introspectible intrinsic features
  of a representation in virtue of which it represents what it does.
- **WIL property** (§5): "what it's like" in the higher-order sense, a
  property of a quality or of the experience that has it.
- **Phenomenal intentionality** (§4.3): intentionality constituted by
  phenomenology and prior to wide content.
- **Perspectivalist response** (§5.2): Mary learns an old fact under a new,
  first-person mode of presentation. "Perspective" here is a way of
  representing a fact, not a standpoint.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpdga80: the exchange's description needs correcting.** The
  exchange took strong representationalism as "the standing position
  nearest the constitutive thesis". On this entry that is true only for
  the character of experience, not for its occurrence.
  - **What is near.** Strong representationalism is an identity, not a
    production claim: sensory qualities "just are representational contents
    of a certain kind, but not that this is analytically or conceptually
    true" (§4.5.3, the representationalist's claim as stated). It also needs
    a further organizational condition beyond representation (§2), as the
    claim needs "appropriately organized".
  - **What is not.** The theory "addresses only sense (3)", and it holds
    that a quality can occur with nothing it is like to have it (§4.2,
    §5.1). What makes a state experienced is handed to "whatever is called
    for by one's account of awareness-of" (§4.2), that is, to the
    higher-order entry. The claim says that organization constitutes the
    experience itself, occurrence included. So representationalism is not
    a version of the claim. It is the claim's part about content, with the
    occurrence question split off.
  - **Wide content cuts against the claim.** The defenders the exchange
    named (Dretske, Tye, Lycan) are wide representationalists. On their
    view molecular duplicates can differ in quality. The claim, as it
    reads THEORY-095, needs its interpretation to be internal. The
    nearer relatives are the narrow representationalists (Shoemaker,
    Kriegel, Levine, Chalmers's Fregean content), and of these the
    nonreductive ones define content by reference to the qualities, which
    the claim's nonphenomenal definition forbids.
  - **The phenomenal-intentionality programme** runs the other way, as the
    exchange said: phenomenology constitutes the fundamental kind of
    intentionality (§4.3). The entry adds that its proponents must treat
    the intentionality of unconscious states as derived or "interpretivist".
- **CLAIM-tmp1lvwf: supports it, from the other side of the
  literature.** The objection that "representation can occur
  unconsciously" (§4.2) is this claim's objection, put to
  representationalism. The entry's answer is the claim's own diagnosis:
  representation is "cheap and ubiquitous" (§2.1), so the theory must
  name a further condition. For the quality, that condition is sensory
  and functional. For its being experienced, it is an account of
  awareness the entry does not give. So representationalism has the same
  open sufficiency condition the claim names, and it moves the condition
  to a different theory.
- **QUESTION-tmpc4j7p.** Two points.
  - **The autopilot driver.** On the entry's view this is a case of
    sensory quality without experience. Any answer to the question must
    classify the case, and the entry takes the side on which it is not
    experienced.
  - **The further factors** that make a representation sensory
    ("systemic", "poised", functional role; §2) are candidates for the
    question's processing-against-perspective step only if they fix
    occurrence. The entry uses them to fix which quality is represented,
    not whether it is experienced.
- **TERM-tmpgqmop.** "Interpretation" in this entry is the "interpretivist"
  intentionality of Davidson and Dennett (§4.3): content ascribed by a
  charitable interpreter. That is the sense the TERM excludes, an
  "association supplied by an outside observer". The entry's own process
  word is "representation", for environmental properties, which is narrower
  than the TERM's determination of significance within an organization.
- **TERM-tmpe36gg.** The entry's only "perspective" is the
  perspectivalist's first-person mode of presentation (§5.2), a way of
  representing a fact. It is nearest the TERM's sense 2 (represented), and
  the entry says it "encourages a representational theory of higher-order
  conscious awareness". It is none of the three senses exactly, so a claim
  that cites this literature for "perspective" should say which it means.
- **THEORY-095 (Inverted Earth).** The entry gives the representationalist
  side the THEORY's `promote_when` names and the record did not hold.
  - **Lycan's reply** is (iii): qualities are wide, so the traveller's
    qualities shift with content. On memory, his reply is the error theory
    the THEORY's refutation clause describes: post-shift memories "are
    false" (§4.6).
  - **It does not meet the refutation clause.** The clause asks for a
    reason "not drawn from representationism itself", and this reason is
    drawn from it: memory contents are wide by the same psychosemantics.
    Nor does it promote the THEORY. It denies phenomenal sameness rather
    than accepting the shift and finding sameness.
  - **The entry's verdict** is that neither narrow nor wide is now
    "uncontestable" (§4.6). The THEORY stays Proposed.
  - **Its other leads.** Stalnaker's (1996) version of the argument does not
    assume narrowness, and Pautz's structural mismatch argues for
    narrowness on independent grounds. Neither is held.
- **THEORY-633 (illusionism).** §5.3 states illusionism as the denial
  of WIL properties (sense 4), keeping sensory qualities as represented
  properties. That matches the THEORY's "quasi-phenomenal properties" if
  these are the qualities. The entry reports Kammerer's (2018) verdict
  that no existing account explains "the strength of the illusion". That
  is the gap the THEORY's `promote_when` asks a worked theory to fill. It
  names critics (Strawson, Prinz, Balog, Nida-Rümelin, Schwitzgebel,
  Chalmers 2018) and none is reproduced.
- **THEORY-103 and THEORY-091.** Block's overflow is experience
  beyond access. The entry's unnoticed quality is a quality short of
  experience. The two disagree over the autopilot case, which Block
  (1995, LIT-643) uses for phenomenality without access. The entry
  notes that Block "fails to distinguish" senses (3) and (4) (§4.5.3,
  §5.1).
- **QUESTION-tmpzje42 and THEORY-063.** Bourget (2017a) argues that only
  individuating the senses by content explains multimodal experiences
  "which unify distinct sub-experiences occurring in different
  modalities" (§3.3). That is a third candidate for unity beside the
  question's two: unity as the content of one represented scene. The
  entry gives it one sentence.
- **Filed from this reading:** THEORY-tmptw1fe, that representing a
  sensory quality does not make it experienced.

## Limitations

- A survey by a protagonist. Lycan's own replies (wide qualities, the
  memory reply, the pain split, the layered "shapes") are given more room
  than the opposition's, and the entry says of two of its arguments for
  wideness that they are "not very strong".
- Every argument reported is summarized, and none of the cited works was
  read here.
- It treats representationalism as a theory of sensory qualities only. It
  says almost nothing about affect beyond pain and mood (§4.5.1), and
  nothing about the neural or empirical literature.
- The 2023 revision leaves its newest material thin: Kriegel 2017 and
  Bourget 2017 on affect and vividness, Pautz 2017 and 2019, Shabasson
  2022. It does not report the later phenomenal-intentionality literature
  in detail.
- Cross-reference slips, noted under Connections.

## Open questions

- What is the account of awareness that turns an unnoticed quality into one
  there is something it is like to have? The entry defers it to the
  higher-order literature (LIT-tmpx8g8s).
- Is the absent-minded driver's perception unexperienced, as this entry
  holds, or experienced and forgotten or unattended, as Block would hold?
  No evidence is given either way, and QUESTION-048's difficulty applies.
- Does Pautz's structural mismatch (§4.6) favour narrow qualities over
  wide ones, as he argues, or only show illusion about resemblance, as the
  reply says? Reading Pautz (2014, 2019) would test it.
