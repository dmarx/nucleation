# NOTE-2038: Wenar — Rights and what we owe to each other

## Contribution

A conceptual challenge to contractualism from rights theory: the locating
problem (which valid principles ascribe rights, and to whom), and the
argument that a theory of owing is one-level, so that authority to change
obligations needs a second level.

## Key results

- **Two orders** (§1, p. 376): "First-order rights define requirements on
  conduct; second-order rights assign authority to change requirements on
  conduct."
- **The question** (§2, p. 377): "how could we tell which of these norms
  ascribe what rights?"
- **Passive only** (§2, pp. 382–83): "On Scanlon's analysis all rights limit
  the discretion of others. No rights entitle the rightholder to act
  herself." "it cannot capture the many rights in the upper-left hand
  quadrant: the active rights of authority." But "the inadequacy of
  Scanlon's characterizations of rights is entirely harmless for his
  substantive work on basic rights" (p. 384).
- **Wrongness, not wronging** (§3, p. 385): "All of Scanlon's fleshed-out
  examples of contractualist principles are principles of wrongness, not
  of wronging."
- **The right rightholder** (§5, p. 392): "Within the justification of many
  principles, the party whose interests or complaints are weightiest is not
  the party we believe to be the rightholder." "It is quite hard for
  Scanlon to say exactly what we owe to whom" (p. 396).
- **Authority** (§6, pp. 398–99): "Contractualism cannot plausibly be only a
  "first-order" theory that explains the duties and obligations that exist
  at one moment"; otherwise it re-theorizes "whenever changes to these
  duties and obligations are helicoptered in from a different domain".
  "Rights of authority explain who can change what we owe to each other and
  how." ""What we owe to each other" is a one-level concept; contractualism
  must be a multi-level theory." "It must be a theory not only of owing to,
  but also a theory of authority over."

## Assumptions

Hohfeld's correlativity of directed duty and claim-right, "absolutely
standard in rights theory" (p. 387); Hart's primary and secondary rules;
the full range of ordinary rights-assertions, trivial ones included, as the
data an analysis must fit.

## Concepts

First- and second-order rights; active and passive rights; rights of
authority; the constraint analysis; directed requirements; wrongness and
wronging; correlativity; the right-rightholder problem; one-level and
multi-level theory.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-483](../questions.d/QUESTION-483.md).** A published form of the question's second option:
  contractualism needs a second level, a theory of authority over
  obligations, or it takes changes to them from elsewhere. Wenar does not
  show that reasonable rejection cannot supply that level; Scanlon's reply
  ([LIT-2746](../literature.d/LIT-2746.md)) says it can, for promises.
- **[CLAIM-735](../claims.d/CLAIM-735.md).** Wenar separates justifying what is owed from the
  authority to change it ("who can change what we owe to each other and
  how"), which the claim needs. He does not take the claim's further step,
  that justified powers become the structure within which later
  justification occurs.
- The exchange's A102 is faithful ("multilevel" is Wenar's "multi-level")
  and omits the paper's other main thesis, the locating problem. The
  identification of the paper, inferred on registration, is confirmed:
  Scanlon's reply answers it by name.

## Limitations

The theory of directionality is promised, not given (n. 46). §5's reason
why Scanlon omits rights is marked a guess. The paper concerns where
rights sit in the theory, not which principles are valid.
