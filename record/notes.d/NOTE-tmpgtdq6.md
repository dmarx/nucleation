# NOTE-tmpgtdq6: A Mathematical Theory of Saving

## Contribution

It solves the problem of optimal saving for a whole community over
infinite time, by the calculus of variations, and states the solution as
one rule. It shows how far the answer depends on the production function
and the interest rate (hardly at all), and how it changes when utility is
discounted. It then uses the same equations to ask what fixes the rate of
interest. It is the founding text of optimal-growth theory and the
reference for refusing to discount the future for being later.

## Key insight

If the community can approach a finite maximum of enjoyment, Bliss, then
any plan that never approaches it loses an infinite amount of enjoyment
over endless time. So the problem becomes: minimize the total shortfall
from Bliss. Changing the variable of integration from time to capital
reduces it to minimizing an integrand at each level of capital. The rule
follows: "rate of saving multiplied by marginal utility of consumption
should always equal bliss minus actual rate of utility enjoyed" (p. 547).
Keynes's version: saving £1 less this year postpones the whole approach to
Bliss by 1/z of a year, at a cost of that fraction of a year's shortfall
(pp. 547–548).

## Assumptions

- **No discounting of utility in Section I.** "It is assumed that we do not
  discount later enjoyments in comparison with earlier ones, a practice
  which is ethically indefensible and arises merely from the weakness of
  the imagination" (p. 543). Discounting is reintroduced in II(c) and III.
- **One community for ever.** It does not change "either in numbers or in
  its capacity for enjoyment or in its aversion to labour" (p. 543), and
  enjoyments at different times are independent and additive.
- **The same motives in every generation**, "so that there is no chance of
  our savings being selfishly consumed by a subsequent generation; and
  that no misfortunes will occur to sweep away accumulations" (p. 544).
- **No distribution.** Total satisfaction is a function of total
  consumption and labour only (p. 543).
- **A finite Bliss.** "Economic causes alone could never give us more than
  a certain finite rate of enjoyment" (p. 545). Without it the integral
  need not converge.
- **Homogeneous goods and labour; no technical progress** except what
  accumulation itself brings (p. 543).

## Key results

- **The equations** (pp. 544–546). Income f(a, c) from labour a and capital
  c; dc/dt + x = f(a, c) (1). Marginal disutility of labour equals its
  marginal product times the marginal utility of consumption,
  v(a) = (∂f/∂a)u(x) (2). Marginal utility falls at the rate of interest,
  d/dt u(x) = −(∂f/∂c)·u(x) (3).
- **The rule** (pp. 546–547). Integration gives
  dc/dt = {K − (U(x) − V(a))}/u(x) (4), and minimizing the shortfall from
  Bliss identifies K with B (5).
- **Independence** (p. 548). The rule "is altogether independent of the
  production function f(a, c), except in so far as this determines bliss".
  How much to save out of a given income is independent of the interest
  rate, "unless this is actually zero".
- **How much** (pp. 548–549). An illustrative scale (not data) gives total
  utility 2 to 8 for family incomes £150 to £5,000, with 8 as Bliss.
  Fitting a parabola gives marginal utility 1/60 at £200. A family on £500
  should then save about £300, since 300 × 1/60 = 8 − 3. Ramsey calls this
  "greatly in excess of that which anyone would normally suggest".
- **What was neglected** (p. 549).
  - Population growth, and inventions that raise Bliss, are reasons to save
    more. Inventions that make income cheaper are a reason to save less.
  - "The most serious factor neglected is the possibility of future wars
    and earthquakes destroying our accumulations." These cannot be handled
    by a low rate of interest, since they destroy principal as well.
- **Constant returns** (II, pp. 549–551). With f = pa + rc, a tangent
  construction on the curve of utility of unearned income, W(y), tells how
  much unearned income to consume (Fig. 1, eq. 6). Marginal utility then
  falls as w(y) = Ae^(−rt) (7).
- **A finite life** (II(b), pp. 551–553). An individual with T years to live
  and a bequest c₃ to leave saves less than an everlasting community. Over
  a long life he saves first and then dissaves: "to begin with there is
  saving, and subsequently splashing".
- **Discounting** (II(c), pp. 553–555).
  - Utility discounting is distinct from money discounting. Anyone who can
    borrow at r must value £1 now as £(1 + r) in a year, whatever his
    discount for utility.
  - At "about ¾ per cent", utility now is worth twice that in a hundred
    years and four times that in two hundred.
  - A constant rate "is the only assumption we can make, without
    contradicting our fundamental hypothesis that successive generations
    are actuated by the same system of preferences". With a higher rate for
    the first fifty years, our ranking of 2000 against 2050 would differ
    from that of the people alive in 2000.
  - For ρ < r, d/dt u(x) = −u(x)(r − ρ) (9). The solution is as before with
    a "modified utility", the integral of w^(r/(r−ρ)). This translates
    "discounting of the future into a discounting of high incomes", at a
    rate set by ρ/r alone (p. 554).
  - Special case w(y) = Dy^(−α), α > 1: a constant share of income is
    saved, printed as (r − ρ)/(r(α − 1) + ρ), or 1/(α − 1) if ρ = 0
    (p. 555).
  - For ρ > r, marginal utility rises at ρ − r. Consumption falls toward
    subsistence, capital is exhausted and debt taken on to the limit of
    credit (p. 555).
- **The rate of interest** (III, pp. 555–559).
  - (α) If all discount at ρ, equilibrium needs ∂f/∂c = ρ. Out of
    equilibrium, which may last centuries, the rate is fixed by the demand
    for the existing capital stock and "may greatly exceed the reward
    ultimately necessary to induce abstinence" (p. 556). In a socialist
    state's accounts the rate of interest would allocate existing capital,
    not guide how much to save (p. 557).
  - (β) People "are not so interested in their heirs as in themselves"
    (p. 557). If they ignore heirs, live off annuities and differ in ρ,
    capital has a supply price, that of the "marginal saver". Those beyond
    the margin supply negatively, borrowing when young (p. 558).
  - (γ) Families that live for ever and differ in ρ: in equilibrium those
    with ρ < r are at Bliss and the rest at subsistence. Society divides
    into "the thrifty enjoying bliss and the improvident at the
    subsistence level" (p. 559).

## Concepts

- **Bliss (B)**: the maximum obtainable rate of net enjoyment, reached at a
  finite capital or approached as capital grows without limit; to be
  distinguished from the maximum *conceivable* rate (pp. 544–545).
- **rate of discount for utility versus for money**: the first a
  preference over when enjoyment comes; the second fixed at the interest
  rate for anyone who can borrow and lend (p. 553).
- **modified utility**: the integral of marginal utility raised to the
  power r/(r − ρ), which turns discounting of the future into discounting
  of high incomes (p. 554).
- **earned and unearned income**: pa and rc under constant returns (p. 550).
- **splashing**: dissaving late in a finite life (p. 553).
- **marginal saver**: the person whose discount rate equals the interest
  rate, who neither saves nor borrows except for old age (p. 558).

## Connections

- **Greaves and MacAskill ([NOTE-tmpozxwf](NOTE-tmpozxwf.md)).** They name Ramsey among those
  who endorse a zero rate of pure time preference, the assumption their
  case calls "near-essential". That is right of p. 543. It leaves out that
  Ramsey gives no argument there, and that he models discounting in the
  rest of the paper.
- **Meyer, SEP ([NOTE-tmps9poq](NOTE-tmps9poq.md)).** Its description, optimal savings without
  distributional concerns and no pure time discounting, matches p. 543.
- **Thorstad ([NOTE-tmpu4i35](NOTE-tmpu4i35.md)) and Tarsney ([NOTE-tmp0l9dk](NOTE-tmp0l9dk.md)).** Both discount the
  future for risk, not for time. Ramsey draws the same line in 1928: he
  rejects discounting for time, assumes no misfortunes, and names wars and
  earthquakes as "the most serious factor neglected", which a low interest
  rate cannot capture (pp. 544, 549).
- **Gardiner ([LIT-tmp12vk2](../literature.d/LIT-tmp12vk2.md)), unread.** The intergenerational storm is
  assumed away on p. 544: no chance "of our savings being selfishly
  consumed by a subsequent generation".
- **Hershfield ([NOTE-tmp70rn1](NOTE-tmp70rn1.md)).** Ramsey's diagnosis of discounting, "the
  weakness of the imagination", is a psychological claim. Hershfield's
  finding, that people who feel less connected to their future self, or
  see it less vividly, discount more, is a modern version for one life.
- **Narveson ([LIT-tmpoc3e4](../literature.d/LIT-tmpoc3e4.md)), unread.** Population is fixed by assumption.
  Ramsey's one remark on it, that growth is a reason to save more (p. 549),
  counts more future people as more to provide for, but says nothing about
  whether to create them.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-tmpmx9ha](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.md).** The question files Ramsey under the impersonal
  ground. Read, he fits it less neatly. These are inferences.
  - His unit is not future strangers but one community that "goes on for
    ever" with the same motives, and he writes its saving in the first
    person plural: "we shall be in 1 + 1/z year's time exactly where we
    should have been" (p. 547). The zero discount is stated as the
    prudence of an everlasting "we", not as a duty to others.
  - That "we" holds together only by assumption. Section II(c) needs
    successive generations "actuated by the same system of preferences"
    (p. 553), and p. 544 needs no generation to consume selfishly what
    earlier ones saved.
  - His reason against discounting is a diagnosis, not a principle: a
    failure of imagination. That is a claim the question's psychological
    side can test. The record's lay-judgement result, that most people
    judge extinction uniquely bad only when the lost future is made
    salient (Schubert et al., [NOTE-tmpbxw6b](NOTE-tmpbxw6b.md)), fits it.
  - When he describes how people do save (§III), they discount "for
    himself or his heirs" (p. 556) and are "not so interested in their
    heirs as in themselves" (p. 557). Concern falls off with distance from
    the self.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** The founding statement of the
  zero-discount view is written in the thesis's shape: a collective self
  extended through time, saving for itself. This is a modelling choice,
  not a claim about motive, and Ramsey offers it as what ought to be, not
  what is. His description of actual savers puts concern on a gradient,
  self first, heirs after, which is the scale reading the claim's note on
  Hershfield already gives. A dated note is proposed for the claim:

  > Ramsey (NOTE-tmpgtdq6), the source of the refusal to discount the future,
  > states it as the saving of one community that "goes on for ever" with
  > unchanged motives, in the first person plural. Its case for the far
  > future is the prudence of a collective self extended in time, held
  > together by the assumption that every generation shares one system of
  > preferences. His actual savers are "not so interested in their heirs as in
  > themselves", and he blames discounting on "the weakness of the
  > imagination". Both fit the thesis's reading of concern as scaled by
  > the self's reach, but neither is evidence about motive.

- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** Not addressed. Ramsey's
  "same system of preferences" is a criterion of a community's identity
  over time, but it is a sameness of what is cared about, so it is not
  the independent criterion the objection asks for.

## Limitations

- **The refusal to discount is asserted**, in one clause, and the paper
  does not defend it.
- **Strong assumptions.** Fixed population, unchanging tastes and motives
  for ever, no catastrophes, no distribution, additive utility, a finite
  Bliss. Ramsey names several of these as the main things neglected
  (p. 549).
- **The saving figure is illustrative.** The utility scale on p. 548 is
  invented, "put forward merely as an illustration".
- **One community or individual at a time** until §III; nothing on
  conflict between generations except by assumption.
- **Typos and slips in the source.**
  - p. 546: "Δx( + ∂f/∂c Δt)" drops the 1, for Δx(1 + ∂f/∂c Δt).
  - p. 548: "bliss minus actual rate of utility = 8 — = 5" drops the 3.
  - p. 551, eq. (8): "(Ux − V(a))" for (U(x) − V(a)).
  - p. 555: the denominator of the savings formula carries a stray "dy".
  - p. 555: on this reading's arithmetic, savings = y/(β − 1) makes
    (r − ρ)/(r(α − 1) + ρ) the ratio of saving to the unearned income
    *consumed*, not to unearned income as printed; as a share of unearned
    income it would be (r − ρ)/(rα). Checked once, not against later
    literature.
  - "9(a)" (p. 555) and "(9a)" (p. 554) for the same equation.

## Open questions

- Did Ramsey argue for the refusal to discount anywhere else? The record
  holds none of his other work.
- Is "the weakness of the imagination" right? A study that made the far
  future vivid and measured the implied rate of pure time preference,
  apart from risk, would test it.
