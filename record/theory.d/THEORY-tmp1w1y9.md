# THEORY-tmp1w1y9: Luper argues that Epicurus's case that death is nothing to us needs intrinsic hedonism and a narrow view of extrinsic harm, that without them death can harm by depriving one of goods, and reports Pitcher's preferentialist view that posthumous events harm the living person by thwarting desires held while alive, a view the entry shows contested

## The account

Whether death and posthumous events can harm us turns on what welfare
is. Epicurus's argument, reconstructed in nine steps, needs intrinsic
hedonism and extrinsic instrumentalism; preferentialism and Nagel's
point that things can be bad by precluding goods defeat it, since
"desire fulfillment need have not experiential upshot" (§3.1). On
Pitcher's priorism, "a desire that you have now may be thwarted by your
death", so that the death "may harm you retroactively" (§4.2).
Posthumous events "harm us by being responsible for truths that thwart
our desires": slander after my death harms me at the times I desired my
reputation untarnished (§6.2).

## Evidence

- A survey that sets out each position's premises and takes sides; it
  argues rather than reports data.
- Against Pitcher the entry reports Velleman, for whom well-being is "a
  fact intrinsic to the present", and those who hold that a desire one
  no longer has cannot be thwarted; Parfit replies that some desires are
  not conditional on their own persistence (§6).
- The conditions on desires whose fulfilment counts are called
  "controversial" and set aside (§3.1).

## What it bears on

QUESTION-301 lists Pitcher's interests that outlast death as one of its
three readings, and its note of 2026-10-29 says: "Pitcher's reading
depends on the theory of welfare (Luper, SEP, NOTE-1294). Posthumous
