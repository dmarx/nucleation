# LIT-tmpmb8cw: What Psychological States are Not

N. J. Block and J. A. Fodor (1972), *The Philosophical Review* 81(2),
April 1972, pp. 159–181 — DOI-10.2307/2183991.

The brief's citation (Phil Rev 81, 1972) is correct; Crossref and the scan
agree on volume, issue, month and first page, and the scan ends on p. 181.

## Key takeaways

- **Against behaviourism and physicalism (§I).** What an organism does is
  "a very complicated function of its beliefs and desires", so
  "mind/behavior correlationism is not true"; and Lashleyan equipotentiality,
  convergent evolution and the possibility of psychologically similar
  machines make type physicalism "empirically false" (pp. 160–161). Both
  theories set conditions that are "insufficiently abstract". A disjunctive
  (many-to-one) version is no help: the disjunctions need not be distinct,
  need not be lawlike, and a theory pairing each psychological predicate with
  a disjunction contradicts the fact that one behaviour can express
  different states at different times (pp. 162–164).
- **FSIT (§II)** says each psychological state of an organism is identical
  with one machine-table state of its *unique best description* as a
  probabilistic automaton. It is more than the trivial claim that everything
  has some automaton description: Putnam's "null" Turing machine fits
  anything (p. 166).
- **Six arguments against FSIT (§III).** (1) Some dispositions ("believes
  that P") are never occurrent, but every machine-table state is a possible
  occurrent state. (2) A single automaton is in one state at a time, but
  organisms are in several, so the model must be "a set of intercommunicating
  automata operating in parallel". (3) Inverted or absent qualia. (4)
  Machine-table identity is too fine-grained: if your toe-stubbing pain issues
  in "damn" and mine in "darn", our pains are type-distinct, and the point
  "iterates in an embarrassing way" through successor states (pp. 174–175).
  (5) Machine tables are lists, but a person's psychological states are
  productive ("the belief that S", for infinitely many S). (6) Lists cannot
  represent the structural relations between believing P and believing P & Q.
- **What is left (§IV).** Type identity in any science is fixed by "the
  character of the laws which operate upon entities at that level"
  (pp. 179–180). Psychological states correspond, if to anything, to the
  *computational* states of an automaton, not its machine-table states.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, with Block's other papers on
functionalism, meaning, AI and perception. No anthology topic holds a
type-identity theory of mental states, and the paper carries no instruction
for machine-learning practice.

It is the record's earliest statement of the case Block makes at length in
"Troubles with Functionalism" ([LIT-407](LIT-407.md), read in [NOTE-366](../notes.d/NOTE-366.md)). The two papers
argue against different targets. This one argues that psychological states
cannot be *machine-table states*, and five of its six arguments are
compatible with functionalism broadly understood (p. 173). [LIT-407](LIT-407.md) argues
against functionalism itself, from the homunculi-headed robot. The
qualia argument is the bridge: here it is "a serious prima-facie argument"
(p. 172), and absent qualia, the case where "an organism might be in pain
even though it is feeling nothing at all", is singled out as the deeper
problem (p. 173). [LIT-407](LIT-407.md) builds its Absent Qualia Argument on exactly that
case. No relation is declared between the two; the line of descent is in
prose here.

**Correction declared.** The target is the theory Putnam proposed in
"Psychological Predicates" ([LIT-396](LIT-396.md)), which the paper cites as its source
for probabilistic automata and machine tables (pp. 160, 164) and quotes on
the difficulty of "a normal form for the psychological description of
organisms" (p. 175). The paper argues that this theory's central identity,
psychological state = machine-table state, cannot hold, which is why
`corrects:` is set. [LIT-396](LIT-396.md) is held only as a reprint excerpt ([NOTE-335](../notes.d/NOTE-335.md)),
so the correction is stated against the theory as Block and Fodor quote
and paraphrase it.

**Where it bears.**

- *Chalmers on implementation* ([LIT-404](LIT-404.md), [NOTE-342](../notes.d/NOTE-342.md)). Argument 2 anticipates
  Chalmers's move from finite-state automata to combinatorial-state
  automata. Block and Fodor already say a person needs a model with
  simultaneous, interacting states, and that a single automaton equivalent
  to a parallel set cannot also be the best description (p. 171). That
  pairing is mine; Chalmers does not cite this paper.
- *Machine-table China brain* ([LIT-407](LIT-407.md), [NOTE-342](../notes.d/NOTE-342.md)). [NOTE-342](../notes.d/NOTE-342.md) records that
  the machine-table China system implements only an FSA. This paper is the
  earlier argument that a machine table is the wrong formalism for a mind
  at all.
- *Productivity and structure* (arguments 5 and 6) are the first form of
  the productivity and systematicity case for structured mental
  representation that Block's "Advertisement for a Semantics for
  Psychology" ([LIT-tmpsiqov](LIT-tmpsiqov.md)) and "The Mind as the Software of the Brain"
  ([LIT-tmp5vkka](LIT-tmp5vkka.md)) take for granted, and that [NOTE-117](../notes.d/NOTE-117.md) sets out as the
  representational theory of mind's core commitment.
- *Butlin et al.* ([LIT-056](LIT-056.md), [NOTE-052](../notes.d/NOTE-052.md)) adopt computational functionalism at
  Marr's algorithmic level, not the input–output level. That is the level
  this paper points to when it says psychological states are computational
  states, not machine-table states.
