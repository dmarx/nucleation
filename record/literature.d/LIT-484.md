# LIT-484: Qu'est-ce qu'un fait social ?

Émile Durkheim (1894), *Revue philosophique de la France et de l'Étranger* 37 (January–June 1894), pp. 465–498, chapter I at pp. 466–475; in book form as chapter I of *Les Règles de la méthode sociologique* (Paris: Alcan, 1895; 2nd edition with a new preface, 1901) — https://www.jstor.org/stable/41075966

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, as one of the works filling out
the record's coverage of social ontology under the topic [ADR-017](../decisions.d/ADR-017.md) added. It is
the oldest text on that page.

[NOTE-391](../notes.d/NOTE-391.md) is the close reading of 2026-10-02, and it placed the work:
**Active**. It is the place to learn what "social fact" meant before anyone
asked about collective intentions. Its two marks, exteriority and external
constraint, are still the questions a social ontology has to answer: what
makes a rule, a currency or a language exist independently of any one user,
and what makes it bind. Its answers are definitional. The claim that social
facts form "une espèce nouvelle" rests on an elimination (not organic, not
individual-psychic, so social) whose exhaustiveness is assumed, and the
claim that constraint is present even when unfelt rests on the observation
that it shows itself when resisted.

**The citation.** The request cited the 1895 book. The chapter first
appeared in the 1894 *Revue philosophique* instalment, which is why
`published:` is 1894, and the text changed between the two: the 1894
paragraph on generality reads differently, and the sentence saying that a
statistical rate expresses "un certain état de l'âme collective" is not in
the 1894 text. The 1919 Alcan edition, the one read, is the book text.

**Bearing on what the record holds.**

- **Tomasello on obligation ([LIT-125](LIT-125.md), read in [NOTE-185](../notes.d/NOTE-185.md)).** The nearest
  held account of social pressure. Tomasello locates the felt pressure of
  obligation in a joint or collective "we" the agent helped author, which is
  why it feels legitimate rather than imposed. Durkheim's constraint is the
  other case: most social facts come to us "toutes faites" from earlier
  generations (p. 15), and education turns their constraint into habit
  until it is no longer felt (p. 11). The two are compatible; they describe
  pressure from a "we" one belongs to and pressure from a "milieu" one was
  born into. The contrast is this record's, not either author's.
- **The group-mind strand ([LIT-159](LIT-159.md), [LIT-401](LIT-401.md), [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)).** Durkheim speaks
  of collective sentiments as "une résultante de la vie commune" (p. 15) and
  of an "âme collective" (p. 14), which can read like a group-mind thesis.
  In this chapter it is a methodological claim: there is an order of facts
  whose substrate is the group and which sociology studies. Nothing here
  says a group is conscious or is an agent, so the chapter neither supports
  nor moves [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md). It does state, early and plainly, the composition
  intuition that strand argues over: a collective state is "dans chaque
  partie parce qu'il est dans le tout" (p. 15).
- **"Real Patterns" ([LIT-220](LIT-220.md)).** Durkheim's use of rates (marriage, birth,
  suicide) to isolate a collective current from individual circumstances
  that "s'y neutralisent mutuellement" (pp. 13–14) is a case of a pattern visible
  only at the aggregate. The pairing with Dennett is this record's.
- **Griesemer & Shavit ([LIT-188](LIT-188.md)).** Ways of being "ne sont que des
  manières de faire consolidées", and roads are "le lit que s'est creusé à
  lui-même" the regular current of exchanges (p. 18): practice consolidating into
  structure that then constrains practice. That is near what [LIT-188](LIT-188.md) calls
  entrenchment; the comparison is this record's, and [LIT-188](LIT-188.md) does not cite
  Durkheim.
- **Ritchie ([LIT-486](LIT-486.md)).** Ritchie cites Durkheim (from the Halls
  translation, p. 129) for the distinction between a social group and a
  "mere sum of individuals". That passage is in a later chapter, not this
  one; this chapter's version of the point is the generality argument.
