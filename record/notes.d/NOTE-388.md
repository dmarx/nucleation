# NOTE-388: Social ontology: Some basic principles

## Contribution

A compact statement of the theory of *The Construction of Social Reality*
([LIT-485](../literature.d/LIT-485.md)), written for social scientists, with three additions to the
book:

- status indicators can take on "a kind of life of their own" (after De
  Soto), §III;
- some status functions have no X: "free-standing Y terms" (after Barry
  Smith), such as corporations and money without currency, §III;
- "X counts as Y in C" is placed under the more general "We accept (S has
  power (S does A))", §III. Searle says the book already gave that formula
  in a later chapter (p. 65); what is new is ranking it above the first.

There is also a first sketch of a taxonomy of status functions (§IV), a
defence of the method as conceptual analysis rather than empirical
generalisation (§V), and a demarcation of which "institutions" the theory
covers (§VI). Nothing here argues for the account of collective
intentionality. That is referred to Searle (1990), [LIT-490](../literature.d/LIT-490.md) (fn. 3).

## Key insight

Status functions are the glue. A thing performs a function not because of
its physical structure but because a group collectively accepts that it
has a status. That status brings rights, duties and permissions, which
Searle calls deontic powers. To recognise them is to have a reason to act
that does not depend on what one wants. Institutions are systems of such
powers. They exist only as represented, which is why they need language.

## Assumptions

- **Physicalist background.** "The world consists entirely of physical
  particles in fields of force" (p. 52). The problem is how social reality
  fits into that world, not whether it does.
- **Collective intentionality as a primitive.** Collective intentionality
  is "a genuine biological phenomenon", "not mysterious or inexplicable",
  and the "psychological presupposition of all social reality" (p. 57).
  Its analysis is deferred to Searle (1990). In this paper it is
  illustrated by one formula, "I am doing something only as part of our
  doing something" (p. 56), and no argument is given that it is
  irreducible.
- **The examples are the author's own society**: the US presidency,
  California corporation law, a twenty dollar bill, American football.
  §V answers the charge that this is a narrow base. The claim is about
  logical structure, which any society with a deontology must have.
- **Animal capacities.** The human/animal line is drawn on deontology. It
  is held provisionally: if some primates turn out to have deontic
  relations, "so much the better for them" (p. 62).

## Key results

The paper has no theorems. Its results are distinctions and
classifications, each argued informally or by example.

- **Observer-relative versus observer-independent** (p. 53). A rough test:
  could the fact exist with no conscious agents? Observer-relative facts
  are created by observer-independent attitudes.
- **Epistemic versus ontological objectivity** (pp. 54–55). "Observer
  relativity implies ontological subjectivity but ontological subjectivity
  does not preclude epistemic objectivity." That, he says, is why social
  science is possible.
- **Three primitives** (p. 56): collective intentionality, the assignment
  of function, and constitutive rules.
- **Definitions** (p. 57). A *social fact* is "any fact involving
  collective intentionality of two or more human or animal agents", so a
  wolf pack hunting is one. *Institutional facts* are the subclass that
  involve status functions.
- **Functions** (p. 57). "All functions are observer relative",
  argued from the normativity of function. He adds that the main argument
  does not depend on it.
- **"X counts as Y in C"** (p. 58) iterates upward (noises → sentence →
  promise → contract) and interlocks laterally.
- **Deontic powers** (p. 59). Accepting a status function is accepting
  obligations, rights, duties and so on. These are "desire-independent
  reasons for action", and "animals lack a deontology".
- **Language's four roles** (pp. 61–62). The fact exists only as
  represented. Its content is deontic. The deontology persists after its
  creation ("no language, no deontology"). The institution itself must be
  recognised, not only its instances. The dog can see a man carry a ball
  across a line but cannot see a touchdown (p. 60).
- **Status indicators** (p. 63). De Soto's squatters have recognised status
  but no deeds, so their deontic powers "stop at the point where the larger
  society requires some official proof".
- **Free-standing Y terms** (pp. 63–66). A corporation is created by
  declaration with no X; money needs no currency, only recorded numerical
  values. In such cases "the real bearer of the deontology is the
  participant". Chess pieces and bills are "markers for the amount of
  deontic power that the players have".
- **The general form** (p. 65). The book's early chapters gave "X counts
  as Y in C" as the basic form, but "a later formulation I gave in the book
  gives us a much more general account": We accept (S has power (S does
  A)). The two are related thus: we accept that S has the power *because*
  S = X and X counts as Y, and the Y status carries the powers.
- **Desire-based reasons presuppose desire-independent ones** (p. 66). The
  desire to be rich or be president presupposes recognised deontic
  relations.
- **Taxonomy, tentative** (pp. 66–68). Status functions where physical
  capacities are essential (a licensed driver) versus where they are not
  (money). Certification versus authorisation. Positive versus negative
  powers. Taxes versus punishments, and salaries versus prizes, as
  distinct status functions with the same monetary upshot.
- **Scope** (pp. 69–71). A date, a colour word or a unit of weight is not an
  institutional fact. Friendship is, "because friendship carries
  obligations". "Nerd" can become one where a group recognises
  obligations attached to it. Science, religion and education are
  practices containing institutions, not institutions in this sense.

## Concepts

- **Observer-relative**: a feature that exists only relative to the
  attitudes of agents (money, citizenship). **Observer-independent**: one
  that does not (mass, photosynthesis, and the attitudes themselves).
- **Collective intentionality**: intentionality "shared by different
  people", in the forms "we believe", "we desire", "we intend". In action,
  "I am doing something only as part of our doing something" (p. 56).
- **Status function**: a function a person or object performs not by its
  physical structure but by collective acceptance of a status (pp. 57–58).
- **Constitutive rule**: a rule of the form X counts as Y in C. It creates
  the activity it governs.
- **Deontic powers**: rights, duties, obligations, permissions,
  authorisations, requirements, and the like, positive and negative.
- **Desire-independent reason for action**: a reason that comes from
  recognising a deontic power, whatever one wants.
- **Status indicator**: a uniform, deed, licence or passport marking a
  status that nothing physical marks.
- **Free-standing Y term**: a status function with no physical object on
  which it is imposed (Barry Smith's term).

## Connections

The paper restates *The Construction of Social Reality* ([LIT-485](../literature.d/LIT-485.md)) and
sends the reader to "Collective Intentions and Actions" ([LIT-490](../literature.d/LIT-490.md)) for
collective intentionality. Both are filed `Deferred`. It does not engage
with any rival account of collective intentionality: Gilbert ([LIT-477](../literature.d/LIT-477.md),
[LIT-492](../literature.d/LIT-492.md)), Bratman ([LIT-471](../literature.d/LIT-471.md), [LIT-488](../literature.d/LIT-488.md)) and Tuomela & Miller
([LIT-476](../literature.d/LIT-476.md)) are not mentioned.

Its rivals in this record are later. Hindriks & Guala ([LIT-481](../literature.d/LIT-481.md),
read in [NOTE-395](NOTE-395.md)) target the book's constitutive rules. They argue
that the Y term is a theoretical term eliminable in favour of regulative
rules, and that "desire-independent reasons" is either a platitude or an
unargued anti-Humean thesis. This paper's free-standing Y terms bear on
that dispute. When Searle says the deontic power of a corporation or of
money goes "directly to the individuals", and that bills are "markers"
for powers, he treats the Y term much as they do. He does not draw their
conclusion, and he keeps "desire-independent" as a primitive distinction.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md), indirect.** The account says nothing about group minds. It
  bears on the anti-nesting question only through its ontology. A
  society such as "the United States of America" (p. 52) exists as that
  society only because its members represent it as existing. A brain's
  parts do not represent the brain. That is the shape of Kammerer's
  exception ([LIT-409](../literature.d/LIT-409.md)) and could be a principled asymmetry of the kind
  [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)'s `promote_when` asks for. Searle does not argue this, and
  it is the reader's inference. It would also need an argument that a
  society's being observer-relative bears on whether it can be a subject,
  which is a separate question.
- **Tomasello ([LIT-125](../literature.d/LIT-125.md), [NOTE-185](NOTE-185.md)).** Searle's human/animal line on
  deontology is the conceptual counterpart of Tomasello's developmental
  claim that felt obligation comes with joint commitment from about age 3.
  They differ in vocabulary: Searle's collective intentionality includes
  hunting wolves, while Tomasello's shared intentionality is
  species-unique.
- **List & Pettit ([LIT-391](../literature.d/LIT-391.md)).** The corporation here is a deontic
  construction, and its bearers of power are individuals. This is
  compatible with, but does not supply, an account of the corporation as
  an agent.
- **Chinese Room ([LIT-400](../literature.d/LIT-400.md)).** A different subject. The common premise is
  that intentionality is a biological feature of individual brains, which
  here makes the creating attitudes observer-independent.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice. Nothing here points to the
  Anthology of the SOTA.

## Limitations

- It is a summary and update, and it says so. The theory of collective
  intentionality is not argued here, and neither is the claim that the
  three primitives suffice.
- Searle does not reconcile his own revision with the theory's original
  form. A status function is defined as imposed "on a person or an
  object" (p. 57), and then corporations have none. The new general form
  is offered (p. 65), but the paper does not say whether "X counts as Y in
  C" is now derivative or merely the common case.
- The test for observer relativity is admitted to be "rough-and-ready"
  (p. 53), and the paper offers no sharper one.
- The taxonomy (§IV) is offered as unsatisfactory by its author.
- The scope criterion in §VI, a collectively recognised deontology, is
  wider than the X-counts-as-Y form. It makes friendship an institutional
  fact without saying what X counts as what Y.

## Open questions

- Is "X counts as Y in C" eliminable in favour of "We accept (S has power
  (S does A))"? If so, how does the account differ from the
  rules-in-equilibrium view ([LIT-481](../literature.d/LIT-481.md))?
- What is the content of the "acceptance" in "We accept"? Is it
  Searle's irreducible we-intention, a joint commitment (Gilbert), or
  interlocking individual intentions (Bratman)? The paper uses it without
  saying, and nothing here would decide it.
- Where exactly is the animal line? Evidence of deontic relations in
  primates would, by Searle's own statement, move it, and [LIT-125](../literature.d/LIT-125.md) is the
  record's nearest evidence on the human side.
