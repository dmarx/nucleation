# NOTE-tmpxt8qz: Population and Having Children Now

## Contribution

Narveson returns to the population question fifty years after
"Utilitarianism and New Generations", now as a non-utilitarian. He keeps
his original "insight", that bringing someone into existence confers
nothing on them, and draws a new line around it. Creating happy people is
good, but nobody is owed it. Morality's enforceable part protects actual
people only. Procreation is then the parents' own business, limited by
harm to others and by their ability to bear the costs.

## Key insight

Value and obligation come apart. "The existence of people leading good
lives is (normally) a good thing" (p. 50), but not because of what it does
for them: "there is no person upon whom life is conferred when we have
children" (p. 50). Duties are "always and by definition" to people
(p. 50), and only actual people can be harmed. So the good of future lives
gives reasons that may be praised but not enforced.

## Assumptions

- **A comparative account of harm and benefit.** "A is better or worse off
  at t2 than A at t1. But if there is no person at t1, we have lost our
  comparator" (p. 53).
- **All duties are to persons.** Obligations regarding anything else are
  indirect (p. 50).
- **Two departments of morality** (pp. 54–55). Directives that may be
  enforced by punishment, and directives "reinforceable" by praise and
  blame. Only harm to others licenses the first.
- **Contractarian background.** Morality as directives issued by
  "everybody", ideally unanimously (p. 54); dealings with others "by
  negotiation, with a view to advancing mutual interests" (p. 57). The
  foundations are deferred to his *This is Ethical Theory* (2009, n. 4).
- **Persons begin late.** He sets abortion aside and dates a person's
  emergence somewhere between conception and late gestation (pp. 50, 53).

## Key results

- **The original theme** (p. 49). His 1967 point "was that utilitarianism
  did not commit one to making happy people, but only to making people
  happy", and "something like that" ran through the 1973 and 1978 papers.
  He has since "abandoned my original proclivity toward utilitarianism".
- **No conferring** (pp. 49–50). "There isn't any 'one' upon which to
  confer 'life'". Before birth the only candidate is "a bit of germ
  plasm", to which no one has duties.
- **But good lives are good** (pp. 50–51). Asked whether a child's being
  happy is a good reason to have it: "Contrary, perhaps, to what I have
  been (perhaps mis)understood to say in those earlier papers, the correct
  answer is: of course!" (p. 50). Such a life is good to its possessor,
  usually a benefit to its parents, and usually a "public" good.
- **Why it matters to us** (p. 51). "There are just two sorts of answers."
  (1) To parents or "simply benevolent persons" good lives "'just do'
  matter". (2) "Much more importantly", more good people benefit others in
  their social environment.
- **Against Benatar** (p. 52). Coming into existence exposes one to harms,
  but bringing someone into existence is not harming them in the morally
  wrong sense, and goods can outweigh harms, as a dentist's pain is
  outweighed.
- **Not aiding is not harming** (p. 53). People may have children so long
  as they impose no significant costs on others. That they could instead
  have helped the suffering does not make having children a harm. This is
  "an outright rejection of utilitarianism, and certainly of
  'prioritarianism'". He then qualifies it: it may not entail such a
  rejection "unless those views are interpreted in a certain way"
  (p. 54), which the two departments of morality are meant to show.
- **Costs fall on parents** (p. 54). A child costly to raise is the
  parents' charge; if they cannot internalize the cost they acted wrongly
  or irresponsibly.
- **When others may interfere** (p. 55). Only if having or not having
  children "would work ill for some persons. Not, however, for some
  fancied future possible persons, but for actual persons, alive,
  breathing, and acting now (or at any rate in their lifetimes.)"
  Note 2 (p. 53) allows that "some of those persons are in the future".
- **Why people have children** (p. 55). Their parents want grandchildren;
  they love children; "we want to continue our particular DNA type"; old
  age support; failed contraception; the community needs people of their
  kind. "Our values are what move us to do what we do—not the values of
  ... other persons, future or present" (p. 56).
- **Resources** (pp. 56–59).
  - Resources "are not fixed: they are created, by us", and more people
    make more (p. 58). Malthusian scarcity "simply isn't on any more"
    (p. 57).
  - Climate: serious perils, if any, are decades off; current abatement
    is "fabulously inefficient"; "What's our hurry?" (p. 58).
  - Ontario's wind and solar more than doubled consumer electricity prices
    with little or no CO2 gain; wind blows usefully "about 26% of the
    time" (n. 8, p. 59). No data are given beyond cited books.
- **Possible people owed nothing** (pp. 59–60). Even if one's child would
  keep "some identifiable future person" from existing or from being as
  happy, the present person keeps the right, because that future person
  "doesn't exist". With a scarce medicine and two births, the one born
  first has the prior claim.
- **No bargain** (p. 60). A bargain obliging us to have fewer children for
  future generations' sake is possible, but "why would present persons
  really desirous of having families engage in any such bargain?" "A
  supposedly philosophical interest in the welfare of future generations
  evaporates in the face of reality."

## Concepts

- **conferring life**: the idea, rejected, that creating a person gives
  something to someone.
- **enforceable versus reinforceable directives**: morality backed by
  punishment versus morality backed by praise and blame.
- **actual versus possible persons**: those who exist now "or at any rate
  in their lifetimes", against "fancied future possible persons".
- **internalizing costs**: the parents bear what their child costs.

## Connections

- **Narveson 1973 ([LIT-tmpoc3e4](../literature.d/LIT-tmpoc3e4.md)), unread.** Named as continuing
  the 1967 theme and listed with its reprint in Bayles (1976). Not
  restated. The 2017 paper suggests the earlier papers were read as
  denying that happy lives are a reason to create them, and says that was
  perhaps a misreading.
- **Caviola et al. ([NOTE-tmpaz0p7](NOTE-tmpaz0p7.md)).** They test the 1973 slogan and
  find lay judgement counts adding a happy person as good. Their note
  observes that this counts against neutrality about value, not against
  a person-affecting view of obligation. Narveson 2017 holds exactly the
  second without the first.
- **Meyer, SEP ([NOTE-tmps9poq](NOTE-tmps9poq.md)).** Meyer traces the procreation asymmetry
  to Narveson 1967 and 1973 and keeps its half that gives no reason to
  create the happy. Narveson 2017 now gives such a reason, though not a
  duty. On the other half he allows exceptions, "when foreseeably a baby
  would be born with some horrible disease" (p. 52), "the case is
  different", but he does not say why.
- **Parfit ([LIT-tmpnjafv](../literature.d/LIT-tmpnjafv.md)), unread.** Named as the occasion for
  returning to the subject (p. 49), and his repugnant conclusion is used
  as a scene (p. 56), but the non-identity problem is not engaged.
- **Gardiner ([LIT-tmp12vk2](../literature.d/LIT-tmp12vk2.md)), unread.** Narveson grants the structure
  Gardiner calls a storm: the present will not bargain with the future,
  and "each person does his or her best to promote her interests, letting
  the chips fall where they may—and rightly so" (p. 60). He reads it as
  permission, not as a problem.
- **Scheffler ([NOTE-tmpmsmxw](NOTE-tmpmsmxw.md)).** Both put the value of future lives in
  the living's attitudes. Scheffler finds a deep dependence on humanity
  going on; Narveson finds parents' and the benevolent's liking, and
  benefit to others.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-tmpmx9ha](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.md).** These are inferences; Narveson does not frame his
  view by the question's grounds.
  - The person-affecting ground, in its founder's late version, does not
    deny that future good lives matter. It denies they are owed. What is
    owed is non-harm to actual people, and the future enters only as
    people who will be actual "in their lifetimes".
  - Why future lives matter is given entirely through the living: the
    liking of parents and the benevolent, and the benefit to others. That
    moves him from the "good of future people" ground to the question's
    third, "what the living value", with a harm constraint added.
  - He has no answer, and wants none, for the far future: a world without
    those people wrongs no one.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** Narveson says that "our values
  are what move us" (p. 56) and that interest in future generations'
  welfare "evaporates" against a person's wish for a child (p. 60). That
  fits the thesis's prediction that concern stops where the self's stake
  stops. But it is the trivial reading [CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md) warns of: every
  motive is the agent's own value, which does not make it self-interested.
  His list of reasons to have children includes one that is extension of
  the self in the thesis's sense, continuing "our particular DNA type"
  (p. 55), beside others that are not. He gives no evidence on motive.
  No dated note on the claim; the point belongs to the question (section
  4).
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** An example of the
  objection's target, not a test of it.

## Limitations

- **Not the 1973 paper.** What "Moral Problems of Population" argued is
  still unread; this paper summarizes the earlier work in one sentence.
- **Polemical in its second half.** The empirical claims on resources,
  climate and Ontario's electricity rest on popular books (Ridley, Bailey,
  Moran, Etherington) and are not argued. He recommends reading
  Nuccitelli alongside Moran (n. 7).
- **The scope of "actual persons" is unclear.** The text limits it to
  those "alive, breathing, and acting now (or at any rate in their
  lifetimes)" (p. 55) and "currently known persons" (p. 60). Note 2
  (p. 53) allows future persons. Which future persons count is not said.
- **Non-identity is named, not answered.**
- **Typos.**
  - "Might this be on argued the ground" (p. 52).
  - "Daniel Benatar" for David Benatar (n. 6, p. 57).
  - "Erlich" in the references (p. 60), "Ehrlich" in n. 1 (p. 52).
  - "CO2 admissions" for emissions (n. 8, p. 59); "these who supply" for
    those (p. 59).
  - A stray parenthesis: "(see Nuccitelli, 2015, pp155-6) for perhaps the
    most extreme current view)" (p. 58).
  - "St, Martin's Press" and "l973", "l976", "l978" with a letter l
    (pp. 60–61).

## Open questions

- Did the 1973 paper deny that happy lives give a reason to create them,
  or only that utilitarianism requires creating them? Only the 1973 text
  can settle Narveson's "(perhaps mis)understood".
- Does a person-affecting view of obligation, joined to an impersonal
  view of value, have anything to say about extinction? Narveson's does
  not.
