# THEORY-tmpef8lf: Brushing a seen rubber hand in synchrony with the hidden real hand makes touch be felt at the rubber hand, makes it be reported as one's own, and shifts the felt position of the real hand toward it; a small asynchrony removes these effects

## Source

Botvinick & Cohen (1998), [LIT-tmpu9yys](../literature.d/LIT-tmpu9yys.md), *Nature* 391:756, a one-page
scientific correspondence, read in full in [NOTE-tmpt0l4z](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpt0l4z.md): the text, both
figures and their captions. The copy read is the publisher's typeset page
on a third-party host, as the NOTE's history says.

## What was actually shown

**The illusion.** Each subject's left hand was hidden behind a screen and a
life-sized rubber left hand was placed in view. Both were stroked with
paintbrushes, "synchronising the timing of the brushing as closely as
possible". After ten minutes, ten subjects rated nine statements in random
order. The three predicted ones were affirmed: that touch was felt where the
rubber hand was touched, that it was caused by the brush on the rubber hand,
and "I felt as if the rubber hand were my hand" (P<0.002, Fig. 1). Five of
the six control statements had means on the disagree side. Eight of ten
subjects used terms of ownership unprompted.

**The drift.** After thirty minutes of exposure, blind intermanual reaches
toward the real hand were displaced toward the rubber hand. The
displacement grew with the reported share of the exposure during which the
illusion was felt (r² 0.47, p<0.03, about ten points).

**The control.** With "a small asynchrony" the illusion was reported for 7%
of the exposure against 42%, and reaches moved 13 mm away from the rubber
hand against 23 mm toward it in the synchronous group. This could have come
out otherwise: seeing a hand brushed beside one's own, at any timing, could
have produced the same reports and drift. It did not.

## What this does not say

- **It does not separate synchrony from expectation.** The predicted items
  were known to the experimenters, the intended answer is guessable from
  the set-up, and the paper does not discuss demand characteristics. The
  control items guard against blanket agreement, not against suggestion
  specific to the touch and ownership items. That is what the promotion
  test asks for.
- **It does not give effect sizes or the control group's size.** The tests
  behind the P-values are not named. Figure 1's corrected threshold
  (P<0.018) is looser than the uncorrected one (P<0.002), which the paper
  does not explain.
- **It does not say what the drift measures.** The drift may be ownership
  or only visuo-proprioceptive recalibration. It was measured after a
  longer exposure than the questionnaire, and the paper ties the two only
  through the reported duration.
- **It does not establish the mechanism.** The authors' constraint-
  satisfaction account rests on a connectionist model that is neither
  shown nor cited. The predictive accounts filed alongside this one,
  [THEORY-tmpxdoe8](THEORY-tmpxdoe8.md) and [THEORY-tmpom3z2](THEORY-tmpom3z2.md), are explanations of this finding, not
  part of it.
- **It is about a hand.** The conclusion that intermodal matching "can be
  sufficient for self-attribution" concerns a body part. Whether ownership
  of the whole body, self-location or a self works the same way is
  [THEORY-tmpuehzc](THEORY-tmpuehzc.md)'s question, and Blanke & Metzinger ([LIT-tmpnbv1d](../literature.d/LIT-tmpnbv1d.md)) argue
  that it does not.
