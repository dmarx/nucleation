# THEORY-1629: Chen and colleagues report that the mortality-salience literature, which uncorrected reproduces Burke and colleagues' average effect, shrinks under corrections for publication bias to about r = .18, or to no effect on one method, with true effects so heterogeneous and studies so underpowered that many published effects are likely spurious

## The account

Burke et al.'s moderate effect is reproduced when their uncorrected
method is used, and shrinks by about half, or to nothing, when
publication bias is modelled. The literature is also so heterogeneous
that an average says little about any one paradigm. The authors conclude
that it "contain[s] studies that appear to be testing nonzero effects,
although the literature is highly heterogeneous and underpowered,
rendering many individual effects to be likely spurious" (Conclusion).

## Evidence

- Uncorrected: g = 0.805, 95% CI [0.757, 0.854], over 635 tests from 557
  studies; strongly asymmetric funnel.
- Selection model: g = 0.357, 95% CI [0.168, 0.546], τ = 0.716; 95% of
  true effects between r = −.47 and .66.
- WAAP-WLS: g = 0.385; only 139 of 635 effects (21%) had 80% power. PET:
  g = −0.114, judged spurious, so no positive effect by the procedure's
  rule.
- p-curve over 825 focal tests: right skew, both p < .001, average power
  25%. z-curve: expected replication rate .19, expected discovery rate
  .08.

The review tests whether death reminders change worldview defense, not
the mechanism theorized for it.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-301](../questions.d/QUESTION-301.md)'s "Death and the group" strand rests on terror management
theory, and its note of 2026-10-29 records that "Terror management is
weaker support (Many Labs 4, [NOTE-1322](../notes.d/NOTE-1322.md); Chen et al., [NOTE-1271](../notes.d/NOTE-1271.md))" and
that corrected for publication bias "the meta-analytic effect falls from
about r = .35 to about .18, or to nothing". The reading is that
evidence.
