# NOTE-1222: Hansen & Ghrist — Opinion dynamics on discourse sheaves

## Contribution

It recasts linear network opinion dynamics on a cellular sheaf, the
"discourse sheaf". A discourse sheaf gives each agent its own opinion space
and each edge its own space of topics under discussion, and it attaches an
expression map from each agent to each edge. The Laplacian diffusion,
harmonic extension and control results of the classical graph models
(Taylor, DeGroot, Friedkin–Johnsen) carry over. Global sections, sheaf
cohomology and relative cohomology replace consensus, connectivity and
"no consensus vanishes on the input set". The new elements are these: the
limit is agreement in expression, not identity of opinion (§§3–4); the
expression maps themselves can be evolved, alone or jointly with the
opinions (§§8–9); and bounded-confidence and antagonistic dynamics are
written as nonlinear Laplacians (§§10–12). The paper is mathematics with
toy examples. It contains no data and no social-scientific test.

## Key insight

The paper puts consensus on the edges and leaves the vertices alone.
Agents u and v "have expressed consensus when F_{u⊴e}(x_u) = F_{v⊴e}(x_v)
… Note that this does not imply that u and v have the same opinions: it
means that their expressions of personally held opinions have the
appearance of agreement" (§3, p. 7). Diffusion drives the network to the
kernel of the coboundary, so everything the theory can say about the limit
is about expressions. Opinions held privately can be distinct, opposite
or "incomparable" (p. 3). The same split makes the expression maps a
second place where disagreement can be removed. If the maps rather than
the opinions are evolved, discord falls because what agents say changes,
which the paper calls "learning to lie" (§8).

## Assumptions

- **Linear algebra on a graph.** Stalks are real, finite-dimensional inner
  product spaces with a fixed orthonormal basis, and restriction maps are
  linear (§2, p. 3). Edges are undirected and pairwise. Multi-agent
  interactions and probabilistic elements are named as future work (§13,
  p. 22).
- **Topics are basis vectors, and opinions are intensities.** "points on
  each axis correspond to negative, neutral, or positive opinions on the
  topic" (§3, pp. 6–7). An edge's discourse topics need not be any
  agent's basis topics (p. 7, the pub example on p. 8).
- **Continuous time, deterministic.** There is no noise anywhere. The paper
  says discrete-time versions follow by Euler discretization (§1.1, p. 2).
- **Expression is fixed in §§4–7.** The maps are given, and only the
  opinions move. In §8 the opinions are fixed and only the maps move. In
  §9 both move.
- **What an agent can see (§8, p. 13).** Each agent "is able to modify all
  its restriction maps F_{v⊴e} (for edges e incident to v) and is able to
  observe their neighbors' translated opinions F_{u⊴e}x_u". Its only
  objective is that "apparent consensus is reached".
- **The model is not offered as realistic.** Evolving opinions "is perhaps
  not how real people engage in discourse" (p. 8). §8 sets aside "the
  sociological questions of whether a typical person in the face of
  opposition actually changes opinions or simply "learns to communicate
  better"" (p. 13).

## Key results

### The structure (§§2–3, pp. 3–8)

- Coboundary (δx)_e = F_{v⊴e}x_v − F_{u⊴e}x_u (eq. 2.3, p. 4); global
  sections H⁰(G; F) = ker δ (eq. 2.4, p. 5); sheaf Laplacian
  L_F = δᵀδ (eq. 2.5, p. 5); Hodge theorem H⁰(G; F) = ker L_F
  (Theorem 2.2, p. 6). Relative cohomology H⁰(G, A; F) is "global sections
  of F on G that vanish on A" (p. 5).
- The zero sheaf: replacing the constant sheaf's identity maps with zero
  maps "gives a very different sheaf in which all communication is devoid
  of information. Such a sheaf is a jumble" (§2.1, p. 4).
- What a discourse sheaf permits (§3, p. 8): private basis topics, so
  agents "need not have any basis topics in common"; edge topics
  unrelated to anyone's basis opinions; "the formation of policies from
  principles"; exaggeration and modulation by positive scalars; and lying
  by negative ones, "selectively. What C says to B need not match what C
  says to A (even if they are discussing the same topic)".

### Opinion diffusion (§4, pp. 8–9)

- **Theorem 4.1 (p. 9).** Solutions of dx/dt = −αL_F x (eq. 4.1) converge
  to the orthogonal projection of x(0) onto H⁰(G; F), exponentially, at a
  rate set by the Laplacian's spectrum.
- "If this sheaf has no nontrivial global sections, the only stable
  opinions will be everywhere zero: an uninteresting solution" (p. 9).
- **Example 4.2, "In polite company" (p. 9, Fig. 4 on p. 10).** Four agents
  on a cycle, all stalks one-dimensional. Two agents hold a positive
  opinion of a politician and two a negative one. The right-hand pair use
  the map −1 toward the left-hand pair and +1 toward each other: "The two
  agents on the right tell a polite lie to their neighbors on the left but
  are frank with each other. A global section of this sheaf maintains the
  public agreement." Any initial distribution converges to one of these
  polarized distributions. "although each agent knows the veracity of
  their opinion expressions, they do not know the veracity of their
  neighbors' expressed opinions."

### Stubbornness, control and reluctance (§§5–7, pp. 9–13)

- **Theorem 5.1 (p. 10).** With the opinions on U held fixed, the rest
  converge exponentially to the harmonic extension of u = x₀|_U nearest to
  x₀. The limit minimizes ‖δx‖² subject to x|_U = u, and "harmonic
  extensions are not in general global sections" (p. 11). Unique when
  H⁰(G, U; F) = 0 (p. 10).
- **Theorem 6.1 (p. 11).** If H⁰(G, U; F) = 0 the control system (6.1),
  with input on U, is stabilizable. **Theorem 6.2 (p. 12),** "presented
  without proof": if H⁰(G, Y; F) = 0 it is detectable from Y. Example 6.3:
  on the constant sheaf over a connected graph, influence over one agent
  suffices to steer global consensus (p. 12).
- **§7 (pp. 12–13).** Reluctance γ_v is modelled by giving each agent a
  stubborn "parent" vertex joined by maps √γ_v I, so that
  dx_v/dt = −α(L_F x)_v + αγ_v((x₀)_v − x_v) (eq. 7.1) and the limit is a
  harmonic extension on the augmented graph.

### Learning to lie (§8, pp. 13–14)

- **The dynamics.** dF_{v⊴e}/dt = −β(F_{v⊴e}x_v − F_{u⊴e}x_u)x_vᵀ
  (eq. 8.1), or dδ/dt = −βP_δ(δxxᵀ) with P_δ the projection onto the
  coboundary sparsity pattern (eq. 8.3). The stated rule: agents "should
  alter their restriction maps to remove the part of the image that
  contributes to disagreement with neighbors" (p. 13).
- **Theorem 8.1 (p. 13).** With x fixed, F(t) converges to "the nearest
  sheaf such that x is a global section", in the Frobenius distance
  ‖δ − δ′‖²_F (eq. 8.4). The proof is edge by edge: each edge's flow is a
  projection onto {δ_e : δ_e x_e = 0}. The paper notes "a sort of duality"
  with the heat equation (p. 14).
- **Example 8.2 (p. 14, Fig. 6).** The constant sheaf on one edge, with
  opinions −4 and +1. One map changes sign: the agent "learns to lie
  about their opinion … changes their expressed opinion's sign, but also
  downplays its magnitude." The figure labels the final maps −0.2 and 0.8.
  My computation of Theorem 8.1's limit from these data gives −3/17 ≈
  −0.18 and 12/17 ≈ 0.71, so both agents express about 0.71. The ratio
  agrees with the figure; the figure's magnitudes do not (see Limitations).

### Joint opinion–expression diffusion (§9, pp. 14–17)

- **Eq. (9.1).** dx/dt = −αδᵀδx and dδ_e/dt = −βδ_e x_e x_eᵀ together.
- **Lemma 9.1 (p. 15).** Ψ(x, δ) = ½xᵀδᵀδx = ½‖δx‖² is nonincreasing, and
  dΨ/dt vanishes iff δx = 0. Eq. (9.1) "is actually a gradient descent
  equation on Ψ" for the inner product (1/α)xᵀx′ + (1/β)Σ tr(δ_eᵀδ′_e), so
  trajectories converge to pairs of a sheaf and a global section of it.
- "Because the stationary points of (9.1) are not isolated, there is no
  global asymptotic stability" (p. 15). The equilibria form a singular
  algebraic variety. **Theorem 9.2 (p. 15):** its smooth points are
  Lyapunov stable (center-manifold argument).
- **The trivial solution, named and guarded against (p. 16).** The
  highest-dimensional parts of the variety have ker δ = 0, so "This would
  seem to indicate that we should expect most trajectories to converge to
  points with x = 0. However, this is not the case." **Theorem 9.3:** if a
  diagonal block of αδ₀ᵀδ₀ − βx₀x₀ᵀ is not semidefinite, then x_∞ ≠ 0. (The
  statement prints x₀ᵀx₀; the proof uses xxᵀ.) The proof shows that the
  diagonal blocks of M are constant in time, so an indefinite block stays
  indefinite and xxᵀ cannot go to zero. Hence "given any initial δ₀ and
  x₀, there exists some scaling factor κ such that the initial condition
  (δ₀, κx₀) converges to a sheaf with a nontrivial global section."
- **Theorem 9.4 (p. 16).** ‖δ‖²_F, ‖x‖², ‖δx‖² and ‖δx‖²/‖x‖² are
  nonincreasing. The last, the Rayleigh quotient, means that "x(t) becomes
  a global section of F(t) at least as quickly as it approaches zero"
  (p. 17; the proof uses Jensen's inequality).
- **Example 9.5 (p. 17, Fig. 7).** The same edge with α = β = 1. One agent
  again ends up lying, "because the discrepancy between opinions is so
  great, the sheaf is able to adapt more easily than the opinions
  themselves."

### Nonlinear Laplacians (§§10–12, pp. 17–21)

- L^Φ_F = δᵀ ∘ Φ ∘ δ with Φ applied edge by edge (§10). With Φ = ∇U from
  edge potentials, the heat equation is gradient descent on
  Ψ(x) = Σ U_e(δ_e x). **Proposition 10.1 (p. 18):** if each U_e is
  radially unbounded with a unique local minimum at 0, the limit is the
  same projection onto H⁰ as in the linear case.
- **Bounded confidence (§11).** U_e(y) = ψ_e(‖y‖²) with ψ′_e > 0 below a
  threshold D_e and 0 above it. **Theorem 11.1 (p. 19):** x is an
  equilibrium iff every edge whose expressions disagree disagrees by at
  least D_e. Equilibria are the global sections of the sheaf F_x on the
  subgraph of edges still in confidence. **Theorem 11.2 (p. 20):** near
  an interior equilibrium, trajectories converge to the nearest global
  section of F_{x₀}.
- **Antagonism (§12).** U_e = −‖y‖² on negative edges gives the signed
  Laplacian L^S_F = δᵀSδ, which is linear and possibly indefinite.
  **Proposition 12.1 (p. 20):** if the negative edges form a cutset and
  some local section on one side does not extend by zero, L^S_F is
  indefinite. For the constant sheaf this always holds, "and hence
  unstable opinion dynamics" (p. 21). Flipping a restriction map's sign
  instead models an "agreement to disagree", the approach of Altafini
  [2, 3] (pp. 20–21).

### What the authors leave open (§13, pp. 21–22)

"Deeper analysis of the nonlinear dynamical systems (9.1) and (11.4) is
needed", along with probabilistic elements and simplicial complexes for
multi-agent interaction. "sheaf dynamics are applicable to more than simply
opinion dynamics", for example neuroscience and game theory. Much
sheaf-theoretic language was "excised" for accessibility.

## Concepts

- **Discourse sheaf** (§3, p. 6): a cellular sheaf on a social graph. Its
  vertex stalks are opinion spaces, its edge stalks are discourse spaces
  of topics under discussion, and its restriction maps are expressions of
  opinion (Fig. 3, p. 7).
- **Expression (restriction) map** F_{v⊴e}: how agent v states its private
  opinions on edge e's topics. It can be selective, combining, scaling
  ("exaggerating or modulating") or sign-flipping ("lie"), and it can
  differ from one edge to another (p. 8).
- **Global section / harmonic opinion distribution**: x ∈ ker δ = ker L_F,
  in which every pair's expressions agree (pp. 5, 8).
- **Discord**: the value of L_F x at a vertex, "the difference between x_v
  … and the opinions which would bring v in harmony with all its
  neighbors" (p. 8).
- **Harmonic extension**: the minimum-disagreement completion of opinions
  fixed on U (§5).
- **Relative cohomology H⁰(G, A; F)**: sections vanishing on A, read as "a
  measure of independence of the agents in A from the rest of the social
  network" (p. 8).
- **Learning to lie** (§8): diffusion of the expression maps toward a sheaf
  of which the current opinions are a section.
- **Nonlinear sheaf Laplacian** (§10): δᵀ ∘ Φ ∘ δ with an edgewise Φ; the
  bounded-confidence and signed Laplacians are instances.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-471](../claims.d/CLAIM-471.md): the paper is a worked formal case of the claim's
  "agreement on what is shared and not identity", and of a correspondence
  changed by interaction.** Each edge carries its own comparison space, and
  compatibility is F_{u⊴e}x_u = F_{v⊴e}x_v, with private opinions "distinct
  (or even incomparable)" (pp. 3, 7). That is the claim's form exactly.
  Two refinements follow from the text. First, in §§3–7 the
  correspondences are fixed background and only the states move. In §8
  they move too, and they are driven by the interaction: each agent
  observes its neighbours' expressions and revises its own map (p. 13).
  So the paper has both of the claim's regimes, and the second is a
  formal instance against its defeater (a). It is a constructed model,
  though, not a finding that real correspondences are made this way.
  Second, the paper shows that compatibility on what is shared can be
  reached by changing what is shared rather than what is held. A global
  section then says nothing about the private states. Its own example
  calls this lying (Example 4.2, p. 9; §8). Of the claim's three
  operations, opinion diffusion is enforcement (§4), and §8's learning is
  enforcement acting on the correspondence. The paper has no separate
  evidence operation.
- **[CLAIM-342](../claims.d/CLAIM-342.md): the paper is an instance of the self-confirming loop, and it
  guards against degeneracy, not against self-confirmation.** The
  expression-learning rule uses only the edge disagreement δx (eqs. 8.1,
  9.1). The evidence each agent revises against is its neighbours'
  expressions, which are produced by the maps being revised, and nothing
  in the model scores an expression against the opinion held or against
  anything outside the network. The paper names the outcome as a loss of
  fidelity ("learning to lie") and never as an error. On the risk
  [THEORY-299](../theory.d/THEORY-299.md) recorded, the paper has three guards and one gap. (i) The
  paper says that a sheaf without nonzero sections sends opinions to zero,
  "an uninteresting solution" (p. 9). (ii) Theorem 8.1 moves the maps only
  to the nearest sheaf in Frobenius distance. That is a rank-one change
  along x_v at each vertex, so the maps are not sent to zero unless zero
  happens to be nearest. (iii) For joint diffusion it names the risk
  itself ("we should expect most trajectories to converge to points with
  x = 0") and rules out x → 0 when a diagonal block of αδ₀ᵀδ₀ − βx₀x₀ᵀ is
  indefinite (Theorem 9.3, p. 16). (iv) The gap: it does not address the
  maps collapsing, and ‖δ‖_F is nonincreasing (Theorem 9.4). A consequence
  of the same conserved quantity, which I derived and the paper does not
  state, is that a vertex's maps cannot all go to zero if its block
  α Σ_e F_{v⊴e}ᵀF_{v⊴e} − βx_v x_vᵀ starts with a positive eigenvalue. So
  the trivial solution is avoided by conservation and minimal change,
  which are internal to the loop, not by evidence from outside it. The
  result is coherence, and A22 §10's distinction between "becoming more
  coherent" and "becoming more knowledgeable" applies: this paper's
  learning is the first by construction.
- **[QUESTION-285](../questions.d/QUESTION-285.md), third answer criterion: a concrete G for an
  apparatus that revises its correspondences, with what it cannot reach
  stated.** In §8, G is the gradient flow (8.3). It cannot change the
  graph, the stalks, the sparsity pattern or (in §8) the held opinions,
  and it is bound to the nearest admissible sheaf. These are structural
  limits on G of the kind the question asks for. They secure that the
  revision stays small and keeps the data nonzero. They do not secure
  answerability to anything the system did not produce, because the
  model has no outside. The paper is a clean negative case for the
  question's last clause: a revising apparatus with well-defined limits on
  G that converges to agreement, and that the authors themselves describe
  as having learned to misreport.
- **The exchange's description (A22 §11, "Richer observation and
  communication maps, including selective expression") is right about
  communication and selective expression.** The abstract says "selective
  opinion modulation and lying". Example 4.2 says the sheaf "permits
  selective expression of opinion" (p. 9), and p. 8 says deception "can
  be done selectively". "Observation maps" overstates it. The sheaf's maps
  are expression maps, and what an agent observes is its neighbours'
  expressions (p. 13). The only observation operator is the control-theory
  output C of §6, which is the identity on the observed vertices.
- **[CLAIM-481](../claims.d/CLAIM-481.md).** The convergence rate is "related to the spectral
  properties of the sheaf Laplacian" (p. 9), which is consistent with the
  claim's Laplacian-spectrum rates. The paper only cites this to [23] and
  does no more with it.
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- **No empirical content.** The social reading (lying, polite company,
  learning to communicate) is interpretation of the mathematics. The
  authors themselves disclaim realism (pp. 8, 13).
- **A slip in Theorem 5.1's proof (p. 10).** It says L_F[Y, Y] is positive
  definite "if H⁰(G, Y; F) = 0". By the paper's own definition, a section
  vanishing on A, the condition is H⁰(G, U; F) = 0, which is what the text
  before the theorem says for uniqueness.
- **Theorem 9.3's weights.** The statement uses αδ₀ᵀδ₀ − βx₀ᵀx₀ (read
  x₀x₀ᵀ). The proof defines M = δᵀδ − xxᵀ but differentiates
  αδᵀδ − βxxᵀ, and later writes tr(M) = ‖δ‖²_F − ‖x‖². A numerical check
  here, on a three-vertex path with two-dimensional vertex stalks, found
  the diagonal blocks of αδᵀδ − βxxᵀ conserved for α = 2, β = 0.5, and
  those of δᵀδ − xxᵀ not conserved. So the theorem holds with the
  weights, and the proof's M and trace are correct only when α = β.
- **Figure 6's labels.** The figure's final maps are −0.2 and 0.8. Theorem
  8.1's limit from the stated data (−4, +1, the constant sheaf) is
  −3/17 and 12/17. The ratio matches but the magnitudes do not, and the
  labels lie off the straight path the flow follows. They may be rounded
  or schematic. The paper does not say.
- **Theorem 6.1 proves stabilizability.** The following text says "we can
  ensure that the dynamics converge to any global section" (p. 12). That
  is argued from uniqueness of harmonic extension, not from a
  controllability theorem. Theorem 6.2 has no proof.
- **The nonlinear and joint systems are only partly analysed.** There is
  "no global asymptotic stability" for (9.1), and stability is shown only
  at smooth equilibria. The authors say deeper analysis is needed (p. 21).
- **Typos.** "which is in U by condition (1)" in the proof of Theorem 11.2
  (p. 20) should be W. The proof of Theorem 5.1 labels the kernel part y⊥
  and the image part y∥.
- The published SIAM text was not compared with the preprint.

## Open questions

- What would make expression learning answerable to something it does not
  control? For example, a penalty on ‖F_{v⊴e} − F⁰_{v⊴e}‖ held by the agent
  (fidelity to its own truthful map), or an edge observable scored
  against an outside signal. Neither is in the paper.
- Under joint diffusion, for which initial conditions do some of a
  vertex's expression maps go to zero, so that it stops communicating on
  some edges? The paper bounds x away from zero, not δ.
- Which global sections does joint diffusion select, given that its
  equilibria form a singular variety (p. 15)?
- Does anything like the "polite lie" sheaf of Example 4.2 describe
  observed discourse, and how would its restriction maps be measured?
