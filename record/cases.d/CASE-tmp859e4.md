# CASE-tmp859e4: Gracie jiu-jitsu against luta livre: two Brazilian grappling traditions whose contests were fought over the rules as well as under them, and whose matches placed them in a racial hierarchy as well as ranking them by results

## The case

The owner, at U35: "various contemporaneous and geographically related
schools of martial arts vying for (regional/ideological/demographical/
physical) dominance might be another useful case to examine."

Penglase ([LIT-tmpv9nx9](../literature.d/LIT-tmpv9nx9.md), unread) studies the challenge matches the Gracie
family fought against practitioners of luta livre, a Brazilian
catch-wrestling style, and argues, from its abstract, that the matches
located jiu-jitsu within Brazil's racial hierarchy as "white", usually
implicitly. The exchange read him further: A35, "the contrasting social
positions of the traditions significantly affected their relative
commercial and cultural fortunes". That reading is the exchange's.

The replies gave episodes from that history, which the record has not
checked against the paper: a 1931 contest between Carlos Gracie and Manoel
Rufino whose rules had to be negotiated first (time limits, whether a pin
counted, whether to wear a gi), and whose result was disputed; a 1991
challenge at the Grajaú Country Club, won by the jiu-jitsu fighters; and a
1997 bout between Renzo Gracie and Eugênio Tadeu stopped by a riot.

## What it shows

- **Contests over the test.** A35: "a contest between traditions requires
  them to negotiate **which world their techniques will be tested in**".
  Winning under a rule and influencing the rule are two forms of power,
  and "winning according to the rules doesn't by itself establish superior
  authority to determine the rules". This is [CLAIM-tmp6bxtr](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp6bxtr.md)'s case.
- **Standing is not read off results.** On the exchange's reading of
  Penglase, the traditions' social positions shaped which one prospered. A40: "Nor can
  we infer its technical superiority from its commercial or institutional
  success."
- **A perspective carried in bodies.** A35: "a perspective can be carried
  in trained bodies, not just institutions, concepts, or written rules".
  Trained practitioners may perceive different affordances in the same
  movement, which Wacquant's account of habitus ([LIT-tmpd4qxn](../literature.d/LIT-tmpd4qxn.md)) and his
  boxing ethnography already hold.
- **What persists can come apart.** A36 separated embodied, genealogical,
  institutional, perspectival and narrative continuity, "All five could
  come apart", and observed that "Two competing traditions can become
  technically more similar while becoming socially and ideologically more
  antagonistic." That is [CLAIM-tmp8lheg](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp8lheg.md)'s realization, character, lineage
  and representation, in a lineage of practice.

## What it does not show

- **Standing.** The rivalry and its matches are documented; the
  interpretation offered here rests on an unread paper and on the
  exchange's summary of it. `standing: documented` is the event's, not the
  reading's, until Penglase is read. (Added the same day after a review
  the owner relayed.)

- That either tradition was better, or that any contest showed it.
- That rules were changed in order to entrench a tradition. A36 kept that
  apart: "We should not infer the second from the first."
- The other martial-arts cases the replies raised: taekwondo's
  institutional unification and its narrative (Moenig and Kim), Chinese
  lineages (Cui-Laughton, Brummans and Cooren, doi:10.1177/08933189251395078),
  and rule changes in judo and taekwondo. Their sources were not
  registered, and A35 itself held the material back "until we have examined
  whether the existing martial arts studies literature already explains
  the proposed mechanisms better than our framework does".
