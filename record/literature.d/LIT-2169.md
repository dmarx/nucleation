# LIT-2169: Alternative states and positive feedbacks in restoration ecology

Suding, K. N., Gross, K. L. & Houseman, G. R. (2004). Alternative states and positive feedbacks in restoration ecology. *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 19(1): 46–53. doi:10.1016/j.tree.2003.10.005.

## Key takeaways

*Read in full on 2026-11-06 in [NOTE-1700](../notes.d/NOTE-1700.md). Page numbers are the journal's.*

- **Why restoring conditions can fail** (pp. 46–47). Degraded systems
  "cannot be restored to the previous conditions solely by
  re-establishing physical conditions or disturbance regimes". Fire put
  back after a 25-year hiatus "shifted community composition but did not
  return it to a barren community"; in semi-arid rangeland "reduction of
  grazing intensity does not restore the grass dominance". Where the
  successional route works, as in prairie potholes, it is conditional: "If
  hydrology can be restored and the seed bank has persisted".
- **Four constraints that hold the degraded state** (pp. 48–50): species
  effects on biogeochemistry (Hawaiian grasses "alter nitrogen cycling and
  promote fire, which further benefits introduced grasses"); trophic
  interactions (deer "selectively feed on regenerating saplings");
  landscape connectivity and seed source; and long-term regional or
  climatic change, which "will be more difficult, and perhaps impossible,
  to reverse through local management efforts".
- **Two routes back** (Box 2, p. 48). Lower the pressure past the lower
  threshold of a hysteresis loop, a recovery that "could be costly and
  slow" and "possibly could not occur at all"; or perturb the state
  variable directly. Fig. 2 (p. 49) sets out three cases: conditions
  restored and succession returns; conditions restored with the feedbacks
  intact, so the system goes elsewhere; feedbacks addressed first, after
  which "disturbance/abiotic re-establishment might be sufficient".
- **What management must do** (p. 50). "the internal feedbacks that
  constrain restoration of the degraded state should be disrupted and the
  system perturbed in ways that will facilitate the transition to the
  desired state". Actions on several constraints at once "often prove more
  successful than would either approach alone", and those on strong
  feedbacks "might need to be implemented before other tools will be
  effective". Cited effect sizes, none re-analysed: carbon addition
  "reduced weed biomass by 50% and increased the abundance of desirable
  prairie plants sevenfold"; topsoil on a mine site "increased species
  richness by 250%". On Hudson Bay, "Although the reduction of herbivory
  does not facilitate recovery, salt marshes can be restored when plugs of
  Puccinellia phryganodes, a former dominant species, are transplanted into
  the mudflats". In the desertification case (Fig. 1a, p. 47), once
  micro-catchments hold water, "grasses regenerate spontaneously, creating
  a structure that catches more water, thereby reversing desertification".
- **Whether the intervention can stop.** Only where a single constraint
  exists does restoring the regime "enable the rest of the system to
  self-organize and assemble with little or no further management
  intervention" (p. 50). Otherwise the cycle of Fig. 3 (p. 51) ends in
  maintenance, "which could involve very different actions than those
  required to shift states". No case measures a withdrawal.
- **The evidence, in the authors' words** (p. 48). "Rigorous tests of
  whether a degraded system truly represents an alternative and STABLE
  EQUILIBRIUM" "are beyond the scope of most restoration efforts"; the
  fallback is a decision rule, that "it might be more costly to assume that
  a single dimension controls system dynamics rather than that alternative
  states exist".

## Standing in the record

Active, read in full on 2026-11-06 in [NOTE-1700](../notes.d/NOTE-1700.md), which produces
[THEORY-1510](../theory.d/THEORY-1510.md). It is held as the paper most cited for the claim that
restoring conditions need not restore a degraded ecosystem because the
degraded state maintains itself: the ecological form of [CLAIM-463](../claims.d/CLAIM-463.md)
(Active), and support in kind for [CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md), whose collapse and
unrelightable outage it matches qualitatively (Box 2; Fig. 1a), with no
fitted model. It supports the separation in [CLAIM-566](../claims.d/CLAIM-566.md) (restored assembly
conditions do not regenerate the community without a surviving seed bank)
and, on combined and ordered interventions and a maintenance phase in
place of withdrawal, bears on [CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md). It bears on [QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md) with a
typology, not a measure, and on [QUESTION-399](../questions.d/QUESTION-399.md) and [TERM-117](../terms.d/TERM-117.md) through the
Puccinellia plugs and the micro-catchments, interventions that establish a
feedback which sustains itself; these are the same move as the record's
restoration-nucleation entries, [LIT-2179](LIT-2179.md) (applied nucleation), [LIT-2172](LIT-2172.md)
and [LIT-2177](LIT-2177.md). It contrasts with [CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md) only so far as its success cases
run the other way, and it measures no withdrawal. It applies to
restoration the models of [LIT-726](LIT-726.md) and [LIT-720](LIT-720.md); uses "resilience" of the
degraded state and in a speed-of-return sense, which [THEORY-132](../theory.d/THEORY-132.md)
distinguishes from Holling's; cites lost grazers on reefs, consistent with
[THEORY-128](../theory.d/THEORY-128.md); and concedes the bar of [THEORY-136](../theory.d/THEORY-136.md), which its evidence of
persistence after restored conditions does not meet.
