# NOTE-007: From the origin of life to pandemics

## Contribution

When many similar units interact locally with one another and their environment, unexpected patterns can arise at larger spatial and temporal scales. This is emergence, often tied to collective behaviour and seen in systems from synchronizing oscillators to flocks and schools. Despite abundant examples, there is no widely accepted rigorous definition and no agreed account of the conditions that favour emergence. The article gives a short overview, lists current and future challenges, and introduces and synthesizes the theme issue's contributions, from the origin of life to disease spread.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- Structure (contents): introduction, historical overview, what emergence is and why it matters (a survey spanning quantum, physical, biological, ecological, social and urban systems, with a network-science focus), a mathematical definition, a summary of the theme issue, and conclusions.
- Framing (§3): characterizing emergence requires at least two well-separated scales and an observer who identifies patterns at one scale and not the other. Mass (additive, with a nuclear binding-energy exception) is the worked non-emergent example.
- Definition (§4, Fig. 1): the authors adopt the weak/strong split (Chalmers; Bedau) and treat only weak emergence as scientific. Their operational picture is local mechanistic rules plus an initial condition evolving to a high-level pattern, in a Game-of-Life-style schematic; not all rule/initial-condition pairs produce such patterns.
- Outlook (§6) names open problems: finding common generative rules across domains, and the inverse problem of predicting macro outcomes from micro rules ("new universality classes"). Applications named include robot swarms and "swarm learning" (cited as outperforming federated learning).
