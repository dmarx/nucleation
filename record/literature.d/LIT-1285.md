# LIT-1285: What's Wrong with Copying?

Drassinower, A. (2015). *What's Wrong with Copying?* Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press.

Not read. The summary is drawn from the publisher's description (as
reprinted for the Bournemouth roundtable of 22 May 2015), Jessica
Silbey's review (IP Law Book Review 7, 2017, abstract), James A. Hayes's
book note (Osgoode Hall Law Journal 53.3, 2016, abstract) and Rebecca
Tushnet's notes of the Notre Dame roundtable (2 October 2015). A reading
should check it first.

## What the sources say it argues

- **Against the economic frame.** The publisher's description says the
  book "disentangles copyright theory from its focus on the economic value
  of an authored work as a commodity or piece of property". Silbey says it
  "shoves to the side the question of copyright incentives" and reads
  copyright on its own terms, from its cases since the Statute of Anne.
- **A work is speech.** The work is framed "as a communicative act", and
  infringement as "an unauthorized appropriation of another person's
  speech" (publisher). Hayes lists the parts: authors as speakers, works
  as communication, audiences as interlocutors, and the public domain as
  conditions of equality between authors and users.
- **Rights end where response begins.** The rights reach only
  communicative uses, and only uses consistent with others' communicative
  rights: "unauthorized reproduction of a work is lawful when responding
  to the work requires its reproduction" (publisher). At the roundtable,
  Wendy Gordon put the test as whether the defendant duplicated the
  plaintiff's speech for the same purposes, not whether a physical copy
  was made. A purely mechanical use, such as search indexing, is on this
  account not a use of the work as speech.
- **Objections raised.** At Notre Dame, Gordon questioned the cost of a
  standard tied to copyright's purposes and the value of freezing an
  author's speech; Pamela Samuelson said the book does not account for
  transfers; Shyam Balganesh pressed whether its method is rationalist.

## Why this is here

Filed at the owner's request on 2026-10-20 as contemporary work in
conversation with Lessig's *Remix* ([LIT-1298](LIT-1298.md)) at the intersection of
property and expression. Lessig argues from the copy: every digital use
makes one, so copyright reaches every use ([NOTE-1023](../notes.d/NOTE-1023.md)). Drassinower
argues that copying as such is not the wrong, which is the opposite
starting point to the same complaint.

It would be read for the record's line on what a work is across its
renderings. If a work is a communicative act, then [CLAIM-002](../claims.d/CLAIM-002.md) (a text's
identity across retellings as identity of artifact, continuity of
transmission and similarity of communicative function) has a legal
counterpart, and the line between using a work as speech and using it
mechanically is a candidate for [CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md)'s line between being a
rendering and only sharing a kind. Both bear on [QUESTION-002](../questions.d/QUESTION-002.md) (what
survives translation, retelling or rendering). Whether the book says
anything usable on those points is not known until it is read.
