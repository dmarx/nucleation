# NOTE-1150: Foerster et al. — Learning with opponent-learning awareness

## Contribution

Learners that each follow their own gradient "simply treat the other agent
as a static part of the environment" (p. 1), and in the iterated
prisoner's dilemma they learn to defect. LOLA gives a learner a model of
the other's learning and lets it choose its own parameters partly for
their effect on the other's next update. "To the best of our knowledge,
LOLA is the first method that aims to shape the learning of other agents
in a multi-agent RL setting" (p. 3).

## Key insight

Replace agent 1's objective V¹(θ¹, θ²) with V¹(θ¹, θ² + Δθ²), where
Δθ² = η∇θ²V²(θ¹, θ²) is the opponent's naive step. To first order this is
V¹ + (Δθ²)ᵀ∇θ²V¹. Differentiating in θ¹ while holding ∇θ²V¹ fixed gives
the LOLA update

    θ¹ ← θ¹ + δ∇θ¹V¹ + δη (∇θ²V¹)ᵀ ∇θ¹∇θ²V²   (Eq. 4.4),

the naive gradient plus a term that moves θ¹ in whatever direction makes
the opponent's next step better for agent 1 (re-derived when read). The
dependence of ∇θ²V¹ on θ¹ "is dropped during the backward pass" (p. 3), a
choice the authors leave for future work.

## Assumptions

- **Two agents** in the derivations, said to generalise (p. 3).
- **The opponent is a naive gradient learner** with a known step size;
  first-order LOLA models nothing deeper (Section 4.5).
- **Access to the opponent's parameters**, or a behavioural clone of them
  fitted by maximum likelihood to its trajectories (Section 4.4).
- **Exact values** for the iterated games: memory-one policies of five
  probabilities each, with V computed in closed form (supplement A.2);
  policy-gradient estimates elsewhere, with learning only between batches
  of 4000 episodes (p. 5).
- **Discounting** γ = 0.96 for the IPD and Coin Game, 0.9 for matching
  pennies (p. 6).

## Key results

- **IPD and IMP (Table 3, p. 6).** Fifty runs each.

  | | IPD %TFT | IPD return | IMP %Nash | IMP return (std) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | NL-Ex | 20.8 | −1.98 | 0.0 | 0 (0.37) |
  | LOLA-Ex | 81.0 | −1.06 | 98.8 | 0 (0.02) |
  | NL-PG | 20.0 | −1.98 | 13.2 | 0 (0.19) |
  | LOLA-PG | 66.4 | −1.17 | 93.2 | 0 (0.06) |

  Mutual defection returns −2 per step and tit-for-tat −1 (p. 5).
- **Tournament (Figure 4).** Against naive Q-learning, joint-action
  learning, policy hill-climbing and WoLF-PHC, LOLA-Ex had the highest
  average return in the IPD and middling, within error bars, in IMP.
- **Shaping and exploitation (Table 4, p. 8).** NL-Ex vs NL-Ex −1.99 each;
  LOLA-Ex vs NL-Ex −1.28 for LOLA, −1.54 for the naive learner; LOLA vs
  LOLA −1.04 each; 2nd-order LOLA vs LOLA −1.14 and −1.17. "there is an
  incentive for either agent to switch from naive learning to first order
  LOLA" (p. 7), and the higher order gains nothing.
- **Coin Game (Figure 5).** LOLA-PG pairs picked up their own colour about
  80% of the time, naive pairs indiscriminately; with opponent modelling
  about 60%, "less stable".

## Concepts

- **Naive learner**: one that ascends its own value at the current joint
  parameters, the other's held fixed (Eq. 4.1).
- **Opponent-learning awareness**: optimising one's return after the
  other's anticipated update, and differentiating through it.
- **Opponent shaping**: the effect of the added term, changing the
  direction of the other's learning to one's own advantage; the paper's
  word is "shape".
- **Higher-order LOLA**: modelling the opponent as itself a LOLA learner,
  which needs third derivatives (Section 4.5).

## Connections

Related to opponent modelling and fictitious play, but those predict and
best-respond and "do not address the learning dynamics of opponents"
(p. 2). Closest in setting to Lerer & Peysakhovich's hand-built
tit-for-tat; in spirit to Metz et al.'s unrolled GANs, which also
differentiate through an opponent's steps. Zhang & Lesser predict the
opponent's update but do not differentiate through it. Later work on the
consistency and stability of LOLA exists but was not read here.

## Bearing on the record

- **The lead's description, "a learning-aware model of authority shaping
  others' learning" ([CASE-124](../cases.d/CASE-124.md), [CLAIM-448](../claims.d/CLAIM-448.md)), holds as an analogy with a
  precise mechanism.** [CASE-124](../cases.d/CASE-124.md)'s ε arrow has authority move the regime
  through which others' evidence is read. LOLA's second-order term is a
  formal version of acting for one's effect on another's update rather
  than on the present outcome. The match is the form of the derivative.
  The difference is the channel: LOLA acts through its own policy, which is
  the other's environment, not through a regime that filters the other's
  evidence.
- **But the paper's setting is symmetric.** Both agents shape and both are
  shaped. Its headline result is that mutual shaping produced
  reciprocity, not capture. The one asymmetric pairing, LOLA against a
  naive learner, left both better off than two naive learners (−1.28 and
  −1.54 against −1.99), with the shaper ahead. So it does not support the
  record's worry that a shaping process entrenches itself; it shows that
  shaping another's learning can be the route to cooperation. Nothing here
  models a shaper with authority over the other's evidence.
- **[CLAIM-448](../claims.d/CLAIM-448.md) (selective contestation).** Complicates it slightly.
  LOLA is a case in which one party deliberately acts on another's
  learning and the outcome is better for both. Influence on the other's
  learning is not by itself evidence of suppression; what the claim needs
  is influence on which contests are recorded.
- **[CLAIM-451](../claims.d/CLAIM-451.md), [TERM-077](../terms.d/TERM-077.md) and [THEORY-894](../theory.d/THEORY-894.md) (cycles).** A direct link,
  mine. Matching pennies is the textbook harmonic game, all cycle and no
  potential, and naive gradient learners there "fail to converge" (p. 7).
  LOLA learners settled at the mixed equilibrium (98.8% of exact runs).
  The authors' reading: "LOLA agents anticipate that exploiting a
  deviation from Nash increases their immediate return, but also renders
  them more exploitable by the opponent's next learning step" (p. 5). So
  modelling another's learning is one way the cycling of a nonintegrable
  negotiation can be relieved: a fourth candidate beside [CLAIM-451](../claims.d/CLAIM-451.md)'s
  accommodation, entrenched precedence and cheap coordination, or a form
  of accommodation. Shown in one two-action game.
- **[CLAIM-444](../claims.d/CLAIM-444.md) and [CLAIM-443](../claims.d/CLAIM-443.md) (authority dynamics; a learned
  prior over governance).** A model class the record lacks: processes
  whose influence on each other runs through each other's learning rules,
  not only through scores. The replicator reading treats processes as
  scored by a fixed environment; LOLA is the case where the environment
  includes a learner one can steer.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice is drawn here.

## Limitations

- **Small games and one-step shaping.** Two players, memory-one iterated
  games, one grid game. The opponent is modelled one naive step ahead.
- **An inconsistency in Table 3.** NL-Ex is reported as 20.8% tit-for-tat,
  yet its return is −1.98, the mutual-defection value, and the text says
  naive agents "learn to defect in all states" (p. 7). A fifth of runs at
  tit-for-tat would raise the mean return well above −1.98. NL-PG has the
  same pattern. What the %TFT column counts for naive learners is unclear.
- **Exploitability is tested only against gradient learners** and at one
  step size (δ = 0.5); the authors expect LOLA to be exploitable by other
  means (p. 8).
- **Opponent parameters.** The main results assume them; the clone is
  noisier and works worse (Figure 5).
- **A slip.** The IPD transition matrix in supplement A.2 has (θ¹ − 1)θ²
  for (1 − θ¹)θ². It does not affect the results.

## Open questions

- Does opponent shaping produce entrenchment when it is asymmetric in
  capacity, one side modelling the other's learning and the other not, or
  one side controlling what the other observes? The paper's one asymmetric
  pairing is too small to say.
- What happens with more than two learners, where shaping one may
  disadvantage a third?
