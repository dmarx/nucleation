# CASE-279: Two couples, stipulated

## The case

A3 §1 of the exchange (https://chatgpt.com/share/6afa2a8d-2598-83ea-8d1a-d9ceb992a99c),
in A's words.

**Relationship A.** "Alice and Bob have a terrible relationship. They argue
constantly, insult each other, get jealous, and occasionally shove or slap
one another during heated confrontations. Neither is especially frightened
of the other outside those episodes. Neither systematically controls the
other's friendships, money, movements, or decisions. Both feel free to
leave".

**Relationship B.** "Carol and Dan also have physical fights. Perhaps Carol
has even struck Dan more times than Dan has struck Carol. But Dan controls
their finances, monitors Carol's messages, threatens to destroy her
reputation if she leaves, isolates her from friends, and punishes her
whenever she challenges him. Carol sometimes lashes out violently. Yet she
lives in continual fear of what Dan will do next, while Dan can generally
behave however he likes."

What is stipulated: in B, Carol commits as many wrongful violent acts as
Dan or more, and Dan alone holds control over the terms of Carol's life
(money, communication, friendships, exit). In A, both commit wrongful acts
and neither holds that control.

A3 §5 restates Relationship B as "an extreme illustration" in a table
(its Person A is Carol's position, its Person B Dan's), carried here from
the continuation's filing of the same turns:

| Question | Person A | Person B |
|---|---|---|
| Number of times they struck their partner | 4 | 2 |
| Threatened to kill their partner | No | Yes |
| Controls their partner's finances | No | Yes |
| Prevents their partner seeing friends | No | Yes |
| Afraid of their partner | Yes | No |

"If you only counted strikes, A would appear more violent. If you examined
the full pattern, B might emerge as the primary coercive aggressor." A3
§3's six questions (can each say no, keep friends, spend money, leave the
room, disagree, leave the relationship) are what the second column
answers: they ask about a capacity to set terms, not about acts.

## What it can show

That the count of wrongful acts and the distribution of control can come
apart in either direction: A has mutual wrongdoing with no domination, and
B has the more frequent wrongdoer as the dominated party. A3 names the
pattern in Johnson's typology (situational couple violence, coercive
controlling violence, violent resistance, mutual violent control), which
the record registers unread ([LIT-2715](../literature.d/LIT-2715.md)), with Stark on coercive control ([LIT-2675](../literature.d/LIT-2675.md)).

## What it cannot show

- **That any real couple is B.** It is fictional, and A3 §6 lists the
  limits of applying the typology to real relationships: motives are
  opaque, power is domain-specific, relationships move between patterns,
  and the typology's empirical support is mixed.
- **That Carol's acts are excused.** The case fixes who did what and who
  controlled what; it says nothing about how much blame Carol's acts carry,
  which A3 keeps as a separate question ("Experiencing abuse does not give
  someone unlimited moral permission to hurt their partner").
- **Anything about the Depp and Heard dispute,** where the exchange began.
  The curation entry of 2026-11-15 records why no case is filed for it.

## Where it sits

[CASE-264](CASE-264.md) is the same contrast for an organization: two
workshops equal in dependence and different in who controls the terms. This
case varies the count of wrongful acts as well as control, and so separates
a thing [CASE-264](CASE-264.md) holds equal.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Cunha and colleagues, read

The case's own caution, "Counting physical assaults could make Carol appear to be the more violent partner", names a known property of the field's main instrument. This review documents it ("This limitation can lead to the misclassification of individuals who act in self-defense as engaged in BV"). The review shows the case's contrast is a live measurement problem, not only a thought experiment. It says nothing about how often Relationship B's pattern occurs. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Lohmann, read

The case stipulates control as a dimension separate from the count of assaults. The review treats coercive control the same way, as its own construct, but finds its measured associations with PTSD and depression "comparable to those involving psychological IPV". So the distinction the case draws is not yet one the field's instruments reliably separate. The exchange's gloss on this review (A5 §1, "sustained control can be profoundly harmful even when physical assaults aren't the central feature") is more than the review shows. It did not separate control from co-occurring physical violence. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Mazinani and O'Connor, read

Relationship B, in which the controlled partner strikes more often, is coherent as stipulated. The review reports one population-level finding against its being a common route: "Spencer et al. (2022) found that male partners’ controlling behaviors were not significantly associated with women’s IPV perpetration." This qualifies any use of the case as typical. It does not touch its use as an existence proof ([ARG-113](../arguments.d/ARG-113.md)). Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Johnson, read

In Johnson's terms (2006 article, read as the stand-in for the 2008 book), Relationship A is situational couple
violence and Relationship B is intimate terrorism by Dan with violent resistance by Carol. The case's stipulation that
"Carol has even struck Dan more times than Dan has struck Carol" is coherent under a classification by control, but
atypical: in Johnson's data, "In men’s intimate terrorism, wives rarely respond with violence", the median ratio of
the wife's acts to the husband's being .17. The case shows what is possible, not what is common, which is all
[ARG-113](../arguments.d/ARG-113.md) uses it for. The case's "Where it sits" names Johnson's four types with A3's label "coercive controlling
violence"; Johnson's own term is intimate terrorism (the other label is Kelly and Johnson's, 2008). Relation:
draws_on (the case uses the typology's categories) from the case to the THEORY, if the filer wants it; otherwise none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Stark and Hester, read

Dan's tactics in Relationship B, finances, monitoring, isolation and threats about leaving, are the control tactics of
the definition Stark and Hester quote ("isolating them from sources of support, exploiting their resources and capacities for personal" gain, "depriving them of the means needed for independence, resistance and escape"). The case
can cite the review for its sense of control. Relation: draws_on, if the filer wants it.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Anderson, read

In the stipulated second couple, the partner who strikes more often is the
controlled one. The SEP entry on coercion ([LIT-2673](../literature.d/LIT-2673.md), §3.2) reports
Galoob and Sheley's proposal that such a partner, if directed by the
controller to act unlawfully, should have a defence "when the coercer has
usurped the target’s decisional authority". The proposal covers acts the
controller directs. It does not cover the controlled partner's own strikes
against the controller, which the entry does not discuss. Relation: if the
case's method draws on the coercive-control notion, `draws_on:
[LIT-2673]` would be fitting. Otherwise none.

## Note of 2026-11-17: Johnson's typology read through neighbours, and a documented group with control and no violence

Johnson 2006, Hardesty et al. 2015, Mazinani & O'Connor 2026 and Stark 2015, read ([THEORY-1961](../theory.d/THEORY-1961.md), [THEORY-1991](../theory.d/THEORY-1991.md), [THEORY-1929](../theory.d/THEORY-1929.md), [THEORY-1986](../theory.d/THEORY-1986.md)). The case's "Michael P. Johnson's typology as A relays it" can now cite Johnson's own words, though the 2008 book ([LIT-2715](../literature.d/LIT-2715.md)) is unread.

- **Carol's strikes are violent resistance in Johnson's terms**: "Violent resistance is the violence involved when a victim of intimate terrorism fights back" (Johnson 2006, [NOTE-2066](../notes.d/NOTE-2066.md), p. 3). The case goes further than Johnson: her acts keep their own verdict. Johnson says only that a case "can sometimes be made that the violence was justified".
- **Mazinani & O'Connor** say what the case needs about her acts: where intimate terrorism holds, "a victim’s resistance or constrained responses should not be treated as equivalent to the perpetrator’s controlling violence".
- **Hardesty et al.** document a plainer divergence of the two questions: a "nonviolent coercive control" group (n = 12 of 190 divorcing mothers), high control with no violence at all ([NOTE-2046](../notes.d/NOTE-2046.md)).
- **Stark (2015)**: Dan's control of money, monitoring and isolation are Stark's tactics of "isolation and control".

If the owner wants the case to name its classification, `draws_on` [THEORY-1961](../theory.d/THEORY-1961.md) (Johnson's three types) is the relation for it; that is the case's own field and is left to the lead. The fourth type in A's table, mutual violent control, is not confirmed by anything read.
