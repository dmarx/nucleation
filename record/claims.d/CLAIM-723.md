# CLAIM-723: Acting before a recognized opportunity for intervention can take effect is a wrong of its own, apart from acting without consent: where a party has standing to authorize, suspend or contest an act, an actor who without adequate justification makes the act's consequences irreversible before that standing can be exercised defeats it, even where the objection would have failed

## The claim

The owner, U27 of the exchange
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b), after
A55 and A57 on fait accompli breach:

> would it help if we formalized the notion of consent violation? maybe something like acting before a recognized opportunity for intervention is able to take effect? It feels like we're moving towards something like a formal account of (a type of) bullying ("wrongful unilateral action"?).

A59 accepted it "with one important adjustment": "**The fundamental
wrong may not be acting without consent. It may be acting in a way that
defeats another party's legitimate authority to determine, constrain, or
contest what happens to them.**" Consent is one such authority; notice,
refusal, negotiation, suspension and prior review are others. So the
owner's criterion is "especially well suited to a subtype of the
problem", which A called "**preemptive circumvention of intervention
rights**".

**Three wrongs** (A59 §1), for a renovation that needs B's approval to
demolish a wall: unauthorized action (A demolishes without asking),
disregarded refusal (B refuses and A demolishes), and preemptive
foreclosure (A demolishes overnight, knowing B could object in the
morning). The third adds something: "**A has arranged events so that an
objection cannot arrive in time to matter.**" And "**absence of an
objection and absence of an effective opportunity to object are different
facts**".

**Three kinds of standing** (A59 §2): "**Consent authority:** B must
authorize the action." "**Intervention authority:** B is entitled to
challenge, suspend, constrain, or seek review of the action, but need not
have a final veto." "**Consultative standing:** B is entitled to
participate meaningfully in the decision, without necessarily possessing
either authorization or suspension power." "The relevant violation
depends on which kind of standing exists." The owner's criterion covers
all three, since each is an opportunity for intervention.

**The conditions** (A59 §3). With N the governing standard, T_opportunity
the earliest time B could exercise a protected intervention and
T_foreclosure the time A's act makes it ineffective, the asymmetry
T_foreclosure < T_opportunity "alone does not establish wrongdoing". A's
four conditions: "**Protected standing:**", "**Foreclosure:**",
"**Attributable circumvention:** A's conduct, timing, withholding of
notice, or control over execution causes or materially contributes to the
loss of that opportunity", and "**Lack of adequate justification:**". It
"identifies a wrong even where the eventual substantive decision might
have gone the same way": "The process may protect B's standing as a
participant in the decision, not merely improve the probability of a
different outcome."

**The order of moves** (A59 §6). An authority-respecting game runs A
proposes, B consents or objects, outcome. A fait accompli runs A executes,
alternatives foreclosed, B objects or settles. "So we must distinguish
*strategic first-mover advantage* from *wrongfully appropriated
first-mover advantage*." "**The violation changes the game in which the
violation will subsequently be judged.**" A59 §4 calls the doubled wrong
"**an agency-foreclosing violation**": a first-order violation, and
interference with "the second-order capability through which B could
make that obligation binding in practice". A cites *Fuentes v. Shevin*,
407 U.S. 67 (1972), for the point that a later hearing cannot undo a
deprivation made without the process required before it (unread here;
https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/407/67/).

## The bullying mechanism

The owner proposed that the criterion moves toward "a formal account of
(a type of) bullying". A59 §5 narrowed it, since standard definitions of
bullying include "unwanted aggression, a power imbalance, and repetition
or the likelihood of repetition", and a single fait accompli has none of
them: "So I wouldn't define bullying as wrongful unilateral action." A's
version:

> **One mechanism of bullying is the repeated use of asymmetric capabilities to impose consequential changes while making the target's resistance ineffective, costly, or socially punishable.**

A's case: a manager publishes schedules without the required notice, and
answers an objection with "*The schedule is already published. Everyone
else has rearranged their plans. Are you really going to cause problems
for the team?*" The act changes the schedule, makes reversal costly, casts
the objection as the disruption and enlists the employee's obligations to
coworkers. "**The manager acts first, creates dependencies on the new
arrangement, and then uses those dependencies to make resistance appear
irresponsible.**" Repeated, it teaches that objection is futile. The
proposal that the wrong yields an account of a type of bullying is the
owner's; the narrowing to one mechanism, which the owner's "(a type of)"
already allowed, is A's.

## Wrongful unilateral action, the family

The owner's name, "wrongful unilateral action", is kept here for the
family. A59 §7 and its closing proposition called it "**Wrongful
unilateral imposition**": an actor "exercises consequential power over
another's protected interests in a manner that unjustifiably bypasses,
overrides, or undermines the affected party's legitimate authority over
the action or the conditions governing their participation." A59 §9 lists
its kinds: unauthorized imposition, overriding, preemptive foreclosure
(this claim), remedial hold-up ([CLAIM-716](CLAIM-716.md)), "**Counter-corrective
domination:** Responding to challenge by weakening the channels through
which later challenges could succeed" ([CLAIM-619](CLAIM-619.md)), and bullying by fait
accompli. Each calls for a different response: consent protections;
notice, standstill or suspension; preventive remedies; independent
enforcement and protection against retaliation; a change in who holds
discretion.

**Attributions.** The phrase "wrongful unilateral action" is A57's first
("I think we're approaching an account of **the political economy of
wrongful unilateral action**"); the owner's U27 adopted it, with a question
mark, as the name of the account. A59 §7 opens its cost-of-resistance
extension with "Your suggestion also points toward something beyond
temporal preemption", but U27 says nothing of costs; that extension is
A's, and the owner's own extension to costs came next, at U28
([CLAIM-748](CLAIM-748.md)). A59 §8's "the normative-morphology method you've been
advocating" credits the owner's U19 method correctly ([CLAIM-718](CLAIM-718.md)); the
name is A's.

## How it sits with the record

- **[CLAIM-464](CLAIM-464.md)** (Active) holds the timing: an intervention can change
  what it would take to correct it before it is reviewed. It is causal and
  says nothing about standing. This claim adds that when the outcome made
  unreachable is a protected opportunity to intervene, defeating it is a
  wrong in itself.
- **[CLAIM-624](CLAIM-624.md)** and **[CASE-229](../cases.d/CASE-229.md)** (Proposed): a procedural wrong that does
  not reduce to the harm, where an institution disables its challenge
  procedure and the remedy comes from outside. The futile objection is the
  same structure for one act. A59's last paragraph separates the act from
  "the wrong of constructing a relationship in which such acts can
  routinely prevail", which is [CLAIM-624](CLAIM-624.md)'s.
- **[CLAIM-523](CLAIM-523.md)** (Active): correctability is achievement within deadline
  and resources. This claim says when a missed deadline is someone's
  doing.
- **[TERM-124](../terms.d/TERM-124.md)** (Proposed): domination as unanswerable capacity to set the
  terms on which a party can contest. Repeated preemption is how such a
  capacity is exercised.
- **[QUESTION-454](../questions.d/QUESTION-454.md)** (Open): authority to decide for one with standing who
  cannot decide. A59 §8's parent and emergency cases sit there.

## What it does not say

- Not that unilateral action is wrong. A59 §8: "**unilateralism alone is
  not the wrong**". The emergency override (absence of consent is not
  absence of valid authority), the unauthorized veto (B lacks standing)
  and the justified restriction of a child fail its conditions.
- Not that every standing is a veto. Intervention authority and
  consultative standing are defeated by preemption too, and neither
  carries a veto.
- Not that the law recognizes the test. A59: "This is a candidate normative
  definition, not a legal test already recognized by courts."

## Note of 2026-11-17: Fuentes v. Shevin, read

Fuentes v. Shevin (1972), read ([NOTE-2072](../notes.d/NOTE-2072.md), [THEORY-1972](../theory.d/THEORY-1972.md)), which the claim cites from A59 as unread. The Court locates the wrong in the timing of the opportunity: it "must be granted at a time when the deprivation can still be prevented", because "no later hearing and no damage award can undo the fact that the arbitrary taking that was subject to the right of procedural due process has already occurred" (Part IV). It holds the right independent of the merits, "The right to be heard does not depend upon an advance showing that one will surely prevail at the hearing" (Part V), which is the claim's "even where the objection would have failed". It counts a temporary deprivation as one. Its "extraordinary situations", which "must be truly unusual" (Part VI), correspond to the claim's "without adequate justification". Scope: a constitutional rule about state-assisted seizures of property from a possessor, decided 4–3 and called "a narrow one" by the Court; it supports the claim as a legal analogue, not as a moral principle, and its later doctrinal standing was not checked. A59's wording is a close paraphrase of Part IV, not a quotation. No status changes.
