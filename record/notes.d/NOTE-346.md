# NOTE-346: Dennett — Facing Backwards on the Problem of Consciousness

## Contribution

It is the first published deflationary reply to the hard problem, written for
the JCS symposium on Chalmers's paper. It gives the hard problem's critics
their standard analogies: vitalism, a fictional neuroscientist "Crock" who
postpones perception, and "cutism". It also states the evidential
challenge, that a fundamental posit needs data that drove us to it.

## Key insight

Chalmers defines the hard problem as what is left after every function is
explained. Dennett asks what anyone ever had in mind when they wondered about
their own consciousness, and lists only functions. If that list is complete,
the "hard problem" is the residue of an act of subtraction that leaves
nothing, and the felt residue is a belief that something is left.

## Assumptions

- **The introspective catalogue is exhaustive.** That everything that
  impresses one about one's own consciousness is "'merely' the 'performance
  of functions' or the manifestation of various complex dispositions to
  perform functions" (p. 5). It is offered from the author's own case.
- **Theoretical posits need independent evidence** (p. 6), as mass and charge
  had. This is a standard of scientific practice, stated, not argued.
- **Analogy carries weight.** The vitalist and Crock cases are taken to have
  the same structure as Chalmers's; Chalmers's disanalogy is what the paper
  disputes.

## Key results

- **Vitalism (§1).** Quoting *Consciousness Explained*, pp. 281–282: the
  vitalist's "residual mystery of life" is an imagination that has misfired.
- **Crock (§2).** A neuroscientist who said he had brushed perception aside
  would have created an artifactual hard problem, the error of "vision as
  television". The paper says that postponing qualia is the same mistake,
  and that unless experience is broken into its functional components "from
  the outset" one creates "an imaginary dazzle in the eye of a Cartesian
  homunculus" (p. 5, citing Dennett 1995, a review of Damasio's *Descartes'
  Error*).
- **"Almost unique."** Chalmers says the hard problem is "almost unique" in
  going beyond functions, but names no other case; his support "simply
  repeats the claim in different words" (p. 5). The quoted passage is
  Chalmers 1995, p. 203.
- **The vitalism disanalogy rejected.** Chalmers (p. 209) says that the
  vitalist doubted that mechanisms could perform the functions, while for
  experience the explanation of functions is not in question. Dennett: "I
  submit that he is flatly mistaken". It is the remarkable functions that
  make people wonder.
- **Cutism (p. 6).** Nobody would take vitalism seriously had it not had
  independently describable phenomena to explain. Cuteness posited as
  fundamental would have none. Unless Chalmers gives "an independent ground"
  for adding experience to mass, charge and space-time, the proposal goes
  on "the back burner". Roberts (1995) is quoted: "The evidential argument
  is circular."

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | The easy/hard distinction misdirects research and generates illusions | weak–moderate | by analogy with vitalism and with "Crock" (pp. 4–5) |
| C2 | Chalmers's reason why experience is unlike life restates the claim | moderate as a reading of Chalmers 1995 pp. 203, 209 | close reading of two quoted passages |
| C3 | When every function is subtracted from one's own consciousness, nothing is left but a conviction | assertion | the author's introspective catalogue (p. 5) |
| C4 | Positing experience as fundamental requires independent evidence of the kind physics had for mass and charge, and none is given | moderate as a challenge | the cutism analogy; Roberts 1995 |

## Concepts

- **Crock.** An imaginary neuroscientist who, substituting "perception" for
  Crick's "qualia", brushes perception aside to work on the easy problems.
- **Cutism.** The proposal to add cuteness to the fundamental properties of
  physics, because "some things are just plain cute" and it is hard to
  describe or explain (p. 6). A reductio for posits without independent
  evidence.

## Limitations

- **Three pages.** It is a symposium reply; every argument is compressed and
  C3 is asserted.
- **Analogies are contestable.** Chalmers's §5 already argued that vitalism
  is disanalogous; this paper repeats that it is analogous and does not add
  an argument about what makes it so, beyond C3.
- **No positive theory here.** It points to *Consciousness Explained* for
  one.

## Open questions

- What would count, for Dennett, as the "independent ground" Chalmers owes?
  The paper does not say what data could support a fundamental posit about
  experience, so it is unclear whether the challenge could be met in
  principle.
- How did Chalmers answer? His "Moving Forward on the Problem of
  Consciousness" (JCS 4(1), 1997) replies to this symposium and is not
  filed.
