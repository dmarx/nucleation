# LIT-567: A Planning Agent's Self-Governance over Time

Michael E. Bratman (2018), "A Planning Agent's Self-Governance over Time",
essay 11 in *Planning, Time, and Self-Governance: Essays in Practical
Rationality*, New York: Oxford University Press — DOI-10.1093/oso/9780190867850.003.0011.
Reprinted in *Time in Action* (Routledge, 2022), pp. 75–107. Read in the
author's draft of 10 July 2017.

## Key takeaways

- **What diachronic self-governance is.** It needs synchronic
  self-governance along the way, which is guidance by a practical standpoint
  "sufficiently coherent to constitute a clear place where the agent stands".
  It is "not merely a concatenation" of that, and needs "appropriate
  inter-connections across time". Ulysses at the mast manages himself over
  time but does not govern himself.
- **The glue is shared agency within one life.** In planned activity, an
  intention at t1 to A at t2 interlocks with the intention at t2. The two
  intend mutual responsiveness and meshing sub-plans, depend on each other,
  expect success, and are out in the open. These are intrapersonal analogues
  of Bratman's conditions (A)–(E) for shared intention. So in diachronic
  self-governance the agent at each time is acting "together" with herself
  at other times. The basic unity is this, not "cross-temporal narrative
  unity".
- **Willpower without a bargain.** Willpower in temptation, and resistance
  to "shuffling" between options one judges non-comparable (Sartre's young
  man), are self-governed when the agent's standpoint includes a
  "conditional, reflexive end" of her own diachronic self-governance. That
  end is not a taste for "mere cross-temporal psychic tidiness". The plan's
  time-frame runs to "plan's end" and includes one's later reflection. That
  is why sticking to a resolution against temptation is supported and
  drinking Kavka's toxin is not: one anticipates no regret in the first case
  and regret in the second.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, as the read statement of
Bratman's planning theory of self-governance over time. It stands in for
*Planning, Time, and Self-Governance* (2018), which is closed and is not
seeded separately: by its note 1 this essay develops the account of
self-governance over time sketched in the book's essay 10, "Rational Planning
Agency". It also stands in for
the planning theory of *Intention, Plans, and Practical Reason* (1987,
[LIT-593](LIT-593.md), Deferred), which it summarises in its first paragraphs: plans
that "settle practical issues, pose problems of means and the like, filter
solutions to those problems, and guide action".

It **extends** three earlier works in the LIT scheme's sense, each of which it
needs:

- [LIT-593](LIT-593.md), for the planning theory itself: the settling, filtering and
  stability of intention, the snowball effect, and the rational
  non-reconsideration it cites.
- [LIT-596](LIT-596.md) ("Three Theories of Self-Governance", another reader's
  filing in this batch), for its account of synchronic self-governance. The
  plan-infused standpoints and "self-governing policies" it relies on are
  cited to that essay.
- [LIT-488](LIT-488.md) (*Shared Agency*, 2014), whose conditions for shared intention are
  the model for the intrapersonal analogues. Without them the central
  argument has no content.

It is a **rival** of Ainslie's picoeconomics ([LIT-586](LIT-586.md)). Both explain
willpower against temptation, and the accounts exclude each other. For
Ainslie, will is a recursive self-prediction in an intertemporal bargaining
game among successive interests. Bratman rejects the bargaining model. He
cites Ainslie's *Picoeconomics* (1992) together with McClennen's bargain
between "time-defined selves", and says a bargain "seems to involve a kind of
reciprocal inter-relation that is not possible here", because the earlier
self "will not be around" to respond. His alternative is an end of
diachronic self-governance in the agent's standpoint. Neither tests the
other. The account built on each is filed: [THEORY-076](../theory.d/THEORY-076.md) on this essay,
[THEORY-067](../theory.d/THEORY-067.md) on Ainslie's précis.

Tags. `self-governance` and `agency` follow the owner's distinction.
`personhood` is for cross-temporal agency: plans induce "psychological
connectedness and continuity of intention and plan that are in the spirit of
broadly Lockean models of personal identity over time", and the chapter
borrows Shoemaker's account of Lockean continuity for its first argument.
`behavioral-integration` is for temptation, willpower and intertemporal
conflict. `social-ontology` is because the chapter restates the theory of
shared intention it uses.

Where it bears on the record:

- **[THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)** (no higher-order attitude makes a motive one's own by its
  order alone). Bratman locates agential authority in a coherent
  plan-infused standpoint, not in the order of an attitude. This chapter
  adds a reflexive end in the standpoint ("a standpoint that includes this
  very end"). Whether that end is specifiable without presupposing the
  agent's participation is exactly what [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)'s promote_when asks
  Bratman's self-governing policies to show. This chapter does not argue it:
  it takes the end as given and asks what it explains.
- **Korsgaard** ([LIT-587](LIT-587.md)). She reads Bratman as a naturalist and asks
  whether a post-hypnotically installed intention would be the agent's own.
  She reports that he answered that it would. Her alternative is that the
  norms intentions commit us to are what constitute a self. The two
  accounts of the unity of agency over time meet there.
- **Narrative identity** ([LIT-595](LIT-595.md)). Bratman grants that narrative unity
  "may apply to many cases", but holds that the "more basic" unity is acting
  together with oneself.

No anthology topic holds it, and it carries no instruction for
machine-learning practice.
