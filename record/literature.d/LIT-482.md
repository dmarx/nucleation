# LIT-482: Social ontology: Some basic principles

John R. Searle (2006), *Anthropological Theory* 6(1): 12–29 — DOI-10.1177/1463499606061731. Also printed in English as "Social Ontology: Some Basic Principles", *Papers: Revista de Sociologia* 80 (2006): 51–71, which is the text read.

## Key takeaways

- Social reality is ontologically subjective but epistemically objective.
  It exists only because people take it to exist. Yet "this is a twenty
  dollar bill" is a fact, not an opinion, and the attitudes that make it
  so are observer-independent features of the people who hold them.
- Institutional facts are status functions, imposed by collective
  acceptance on things that cannot perform the function by their
  physical structure alone. Status functions carry deontic powers
  (rights, duties, permissions), and these give desire-independent
  reasons for action. That, Searle says, is "the glue that holds society
  together".
- The 1995 formula "X counts as Y in C" is not general. Corporations and
  money held as ledger entries have a Y with no X. The more general form
  is "We accept (S has power (S does A))", which Searle says the book
  itself gave in a later chapter.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request to fill out the record's
social ontology, under the topic `social-ontology` that [ADR-017](../decisions.d/ADR-017.md) added.
It stands as the readable entry for Searle's account of institutions.
*The Construction of Social Reality* ([LIT-485](LIT-485.md)) is `Deferred` with no
lawful full text, so this paper, which restates the book and revises it,
is what the record has read of that theory. The reading is [NOTE-388](../notes.d/NOTE-388.md).

This is a different Searle subject from the Chinese Room ([LIT-400](LIT-400.md)). The
link between them is thin but real. In both, Searle treats intentionality
as a biological feature of individual minds. Here that is why the
attitudes that create social facts are themselves observer-independent.

Neighbours in the record:

- **Hindriks & Guala** ([LIT-481](LIT-481.md)). They argue that constitutive rules
  reduce to regulative rules plus a theoretical term, and that Searle's
  "desire-independent reasons" is either a platitude or an unargued
  anti-Humean thesis. Searle's own move here, sending the deontic power of
  a corporation or a sum of money "directly to the individuals" with no X,
  is a step toward their view: the Y term is a label for a bundle of
  powers. That reading is mine; the paper does not consider it.
- **List & Pettit** ([LIT-391](LIT-391.md)). Searle treats the corporation as a "fictitious
  person" created "out of nothing" by declaration. The corporation is not
  identical with any object or set of persons, and the "real bearer of the
  deontology" is the participant. Group Agency's thesis, per its
  publisher, is that there are corporate agents "over and above" their
  members. The two are about different questions, the deontic status of
  the corporation and its agency, and this paper does not say whether a
  corporation can be an agent.
- **Tomasello** ([LIT-125](LIT-125.md), [NOTE-185](../notes.d/NOTE-185.md)). Searle says animals, "as far as we can
  tell", lack a deontology, and he hedges the claim in a parenthesis.
  Tomasello's account locates the first felt obligation in children's
  joint commitments from about age 3, and denies the same behaviour to
  chimpanzees. That is the empirical side of the line Searle draws by
  conceptual analysis. But Searle's "collective intentionality" is wider
  than Tomasello's "shared intentionality": a wolf pack hunting together
  counts for Searle as a social fact, while Tomasello treats shared
  intentionality as species-unique.
- **The group-mind strand** ([THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md), [LIT-159](LIT-159.md)). Searle lists "the United
  States of America" among his social objects. On this account it exists
  only as represented, through status functions sustained by members'
  attitudes. That gives the group-mind debate an asymmetry between a
  society and a brain: the society's existence as that society depends on
  its parts representing it. Kammerer's exception for wholes whose function
  runs through parts that represent them ([LIT-409](LIT-409.md)) has the same shape.
  The paper itself says nothing about group minds; the connection is mine.

No instruction for machine-learning practice is in it, so nothing here
points to the Anthology of the SOTA.
