# CLAIM-tmpm3ah3: Material interdependence couples parties' outcomes without fixing the perspective that organizes the coupling: the same dependence can be organized as mutual opportunity, as competitive vulnerability or as coercive leverage, so interdependence makes for peace to the extent that cooperation also changes which interests the parties' institutions recognize and who holds influence over them, and payoff coupling alone does not settle which

## The claim

The commercial-peace hypothesis says trade raises the cost of war. A6
distinguished three levels of coupling:

| Level | What changes |
|---|---|
| Material | Outcomes and payoffs become interdependent |
| Political | The distribution of influence over decisions changes |
| Perspectival | Which interests count, and how the other's moves are read |

The first is the classical argument, with mixed evidence: Oneal, Russett
and Berbaum (LIT-{ORB}) report that trade predicts fewer disputes, and
Barbieri (LIT-{BAR}) that extensive interdependence can predict more. The
second is McDonald's (LIT-{MCD}): liberalization shifts domestic
influence toward commercial interests. The claim is about the third.

- **One dependence, three perspectives.** A6: "Mutual opportunity: *We
  benefit from each other's success.* Competitive vulnerability: *My
  survival depends on something you control.* Coercive leverage: *Your
  dependence gives me power over you.*" These organize different policies
  and different readings of the other's next move. Copeland's
  trade-expectations theory (LIT-{COP}) makes peace depend on expected
  future access, and Farrell and Newman's weaponized interdependence
  (LIT-{FN}) is the third organization built on network asymmetry.
- **The state and the coupling.** By [CLAIM-tmpm3bml](CLAIM-tmpm3bml.md), an increase in trade
  changes the relationship's state; an agreement that also builds shared
  dispute procedures, reciprocal obligations and domestic constituencies
  invested in cooperation may change its coupling. A6: "some cooperative
  encounters change the rules under which future cooperation becomes
  attractive, rather than merely increasing its attractiveness under
  existing rules."
- **The nearest evidence.** Kleinman, Liu and Redding ([CASE-tmptjtlr](../cases.d/CASE-tmptjtlr.md))
  find that economic alignment causes political alignment. A6: their
  exposure measure "is essentially a **coupling coefficient**", the
  response of one country's welfare to another's productivity.

The perspective realized here is not a nation's. A6: trade can change
"the distribution of influence among the domestic processes that construct
and interpret national interests", so the unit is a network of
cross-border constituencies and practices, [CLAIM-417](CLAIM-417.md)'s perspective
realized across institutions, and not a collective agent ([CLAIM-418](CLAIM-418.md)).

## What it does not say

- **Not that trade makes peace.** Interdependence also stabilizes rivalry
  and coercion; the claim says what decides which.
- **Not that Kleinman et al. show a perspectival change.** A6: "political
  alignment does not by itself establish that the countries' interpretive
  organizations have changed. Strategic incentives alone could explain the
  alignment." The defeater names what would.
- **Not the Ben Franklin effect writ large.** A6: "The mechanism need not
  be identical at each scale." States do not feel dissonance. The common
  element is a loop in which cooperation changes the conditions for
  further cooperation; A6: "cooperation may produce its own future
  reasons for cooperating."
