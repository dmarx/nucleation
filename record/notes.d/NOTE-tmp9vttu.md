# NOTE-tmp9vttu: Dagan & Benvenisty — Autocratization and states of emergency (IDI research summary)

## Contribution

It names three channels through which emergency governance and
autocratization meet, from five country cases: formal emergency powers;
"extraordinary powers functionally equivalent to emergency powers, even if
not formally defined as such in law or in the constitution"; and "emergency
rhetoric that frames certain measures as urgent and necessary" (para. 3). It
argues the relationship has had "only limited and fragmented attention"
(para. 2). The summary adds no data.

## Key insight

The emergency need not be declared to do the work. Undeclared rule by
decree (India), a temporary enabling law (Venezuela) and rhetoric casting
opponents as enemies (South Korea) belong with formal emergencies, because
they share the function: urgent necessity licensing the executive.

## Assumptions

- **Case selection on the outcome.** All five "were all previously stable
  democracies and later experienced substantial autocratization": V-Dem
  Regimes of the World score of 6 or higher and at least 15 consecutive
  years of democracy before the change (para. 4). No case of emergency
  without autocratization is studied.
- **Variation sought** in democratic quality, parliamentary or presidential
  design, region, language and culture (para. 4).
- **Method unstated.** No sources, time frames for coding, or criteria for
  what counts as a functionally equivalent power.

## Key results

- **Hungary (para. 9).** "Autocratization in Hungary was systematically
  driven by intensive use of states of emergency." Emergency provisions
  were amended repeatedly, a "state of emergency due to migration" created
  by ordinary law, and "Since 2015, Hungary has operated under successive,
  extended states of emergency, at times simultaneously", for migration,
  COVID-19 and the war in Ukraine, "often granting the government
  far-reaching authority unrelated to the immediate crisis at hand."
- **Turkey (para. 11).** The emergency after the 2016 coup attempt "proved
  pivotal": purges, arrests, media closures and a constitutional overhaul
  under restricted political activity; decree powers continued under the
  2018 constitution.
- **Venezuela (para. 10).** A 2000 enabling law, "a temporary law akin to
  emergency legislation", transferred legislative power to Chávez, who
  issued 49 decrees; formal emergency powers used widely under Chávez and
  Maduro, though "not every instance can be directly linked to
  autocratization".
- **India (para. 8).** Emergency measures in opposition-governed states, the
  revocation of Jammu and Kashmir's autonomy, and presidential orders
  amounting to "an undeclared rule by executive decree".
- **South Korea (paras. 6–7).** Gradual erosion from 2013 by decree and
  enemy rhetoric; the failed martial law of 3 December 2024. Flagged as the
  one case V-Dem still classes as a democracy, with consequences "too early
  to determine".
- **The conclusion (para. 12).** "the study demonstrates a clear link
  between states of emergency and the accelerated erosion or dismantling of
  democracy, as well as the consolidation of autocratic rule in countries
  undergoing regime transformation."

## Concepts

- **Autocratization**: "an intentional and incremental process through
  which democracy is gradually dismantled from within" (para. 2).
- **Functionally equivalent extraordinary powers**: powers that do the work
  of emergency powers without the legal designation (para. 3).
- **Emergency rhetoric**: framing measures as urgent and necessary
  (para. 3).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-tmp28sti](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp28sti.md).** Cited, unnamed, as a five-country study of emergency
  rule and democratic erosion. The reading confirms the description and
  limits the weight it can bear.
  - **Supports, as illustration.** Hungary is the clearest case in the
    record of an emergency regime renewing itself: successive and
    simultaneous emergencies since 2015, powers "unrelated to the immediate
    crisis at hand", and emergency provisions rewritten by those who use
    them. That is the claim's political-institutional feedback, authority
    defending itself by redefining its own conditions. Rhetoric as a
    channel is the claim's securitizing move.
  - **Narrows.** The study selects five cases because they autocratized, so
    it cannot show that emergencies produce erosion or self-maintain; the
    authors themselves say the convergence "is not inevitable" and that
    "many democracies have navigated states of emergency without suffering
    democratic breakdown" (para. 5). Their causal picture is mostly
    intentional, "whether because political leaders deliberately exploit a
    crisis to consolidate power or because an unforeseen crisis creates the
    opportunity" (para. 5): exploitation, not the claim's mechanism, which
    needs no bad faith.
  - **Not peer-reviewed, unsourced.** The record should cite it for the
    channels and the Hungarian description, not for a link.
  - Filed as [THEORY-tmpaf612](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpaf612.md), narrowly.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- A summary of an unpublished report; the full study, in Hebrew, was not
  found.
- Selection on the dependent variable; no comparison cases.
- No sources for any country account; claims about India, Turkey and
  Venezuela are contested politically and here unreferenced.
- The South Korean case is unsettled by the authors' own account.

## Open questions

- In democracies that declared emergencies without autocratizing, were the
  same channels present? A matched comparison would say whether the
  channels distinguish the cases.
- What does the full report code as a functionally equivalent power, and
  how?
