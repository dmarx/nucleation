# NOTE-tmpsq37b: Nord — Translating as a purposeful activity: a prospective approach

## Contribution

A compact, example-led statement of Nord's functionalism, adapted from a
2004 lecture. It replaces the source text as the yardstick with the purpose
the translation is to serve for its target audience. It gives a four-way
classification of communicative functions, and a two-way choice of strategy
for each function when target-culture conditions differ. It cites her
1997 book for the model and is short where the book is not.

## Key insight

Whether a translation is faithful cannot be read off the pair of texts. It
depends on whom the translation is for and what it is meant to do there. A
school report translated so that a German university can read the Brazilian
marks is a good translation precisely because it does not try to be a
German report (p. 26).

## Assumptions

- **A text is an "offer of information"**, after Reiss and Vermeer (1984),
  from which each receiver "accepts what he wants or needs"; readers with
  different knowledge get "quite different 'messages' out of the very same
  text" (p. 16).
- **Communication is action in a situation embedded in a culture**: agents
  in roles (sender, receiver, initiator or client, translator), with
  purposes, through a medium, in a situation-in-culture (pp. 18–19).
- **The translator works for a client under a brief**, whose purposes "may
  or may not be identical with" the sender's or the receiver's (p. 19).
- **Bühler's organon model plus Jakobson's phatic function** classifies
  purposes. Nord says it is "no more than an example", chosen for its use in
  teaching and practice (p. 19).

## Key results

- **Retrospective against prospective (pp. 16–17).** Traditional
  approaches treat the source text as "the main yardstick governing the
  translator's decisions". But since one text "may be as many texts as there
  are receivers of it", the translator should take "a prospective view of
  translation as being an activity geared toward a communicative aim or
  purpose", transforming the source text to suit "a particular target
  audience from among various possible audiences" (p. 17).
- **The youth hostel (pp. 17–18).** A request not to disturb others is
  phrased differently for each nationality ("Germans: Please, don't get up
  before 6 a.m.!" and so on): the same purpose, different texts.
- **The four functions (pp. 19–23).**
  - **Phatic**: opening, keeping open and closing the channel, relying on
    conventional forms. In a Bremen hotel list, the French proverb works,
    and the English one tells readers they should not have come. Forms of
    address belong here.
  - **Referential**: reference to objects and phenomena, which depends on
    presupposed knowledge ("the waters of Maine").
  - **Expressive**: the sender's attitude, explicit or implicit in a shared
    value system. *Une mort très douce* comes out as an evaluation in
    English and keeps both readings in German; comparing a wife's eyes to a
    cow's is praise in India.
  - **Appellative**: inducing a response, through direct or indirect
    indicators.
  Most texts show three or four functions, one usually dominant (pp. 23–24).
- **Functions across cultures (pp. 24–26).** Each function works only
  under conditions: shared phatic conventions, shared presupposed knowledge,
  a shared value system, a receiver able and willing to respond. Where the
  target culture lacks them, the translator either adapts the text so the
  function works for the target audience (option A), or explains it in a
  meta-text, changing the function into a meta-function, "which is in fact
  referential" (option B). For a joke, option B "kills the appellative
  function", yet may be what the client wants (p. 26).
- **Documentary and instrumental (pp. 26–27).** "In a large number of
  professional translation tasks, equivalence is not required or not even
  desired" (p. 26). The two options echo Cicero, Luther, Schleiermacher,
  Nida's dynamic against formal equivalence, and Venuti's domesticating
  against foreignizing. Nord's own terms are instrumental (option A) and
  documentary (option B). "Unlike the other authors mentioned above, I do
  not propose that one of the two types is generally 'better' or 'more
  appropriate' than the other. It all depends on the translation brief"
  (p. 27). "It is no longer the source-text that guides the translator's
  decisions but the overall communicative purpose the target text is
  supposed to achieve in the target culture" (p. 27).

## Concepts

- **Offer of information**: a text as what receivers take from it
  according to their knowledge and needs, after Reiss and Vermeer (p. 16).
- **Prospective approach**: translation directed at the purpose the
  target text is to serve for a chosen target audience (p. 17).
- **Translation brief**: the client's specification of the audience and
  purpose, which the translator interprets (pp. 18–19, 27).
- **Instrumental translation**: a target text that works as a
  communicative instrument in its own right, with the same or a similar
  function (option A) (p. 27).
- **Documentary translation**: a target text that tells its audience how
  the source text worked for its source audience, changing functions into
  meta-functions (option B) (pp. 24–27).

## Connections

It summarizes Nord's *Translating as a Purposeful Activity* (1997;
LIT-829, unread) and her 1989 typology, and stands in the line of Reiss and
Vermeer's skopos theory. It places Nida's dynamic and formal equivalence
(LIT-791, unread) among the historical dichotomies its two options recall,
and differs from Nida in refusing to rank them. Gutt's thesis
(LIT-tmp1eyaz, read alongside) also refuses a general ranking of
translation types, but grounds the choice in relevance to the audience, not
in a client's brief.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-205: partly, for its functional half.** The claim says a
  functional account "need not be committed to only one context or a
  static stance profile". The article says this in Nord's own words. The
  audience is chosen "from among various possible audiences" (p. 17), the
  brief fixes which, and the same source text is translated documentarily
  for one purpose and instrumentally for another (pp. 26–27). Each function
  is assessed against the conditions of a particular receiving culture
  (pp. 24–26). So for Nord there is no single context, and no profile of
  the rendering read once, apart from a stated purpose and audience. The
  article offers no operationalization. It does not bear on the claim's
  proposed "rich contextual baseline of context-response profiles and
  supervised prediction", which stays the record's proposal. The book stays
  unread.
- **Beside CLAIM-233 (not among the entries this reading was sought
  for).** On Nord, a documentary translation does not aim to reproduce the
  original's effect at all, so "functional equivalence" is not one account
  but a family. What a functional rival predicts about fidelity judgements
  depends on the brief the raters assume. The status review may want to
  note this where CLAIM-233 speaks of "a functional-equivalence account".
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **A summary.** It adapts a lecture, gives examples rather than
  arguments, and defers the classification's grounds to the 1997 book.
- **No evidence about readers.** Whether a function "works" for an
  audience is asserted from the examples, never measured.
- **The brief is taken as given.** How the translator infers it, or what
  happens when no brief is stated, is not discussed beyond "the conclusions
  the translator draws from the brief" (p. 27).

## Open questions
