summary: >-
  Two small experiments with US undergraduates (N = 42 and 73) compare four ways of making minimal groups. In the classic one, participants are falsely told their painting preferences put them in a group. In the others, they imagine being assigned, are assigned at random, or only memorize five names from one group, with no categorization at all. On explicit liking and identification, and on a bonus-money allocation between two other participants, all four produced comparable in-group favouritism. On IATs of attitude (pleasant/unpleasant) and identification (self/other), only memorization produced large effects; the classic procedure produced none. Implicit and explicit favouritism correlated weakly. Explicit identification correlated with allocation favouritism (r = .37). The allocation result was carried mostly by one of the two colour groups.
