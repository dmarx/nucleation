# NOTE-tmpozxwf: The Case for Strong Longtermism

## Contribution

It states strong longtermism precisely and argues for it. Axiological
strong longtermism (ASL) says that in the most important decision
situations, far-future effects fix which options are near-best. Deontic
strong longtermism (DSL) says one ought to choose accordingly. The paper
claims more rigour than Bostrom (2003) and Beckstead (2013), a wider base
of assumptions, and a new argument for the deontic version (p. 3).

## Key insight

Expected value is driven by the largest scenario in which one has any
credence. So a tiny credence in space settlement or digital minds sets
the expected size of the future, and any option that shifts the
probability of a persistent state (extinction, or a lasting
AI-controlled order) carries that size with it. Effects that change
which persistent state the world ends up in do not "wash out" (§4.1).

## Assumptions

- **Expected total utilitarianism, at first.** Ex ante value is expected
  value, value is total welfare, and value is time-separable (p. 5).
  Section 6 relaxes the first two.
- **A zero rate of pure time preference.** Called "near-essential" (p. 18).
  "With even a modest positive rate of pure time preference ... the
  argument would not go through" (p. 18).
- **Lives are on average worth living** (p. 10), needed for the extinction
  examples, not for the AI one.
- **Digital minds would have comparable moral status** (p. 8).
- **The status quo is wasted resources** (p. 4). Benefits are measured
  against it.
- **Neglectedness.** The claim is about the current margin. Whether it
  would hold if 10% of world GDP already went to the far future, "We
  aren't sure" (p. 14, n. 14).

## Key results

- **The thesis** (p. 3).
  - ASL(i): every option that is near-best overall is near-best for the
    far future.
  - ASL(ii): every such option delivers much larger benefits in the far
    future than in the near future.
  - "Far future" means from some time t on, say 100 years (p. 4).
- **The benefit ratio** (BR, p. 4). The highest far-future ex ante benefit
  attainable without net near-future harm is many times the highest
  attainable near-future benefit. The appendix proves that BR(n) gives
  ASL(ii), and ASL(i) once options with net near-term harm are removed
  (pp. 30–32). With n = 10, a 90%-best option gets at least 80% of the
  far-future best, and at least 8 times as much far-future as near-future
  benefit.
- **The near-future benchmark** (p. 5). Bednets save a life for about
  $4,000, so 0.025 lives per $100 (GiveWell).
- **The size of the future** (pp. 6–9).
  - Natural-risk extrapolation gives at least 87,000 years; mammalian
    species last 0.5 to 6 million years; Earth stays habitable about a
    billion years.
  - Newberry's six scenarios run from 10^14 future lives (Earth,
    mammalian reference class) to 10^45 (Milky Way, digital life) (p. 8).
  - A 0.01% credence in biological settlement of the Milky Way adds at
    least 10^32 (p. 9).
  - Estimates used: main 10^24, low 10^18, restricted 10^14 (p. 9).
- **Tractability, per $100** (pp. 11–15). Read with main / low / restricted
  estimates.
  - Spaceguard asteroid survey ($71 million by 2012): 500 million / 500 /
    0.05 lives. Further asteroid detection: 300,000 / 0.3 / 0.00003.
  - Pandemic preparedness ($250 billion cutting a 1-in-22,000 century risk
    by 1%): 200 million / 200 / 0.02.
  - AI safety (at least 0.1% risk; $1 billion averting 1% of it, so 0.001%
    absolute): one trillion / one million / 100. Only this example beats
    bednets on the restricted estimate.
- **Meta-options** (§4.4). Research and a longtermist fund that saves for
  later raise the bar for intractability objections. A 2% annual failure
  rate for such funds is called "conservatively high" (p. 16, n. 16).
- **Individuals** (§5). Donors and career choosers are in the same
  position, given diminishing rather than increasing returns.
- **Robustness to axiology** (§6, pp. 17–19).
  - Risk aversion and prioritarianism strengthen the case for mitigating
    catastrophe.
  - Person-affecting views: "premature extinction is in itself at worst
    neutral" (p. 18). The extinction examples may then fail. The AI
    example does not, since a large population exists either way.
    "Narrow" person-affecting views are set aside as implausible; "wide"
    ones agree with totalism on the AI cases (p. 18, n. 20).
  - A future worse than nothing defeats the case for avoiding extinction
    but not the AI case.
  - Discounting: conceded as above. A zero rate is endorsed by Sidgwick,
    Ramsey, Stern and others; 38% of surveyed experts on social
    discounting agreed (p. 19, n. 21). Models of partiality that weight
    contemporaries more are judged unlikely to discount enough (n. 21).
- **Cluelessness** (§7). Simple cluelessness cancels out for trivial
  choices but not for deliberate attempts to make AI safer. Conscious
  unawareness, arbitrariness, imprecision and ambiguity aversion are each
  answered; ambiguity aversion is called "anyway irrational" (p. 24).
- **Fanaticism** (§8). "One of the most plausible ways in which the
  argument for strong longtermism might fail" (p. 25). Replies:
  - Beckstead and Thomas show that avoiding it forces "timidity".
  - At the societal level the probabilities (one in 100,000 for $1
    billion on AI) are above the one-in-a-million risks people act on.
  - At the individual level ($10,000 for one in ten billion) the authors
    concede the probabilities may be problematically small (p. 26).
- **Deontic strong longtermism** (§9, pp. 26–29).
  - Stakes-sensitivity argument: when the stakes are very high, no serious
    side-constraint applies and the prerogatives at issue are minor, one
    ought to choose a near-best option (P1). The most important decisions
    today are like that (P2).
  - Against non-aggregation: voting, fighting in WWII, protest.
  - Against a non-identity restriction: it would deny that governments
    must act on climate change, "clearly incorrect" (p. 28).
  - Demandingness is left to the literature. Society is nowhere near any
    cap on required sacrifice: "We ought to be doing a lot more
    for the far future than we currently are" (p. 29).
  - Side-constraints rule out atrocities; there is usually a permissible
    alternative (p. 29, n. 38).

## Concepts

- **axiological / deontic strong longtermism**: far-future effects are
  the most important determinant of the value of options / of what one
  ought to do (p. 3).
- **benefit ratio (BR)**: far-future attainable benefit without near-term
  harm is many times near-future attainable benefit (p. 4).
- **persistent state**: a set of world states that, once entered, the
  world is expected to stay in for an extremely long time (p. 10).
- **washing-out hypothesis**: expected value differences between options
  decay with time fast enough that near-future effects dominate (p. 10).
- **stakes-sensitivity**: minor constraints and prerogatives give way when
  the axiological stakes are very high (p. 27).
- **fanaticism / timidity**: sacrificing arbitrarily much for a tiny
  chance of a vastly larger payoff / refusing to (p. 25).

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** The paper is the clearest case
  in the record of an obligation grounded in an impersonal good. These
  are inferences; the authors say nothing about the self.
  - **The ground is the total good, not anyone's.** Value is total
    welfare, counted the same whoever and whenever it falls on. The
    thesis has no quarrel with that as a view. It bears only on what
    could move someone to act on it.
  - **Nothing is said about motivation.** The agent is "a rational
    altruistic actor" (p. 17) with an "altruistic budget" (p. 29). Being
    moved is presupposed. The "ought" is subjective but normative, and
    demandingness is left to the literature. Even the one sentence on
    prudence treats it as a cost: devoting resources to the far future
    "at the large expense of our own prudential interests" (p. 29).
  - **The governing image is prudential.** "Humanity, today, is like an
    imprudent teenager" (p. 2), whose most important choices matter for
    the rest of its life. That models the obligation on one agent's
    concern for its own future. The thesis can read this as humanity as
    a self extended in time. The authors use it only as an analogy.
  - **Partiality is the nearest the paper comes to a self.** Models that
    weight one's contemporaries more (Setiya, Mogensen) are mentioned and
    judged not to discount enough (n. 21). A thesis that grounds concern
    in the self predicts some such discount. The paper does not dispute
    that people have one, only that they ought to.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** Not addressed. But the
  paper sharpens what a counterexample to the thesis must be: someone who
  acts on strong longtermism at a real cost, with no identification with
  humanity or its future. The paper says nothing about whether such
  people exist.
- **Proposed dated note on CLAIM-tmpjylq8**: "Greaves and MacAskill (NOTE
  of 2026-10-29) ground obligations to the far future in total welfare,
  undiscounted and owed to no one's self. They say nothing about why
  anyone would act on them; their agent is simply 'a rational altruistic
  actor'. So the thesis is not contradicted by the view, only challenged
  to explain its adherents. Their one image, humanity as 'an imprudent
  teenager', models the obligation on prudence across one agent's life,
  which is the thesis's shape, offered as analogy."
- **No THEORY filed.**
- **No ML instruction.** AI safety is an example, not advice on practice.

## Limitations

- **Not the published chapter.** The 2025 text may differ.
- **The decisive numbers are judgement.** The AI estimates (0.1% risk, 1%
  averted by $1 billion) have, by the authors' account, "no hard
  quantitative evidence" (p. 14). Only that example passes on the
  restricted estimate.
- **Person-affecting robustness rests on the weakest example.** C4
  succeeds by moving to AI, the least quantified case.
- **The individual-level fanaticism problem is conceded, not answered**
  (p. 26), yet §5 extends the thesis to individuals.
- **An inconsistent illustration.** Section 5 supposes $1 billion could
  cut the chance of an asteroid collision this century by 1 in 120,000
  (p. 16). Page 11 gives the remaining risk of a 10km+ collision as 1 in
  150 million, so the cut exceeds the risk. It is an illustration, but it
  does not match the paper's own figures.
- **Typos and slips.**
  - "miniscule" for "minuscule" (p. 10).
  - "John Hopkins Center" for "Johns Hopkins" (p. 13); "Center for New
    American Security" for "Center for a New American Security" (p. 14).
  - "Sandberg and Armstrong 2013" in the text (p. 7); the reference list
    has Armstrong and Sandberg.
  - "near-future benefits of bednet mitigation" for "distribution"
    (p. 23).
  - "several centuries' hence" (p. 20); "500 additional million lives"
    (p. 11).
  - "The expected value approach we assumed in section 3" (p. 20); it was
    set out in section 2.2.

## Open questions

- Who acts on strong longtermism, and why? A study of committed
  longtermists that measured identification with humanity or with future
  generations, alongside their stated reasons, would show whether the
  impersonal ground is the operative motive.
- Does the 2025 chapter keep "near-essential" for zero pure time
  preference, and the same person-affecting reply?
