# LIT-1379: Human Nature and the Social Order

Cooley, C. H. (1902). *Human Nature and the Social Order*. New York: Charles Scribner's Sons. Project Gutenberg edition, https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/75145.

Skimmed: chapters I, III, V, VI and VIII read in full, [NOTE-1080](../notes.d/NOTE-1080.md). The
summary was corrected from them.

## Why this is here

The owner's U39 asked about the phrase "I am whatever you say I am". A39 placed it beside Cooley's looking-glass self. Bears on [CLAIM-415](../claims.d/CLAIM-415.md).

**From the reading, 2026-10-22.** Cooley holds more of [CLAIM-415](../claims.d/CLAIM-415.md) than the
claim credits. On its first step, the audience in the self is always
imagined for him, and may be unreal ("There is no separation between real
and imaginary persons"), and a person attributes sentiments to "his
countrymen or contemporaries in their collective aspect" (ch. III). What he
does not say is that the attributed collective can be a unity its members
do not compose; that remains the claim's. On its second step, chapter
VIII's "contrary suggestion" already makes opposition a way of being
steered by what others enjoin ("if others are against him one feels sure
they _are_ his own"). The phrase "I am whatever you say I am" is closest to
chapter VI's vanity, "becoming, so far as he can, what any flatterer says
he is", which Cooley treats as the unstable pole of the social self, not
its rule. See [NOTE-1080](../notes.d/NOTE-1080.md).
