# NOTE-tmp7h7gb: I. Taking Ourselves Seriously; II. Getting It Right

## Contribution

The two lectures give one argument: "the authority of practical reason is less fundamental than that of love … its authority is grounded in and derives from the authority of love", and since love is "essentially … a volitional matter", "the ultimate source of practical normative authority lies not in reason but in the will" (p. 170). Lecture I builds the moral psychology. Reflexive self-division makes higher-order responses possible. Identification with some of our desires makes them reasons. Volitional unanimity is free will, and caring is a commitment to a desire. Lecture II argues that the standards of "volitional rationality" are fixed by what we cannot help caring about (above all by what we love) and that these "volitional necessities" are objective in the sense that they are "outside the scope of our voluntary control" (p. 190). Morality elaborates the universal ones.

## Key insight

A desire does not give its owner a reason. It gives him "a problem": whether to identify with it or to externalize it (p. 175). The problem is settled by the will, not by reason. At bottom the will is constrained by necessities it did not choose and cannot drop, what we cannot help loving. These necessities are ours rather than coercive because we are "wholeheartedly behind them" (p. 197). The self is thus the source of reasons, and the "objective normative reality" that normative realists locate outside us "is within ourselves" (p. 190).

## Assumptions

The lectures offer premises, not formal conditions.

- **Reflexivity is uniquely (probably) human.** We can "separate from the immediate content and flow of our own consciousness", which introduces "a sort of division within our minds" (p. 170). Nonhuman animals and small children are "one-dimensional" (p. 172). This is asserted.
- **Having a reason means having a goal**, and "Having a goal is not the same … as simply being moved by a desire" (p. 175). This is the premise that links identification to practical reason.
- **Importance depends on caring.** "Nothing is truly important to a person unless it makes a difference that he actually cares about" (p. 183). This is argued by a regress: grounding inherent importance would require already knowing which differences are important (p. 183). Note 1 (p. 181) sets aside whether this should extend to what we *would* care about if properly acquainted with it.
- **Love and wholeheartedness are stipulated simple.** "I therefore propose just to stipulate that a lover is never troubled by conflict, or by ambivalence" (p. 197). The account of love's authority is developed only for this idealised case. Frankfurt himself says real people are "so endlessly ambivalent and conflicted" that often "part of them loves it and part of them does not" (p. 197).
- **The basic volitional necessities come from human nature**, "presumably as an outcome of natural selection" (p. 192). The necessities are life, health, contact with others and freedom from suffering and boredom (p. 193). This is asserted.

## Key results

What the lectures argue, in Frankfurt's section order. Page numbers are the printed Tanner pagination.

**Lecture I: Taking Ourselves Seriously**

- **§§1–2 Reflexivity (pp. 169–171).** Self-division "puts in place an elementary reflexive structure" (p. 171). We can "form reflexive or higher-order responses" to our mental life: approval, disapproval, wanting to remain as we are or to be different, "supervisory desires, intentions, and interventions" (p. 171). It has two opposed consequences. It threatens our well-being: self-objectification "facilitates both an inhibiting uncertainty or ambivalence and a nagging general dissatisfaction with ourselves" (p. 171). It also founds "practical rationality, … freedom of the will, and … love" (p. 171).
- **§3 Responsibility for character (pp. 172–173).** Higher-order responses are "fundamental to achieving the status of a responsible person". They need not be "especially thoughtful, or even entirely overt" (p. 172). Against Aristotle (*NE* III.5), becoming responsible for one's character "is not essentially a matter of producing that character but of taking responsibility for it". That happens when a person "selectively identifies with certain of his own attitudes and dispositions, whether or not it was he that caused himself to have them" (p. 172). The account is historical-indifferent: "What counts is our current effort … and not the story of how we came to be" (p. 172). Renouncing an inclination one caused ends responsibility for its continuation (pp. 172–173).
- **§4 Identification as default (p. 173).** Identification is "willing acceptance". We are "willing for them to represent us", "Whether with a welcoming approval, or in weary resignation". It makes the accepted states ours: "Their force is now our force … We are active, since we are being moved just by ourselves." Being identified is "very elementary", mostly unnoticed, and "In very large measure, it is simply the default condition."
- **§§5–6 Non-identification and externalization (pp. 173–175).** There are two cases. Mental "tics, twitches, and convulsions" (obsessional thoughts, reckless impulses, surges of emotion) are ours "in a gross literal sense" but not what we "really" think or want (p. 174). Actively repudiated desires are externalized by a "psychic immune response". They are "outlawed and disenfranchised" and denied "any entitlement to supply us with motives or with reasons". An irresistible externalized desire's "dominion is merely that of a tyrant" (pp. 174–175).
- **§6 Desires give problems, not reasons (p. 175).** Frankfurt rejects the view that every desire gives at least a weak reason. Identifying with a desire "validate[s] it as eligible for satisfaction" and gives it "some position—however inferior—in the order of his preferences and priorities". Externalizing it gives it "no position in that order at all".
- **§§7–8 Reflexivity and identification constitute practical reason (pp. 175–177).** "Reflexivity and identification have fundamental roles in the constitution of practical reason … Without their intervention, we could not regard any fact as giving us a reason" (p. 175). The example is the father with an exogenous desire to kill his beloved son: "it is not true that I want to kill my son", so he has no reason, not even a weak and overridden one, to fire (p. 176). Deciding is "an inherently reflexive act, which the mind performs upon itself" (p. 176). Being intelligent and being rational differ: the fleeing insect behaves intelligently without deliberating (p. 177).
- **§9 Free action and free will (pp. 177–178).** "Enjoying freedom of action consists in maintaining this harmonious accord between what we do and what we want to do." In parallel, "we will freely when what we want is what we want to want". A person's will is free "when there is in him a certain volitional unanimity. The desire that governs him as he is acting is in agreement with a higher-order volition concerning what he wants to be his governing desire" (p. 177). The fullest statement requires more than 1971's match: the motivating "first-order desire" is the one we want, "and … there is no conflict in us between this motive and any desire at any higher order … we are thoroughly wholehearted". That is "all the freedom for which finite creatures can reasonably hope" (p. 178). The failure case is harsh envy driving a person who wants to act from warmth (p. 178).
- **§10 Compatibilism (p. 178).** The incompatibility of free will and determinism is "a red herring". Determinism threatens "our power", not "our freedom". The volitional structure could come from causes or from "blind chance".
- **§11 Spinoza's *acquiescentia in se ipso* (p. 179).** Frankfurt reads it literally as "acquiescence to being the person that one is", which "comes to much the same thing as having a free will". It "resolves the deepest problem" by restoring the unity broken by reflexive division.
- **§12 Caring (pp. 179–180).** Free will is "purely structural" and implies nothing about importance: one can freely will to flick a crumb and not care about it. "When we do care about something, we go beyond wanting it. We want to go on wanting it". "The caring entails … a commitment to the desire" (p. 180). Free will is "synchronic coherence". Caring is "diachronic coherence, which integrates the self across time" (p. 180).
- **§§13–17 Importance and love (pp. 180–185).** Importance to us derives from what we care about (p. 181). Moral rationalism (Nagel quoted, p. 182) fails because "Our response to sinners is not the same as our response to fools", and "Morality is essentially designed to put people on the hook" (p. 182). The normative question "what should I care about?" rests on the factual question of what one does care about. "No one can pull himself up by his own bootstraps" (p. 183). "Our will is not invariably subject to our will" (p. 184). Love is not voluntary and not a response to perceived value. Parents value children "only because the parents love them". "Love is not a conclusion … It creates reasons" (p. 184). "our final ends are provided and legitimated by love" (p. 185).

**Lecture II: Getting It Right**

- **§§1–2 (pp. 185–187).** Neither value judgments nor morality settles how to live. What is needed is "what to care about and how to measure the relative importance to him of the various things about which he cares" (p. 186). Hume's "destruction of the whole world" preference is not a logical error (p. 187).
- **§§3–4 Volitional rationality (pp. 187–188).** Hume's madman is "volitionally irrational. He has a defect of the will" (p. 187). Volitional rationality limits what is possible, as formal rationality does. Its violations are not "inconceivable" but "unthinkable" (p. 188). "Rationality does not permit us to be open-minded and judicious about everything": having to deliberate whether to destroy the world "would make it clear that something is wrong with him" (p. 188).
- **§§5–6 Against normative realism (pp. 189–190).** Frankfurt takes Adams's argument from the "seriousness of normative discourse" as his foil. "I do not believe that anything is inherently important." Normative standards are "grounded only in what we cannot help caring about". Their objectivity "consists just in the fact that it is outside the scope of our voluntary control" (p. 190).
- **§§7–9 Self-preservation (pp. 190–192).** Self-preservation is the paradigm final end. Williams's view that projects give a reason for living "pertains just to people who are seriously depressed" (p. 191), and "Surely Williams has it backward" (p. 192). The desire to live and its reason-giving force are "not themselves based on reasons. They are based on love … we love living" (p. 192).
- **§§10–11 Universal necessities (pp. 193–194).** Other universal loves are health, contact, freedom from suffering and boredom. They are "elementary constituents of volitional reason itself" (p. 193). Loving death or disability is not contradictory but unintelligible. Such a will "is not so much in error as it is deformed" (p. 194). Justification to others requires that they be "volitionally rational as well" (p. 194).
- **§12 Love defined (pp. 194–195).** "love is a particular mode of caring. It is an involuntary, nonutilitarian, rigidly focused, and … self-affirming concern for the existence and the good of what is loved" (p. 194). Its object can be almost anything. "the lover identifies himself with what he loves", which "consists in the lover accepting the interests of his beloved as his own". Love requires no reciprocity and creates no obligation to the beloved (p. 195). "Love requires no reasons, and it can have anything as its cause". It "creates the reasons" for devotion (p. 195).
- **§13 Volitional necessity (p. 196).** "Love entails two closely related volitional necessities": one cannot help loving, and so cannot help treating benefit to the beloved as "a powerful and often decisively preemptive reason". "The heart of the matter is not affective or cognitive, but strictly volitional." Love's necessities "define what—as active beings—we most intimately and essentially are" and shape "our personal identities" (p. 196).
- **§14 Necessity vs. compulsion (pp. 196–198).** Compulsions, obsessions and addictions "do not involve what I understand by the term 'volitional necessity'". We submit to them "unwillingly"; they are "generated and sustained from outside the will itself" (p. 197). "the necessities of wholehearted love may be irresistible, but they are not coercive … There is no distance or discrepancy between what a lover is constrained to will and what he cannot help wanting to will" (p. 197). "the wholehearted lover cannot help being wholehearted" (p. 197). If he wavered, his love "had never been truly wholehearted to begin with" (p. 198).
- **§15 Morality (pp. 198–199).** Universal volitional necessities are "absolute and unconditional" and objective "despite their origin within us" (p. 198). Moral principles "elaborate and elucidate universal and categorical necessities that constrain the human will" (p. 198). The apparent necessity of moral truths is "a projection of the volitional necessities from which morality derives" (p. 199). Moral blame is anger at someone who "deliberately injures something that we love" (p. 199).
- **§§16–17 Correction and irreducible conflict (pp. 199–201).** Personal loves vary and change, but loving "can go absolutely wrong". We can be wrong about conflicts between loves, about the nature of their objects and about ourselves: "The will is a thing as real as any reality outside us" (p. 200). After those corrections "There is nothing else to get right" (p. 200). Normative conflict may be irreducible; one may have to "separate or to slug it out" (p. 201). Note 5 (p. 201): the same can hold "within a single person", who may have to "separate one part of himself radically from the other or … endure tumultuous inner conflict".
- **§18 Authority (pp. 201–202).** "Wholehearted love definitively settles … what we are to care about". Because it also expresses "what we cannot help wholeheartedly wanting to be", we accept its authority "not merely as inescapable, but as legitimate too. It is the only legitimate authority" (p. 201). "the legitimacy of authority here can derive only from the will of the governed" (p. 202). The foundation is "a confidence in what we cannot help being" (p. 202). Note 6 draws an analogy with Descartes's grounding of theoretical reason in confidence in the necessities of his cognitive nature (p. 202).

## Concepts

- **Reflexivity / self-division.** The capacity to separate from one's own conscious flow and to "objectify" its items, making "higher-order responses" possible (pp. 170–171).
- **Higher-order volition.** The volition "concerning what he wants to be his governing desire" (p. 177). The lectures do not use "second-order volition" or "second-order desire".
- **Identification.** "Willing acceptance" of attitudes as "authentic expressions of ourselves", which makes their force "our force" (p. 173). Taking responsibility for them regardless of their causal history (p. 172). In love, identification also means accepting the beloved's interests as one's own (p. 195).
- **Externalization.** Dissociating from a desire and denying it "any entitlement to supply us with motives or with reasons" (pp. 174–175).
- **Free will / volitional unanimity.** The governing desire agrees with the higher-order volition and there is no higher-order conflict. It is "purely structural" (pp. 177–179).
- **Freedom of action.** "harmonious accord between what we do and what we want to do" (p. 177).
- **Wholeheartedness.** The absence of conflict "between this motive and any desire at any higher order" (p. 178). For love, having "no reservations or conflicts" that would move one to undermine it (p. 198).
- **Ambivalence.** Named as a product of self-objectification (p. 171) and as endemic in love (p. 197), then stipulated away. It is not analysed.
- **Caring.** Wanting to continue wanting; "a commitment to the desire"; gives "diachronic coherence" (p. 180).
- **Love.** "a particular mode of caring … involuntary, nonutilitarian, rigidly focused, and … self-affirming concern for the existence and the good of what is loved" (p. 194).
- **Volitional necessity.** A constraint on the will, such as what one cannot help loving or cannot bring oneself to do, that one is wholeheartedly behind. It is distinguished from compulsions, which are "outside the will itself" (pp. 196–197).
- **Volitional rationality.** Rationality defined by contingent constraints on the will; its violations are "unthinkable" (p. 188).
- **Acquiescentia in se ipso.** Spinoza's term, read as willing acceptance of being the person one is (p. 179).

## Connections

- **Within this batch.** SEP "Personal Autonomy" (Buss & Westlund) and SEP "Compatibilism" §4.2 (McKenna) supply the 1971 apparatus these lectures do not restate: effective desire as will, second-order desire vs. second-order volition, the addicts, and wantons. Velleman's "Identification and Identity" (2002) reconstructs the path from 1971 through 1992. The lectures' account of identification as default, resigned "willing acceptance" (p. 173) and acquiescence (p. 179) is the late position Velleman's chapter discusses; this needs checking against that reading. Velleman's "What Happens When Someone Acts?" (1992) targets the hierarchical model of participation. The lectures' claim that identified-with desires are ones by which "we are being moved just by ourselves" (p. 173) is exactly the claim Velleman disputes. The IEP "Autonomy" entry (Dryden) and the SEP entries discuss the regress and Watson's objection, neither of which the lectures address. SEP Robb (alternative possibilities) covers the 1969 paper, which is not mentioned here.
- **Enoch, "Agency, Shmagency" ([LIT-117](../literature.d/LIT-117.md), Deferred).** Frankfurt grounds normativity in what an agent "cannot help" caring about, as "elementary constituents of volitional reason itself" (p. 193). That is close to the constitutivist strategy Enoch's abstract targets. The link is thematic and unverified, since [LIT-117](../literature.d/LIT-117.md) is known only from its abstract.
- **Pettigrew, "Consent and the Formation of Preferences" ([LIT-202](../literature.d/LIT-202.md)).** Per [NOTE-144](NOTE-144.md), it uses a Frankfurt-style (1969, counterfactual-intervener) case. That is a different Frankfurt paper; nothing in these lectures bears on it. Thematically, Frankfurt's indifference to the causal history of identified attitudes (p. 172) is the "internalist" position that the adaptive-preference literature contests.

## Bearing on the record

- **First-hand anchor for the late Frankfurt.** This is the record's only text by Frankfurt on the will. It can be cited first-hand for: identification as willing acceptance, including resignation (p. 173); desires as problems rather than reasons (p. 175); free will as volitional unanimity plus wholeheartedness (pp. 177–178); caring as second-order commitment (p. 180); love and volitional necessity (pp. 194–198); and normativity grounded in the will (pp. 190, 201–202). It cannot be cited for the 1971 taxonomy (wanton/person, second-order volitions, effective desire), the 1969 cases, "decisive identification", or 1992 "satisfaction". Those must go through the secondary sources, attributed as such.
- **No THEORY in the record bears on this.** No ML-practice content. A distant interest is AI "agency" talk: Frankfurt's structural criterion (a system with preferences over its own motivating preferences) is sometimes invoked there, but the lectures say nothing about artificial agents.
- **For filing.** `agency` first (identification, free will, self-governance). `ethics` (the grounding of morality and practical normativity). `metaphysics` (free will and determinism, p. 178). `identity` is justified by love's necessities defining "what … we most intimately and essentially are" and shaping "personal identities" (p. 196), but in the practical, self-constitution sense, not persistence. `epistemology` is not justified.

## Limitations

- These are public lectures. They have no citations to Frankfurt's own earlier work and do not engage the critical literature (no Watson, Bratman, Velleman, Stump; no regress). Philosophical interlocutors appear only as foils: Aristotle, Nagel, Hume, Adams, Williams, Spinoza, Parmenides and Descartes.
- The central account of love's authority holds only under an explicit idealisation: lovers are stipulated never to be ambivalent (p. 197). Frankfurt concedes that real love is usually partial and conflicted, so C13 and C15 are shown only for a case he says is rare.
- The claim that love grounds reasons is circular-looking at the key point. Wholeheartedness is defined as having no conflicts that would undermine love, and any wavering shows the love "had never been truly wholehearted" (p. 198). The criterion can only be applied retrospectively.
- There is no account of what makes a volition "higher-order" authoritative over a lower one beyond the fact of identification. The authority question (Watson's) is presupposed, not answered.
- Empirical premises are asserted without support: animal and child one-dimensionality (p. 172), the universality and natural-selection origin of the love of living (p. 192), and the claim that depression explains Williams's view (p. 191).
- The ending is candid that normative conflict between persons may be rationally irresolvable ("separate or … slug it out", p. 201). Frankfurt says this "does not imply a deficiency in my theory" (p. 201), which is asserted.

## Open questions

- How does the lectures' identification-as-default, resigned acceptance (p. 173) relate to 1987 "decisive identification" and 1992 "satisfaction"? Is it a restatement of satisfaction or a further weakening? Only the secondary sources (Velleman 2002) can settle this here.
- Without the no-ambivalence stipulation, does love still supply "legitimate" authority, or only the wholehearted part of it? Note 5 (p. 201) raises intrapersonal irreducible conflict and leaves it open.
- Is "volitional necessity" objective enough to ground morality (pp. 198–199), given that personal necessities "can be changed" and "love comes and it goes" (p. 199)?
