# NOTE-tmpylmyu: Radsch (2026) — Same gatekeepers, new tollbooths in the AI content licensing market

## Contribution

A policy synthesis: a three-tier map of AI content licensing, the "publisher
double bind" concept, and a list of interventions; cites convergent findings
from the UK House of Lords and a European Parliament study.

## Key results

1. **Deal premium gone.** "By the fourth quarter of 2025, that “deal premium”
   had essentially evaporated—amid a six-fold collapse in click-through rates
   from AI systems." (section "3 tiers, 1 structural problem", First tier)
2. **Long tail excluded.** Local newspapers and similar outlets "are
   effectively absent from the AI licensing market entirely." (Third tier)
3. **Double bind.** "The same Big Tech firms whose AI products are eroding
   website traffic are now building and controlling the licensing
   infrastructure those publishers must turn to."
4. **No intent needed.** "This dynamic does not require bad intent to produce
   bad outcomes. It is simply how platform capture works".
5. **Leverage runs both ways.** "AI systems depend on a continuous supply of
   high-quality human content to remain useful. Degrade that supply—by
   destroying the economic conditions under which it’s produced—and you
   degrade the AI itself." (§"We've been here before")
6. **Valuation.** Valuing journalism by "referral traffic lost" is "radically
   incomplete"; contributions span "training and fine-tuning; linguistic and
   reasoning capacity; factual grounding; temporal currency; and civic
   legitimacy."
7. **Lock-in.** "The deal structures, price precedents, intermediary take
   rates, and governance norms being established right now will be difficult
   to dislodge once they’re normalized."

## Assumptions

That the search-era history is the right template; that AI firms need a
continuing supply of fresh journalism (so publishers have leverage); that
statutory/collective licensing (Australia, Canada, music) transfers to AI; the
"six-fold collapse" and "deal premium" figures come from the report's data,
which the commentary does not present.

## Concepts

Publisher double bind; three tiers (bilateral deals, intermediaries, long
tail); deal premium; content cannibalization; training vs RAG as one value
stack; platform capture through "standardization lock-in, data asymmetry, and
the magnitude of platform scale"; statutory, collective and opt-out licensing;
must-carry inclusion.

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** A35's account ("a commentary on bargaining power and revenue between
publishers and AI intermediaries in content licensing") is **accurate but
thin**. The piece's sharper points for the record are the exclusion of local
media from licensing (third tier), the double bind, and the claim that
platform capture needs no bad intent.

- **CLAIM-tmpifp69** (attention-weighted demand selects against investigative
  journalism): **qualifies / adds a parallel mechanism**. Radsch's account of
  news economics runs through intermediary market power and referral
  dependence, not reader attention per se: what journalism is "worth" is set
  by the platform's benchmark (referral traffic), which is a criterion imposed
  from outside. It supports the claim's premise that revenue routes shape what
  news can be funded and that local, civically valuable outlets lose most, but
  it offers no evidence on clickbait or investigations specifically. Its
  externality point (journalism's civic and model-quality value not captured
  by the price) parallels the claim's step 2.

Other held entries it bears on (found on recheck):

- **CLAIM-463** (restoring conditions need not restore capacity) and the

- **LIT-1293** (US Copyright Office, Generative AI Training, Part 3) is the
