summary: >-
  Buss & Westlund (2002),
  <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/personal-autonomy/>. The entry sorts
  accounts of self-government into four families: "coherentist"
  (hierarchical desires, Frankfurt; evaluative judgments, Watson; plans,
  Bratman; caring; character), reasons-responsive,
  responsiveness-to-reasoning (Christman, Mele) and incompatibilist, with
  relational conditions as a compatible add-on. It then argues (§3) that
  none of them, alone or combined, gives the *minimal* conditions of
  autonomy. Coherence is not sufficient, because the endorsement itself
  can be manipulated. It is not necessary either: a weak-willed agent, or
  someone acting against her plans, still governs herself. And any attempt
  to ground identification in some attitude restarts the regress. The only
  attitude no agent can be alienated from, the desire to be
  self-governing, cannot be the criterion, because it is present even in
  failures. The 1971 hierarchy gets one sentence, and Frankfurt's later
  replies (decisive commitment, then "satisfaction") get one footnote.

[NOTE-tmpnik8h](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpnik8h.md) is the close reading of 2026-09-29, and it placed the work: **Active** — worth reading as a compact, opinionated map of *why* hierarchical (and every other) account of self-government falls short of minimal autonomy, especially the §3 regress and weakness-of-will arguments; it is **not** a substitute for Frankfurt 1971, whose apparatus (second-order volitions, wantons, persons, freedom of will vs. action) it never states, and it does not report Watson's objection to Frankfurt at all.
