summary: >-
  Ramsey (1928). Asks how much of its income a nation should save, for a community that lives for ever with fixed numbers, tastes and motives, summing utility over time without discounting it, a practice he calls "ethically indefensible" and the product of "the weakness of the imagination". The answer, credited in its simple form to Keynes: the rate of saving times the marginal utility of consumption equals the shortfall of present net enjoyment from Bliss, the maximum obtainable rate. The rule is independent of the production function except through Bliss, and of the interest rate. It asks far more saving than usual: on an illustrative scale, £300 out of a family income of £500. With a constant discount rate ρ below the interest rate r, the same solution holds with a "modified utility" that discounts high incomes instead of the future; with ρ above r, consumption runs down to subsistence. A constant rate is argued to be the only one consistent with successive generations sharing one system of preferences. The last section turns to the rate of interest. Out of equilibrium it is set by capital's demand price and can far exceed the reward needed to induce abstinence. With families discounting at different rates, equilibrium divides society into "the thrifty enjoying bliss and the improvident at the subsistence level".
