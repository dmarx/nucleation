# THEORY-153: A system can keep its identity independently within a shared environment only if it has five interacting subsystems, the first made of elements that are themselves viable systems, so that the structure recurs at every level of recursion; when one is missing or fails, the system degrades in characteristic ways, and without the subsystem for foresight the subsystems for operational control and for identity collapse into each other, leaving a system that can only react

## Source

Beer (1984), [LIT-752](../literature.d/LIT-752.md), read in [NOTE-577](../notes.d/NOTE-577.md) from a reprint that lacks
one page of the VSM section and the figures. *Brain of the Firm*,
[LIT-746](../literature.d/LIT-746.md), which first set the model out, is unread.

## What was actually shown

Nothing here is shown in the sense of tested. Beer states the model and
reflects on it in [LIT-752](../literature.d/LIT-752.md), "without seeking once again to expound
the model in detail or to demonstrate its validity".

- **The claim.** There are "five necessary and sufficient subsystems
  interactively involved in any organism or organization that is capable
  of maintaining its identity independently of other such organisms within
  a shared environment". System One consists of the elements that produce
  the system, and they are viable systems themselves.
- **Recursion.** "Any viable system contains, and is contained in, a
  viable system", with the same topology at each level.
- **Identity.** System Five closes the system, and "only this determines
  an identity". Which people or bodies make up System Five is nominated by
  the observer.
- **Regulation.** Every regulatory relation must meet Ashby's law of
  requisite variety, in its blocks, channels and transducers.
- **Pathology.** Breakdowns are traced to a failing subsystem. Without
  Two, the operational units fight for resources. Without Four, Three and
  Five collapse into each other: Beer's "decerebrate cat", which reacts
  from operational command and is incapable of "planning and foresight
  (Four) and will and judgment (Five)".
- **Method.** The model is claimed to be a scientific model reached by
  homomorphic mapping across systems, not an analogy, and to be testable
  by falsification. Its support is a long list of applications, recalled
  rather than archived.

## What this does not say

- **Not that "five" is fixed.** Beer calls the number, like the system's
  boundaries, arbitrary: with a different rubric there might have been
  another number. What he holds necessary is the closure that determines
  identity.
- **Not that existing is being viable.** The reverse: a system can be
  there without being "effectively there", and many operate "at the
  margins of viability".
- **Not that autonomy means non-interference.** In the model autonomy is
  "a precisely defined term", and recursive cohesion always takes up some
  of a subsystem's variety.
- **Not Aubin's sense of viable.** See Connections.
- **Not shown here.** The functions of the subsystems are referred to
  *Brain* and *Heart*, and the page of this copy that summarised them is
  missing.

## Connections

- **Aubin ([THEORY-134](THEORY-134.md)).** Aubin's viability is a property of a
  constraint set under a given dynamics; Beer's is a property of an
  organization's regulatory structure. Both separate being there now from
  being able to stay, and both leave outside the theory what counts as the
  system's required states or identity. The parallel is the record's.
- **Argyris ([THEORY-145](THEORY-145.md)).** A metasystem collapsed into operational
  control, with no foresight or judgment, resembles an organization able
  to correct errors only within its governing values. That reading is the
  record's; Beer does not use the terms.
- **Ostrom ([THEORY-149](THEORY-149.md)).** Nested enterprises and recursion both make
  nesting a mark of what lasts, Ostrom's as a correlate of survival,
  Beer's as a necessary condition.
- **The owner's essay.** §6.1's distinction between persisting and being
  viable, §8.3's recursion, and §7.1's question of whose identity an
  organization's commitments express.
