# NOTE-tmp938r4: Attributability, Answerability, and Accountability: In Defense of a Unified Account

## Contribution

The most direct rejoinder in the record to the pluralism of responsibility
that Watson's "Two Faces of Responsibility" began, in its three-way form
due to Shoemaker. Smith credits Watson with introducing "aretaic appraisal"
(n. 2) and then argues that the faces do not mark distinct kinds of
responsibility.

## Key results

- **Thesis (p. 575).** "“responsibility as answerability” is indeed the
  only kind of moral responsibility there is".
- **Watson's origin (n. 2).** "The notion of “aretaic appraisal” was first
  introduced by Gary Watson in his article “Two Faces of Responsibility,”
  Philosophical Topics 24 (1996): 227–48". It evaluates "excellences and
  faults—or virtues and vices—as manifested in thought and action" (Watson,
  Agency and Answerability, 10, as quoted).
- **Against attributability (§II).** "What Shoemaker calls “responsibility
  as attributability,” I will argue, is not a form of responsibility at
  all". Shoemaker's test, that a feature explain the agent's attitudes in
  normative domains, would make agents responsible for OCD, Tourette's or
  depression. Aretaic labels are withdrawn when answerability fails: "We
  reserve labels of this sort for agents whom we regard as answerable for
  the judgments reflected in their attitudes and behavior." The exchange in
  n. 14 ends "I guess I was wrong about him."
- **Against a separate accountability (§III).** Accountability "is just
  responsibility as answerability in contexts where an agent has violated a
  specific moral obligation to others". In Shoemaker's Anniversary and
  Cheating cases the responses differ, but "He’s the same guy, doing the
  same thing, in the same way; all that has changed in these two cases is
  Martha’s reaction to his failure."
- **Where differences belong (p. 589).** Responses differ by "(1) the
  seriousness of the moral failure, (2) the nature of our relationship with
  the agent, and perhaps even (3) our own moral standing", and "these
  considerations all have to do with what Scanlon has called “the ethics of
  blame” and not with the basic conditions of moral responsibility".
- **Open questions (§IV).** What answerability requires: chosen?
  intentional? evaluative judgment? "Must it express a morally significant
  quality of will?"
- **On Scanlon (n. 4).** Moral Dimensions departs from his earlier view:
  "he now emphasizes “relational significance” rather than judgment
  sensitivity".

## Assumptions

- Responsibility is a precondition of a range of moral responses, and a
  "conception" of responsibility is individuated by its preconditions, not
  by the response.
- The rational relations view: an agent is responsible for F when F bears a
  rational connection to her evaluative judgments.

## Concepts

- **answerability**; **attributability**; **accountability** (Shoemaker's
  three; Watson's two).
- **aretaic appraisal** (Watson).
- **rational relations view** (Smith).
- **ethics of blame** vs **conditions of responsibility** (after Scanlon).

## Bearing on the record

- THEORY-147 (Wolf: attributability and accountability are independent
  kinds of deep responsibility): Smith's thesis denies that they are kinds
  of responsibility at all. The two cannot both be right. A rival.
- A9 §5 and CLAIM-tmpnw7ez: A's distinction between attribution ("Does
  the action reflect the person's own ... character?") and accountability
  ("is it fair to condemn or sanction them for it?") is Watson's. On
  Smith's view the conduct A describes is answerable, and what changes with
  the agent's history is the fitting response, an ethics-of-blame matter.
  A's practical point, that an abused person's cruelty can be theirs and
  still warrant less harsh treatment, survives in Smith's terms. A's claim
  that these are two "forms of responsibility" does not.
- The SEP "Blame" neighbour: Smith's "our own moral standing" is the
  standing condition reported there.

## Limitations

Smith answers Shoemaker, not Watson. Watson's argument (fairness conditions
on accountability) appears only through Shoemaker's version. The paper
argues from cases and admits its first reply is "admittedly unsatisfying".
