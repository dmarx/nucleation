## WORK 1. Moll & Meltzoff 2011
- Provenance: https://ilabs.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/11Moll_Meltzoff_PerspectiveTaking_TheoryChapter.pdf. This is Meltzoff's lab (I-LABS, University of Washington), so a research-group page: lawful. Scanned PDF with OCR, 19 pp., fetched 2026-11-20. Read in full: all sections and the summary; references skimmed. The OCR has some errors (e.g. "Meltz'!ff", "ofthis"). Quotes below are copied from the OCR text and checked by script against it.
- Bibliographic: Henrike Moll and Andrew N. Meltzoff, "Perspective-Taking and its Foundation in Joint Attention", ch. 16 in N. Eilan, H. Lerman & J. Roessler (eds.), *Perception, Causation, and Objectivity* (Issues in Philosophy and Psychology), Oxford University Press, 2011, pp. 286-304. The cover line on the PDF gives this citation. No DOI is printed; it is not in the OpenAlex query I made.
- Summary: a stage model of perspective-taking from 0 to about 5 years that revises Flavell's level 1 and level 2. It adds level 0 (sharing attention, 9-12 months), splits level 1 into experiential (earlier) and visual (later), and splits level 2 into 2A (taking another's perspective, 3 years) and 2B (confronting perspectives, about 4.5 years). It argues that joint attention is a necessary condition for perspective-taking.
- Contribution: it puts joint attention into the ontogeny of perspectives as their foundation. It explains why visual perspective-taking lags behind experiential perspective-taking: the "curse of social engagement". It reports new colour-filter evidence for level-2-type perspective-taking at 36 months.
- Key results:
  - Intro, p. 286: "joint attention is a necessary condition for appreciating perspectives".
  - p. 287: "infants are at "level 0 perspective-taking"". They share perspectives without knowing anything about perspectives (9-12 months, §1, p. 288).
  - §1.1, p. 289: "just as role-taking is learned within the context of simple cooperative activities, so is perspective-taking acquired within joint attentional sequences."
  - §2.1, p. 292: "The research suggests that young children begin to appreciate that others may not see what they see at around 2 to 2.5 years of age." Footnote 2: an implicit level 1 may be present by 14 months on looking-time measures.
  - p. 287: "Counterintuitively, the latter seems to develop significantly earlier than level 1 visual perspective-taking." Here "the latter" is level 1 experiential perspective-taking, at 12-14 months (§2.2).
  - §2.3.2, pp. 294-295: social engagement "might lead them to overestimate what has been shared". 24-month-olds over-attributed experience to an adult who was co-present or still communicating: "the two-year-olds over-attributed experience to the adult in all these three cases". Conclusion: "the "curse of social engagement" compromise the ability to register others' ignorance". p. 296: "what is primary is the sharedness of the situation".
  - §3, p. 296, on Piaget: the "mountain problem designed by Piaget and Inhelder (1956)" "has led to significant underestimations of children's capacities", citing Borke 1975 on the complexity of the array.
  - §3, p. 297: "level 2 research has consistently shown that 4.5-year-olds are mostly successful in judging how an object looks from perspectives other than their own, whereas 3-year-olds are not."
  - §3, p. 298, colour filters: on one view "level 2 visual perspective-taking has been brought down by about 1.5 years, to 36 months of age."
  - §3.1, p. 299: "While 2A is well in place by 3 years of age, 2B emerges at around 4.5 years". 2B co-emerges with false belief, appearance-reality and alternative naming. This keeps the "unitary view" (Perner) at 2B.
- Assumptions: interactive response measures capture competence. Joint attention involves awareness that the other shares one's focus (§1.3). Sharing perspectives is "knowing how", not "knowing that" (p. 290).
- Concepts: level 0 / level 1 visual / level 1 experiential / level 2A / level 2B; joint attention; triangulation (the "checking looks" that "close the circuit of the triangulation", p. 289); the sharing hypothesis; the curse of social engagement; taking versus confronting perspectives; the I-thou / second person (Heal, Buber).
- Bearing:
  - THEORY-495 (Tomasello's obligation account): supports its premise that joint attention and joint engagement come first and ground later social cognition. By 3 years children take another's perspective (2A) but do not confront perspectives (2B).
  - Owner's open issue (focal point vs. second-order theory of mind): qualifies it. The chapter separates taking or sharing a perspective (available at 3) from confronting two perspectives, the recursive or contrastive kind (about 4.5). That fits the view that a 3-year-old's 50-50 split need not rest on "I expect you to expect". The chapter does not discuss coordination or focal points.
  - Sugden team reasoning (THEORY-1630, LIT-1698): parallel only. "We"-primacy ("the basic subjects of psychological predicates will be 'us'", quoting Heal, p. 294) resembles a we-frame without recursion.
  - THEORY-tmpqxwyk (Schelling 1957, on the branch): background; the chapter supplies the developmental timing.
  - LIT-837 (Piaget, Structuralism): contrast and qualify; the three-mountains task underestimated competence.
- Limitations: a theory chapter that reviews mostly its own lab's interactive studies. The 2A/2B split rests largely on one colour-filter study (Moll & Meltzoff 2011, Child Development). The OCR copy has errors. It does not engage Piaget's own concept of egocentrism beyond the task's difficulty.
- Candidate THEORY: Moll and Meltzoff argue that perspective-taking is founded on joint attention: infants share perspectives at about 12 months before they know anything of perspectives (level 0); they know what others have experienced (14 months) before what others can see (2-2.5 years), because co-present social engagement leads them to over-attribute shared perception; and they take another's differing visual perspective by 3 years (level 2A) but confront two perspectives on one object only at about 4.5 years (level 2B), with false belief.
  - promote_when: independent studies replicate (a) level 1 experiential success before level 1 visual success in matched interactive tasks, (b) over-attribution of experience to a co-present or communicating adult at 2 years, and (c) 3-year-olds' success on a level-2 colour-filter task alongside failure on tasks that require confronting two perspectives.

## WORK 2. Moll & Kadipasaoglu 2013
- Provenance: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2013.00558/pdf. Publisher open access, so lawful. Also in PMC as PMC3767909: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3767909; the Europe PMC XML route returned 503. Also on the author's USC page (https://dornsife.usc.edu/aba/wp-content/uploads/sites/130/2023/10/Moll_Kadipasaoglu_FHN.pdf), not fetched. Text PDF, 11 pp., fetched 2026-11-20. Read in full; references skipped.
- Bibliographic: Henrike Moll and Derya Kadipasaoglu, "The primacy of social over visual perspective-taking", *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 7, Article 558, published 10 September 2013, pp. 1-6 plus references, doi:10.3389/fnhum.2013.00558. Review article; reviewers Ian Apperly and Mark Gardner.
- Summary: a review arguing that social (experiential) perspective-taking, meaning tracking what others desired, witnessed or said, emerges at least a year before level 1 visual perspective-taking. The lag holds across interactive and looking-time methods. The explanation is joint attention and common ground: co-presence creates an illusion of shared perception. Late perspectival drawing is offered as converging evidence.
- Contribution: reverses the standard assumption, and Clark & Marshall's, that physical co-presence is the easiest route to mutual knowledge. Gives updated ages.
- Key results:
  - Abstract, p. 1: "we argue for the developmental primacy of social over visual perspective-taking".
  - p. 1: the standard assumptions "are seriously called into question" by the fact that visual perspective-taking "does not precede but follows social perspective-taking ontogenetically".
  - p. 3, "Visual perspective-taking": level 2 "emerges between 4 and 5 years". Level 1: "level 1 perspective-taking as demonstrated by tests using interactive methods emerges between the second and third birthday" (p. 4).
  - p. 4, looking time: "is 13–16 months (Luo and Baillargeon, 2007" is the youngest age for level 1 visual on that technique; experiential sensitivity appears by 7 months (Kovács et al. 2010).
  - p. 4: "Contra Clark and Marshall (1981) and contra intuition, immediate physical co-presence does not necessarily facilitate the delineation of common ground."
  - p. 5, "A developmental lag": "A more synchronous pattern is found at level 2". Visual and social perspectives co-emerge at 4-5 years, supporting a common thread (Perner).
  - p. 5, "Shared perceptual spaces": "young children have a proclivity to treat social interaction as a sufficient condition for shared perceptual availability".
  - p. 3, "Prior discourse": "Through joint attention, infants construct a common ground (Clark, 1996) with specific other persons". They keep dyad-specific common grounds.
  - p. 6, "A glance at early picture-making": "it is not before 7 or 8 years that they spontaneously create view-dependent images".
  - p. 6, "Concluding remarks": "Only once it can be taken for granted that we attend to the same thing, is there room, in a second step, to “objectify” the different viewpoints". Also: "Joint attention thus directly paves the way to early forms of social, but not visual perspective-taking." Visual perspective-taking in chimpanzees is tied to competition, the social kind in humans to cooperation.
- Assumptions: the reference-disambiguation paradigms measure perspective-taking. Looking-time findings are interpreted as sensitivity to what others registered, although the paper notes that they relativize the role of joint attention (p. 2).
- Concepts: social versus visual perspective-taking; experiential background or common ground; linguistic co-presence; the overestimation thesis; dyad-specific common ground; intellectual realism (Luquet) in drawing.
- Bearing:
  - THEORY-495: supports. Joint attention and common ground are primary in early social cognition. Cooperation versus competition is offered as the reason humans privilege social perspective-taking (p. 6).
  - Owner's issue: supports "joint attention/common ground taken as primitive". Common ground is formed through joint attention with a specific partner, by 1-2 years, before visual perspective-taking and before confronting perspectives. Mutual knowledge here is not built by recursive co-presence inference (the Clark & Marshall / Schiffer route is explicitly rejected for young children).
  - Schelling (THEORY-tmpqxwyk): qualifies the mutual-expectation reading. What is mutually given comes from shared history, not from recursive inference.
  - LIT-2484 (Clark & Brennan, "Grounding in communication"): parallel. Grounding as a developmental achievement.
  - LIT-837 (Piaget): background only. Piaget's "egocentric speech" is mentioned on p. 3.
- Limitations: short and programmatic. It confines the lag analysis to level 1. The evidence is drawn mostly from the authors' own paradigms. The drawing section is interpretive. It does not address Piaget's own concept of egocentrism. It concedes (p. 5) that automatic level 1 perspective-taking in adults (Samson et al. 2010) is in tension with its overestimation thesis.
- Candidate THEORY: Moll and Kadipasaoglu argue that social perspective-taking, tracking what others have desired, witnessed or said, emerges in the second year, at least a year before level 1 visual perspective-taking (2-3 years interactively, 13-16 months on looking time), because joint attention builds a dyad-specific common ground while co-presence induces young children to assume a shared perceptual space; the lag disappears at level 2, which visual and social perspective-taking reach together at 4-5 years.
  - promote_when: matched interactive tasks in independent samples show children solving experiential-reference tasks earlier than visual level 1 tasks, and over-attributing perception to co-present partners, with level 2 visual and false-belief success co-occurring at 4-5 years.

## WORK 3. Choifer 2021
- Provenance: https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s13164-020-00519-0.pdf. Publisher open access ("© The Author(s) 2021"; OpenAlex marks it hybrid OA), so lawful. Text PDF, fetched 2026-11-20. Read in full: Introduction, Parts I-III to §3.5. References skipped.
- Bibliographic: Alla Choifer (University of Gothenburg), "Interpretational Complexities in Developmental Research and a Piagetian Reading of the False-Belief Task", *Review of Philosophy and Psychology* 12 (2021): 923-952, doi:10.1007/s13164-020-00519-0. Accepted 22 November 2020, published online 19 January 2021.
- Summary: argues that later critics (Perner 1991, Southgate 2019) misread Piaget's egocentrism as an adult-style self-preference. For Piaget it is an undifferentiation of self and other, "an illusion of perspective". The paper then reads the false-belief task as a test of self-other differentiation. Before about 4.5 years children do "non-subject-related" perspective-switching (egocentric in Piaget's sense). Passing false belief marks "subject-related" perspective-taking, in which the other becomes an epistemically separate subject. Hence it doubts early implicit false-belief claims.
- Contribution: a defence and re-reading of Piaget's concept, not his ages. It concedes the level 1 / level 2 data and reinterprets them.
- Key results:
  - Abstract, p. 923: "My new interpretation of the test suggests two different ways of perspective-taking of which one is egocentric in Piaget’s sense."
  - §1.3, p. 930, on Perner's reading of the three-mountains evidence ("largely a myth"): "This conclusion displays a crucial misunderstanding of Piagetian thought."
  - §1.3, p. 931: "according to Piaget, childish egocentrism cannot increase but only decrease with age". Also: "Like Perner, Southgate tends to confuse our adult understanding of egocentrism (an inclination to an excessive self-reference) with childish egocentrism (an inability to self-refer)."
  - §2.1, p. 933, current ages as the paper states them: "At the less advanced level – Level 1 – mastered by the age of 2 years". Also: "At the more advanced level – Level 2 – reached around age 4".
  - §2.3, p. 936: non-subject-related perspective-taking "is already developed in the second year of life and is manifested in the child’s ability to switch between different vantage points". Also: "This switching proceeds egocentrically, i.e., without connection to any specific subject."
  - §3.2, p. 941: "Perspective-switching ability is then developed in the second year of life and is widely used in various contexts of joint attention."
  - §3.4, p. 947: "the implicit false-belief paradigms cannot secure the status of the other person as other".
  - §3.5, p. 947: "children’s perspective-taking is egocentric and accomplished without epistemic differentiation between oneself and the other".
- Assumptions: false belief is the criterion of epistemic self-other differentiation. Belief and knowledge are meta-representational, so "belief" does not apply to young children (p. 940). It sides with emergence accounts over expression accounts of executive function (§3.3).
- Concepts: childish versus adult egocentrism; decentration; self-other differentiation (perceptual / epistemic); subject-related versus non-subject-related perspective-taking; switching versus confronting (Perner et al. 2002); altercentric bias (Southgate), rejected as a substitute term; epistemic relation.
- Bearing:
  - LIT-837 (Piaget): supports a charitable reading of Piaget's concept. It holds that the three-mountains criticisms (good-view preference, task complexity) do not touch egocentrism as undifferentiation.
  - THEORY-495 and the owner's issue: qualifies, and points the other way from Moll. Joint attention in the second year is egocentric switching, in which the other "inhabits the child’s egocentric world on the child’s own terms" (p. 948). On this reading a 3-year-old's "we" is pre-differentiated rather than a recognition of two subjects. That would make a symmetric split an affordance of an undifferentiated shared frame, with no recursive "I expect you to expect". The paper does not discuss sharing, fairness or coordination.
  - Tomasello's 2016 reply on joint intentionality (NOTE-tmpe9epc, on the branch): contrast. The paper treats "sharing" talk applied to infants as the observer's ascription (§1.2, citing Barresi & Moore and Müller & Carpendale).
- Limitations: a philosophical argument with no new data. The ages it gives (level 1 by 2, level 2 around 4) are textbook ages. It does not engage Moll & Meltzoff's 2A/2B colour-filter data except through Moll & Tomasello 2012's switching/confronting distinction. Its criterion (passing false belief = differentiation) is stipulated rather than tested.
- Candidate THEORY: Choifer argues that Piaget's egocentrism is an undifferentiation of self and other, not a preference for one's own view, so later "refutations" from the three-mountains task misread it; children under about 4 switch between vantage points without assigning them to subjects (non-subject-related, egocentric perspective-taking), and only passing the false-belief task shows the other grasped as an epistemically separate subject.
  - promote_when: a test that distinguishes subject-assigned from subject-less perspective use shows that children who fail verbal false belief succeed on level-1 and switching tasks only in ways explicable without assigning the view to a particular person, and that this changes when they pass false belief.

## Answers to the brief's questions (from the three readings only)
1. Which of Piaget's claims held and which were revised:
   - Revised: the three-mountains task underestimates competence (MM p. 296, citing Borke 1975; Perner via Choifer p. 929).
   - Revised: young children are not egocentric about what others see. Level 1 is present at 2-2.5 years (MM p. 292; MK p. 4), and experiential perspective-taking at 12-14 months.
   - Held, though dated later than Piaget's preoperational span: a genuine understanding that one thing can be seen in two ways arrives late. Level 2B / confronting perspectives at about 4.5, synchronous with false belief (MM p. 299; MK p. 5).
   - Held in a re-read form: Choifer defends the concept itself (egocentrism as undifferentiation, decreasing with age) and reinterprets early competences as egocentric switching.
   - Also later than Piaget assumed: spontaneous view-dependent drawing at 7-8 (MK p. 6).
2. Current ages:
   - Level 1 visual: about 2-2.5 years interactively (24 months, Moll & Tomasello 2006); 13-16 months on looking time (MK p. 4); 14 months implicitly (MM fn. 2).
   - Level 1 experiential: 12-14 months.
   - Level 2: 4-5 years classically (4.5 on the turtle task). Moll & Meltzoff split it into 2A at 36 months (colour filters) and 2B at about 4.5.
3. Joint attention and social interaction: Works 1 and 2 hold Tomasello's line explicitly. Joint attention is a necessary condition and foundation of perspective-taking, and common ground is built in joint attention. Work 1 uses "triangulation" for infants' checking looks (p. 289; also p. 290, "close the triangulation") but does not cite Davidson; neither work does. Work 2 quotes Campbell 2012: grasping perspectives "is always grounded in a prior knowledge of which thing is in question". That is close to the Davidsonian point that a shared object anchors perspectival difference, but I found no citation of Davidson. Work 3 takes joint attention as the setting of egocentric switching, not of differentiated perspective.
