# CASE-tmpgdy8a: Lindsey Stone's Arlington photograph: one post, taken by friends as a tasteless in-joke and by strangers as contempt

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for: a documented case of a
change of audience without translation, with uptake documented in both
audiences. It is partial. The search asked for identical wording, and this
token is an image.

## The case

Jon Ronson's account, from interviews with Stone eighteen months later
(*The Guardian*, 21 February 2015):

- **The frame.** Off duty, Stone and her colleague Jamie Schuh "had a running
  joke: taking stupid photographs, 'smoking in front of a no-smoking sign or
  posing in front of statues, mimicking the pose.'" At Arlington "we saw the
  Silence And Respect sign… and inspiration struck." Stone posed "pretending
  she was shouting and swearing – flipping the bird, and with her hand to her
  open mouth." Schuh posted it on Facebook and tagged her, "thinking we were
  funny".
- **The first audience: their Facebook friends.** "A few Facebook friends
  posted unenthusiastic comments. 'One had served in the military and he wrote
  a message saying, "This is kind of offensive. I know you girls, but it's
  tasteless."'" Stone answered: "Whoa! It's just us being douchebags! Forget
  about it!"
- **The change of audience.** Schuh's mobile uploads "weren't private". Four
  weeks later the photo reached strangers.
- **The second audience.** "'Lindsey Stone hates the military and hates
  soldiers who have died in foreign wars', 'You should rot in hell'". A "Fire
  Lindsey Stone" Facebook page "attracted 12,000 likes". She lost her job.
- **The speaker.** "I wanted to scream: 'It was just about a sign'". Her
  statement at the time, as NBC4 Washington reported it from Gawker (21
  November 2012): "challenging authority in general … OBVIOUSLY we meant NO
  disrespect".

What is held fixed is the token: one photograph, one post. What changes is the
audience, from people who "know you girls" and the running joke to the public.

## What it can show

That a change of audience alone can change the act verdict, with no
translation and no change to the token. For friends it was a joke in poor
taste, from people they knew. For strangers it was an expression of contempt
for the dead. The in-group reading depends on a frame, the series of photos
that disobey signs, which the second audience did not hold.

That is [CLAIM-tmpcq79k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpcq79k.md)'s point met without translation. The objection says
a translation changes the audience and the norms, and that whether the act
survives then turns on a correspondence of relations that neither structure
supplies. Here the words are not changed at all. The correspondence that
fails is between audience roles. [CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md) version 2 answers that a new
audience in the same role keeps the constituent. The case shows the residue:
the strangers did not occupy the friends' role, and nothing in the token said
which role a viewer was in. It also falls outside [CLAIM-091](../claims.d/CLAIM-091.md)'s model of
translation, which holds the audience fixed. It illustrates and bears no
weight.

## What it cannot show

**The condition it fails: identical wording.** The token is a photograph, a
gesture beside a sign. Whether the post had a caption is unverified. So it
shows a change of audience for an image, not for words, and it is filed as
partial for that reason.

**The split is not clean.** The first audience did not take it as harmless.
The friends' verdict was "kind of offensive" and "tasteless": a joke in poor
taste. The strangers' verdict was an act of disrespect. Both come through
Stone's recollection eighteen months later, and the friends' comments are not
independently archived.

**The strongest reply.** The strangers attributed a different intention
("hates the military"), so an intention-based account
([CLAIM-tmpc25sn](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpc25sn.md)) says they misread a joke, and the act was the joke
throughout. The friend who knew the intention ("I know you girls") still
judged it, but judged its taste, not its category. So the case shows that
verdicts move with the audience. It does not show that the audience's role is
constitutive rather than evidential.

**What would close the gap.** A reader test that holds the photo fixed and
varies only the viewer's relation to the series, as a change of situation,
do(S), not an instruction, do(I) ([CLAIM-130](../claims.d/CLAIM-130.md)). One group sees it in a feed
after the earlier no-smoking-sign photo, as the friends did. Another sees it
alone. A third sees it alone with the note "this was a joke". Raters judge the
act category (joke, insult, contempt), separately from taste and from the
intention they attribute. The photo and the two comment sets could serve as
items for [CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md), as an arm with no rendering. A verbal companion
would meet the wording condition: a documented in-group remark later quoted to
outsiders. The search found none verified.

**The Sacco tweet, unverified.** Justine Sacco's tweet of 20 December 2013
("Going to Africa. Hope I don't get AIDS. Just kidding. I'm white!") has the
wording this case lacks. It is mentioned here only as unverified. The primary
account (Ronson, *New York Times Magazine*, February 2015) could not be reached.
The text and the follower count rest on secondary summaries, and the first
audience's uptake is not documented in anything the search read.
