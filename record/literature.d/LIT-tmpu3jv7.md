# LIT-tmpu3jv7: Biosemantics

Ruth Garrett Millikan (2009; pre-print 2008), in Brian McLaughlin (ed.),
*The Oxford Handbook of Philosophy of Mind*, pp. 394-406 —
[doi:10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199262618.003.0024](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199262618.003.0024);
author's pre-print on her faculty page.

## Key takeaways

- **What teleology does.** Teleological theories share "only a view
  about how falseness in representations is possible" (p. 2): a system
  designed by evolution or learning to make representations sometimes
  fails to, and "[t]eleological theories, just as such, are not theories
  of mental content" (p. 3).
- **What representing is, on biosemantics.** A producer and a consumer,
  "designed by natural selection or by learning to cooperate" (p. 5),
  share a sign. Its content is the affair it must map onto, by a rule,
  if the consumer is to perform its functions in the normal way (pp.
  5-6). Descriptive, directive and pushmi-pullyu icons differ in who is
  responsible for the correspondence (p. 8).
- **Truth needs history; natural signs do not.** Actually representing
  is picturing and carrying natural information, "not a matter
  determined by a history of selection"; but "attributions of truth and
  falsity ... make sense only by reference to function, hence by
  reference to a history of selection", and "true" and "false" "do not
  apply to natural signs" (p. 9).
- **Error without breakage.** The magnetosome led by a bar magnet is not
  malfunctioning, yet "what the magnetosome says is false despite its
  being produced through a normal mechanism" (p. 7): things fail their
  functions because their conditions are not Normal (pp. 7-8).
