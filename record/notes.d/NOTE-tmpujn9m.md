# NOTE-tmpujn9m: Heins, Unrau and Avram — Gift-giving and reciprocity in global society: Introducing Marcel Mauss in international studies

## Contribution

The introduction to a special issue of the *Journal of International
Political Theory* on Mauss. It sets out Mauss's gift as a theory for
international relations: a kind of exchange that is neither a market
nor a contract and yet obliges, and that can make and keep up relations
between groups with no authority over them. It gives one worked aid
case, the Marshall Plan as Dillon (1968) read it, places Mauss in
Martin Wight's map of international thought, and introduces the issue's
papers. A synthesis and literature map, with no new data.

## Key insight

What a gift does depends on whether it can be returned. A gift its
recipient can answer builds a relation; one it cannot answer leaves the
recipient lower and the relation spoiled. Dillon's lesson from the
Marshall Plan is that aid between nations should be built so that the
recipient can give back, as in the circular kula, rather than as a
potlatch that the recipient cannot match.

## Assumptions

- **Transfer from archaic exchange.** That Mauss's account of exchange
  among clans and tribes carries over to modern states and to relations
  between societies (pp. 128–129). The authors argue it; they do not show
  it.
- **States feel what persons feel.** That the psychological and status
  effects of an unreturned gift that Dillon describes, drawn from a case
  study of one French engineer and factory owner, operate between nations
  (p. 131).

## Key results

- **Definition (p. 127).** Gift-giving is "the generous transfer of
  socially valued objects without any (legal or contractual) guarantee of
  reciprocation". Reciprocity is "asynchronous" and in kind, and gifts
  "seem to demand that they are not only accepted but also returned in one
  form or another".
- **The unreturned gift (p. 127).** "The failure to return a gift changes
  its very character." In that case "the gift “poisons” relationships".
- **Purpose (p. 128).** Gift exchange between societies is "not so much
  the redistribution of resources but rather the creation and maintenance
  of relationships"; gifts are "neither fully voluntary nor a strict
  duty", and "sometimes also about establishing hierarchy and exclusive
  prestige".
- **Two forms of reciprocity (p. 128).** The potlatch is "total services
  of an agonistic type" (Mauss); the kula is "circular, not antagonistic",
  and "creates a permanent connection".
- **Hattori (p. 129).** "scholars of foreign aid practices have drawn
  inspiration from Mauss’ essay on the gift (e.g. Furia, 2015; Hattori,
  2001; Heins and Unrau, 2016)". The only mention of Hattori.
- **Mauss on the receiver (p. 131, quoting Mauss 1990: 83–84).** "The
  unreciprocated gift still makes the person who has accepted it inferior,
  particularly when it has been accepted with no thought of returning it";
  "Charity is still wounding for him who has accepted it".
- **The Marshall Plan in France (p. 131).** Dillon treats it as
  "downward giving". Many French "responded instead with resentment and
  anti-Americanism", because "the French felt the obligation to repay
  gifts but did not have the possibility to fulfill their obligation and
  thereby to become accepted as equals". Responses included "the largely
  symbolic pursuit of grandeur" and recasting the Plan "as a
  self-interested business operation".
- **Dillon's remedy (pp. 131–132).** The kula ring "as a useful heuristic
  analogy for the design of better models" for gifts between nations. He
  faults the Plan "for not having allowed Europeans to respond in a
  psychologically gratifying way to the obligation to repay"; "instead of
  being sufficiently kula-like and based on circular reciprocity, the Plan
  took on some features of an archaic “potlatch ceremony”".
- **Hobbes's free gift (p. 137).** An "Antecedent Free-gift" must be met
  with gratitude, or "there will be no beginning of benevolence, or trust".
- **Not only among equals (p. 138).** Mauss "does not start from the
  premise that reciprocity only works among like-units", and studies
  "forms of exchange that trigger cycles of repeated interaction without
  being based on formal equality or symmetry".
- **Solidarity built by giving (p. 138).** "group solidarity is not a
  prerequisite of successful cooperation, but a resource that needs to be
  built up through cycles of gift exchange".
- **Mutual dependence (p. 139).** The Gift's purpose was "to alert its
  readers to the fact that humans are mutually dependent on each other".
- **Indirect and delayed (p. 139, the authors' précis of Ramel).**
  "Reciprocity, he argues following Mauss, is not always direct but can
  also be indirect and delayed", in "chains of reciprocity" (p. 140).
  Second hand: the précis of a paper in the issue, not read here.

## Concepts

- **Gift**: a transfer of something socially valued, with no legal or
  contractual guarantee of return, that yet obliges one (p. 127).
- **The three obligations**: "the obligation to give, to receive, and to
  reciprocate" (p. 140).
- **Potlatch**: agonistic exchange, establishing hierarchy and prestige;
  in Dillon, the form in which "the poor were humiliated" (pp. 128, 132).
- **Kula**: circular exchange, building a lasting connection and trust
  (p. 128).
- **Downward giving**: Dillon's term for the Marshall Plan's gifts from
  the stronger to the weaker (p. 131).
- **Chains of reciprocity**: indirect, delayed return, on Ramel (p. 140).
- **Solidarist rationalism**: where the authors place Mauss in Wight's
  scheme, against Marxist revolutionism and realism (pp. 136–137).

## Connections

It draws on Mauss's *The Gift* (1925) throughout, on Malinowski (1922)
for the kula, and on Dillon (1968) for the Marshall Plan. It is the
neighbour read for Hattori (2001), LIT-tmpr9mis, and restates none of
his results: it confirms only that he applies Mauss to foreign aid. It
does not mention Bourdieu, grants and loans, South–South cooperation,
capacity or exit. Mauss's essay itself and Burning Man's statement of
its principles are the other works of this batch.

In the record, the nearest holding on gift economies is Lessig's *Remix*
(LIT-1298, read in NOTE-1023): sharing against commercial economies,
money crowding out sharing, and Mauss only through Hyde. Lessig's point
is that a price changes the relation; Heins et al.'s is that an
unanswerable gift does. Sewell's theory of structure (LIT-1383, read in
NOTE-1078) uses the potlatch as an example of resources that take their
value from schemas, there the chief's power (pp. 11–12), which is the
hierarchy-building side of the potlatch that Heins et al. set against
the kula. On aid, it complements Ostrom, Gibson, Shivakumar and
Andersson (LIT-tmpnb22c, THEORY-tmph35ra): both say the one-way form of
help matters, they through the recipient's effort, Heins et al. through
the recipient's standing and response. Moore (LIT-tmpsb4vt) and Moss,
Pettersson and van de Walle (LIT-tmp4d1o2) treat aid as unearned revenue
that weakens the state's tie to its citizens; this reading adds that it
also binds the recipient to the donor in an unequal relation.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpqa0qu (reciprocity, not self-sufficiency).** Supports the
  reframing toward reciprocity, in the social register: one-way aid that
  cannot be repaid leaves the receiver inferior and the relation poisoned
  (p. 131), and circular, kula-like reciprocity is offered as the better
  design (p. 132). That adds a cost of one-way supply in standing to the
  claim's cost in capacity. It also qualifies "reciprocal": the return
  may be circular or indirect rather than each partner supplying the other
  (pp. 128, 139–140), and need not be between equals (p. 138). Dillon's
  case is one study read at second hand, and nothing measures the effect.
- **QUESTION-398 (independence from the founding intervention).** Leaves
  it open. On Dillon's case, a founding gift that cannot be returned keeps
  the receiver's relation to the giver unequal after the aid has ended:
  Dillon's study of 1951–60 is "a striking example of how an international
  gift can lead to lingering feelings of humiliation and resentment"
  (p. 138). That is a persistence of the relation, not of a lost
  capacity.
- **THEORY-1351 (mutual dependence).** A loose resonance only: Mauss's
  aim is to show that "humans are mutually dependent on each other"
  (p. 139), and solidarity is built through cycles of exchange (p. 138).
  That is a social dependence, not the reproductive one the theory means.
- **CLAIM-tmpoh07b, CLAIM-597, CLAIM-554, QUESTION-277, THEORY-1048.**
  Nothing bears on them: no capacity learned by doing, no supplied
  maintenance, no niche differentiation, no route to dependence, and gifts
  are treated as relations, not as leaky public goods.
- **Hattori (2001), LIT-tmpr9mis.** Confirms only that Hattori draws on
  Mauss. His argument stays unverified.

## Limitations

- An introduction and literature map. Its aid evidence is one secondary
  study, Dillon (1968), built on a case study of a French engineer.
- Hattori, Kowalski, Stirrat and Henkel, and Mawdsley are not discussed,
  apart from the one citation of Hattori.
- Nothing on development aid after the Marshall Plan, on South–South
  cooperation, or on whether reciprocal framing changes outcomes.
- Nothing on free riding in gift systems.
- Generalized reciprocity is not named (Sahlins is not cited); the kula
  is "circular" and Ramel's reciprocity "indirect and delayed".

## Open questions

- Do aid recipients who cannot reciprocate show lower standing toward the
  donor, or resentment, more than comparable recipients who can? A
  comparative study of aid relations would tell.
- Does an arrangement that lets the recipient give back, to the donor or
  onward, reduce that effect, as Dillon proposed?
