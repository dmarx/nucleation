# NOTE-tmp0v2mf: Tomasello and Vaish — Origins of Human Cooperation and Morality

## Contribution

A review that joins the comparative experiments with apes and children to
the interdependence hypothesis and maps its two steps onto child
development. It is the book's argument in compact form.

## Key results

1. **Morality as cooperation.** "From an evolutionary perspective, morality
   is a form of cooperation." The second step is "an agent-neutral
   morality in which individuals follow and enforce group-wide social
   norms" (p. 231).
2. **Apes.** Their reciprocity is attitudinal: "individuals have more
   positive affect toward those who have helped them" (p. 234);
   "chimpanzees coordinate, but they show no commitment to their partner"
   (p. 236).
3. **First step.** "Interdependence thus breeds helping." "This social
   selection of partners in interdependent contexts thus advantages good
   cooperators." The result is "what one may call a joint morality"
   (p. 239).
4. **Second step.** "what one may call a collective morality" (p. 240);
   "Social norms are thus mutually known group expectations and
   commitments" (p. 245).
5. **Development.** "young children before about 3 years of age may not
   really understand social norms as such." (p. 240). Three-year-olds are
   "more likely to divide up their rewards equally if they obtained the
   rewards by working collaboratively than by working individually or
   receiving a windfall" (p. 243). Toddler morality is second-personal,
   "based on personal relationships and social emotions (Darwall 2006)"
   (p. 245). In norm studies "approximately one-quarter of the children
   protested using normative language" (p. 246), and for conventional norms
   "they protest more against an in-group member than an out-group member"
   (p. 247).
6. **The self.** "I am sanctioning myself or praising myself on behalf of
   the group, as it were." (p. 248). "The transition from a second-personal
   to a norm-based morality is thus not an abrupt one" (p. 250).
7. **Conclusion.** "These collaborative activities are the origins of human
   morality." (p. 251).

## Assumptions

An ecological change forced obligate collaborative foraging (posited);
children recapitulate the phylogenetic steps; the children studied are
WEIRD, and culture is said to modify and shape cooperative tendencies, not
create them.

## Concepts

Interdependence; joint and collective intentionality; second-personal and
agent-neutral morality; attitudinal reciprocity; partner choice; joint and
collective morality; normative self-governance; guilt and shame as
internalized group judgment.

## How far it stands in

For the précis (LIT-tmpfottc) and the book (LIT-504): it covers roughly
the book's chapter 1 (interdependence), the ape half of chapter 2, chapter
3 (second-personal morality) and chapter 4 ("objective" morality), without
those terms; nothing like chapter 5. It predates the book's account of
obligation as self-regulation within joint commitment, which LIT-125
gives. Whether the book concedes a tie between reciprocity and obligation
is not settled by it.

## Bearing on the record

- **LIT-504**, the closest lawful stand-in so far for chapters 1–4.
- **THEORY-495**, background and support: the same two-step shape six
  years earlier; the obligation account develops the passage on
  sanctioning oneself on behalf of the group.
- **LIT-495** and Alexander's account, a contrast: reciprocity here is
  ape-level and attitudinal, and collaboration, not indirect reciprocity,
  is the source of morality; both give partner choice and reputation a
  role.
- **THEORY-457** (Baumard and colleagues), a parallel to the
  joint-morality step and its partner choice.
- **THEORY-075** (Korsgaard on obligation), cited for normative
  self-governance (p. 240); **LIT-2756** (Darwall), the source of
  "second-personal" (p. 245).
- **CLAIM-096 and QUESTION-007**, background: a genesis, not a
  grounding.

## Limitations

A review, mostly of the authors' own lab; the evolutionary steps inferred
from contrasts between living apes and children; predates the book.
