# LIT-tmpm7tp8: Exploring the Assistance Dilemma in Experiments with Cognitive Tutors

Koedinger, K. R. & Aleven, V. (2007). Exploring the Assistance Dilemma in Experiments with Cognitive Tutors. *Educational Psychology Review* 19(3): 239–264. doi:10.1007/s10648-007-9049-0.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, NOTE-tmp607yf. Page numbers are the journal's.*

- **The dilemma.** "Instructional interaction should optimize student
  involvement, not maximize or minimize it" (p. 260). Withholding lets
  students learn by doing and benefit from generation effects; its cost is
  that they get stuck and strengthen errors.
- **The tutor's rule is withhold first, add on failure.** "Cognitive Tutors
  initially withhold information about problem solutions and solution
  steps, and then interactively add information, only as needed"
  (p. 260). Progress is gated on measured mastery, each skill's
  probability of being known passing "a pre-defined threshold, typically
  set to 0.95" (p. 247).
- **Within that design, more help, not less.** Immediate feedback beat no
  feedback; explanatory beat yes/no; so "information should be withheld
  very sparingly" (p. 255). The authors add that this "should not be
  construed as supporting information giving in general" (p. 260).
- **Learners ask badly.** Students "frequently use bottom-out hints to
  obtain answers, without reading prior hints", and "even after multiple
  errors on a step, students often do not request help" (pp. 253–254).
- **Fading.** Worked examples faded into problems "To adjust information
  giving/withholding to learners' growing competence" took less time for
  equal or better post-tests (Schwonke et al. 2007, p. 257).
- **The open problem** is the switch point: "a rough criterion for deciding
  to give rather than withhold is when the task gets too difficult and thus
  the probability of error or unproductive thinking is high" (p. 260), and
  the threshold is unknown.

## Standing in the record

Here as the record's first holding on tutoring and on support designed
to fade, a gap the supplied-maintenance filing left. It is an independent
domain for the order of supply and capacity in CLAIM-602: the tutor keeps
the learner doing the steps and adds help only on failure, and judges
success by tests taken without the tutor. It qualifies that claim and
CLAIM-600 in two ways the record should keep: too little help is the more
common harm within tutored practice, so early withdrawal is not safe by
default; and withdrawal is graded with measured competence, not a single
event. Its hint costs, which lower the mastery estimate and bring more
problems, are a designed counter to the low-effort response CLAIM-603
discusses, which gaming survives. Filed from it: THEORY-tmpngvdn. Wood,
Bruner and Ross (1976), the usual origin of the word scaffolding for
tutoring, was
registered the same day and not reached; Koedinger and Aleven do not cite
it and stand in for none of it.
