# NOTE-tmpeuwqz: Evolution, altruism and cognitive architecture: a critique of Sober and Wilson's argument for psychological altruism

## Contribution

A careful restatement of the evolutionary argument in *Unto Others*' second
half, with diagrams of the motivational structures at issue, and a
rebuttal. It shows that the argument turns on an unstated assumption about
cognitive architecture: that the beliefs a hedonist would need are
ordinary, revisable beliefs.

## Key insight

Whether a desire for someone else's good is ultimate or instrumental is a
question about where it sits in the agent's practical reasoning, not about
its object. A parent who wants to help her child "as an end in itself" is
an altruist. A parent who wants it because she believes she will feel bad
otherwise is a hedonist. The two act identically, and the debate is over
which architecture selection built.

## Assumptions

- **The definitions** (Preliminaries).
  - A behaviour is evolutionarily altruistic if it lowers the actor's
    inclusive fitness and raises another's.
  - It is psychologically altruistic if it is motivated by an ultimate
    desire for another's well-being.
  - An ultimate desire is one not produced or sustained by practical
    reasoning.
  - Egoism says all ultimate desires are self-interested. Hedonism, its
    strongest form, which Sober and Wilson choose to meet, says they are
    only for one's own pleasure and the absence of one's pain.
  - Motivational pluralism (Sober and Wilson's altruism) allows both kinds.
- **Practical reasoning as a causal process** in which a desire and a
  belief produce a further desire (after Goldman 1970).
- **Parental care as the test case**, since it is not evolutionarily
  altruistic and so avoids the group-selection dispute.

## Key results

- **Evolutionary and psychological altruism are logically independent**
  (Preliminaries). Kin-directed help is usually not evolutionarily altruistic on
  the inclusive-fitness definition. A mindless organism can be an
  evolutionary altruist, and an ultimate desire for one's own offspring's
  welfare is psychologically altruistic though fitness-enhancing.
- **The three solutions** Sober and Wilson consider (305): altruism,
  hedonism (pleasure and pain tied to beliefs about the child), and
  pluralism (both). Selection would weigh availability, reliability and
  energetic efficiency. They argue only reliability separates them.
- **Stich's models.**
  - Future Pain Hedonism: the parent believes she will feel bad later if
    she does not help.
  - Current Pain Hedonism: the child's need makes her feel bad now, and
    she believes helping will stop it.
- **Arguments 1 and 2** (315–316): the belief–affect link is imperfect and
  must be strong. Stich replies that fear, anger and disgust are built the
  same way, so selection evidently tolerates such links. Neither argument
  touches Future Pain Hedonism.
- **Argument 3** (320): two pathways (PLUR) are more reliable than one.
  Stich replies that redundancy has costs and the authors give no reason
  it pays here.
- **Argument 4** (314–315): the hedonist's linking belief is empirical and
  could be lost, and the parental care would then collapse. The
  instrumental desire stays only if the organism "is trapped by an
  unalterable illusion". Stich calls this "a powerful argument ... and an
  original one".
- **The reply** (the closing section). If the linking representations are
  sub-doxastic, like Chomskyan grammar or Carey and Spelke's core
  knowledge, they cannot be removed by evidence. The desire they sustain
  is still instrumental and as reliable as an ultimate one. Selection
  often keeps useful representations out of reach of belief revision.

## Concepts

- **ultimate / instrumental desire**: desired for its own sake, or produced
  by practical reasoning from another desire and a belief.
- **psychological egoism / hedonism / altruism (motivational pluralism)**:
  as defined above.
- **sub-doxastic state**: a belief-like state that is not inferentially
  integrated, is hard to revise by evidence and is usually not open to
  introspection (Stich 1978).

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** Stich's terms show where the
  thesis stands. These are inferences.
  - The thesis is a form of egoism: every ultimate desire is
    self-directed. But it widens the self, not the motive. A parent's
    ultimate desire "I do what will be most helpful for my child" is
    altruism to Sober and Wilson, and self-interest on a larger self to
    the thesis. The two may describe one architecture in two vocabularies.
    The debate Stich reports is about whether the desire is instrumental
    to the agent's own feelings. The thesis gives no answer to that.
  - So the thesis needs to say which it claims: that the desire for the
    child's good is instrumental to some good of the agent (classical
    egoism, open to Sober and Wilson's argument and to Batson), or that it
    is ultimate but its object counts as the agent's self (a redescription
    that needs CLAIM-tmprudek's independent criterion).
  - The evolutionary support is of another kind. The claim cites Hamilton's
    rule "as if the self's genetic interest extended to kin". By C1, kin
    selection says nothing about psychological motive. A desire for a
    kinsman's welfare can be ultimate, and so psychologically altruistic,
    while kin-helping is not evolutionarily altruistic.

## Limitations

- **A critique**, so the book's argument is seen through it, though with
  long quotations. The book's treatment of social psychology (Batson) and
  of group selection is not covered.
- **Stich does not argue that egoism is true**, only that this argument
  leaves it open (the last paragraph).
- **Whether sub-doxastically sustained desires are instrumental** is
  asserted as "the natural answer" and could be disputed.

## Open questions

- Are the links between helping and the helper's own affect revisable by
  evidence? If they are, Sober and Wilson's fourth argument stands.
