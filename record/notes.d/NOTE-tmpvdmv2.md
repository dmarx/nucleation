# NOTE-tmpvdmv2: Self-Regulation and the Problem of Human Autonomy: Does Psychology Need Choice, Self-Determination, and Will?

## Contribution

A conceptual defence, not new data. It sets SDT's autonomy beside the
philosophy of autonomy and argues that most psychological scepticism about
autonomy, will and choice attacks a different concept: autonomy as
independence from the environment, as an uncaused mental cause, or as
having many options. Defined as self-endorsement, autonomy survives each
critique, and the SDT evidence reviewed shows it matters across domains and
cultures.

## Key insight

Autonomy is a property of how an action is regulated, not of where its
impetus comes from. "Initiating stimuli typically arise in the environment or
the organism, so the impetus for most actions is not a disconnected
thought" (p. 1572). What makes the act autonomous is the person's
endorsement of it: "When people take interest in an urge or a prompt and
consent to its enactment, their behavior would be autonomous" (p. 1573).
That is why external influence, dependence, determinism, nonconscious
initiation and a single option are all compatible with autonomy, and why
controlling contexts, introjects and seductive primes are not.

## Assumptions

- **Autonomy is a phenomenal and functional property** with distinct
  neural supports. It "requires coordination among prefrontal cortical
  regions ... subcortical striatal-thalamic areas ... and inputs from the
  hippocampus and amygdala" (p. 1564). This is asserted, cited to reviews.
- **Reflective endorsement is the criterion, including counterfactually.**
  An automatized act is autonomous if, "were she to consider it reflectively,
  she would wholly endorse the action" (p. 1572).
- **Levels of explanation are real.** Brain-level description does not
  displace person-level causation; the "regnant" level is the one "most
  relevant to what is to be explained and ... most relevant for effective
  interventions" (p. 1571).

## Key results

What the paper argues, in its order.

- **Phenomenology (pp. 1559–1560).** Pfänder: acts of will are experienced
  "precisely not as an occurrence caused by a different agent but as an
  initial act of the ego-center itself" (p. 1559, quoting Pfänder p. 20).
  Ricoeur: autonomy "need not entail an absence of external influences", so
  long as the person "fully concurs with or endorses" the demand (p. 1559).
  Authentic actions are those "for which one takes responsibility; they are
  not half-hearted or disowned" (p. 1560). Autonomy requires "some relative
  unity underlying one's actions; they must be congruent and endorsed by the
  whole self" (p. 1560).
- **Hierarchical accounts (pp. 1560–1561).** "These accounts by Frankfurt
  (1971), Dworkin (1988), and others commonly maintain that people are
  autonomous only to the extent that their first order motives are (or would
  be) endorsed at a higher order of reflection" (p. 1560). The example is the
  man deciding to "have another drink". Following Dworkin, autonomy is
  endorsement "at the highest order of reflection", which "is not an
  infinitely regressive process because, practically, there are few actions
  for which more than a few levels of reflection are possible. More
  importantly, one can be autonomous in finding a 'decisive identification'
  with a motive or value" (p. 1561). The philosophical debates are "not ...
  about the existence of autonomy but rather about what the processes of
  endorsement or decisive identification entail" (p. 1561).
- **SDT's autonomy (pp. 1561–1566).** Heteronomy includes "inner impulses or
  demands" (p. 1561). Autonomy is graded along the internalization continuum
  (p. 1562). Causality orientations (autonomy, control, impersonal) are the
  trait-level counterpart (p. 1562). People "feel most related to those who
  support their autonomy" (p. 1564). Conditional regard leads children to
  "introject the regulation of expected behaviors" (Assor, Roth & Deci 2004,
  p. 1566).
- **Behaviourism (pp. 1567–1569).** Skinner's "If we do not know why a person
  acts as he does, we attribute his behavior to him" (p. 1567, quoting Skinner
  1971 p. 53). Eisenberger and Cameron's (1996) analysis, reanalysed by Deci
  et al. (1999, [LIT-tmp58ot3](../literature.d/LIT-tmp58ot3.md)), was "plagued by miscalculations, incorrect
  recording of effect sizes, misclassifications, use of the wrong control
  groups, and other errors" (p. 1568). SDT "has never disputed the power of
  reinforcement contingencies"; that power is what makes people vulnerable
  (p. 1568).
- **Bandura (p. 1569).** Bandura (1989) defined autonomy as action "entirely
  independent" of the environment, then dismissed it. Self-efficacy is
  SDT's perceived competence, "a necessary condition for motivation", but it
  "does not address why one acts", so it "is unable to distinguish alienated
  from autonomous actions".
- **Reductive neuroscience (pp. 1569–1571).** Pinker's "gadgets" replace the
  illusory "I" with a reified brain that "pushes buttons" (p. 1570). Bargh's
  view assumes will is a "non-brain-related force", like Descartes's pineal
  gland; SDT agrees such a will does not exist (p. 1570). Regnant causes
  (p. 1571): for improving a school, the person level is regnant; for a child
  who cannot process speech, the neurological level is.
- **Nonconscious determination (pp. 1571–1574).** Implicit versus explicit is
  "related to, but not isomorphic with, autonomous versus controlled"
  (p. 1572). Automatic behaviours are "pushed by controlled processes";
  automatized ones "if reflected upon, would fit with one's values or needs"
  (p. 1572). Libet's readiness potential is read as consistent with
  consciousness's role "in approving (or vetoing)" (p. 1573). Mindfulness
  moderated the power of implicit motives (Levesque & Brown 2005, p. 1573).
  Wegner's "authorship emotion" is taken as conceding that the sense of
  autonomy is functional (p. 1574).
- **Choice (pp. 1574–1576).** Schwartz's "tyranny" conflates options with
  self-determination. In Moller, Deci and Ryan (2006), "true choice was not
  depleting", whereas Baumeister et al.'s pressured "high choice" was
  (p. 1575).
- **Culture (pp. 1576–1578).** Autonomous reasons predicted well-being with
  no moderation by culture in Russia, the United States, Turkey and South
  Korea (Chirkov et al. 2003), and in Brazil and Canada (p. 1577). In
  Iyengar and Lepper (1999), an experimenter-imposed goal undermined
  intrinsic motivation in both Asian- and European-American children
  (p. 1578).
- **Conclusion (p. 1579).** "Independence is not a universal need; having
  many options is not a basic need ... nonconscious determination can
  undermine autonomous functioning, but it need not; and autonomous functions
  still require a brain."

## Concepts

- **autonomy**: "regulation by the self" (abstract); "self-governance, or rule
  by the self" (p. 1561).
- **heteronomy**: "controlled regulation, or regulation that occurs without
  self-endorsement" (abstract); regulation by "forces experienced as alien or
  pressuring, be they inner impulses or demands, or external contingencies"
  (p. 1561).
- **authenticity**: behaviour "really proceeding from its reputed author"
  (p. 1560, in a passage citing Wild 1965).
- **automatic vs automatized behaviour**: pushed by controlled processes and
  not easily brought into choice, versus behaviour that would be endorsed on
  reflection and changed when it no longer fits (p. 1572).
- **regnant level of explanation**: the level "that captures the variables
  most relevant to what is to be explained and that is most relevant for
  effective interventions" (p. 1571).
- **pathologies of autonomy**: forms of psychopathology involving
  "disturbances of autonomy", with "excessively controlling social contexts"
  playing "an etiological role" (p. 1564).

## Bearing on the record

- **How SDT relates to Frankfurt ([LIT-293](../literature.d/LIT-293.md), [NOTE-264](NOTE-264.md)).**
  - *Agreements.* Both make ownership a matter of the person's endorsement,
    not of where a motive came from. Frankfurt's externalized desires, "ours
    in a gross literal sense" but denied "any entitlement to supply us with
    motives or with reasons" ([NOTE-264](NOTE-264.md), pp. 174–175), correspond to SDT's
    heteronomy from "inner impulses" (p. 1561) and to introjects, "within the
    person, but still relatively external to the self" ([LIT-tmpc4275](../literature.d/LIT-tmpc4275.md), p. 235).
    Frankfurt's volitional necessities, "irresistible, but ... not coercive"
    ([NOTE-264](NOTE-264.md), p. 197), correspond to SDT's single option that is "truly
    endorsed" (p. 1576). Both are compatibilist: Frankfurt says determinism
    threatens "power", not "freedom" ([NOTE-264](NOTE-264.md), p. 178), and Ryan and Deci say
    autonomous functions "still require a brain" (p. 1579).
  - *Differences.* SDT makes autonomy graded and measurable (the continuum),
    where Frankfurt's free will is "volitional unanimity" and his love is
    stipulated unambivalent. SDT's criterion is integration with "the whole
    self" (p. 1560) and "congruence" with one's other values ([LIT-tmpckq0r](../literature.d/LIT-tmpckq0r.md),
    p. 73), not the order of an attitude. And SDT ranks Frankfurt-style
    identification below integration: an identified regulation can be
    "compartmentalized (poorly integrated)" ([LIT-tmpc4275](../literature.d/LIT-tmpc4275.md), p. 237).
    Frankfurt's identification can be "weary resignation" ([NOTE-264](NOTE-264.md), p. 173);
    SDT would score resigned compliance as controlled, not autonomous.
- **[THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md) (order cannot confer ownership).** The paper does not engage
  the regress; it waves it away as practically finite (p. 1561), which is not
  an answer to Velleman's dilemma. But SDT's own criterion is not order. It
  is integration with the self, graded, and the self is given an independent
  organismic characterization in [LIT-tmpc4275](../literature.d/LIT-tmpc4275.md) (p. 247). That fits
  [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)'s claim and offers the empirical alternative it points toward.
  It does not escape the dilemma: "integration with the self" either names a
  process specified without the agent (and an integrated regulation could in
  principle be disowned) or presupposes the self it was meant to explain.
- **[THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md) (manipulation).** The paper's counterfactual test ("were he to
  consider it reflectively, he would agree that the behavior was inconsistent
  with his own values", p. 1572) is a reflective-endorsement condition close
  to Christman's counterfactual non-repudiation, which [THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md) names and
  does not assess. It handles the primed purchase. It does not handle a
  manipulation that installs the values against which reflection checks.
  SDT's internalization history is the resource it would need, and the paper
  does not use it here.
- **No ML instruction.** Nothing here belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- **The philosophy is summary.** Frankfurt 1971 is cited and characterized in
  one paragraph; Watson, Velleman, Bratman and the regress and manipulation
  objections are absent. The "would be endorsed" reading of the hierarchical
  view is Dworkin's, not Frankfurt's 1971 one.
- **"Decisive identification"** is presented in a paragraph on Dworkin with no
  citation to Frankfurt.
- **The empirical review is by reference** to SDT reviews; it reports no
  effect sizes.
- **The neural claims** (p. 1564) are asserted from reviews, with no study of
  autonomy specifically.

## Open questions

- What, in SDT terms, is the "whole self" that endorses? If it is the
  integrative process ([LIT-tmpc4275](../literature.d/LIT-tmpc4275.md)), how is a regulation integrated by a
  manipulated history told apart from one integrated under autonomy support,
  other than by the history?
- Can the counterfactual endorsement test be measured, or only stipulated?
