# THEORY-tmpoqu2n: Among US undergraduates in the HERI surveys, students with higher verbal ability and stronger self-reported intellectual dispositions select into philosophy, and after adjusting for their entering SAT or freshman scores philosophy majors still score higher than other majors on GRE Verbal (SMD 0.48) and LSAT (0.35) but not on GRE Quantitative, and report more Habits of Mind and Pluralistic Orientation; the adjusted advantage is an association, not a shown effect of studying philosophy

## Source

Michael Prinzing and Michael Vazquez (2025), "Studying Philosophy Does Make
People Better Thinkers", *Journal of the American Philosophical
Association* 11(4), [LIT-155](../literature.d/LIT-155.md), read in full with its Appendix in [NOTE-167](../notes.d/NOTE-167.md).

## What was actually shown

**Selection (§5.1, Table A1).** In a logistic model of majoring in
philosophy, SAT Verbal has OR 1.57 per SD (p < .001), SAT Math OR 0.99
(p = .872), freshman Habits of Mind OR 1.34 and Pluralistic Orientation OR
1.13 (p = .008). The old fact that philosophy majors top the GRE and LSAT
is therefore uninformative by itself.

**Adjusted differences (§5.2, Table A2).** Mixed-effects models with
institution random intercepts, adjusting for SAT V and M (tests) or for
the freshman value of the scale (self-reports), philosophy against pooled
non-philosophy majors:

| outcome | difference | SMD |
|---|---|---|
| GRE Verbal | +33.25 [23.86, 42.64] | 0.48 |
| GRE Quantitative | −3.91 [−18.51, 10.7], p = .619 | — |
| LSAT | +2.12 [1.46, 2.78] | 0.35 |
| Habits of Mind | +0.21 [0.16, 0.25] | .24 |
| Pluralistic Orientation | +0.19 [0.15, 0.24] | .21 |

**Robustness.** IPTW with a broader propensity model gives GRE Verbal
+30.61, LSAT +2.30, GRE Quantitative −9.99 (p = .228), and self-report
effects of 0.29 and 0.28 SD (Table A3). Selection into taking the GRE or
LSAT shows no SAT × philosophy interaction (§5.3).

**What could have come out otherwise.** The verbal and logical advantage
could have vanished after adjustment, as pure self-selection predicts. It
shrank but survived. The quantitative advantage could have survived too;
there was none to begin with.

## What this does not say

- **That studying philosophy causes the gain.** Adjusting for a baseline
  removes confounders that act through baseline level. Students who choose
  philosophy may grow faster verbally, read more, or prepare harder for
  law school (they are 3.5 times as likely to sit the LSAT). No
  sensitivity analysis is reported. The paper's own wording ("does make",
  "the strongest evidence to date") outruns its design.
- **That the scores are reliable.** SAT, GRE and LSAT scores are all
  student-reported on the surveys. The SAT has no logic section, so the
  LSAT adjustment is incomplete by the authors' own account (§6).
- **That philosophy beats every other major.** The abstract's "outperform
  all other majors" is a ranking of point estimates. LSAT intervals overlap
  Pharmacy, Economics and Aerospace Engineering, and Habits of Mind
  intervals overlap two others; only the pooled comparison is tested. On
  Pluralistic Orientation philosophy ranks sixth of 62.
- **That the dispositions are intellectual virtues.** Habits of Mind items
  ("Ask questions in class", "Support your opinions with a logical
  argument") partly describe what seminars require, and the authors concede
  the measures miss virtue in the philosophers' sense (§6).
- **That one cohort shows both.** Test results are from 1994–2004
  graduates and self-reports from 2010–2019 graduates.
