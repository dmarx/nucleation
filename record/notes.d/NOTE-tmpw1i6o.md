# NOTE-tmpw1i6o: Oskar et al. — Procedural skills decay in emergency medicine

## Contribution

A map of the gaps in a small literature. Asking what is known about
technical or procedural skill decay among emergency-medicine attending and
resident physicians, it searched PubMed, CINAHL and EMBASE in February
2025, screened 1349 records, reviewed 172 in full and included 37. It
shows that the literature cannot say how long a procedural skill lasts
after competence, or how often refresher training is needed, and it lists
required and high-acuity procedures no included study covers.

## Key insight

In the authors' words, "little is known about the warranty on skills
after initial competence has been reached" (Introduction). The studies
that exist mostly measure retention as a by-product of evaluating a
course, over months, in simulation, among trainees.

## Assumptions

- **EM is distinctive** for decay, given "a lack of predictability and
  infrequent opportunities for practice of some procedures".
- **Restricting to EM physicians** controls for training: "Because
  initial skill learning may affect the rate of decay".
- **Simulation and checklist scores measure skill**, with their transfer
  to clinical practice left open.
- **Inclusion** admits mixed-professional studies if "n > 9 or > 50%" of
  participants are EM physicians; purely virtual assessments without a
  haptic component are excluded.

## Key results

- **Abstract.** The review reveals "a literature base that is
  heterogeneous in design, limited in range and scope, and still
  evolving".
- **Results.** "Out of the 37 articles that were determined to meet the
  inclusion criteria, eight were abstracts (22%)." Six (16%) were RCTs;
  most were observational or quasi-experimental, single-site and small.
- **Theme 1, decay seldom the outcome.** "Thirty of the included studies
  (81%) used skill retention as an outcome measure to assess the impact of
  a teaching intervention". "Few studies were explicitly designed to
  isolate and analyze skill decay as a primary outcome."
- **Theme 2, short timeframes.** "Only two of the included studies (5.5%)
  described skill retention or decay beyond a one-year timeframe."
  "These timeframes appear driven primarily by study feasibility rather
  than evidence-based reasoning regarding when decay is most likely to
  occur." "Very few studies attempted to track performance until
  observable decay emerged".
- **Theme 3, few procedures.** "Overall, 43% of all included studies
  involved ultrasound"; CPR 30% and airway 27%. Cardiac pacing, lumbar
  puncture, orthopedic reductions, procedural sedation and vaginal
  delivery are "not represented in any included studies".
- **Theme 4, simulation and checklists.** "Nearly all studies used
  simulation-based technologies"; how these scores relate to real-world
  performance "remains unclear".
- **Theme 5, attendings and community practice.** "only five articles
  (13.5%) reported a study population of exclusively attending EM
  physicians". Published surveys and curricula "do not help answer
  questions around the minimum frequency of training or number of
  procedures necessary to maintain competency over a given period of
  time."
- **Background, from cited surveys.** A 2020 survey found "most programs
  lack a formalized method to assess and maintain attending physician
  procedural competency"; of pediatric EM physicians, "only 49% felt
  clinical practice provided enough opportunity to maintain skills".

## Concepts

- **Skill decay:** "the gradual loss of acquired skills that occurs
  through infrequent practice or extended periods of skill nonuse", also
  called skill fade or deskilling (Introduction).
- **HALO procedures** (high acuity, low occurrence): for these,
  "supplemental training beyond regular clinical shift experience is
  needed", grounded in cited frequency studies, not in this review's data.
- **The skills "warranty":** a metaphor, not a defined term.
- **Kirkpatrick levels** classify outcomes: most included studies are
  level 2, seven level 3 and one level 4.

## Connections

It follows Arksey and O'Malley's scoping framework and PRISMA-ScR. It
excludes frequency-of-performance studies that infer decay without
measuring it. It does not cite or pool the general skill-retention
meta-analyses, such as the line of Arthur et al. (1998) continued by
Tatel (2023), LIT-tmplozja, which give the rates this review says EM lacks.

## Bearing on the record

- **The pasted bibliography on emergency readiness.** A cites it with the
  sentence "evidence on the decay of emergency procedural skills remains
  limited and heterogeneous, particularly for practicing physicians" set
  in quotation marks. That sentence is not in the paper: it is A's
  paraphrase. It is right in substance (the literature is limited and
  heterogeneous, and attending physicians are underrepresented); the
  authors' own words are the abstract's "heterogeneous in design, limited
  in range and scope, and still evolving". A files the work as support
  for its proposition that measuring capabilities by observed use can
  destroy the counterfactual capabilities they were kept for. The review
  says nothing about measurement by utilization, incentives or slack. A
  also leaves out that it gives no decay rates, did no quality appraisal,
  and asserts rather than shows that decay "depends on several variables:
  procedure type, initial learning, experience level of the clinician,
  and frequency of practice".
- **CLAIM-422 (corrective act, procedure, maintained capacity): supports,
  empirically and weakly.** Programmes run curricula and count
  procedures, yet most lack a formal method to assess and maintain
  attending competency: certification is not maintained capacity.
- **CLAIM-414 (matched ordinary trajectories conceal maintenance
  differences): supports by analogy.** Decay shows only under a retention
  test, not in ordinary work.
- **TERM-055 (operational availability): qualifies, at a stretch.** A
  credentialed physician whose skill has decayed is a resource present but
  not deployable in time; the review measures nothing in that form.
- **CLAIM-517:** a loose analogy only (simulation scores with unknown
  transfer to rare clinical events); no support.
- **CLAIM-463:** tangential; nothing here on the cost of relearning.
- **Unrelated:** CLAIM-079, CLAIM-347, and the viability, stability and
  transition LITs A listed (LIT-714, LIT-1893, LIT-931, LIT-932, LIT-1654,
  LIT-1981, LIT-1892, LIT-1881, LIT-1956). QUESTION-132 is glossed wrongly
  by A: it is about initially equivalent adaptive operators
  differentiating into complementary specialists.
- The record holds nothing else on clinical skill maintenance, refresher
  scheduling or HALO training; the only prior mention of skill decay is
  LIT-1059 and NOTE-887, as a cost of offloading.

## Limitations

The authors':

- no gray literature;
- EM physicians only, so other specialties' studies were missed;
- no quality or risk-of-bias assessment;
- many included studies small, single-site and unblinded;
- possible lack of transfer from simulation to practice;
- few patient outcomes.

The reader's:

- 22% of included items are conference abstracts;
- the inclusion rule counts mixed-professional studies in;
- the five themes are descriptive counts, and the stated dependence of
  decay on procedure type, initial learning and practice frequency is not
  tested.

## Open questions

- How long does an emergency procedural skill last in attending
  physicians in practice? A longitudinal study with decay as the primary
  outcome and a clinical, not simulated, measure would tell.
