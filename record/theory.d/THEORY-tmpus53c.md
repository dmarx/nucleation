# THEORY-tmpus53c: Citron argues that automated administrative decision systems merge adjudication and rulemaking while observing the procedures of neither: programmers make unreviewed rules when they translate policy into code, and opacity, missing notice and audit trails and automation bias in human reviewers leave individuals without a meaningful hearing, which she proposes to restore by technological due process

## Source

Citron (2008), Washington University Law Review 85: 1249–1313 (LIT-tmpz93ag), read in NOTE-tmp61it3.

## What was actually shown

An argument from US doctrine and from documented failures (Colorado's CBMS, CalWIN, TIERS, Florida's
ACCESS, the "No Fly" list, the Federal Parent Locator Service). Automated systems "combine individual
adjudications with rulemaking while adhering to the procedural" safeguards of neither (Abstract).
"Programmers routinely change" rules in translating them (p. 1254); in mixed systems "Automation bias
effectively turns a computer" program's suggestion into a final decision (p. 1272); a Mathews analysis of
one case "will capture all of the costs of improved process but only a tiny fraction of its benefits"
(p. 1285). Remedies: audit trails, training and reasons for hearing officers, a recalibrated Mathews,
open code, testing suites, review boards, and automating only what rules handle better than standards.

Corroborates CLAIM-tmpuiaop in its "exposes" reading: in the cases documented, removing the human step
removed the point at which a decision could be overruled ("Eligibility workers were unable to overrule
this decision", p. 1264), and keeping it under automation bias kept the step without the opportunity.

## What this does not say

Nothing measured: the failures are reports and audits of 2004–2007. Nothing on machine learning. It does
not say automation should be avoided; it says when and under what procedures. It does not support the
"characterizes" reading of CLAIM-tmpuiaop: its own remedies restore intervention without delay. It does not
support CLAIM-tmpfmmkt's fixed corrective rate: its point is that correcting code corrects many cases.
