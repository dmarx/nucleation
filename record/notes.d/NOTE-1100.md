# NOTE-1100: EMA: A process model of appraisal dynamics

## Contribution

An updated statement of EMA, with previously unpublished details, and an
argument against multi-level appraisal. The argument is that a single
appraisal level suffices, because the fast and slow dynamics of emotion
come from the processes that build the representation appraisal reads.
It is illustrated by encoding a naturalistic episode, an actor's
surprise, fear, defensive stance, relief and concern when a pigeon flew
into a lab, as a sequence of EMA states.

## Key insight

Appraisal is cheap and constant. What is slow is building the thing
appraised. Keep one uniform representation of the person–environment
relation, appraise every proposition in it continuously, and let coping
act back on it. Emotional dynamics then follow from perception,
inference and action, with no appraisal-specific timing.

## Assumptions

- Appraisal causes emotion (Frijda's law of situated meaning, p. 75),
  with mood allowed as an incidental influence.
- A symbolic, decision-theoretic representation of the agent–environment
  relation, after the Physical Symbol System Hypothesis (p. 76): beliefs
  binary with probabilities over futures, and utilities over states.
- One agent's utilities as the measure of desirability, with others'
  utilities representable from the agent's point of view (p. 80).
- SOAR's operator cycle: operators proposed in parallel and executed
  sequentially, about 50 ms each (p. 79).

## Key results

Model and illustration, not experiment:

- **Per-proposition appraisal** (p. 80). Every proposition in the causal
  interpretation, past, present or future, gets an appraisal frame,
  continuously updated, including relevance, desirability, likelihood,
  expectedness, causal attribution, controllability and changeability.
- **Emotion labels as a convenience** (p. 81, Table 2). Hope, joy, fear,
  sadness, surprise, anger and guilt are read off appraisal patterns, but
  "it is the specific configuration of appraisal variables" that drives
  coping.
- **Mood** (p. 81). Frames aggregated by label, squashed to [0,1], added
  back to frames of the same label. Mood "tends to change slowly over
  time as appraisal frames are added or removed in response to changes in
  the causal interpretation."
- **Arbitration** (pp. 81–82). The coping response follows "the most
  recently accessed appraisal frame with the highest mood-adjusted
  intensity". So "if and event is appraised as equally hope and fear
  provoking, the agent will focus on its fears if its mood state contains
  more fear than hope" (p. 82).
- **Coping as a gate** (p. 82). "Coping acts as a sequential gate-keeper
  that sanctions the action congruent with the current appraisal
  pattern." Strategies are chosen by controllability and changeability
  (p. 83).
- **The bird** (pp. 84–87). Surprise leads to attending, then fear from
  an uncontrollable threat, then avoidance. Distance raises
  controllability and turns fear to anger, then planning and hope. The
  bird caught in the other actor's hair removes the threat, and help
  follows. The model's "anger" at the bird's injury is a miss.
- **Common appraisal as coordination** (p. 89). "Appraisal theory
  suggests a general set of criteria and control strategies that can be
  uniformly applied to characterize, inform, and coordinate the behavior
  of heterogeneous cognitive functions."

## Concepts

- **causal interpretation**: the agent's working-memory representation
  of the agent–environment relation, holding states, actions, causal
  links, beliefs, intentions, probabilities and utilities.
- **appraisal frame**: the continuously updated appraisal values for one
  proposition, with an emotion label and intensity.
- **mood-adjusted intensity**: a frame's intensity plus the mood
  component of the same label, the quantity on which frames compete.
- **coping**: strategies that maintain desirable or overturn undesirable
  in-focus appraisals by acting on the world or on attention, belief,
  desire or intention.

## Connections

Follows Smith and Lazarus (1990) and Lazarus ([LIT-550](../literature.d/LIT-550.md), unread), and
is set against Scherer's sequential checks (Scherer 2001; the record
reads his model in [LIT-1451](../literature.d/LIT-1451.md)) and Smith and Kirby's two-process
model. It takes the interrupt view of emotion from
Simon (1967) and the law of situated meaning from Frijda ([LIT-704](../literature.d/LIT-704.md),
unread). It is a sibling of Lewis's dynamic-systems model ([LIT-1455](../literature.d/LIT-1455.md)),
which also refuses a fixed appraisal sequence but makes the competition
one of attractors, not of discrete frames.

## Bearing on the record

**On the negotiation reading ([CLAIM-440](../claims.d/CLAIM-440.md), part 1).** EMA has an
explicit, implemented arbitration among concurrent evaluations: frames
"each competing to determine the agent's coping response" (p. 81), and a
rule for the winner. To that extent it anticipates "appraisal as the
process by which competing perspectives negotiate". It anticipates the
Moors form of the idea ([LIT-1434](../literature.d/LIT-1434.md)), though, not the record's. The
difference is the same, and sharper.

1. **The competition is on one scalar.** A frame's intensity is "a
   function of the likelihood of the threat times the utility of the goal
   that is threatened" (p. 84), mood-adjusted. Selection is an argmax,
   with recency breaking ties. That is a common currency by construction,
   the compensatory regime of [CLAIM-435](../claims.d/CLAIM-435.md) and [CLAIM-436](../claims.d/CLAIM-436.md) with
   nothing else possible. Sen-type failure of the negotiation to produce
   an ordering cannot arise in EMA.
2. **The competitors are concerns, not perspectives.** What competes is
   evaluations of propositions, all on the one agent's utility. EMA's
   appraisal variable called "perspective" (p. 80) is not the record's
   perspective. It names whose utility function a proposition is judged
   by, and the paper uses only the agent's own. The record should not
   borrow the word from EMA without saying so. A multi-perspective EMA,
   with frames judged under different utility functions and no common
   scale between them, is exactly what the proposal would need and what
   EMA does not provide.
3. **No authority distinct from intensity.** In EMA the precedence a
   frame claims (its intensity) is the authority it is granted (selection
   for coping). [CLAIM-283](../claims.d/CLAIM-283.md)'s distinction has no room in it, except through
   recency, which grants authority on another ground.

**On the marks of potential ([TERM-076](../terms.d/TERM-076.md)).**
- *Priority* is the argmax rule.
- *Persistence* comes only through mood. A frame type that has won often
  raises the mood component of its label, and that biases later
  competitions toward the same label. That is a concrete mechanism for
  persistence, and it works as it should, by a slow aggregate biasing a
  fast competition.
- *Resumption* is not modelled, and the recency rule works against it.
  After an interruption, the interrupting frame, being most recently
  accessed, wins ties. An EMA agent returns to an interrupted concern
  only if it re-accesses it or mood favours its label. Frijda's
  resumption would need something EMA lacks: a standing ranking whose
  authority outlasts the frames it wins.

**On valence.** EMA's valence is per proposition: desirability positive
or negative, on the agent's utilities. It is Carver's "each goal loop its
own valence" ([LIT-1441](../literature.d/LIT-1441.md) through [LIT-1449](../literature.d/LIT-1449.md)) again, the view the
proposal sets against one valence for the potential ordering. EMA's mood
is the nearest thing to a system-level valence, and it is a sum by label,
not an ordering.

**What the record can take.** Two things. First, desire-related coping:
"Distance/Mental disengagement: Lower utility attributed to a desired but
threatened state" (p. 82). Here appraisal revises the ranking itself, not
only which ranking wins. A negotiation that can change its parties'
rankings is a case the proposal has not considered, and it bears on how
an ordering comes to prevail (by winning, or by others conceding). Second,
the engineering point of p. 89. Appraisal as a common characterization
across heterogeneous modules, "By casting the state of each module in
these same general terms, it becomes possible to craft general control
strategies that apply across modules", is Veit's sharing job for a common
currency ([THEORY-101](../theory.d/THEORY-101.md)), built in by design. It is a worked example of the
second half of the proposal's part 2.

**Candidly.** EMA does not threaten the proposal's novelty beyond what
Moors already does. It shows what an arbitration that presupposes the
currency looks like when implemented, and so it serves as a baseline
model against which the record's ordinal, multi-perspective negotiation
could be specified.

## Limitations

One encoded episode, with timing parameters set by hand (p. 88), so the
illustration shows sufficiency, not fit. The anger prediction at t9 is
wrong by the authors' own reading (p. 87). Mood is a placeholder for
"sub-symbolic (brain or bodily) processes ... that we do not yet know how
to model" (p. 81). The world is assumed fully observable in the present
(p. 79, note 3).

## Open questions

Whether frames judged under other agents' utilities could compete with
the agent's own without first being mapped to a common intensity. How
EMA would represent a concern that keeps returning after being displaced,
other than by mood. Whether desire-related coping converges, or can cycle
when two frames each lower the other's supporting utility.
