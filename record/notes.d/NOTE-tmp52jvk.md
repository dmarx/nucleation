# NOTE-tmp52jvk: Christie et al. — Genetic adaptation to captivity in a single generation of hatchery steelhead

## Contribution

Araki, Cooper & Blouin (2007), [LIT-tmpcmwya](../literature.d/LIT-tmpcmwya.md), showed on this population
that the loss of wild fitness under hatchery rearing is genetic and fast;
the cause was open among rearing effects, inbreeding, relaxed selection
and domestication selection. This paper tests two patterns only
domestication selection predicts, on the same pedigree, and finds both.

## Key insight

If the hatchery selects for what it rewards, then (i) fish already one
generation through it should do better in it than wild fish, and (ii) wild
broodstock that do best in it should leave offspring that do worst
outside it. Relaxed selection predicts neither, since genotypes that
differ only in the wild perform alike where that variation is not
expressed. Both hold, so "a single generation in captivity can result in a
substantial response to selection on traits that are beneficial in
captivity but severely maladaptive in the wild" (Abstract).

## Assumptions

- **Reproductive success** is the number of returning adult offspring,
  assigned by parentage at eight microsatellite loci; Powerdale Dam was a
  complete barrier, so every returning fish that spawned in the wild was
  sampled (Introduction).
- **Design.** Wild-born and first-generation hatchery adults were spawned
  in the hatchery; their offspring (F1) were reared to smolt, released, and
  spawned in the wild; F2 fish lived wholly wild (Introduction, Fig. 1).
- **Broodstock success in captivity** is measured by returning F1
  offspring, so it includes survival at sea after release.
- **The hatchery-advantage test** excludes run-year 1995, whose releases
  were treated very differently (Materials and Methods).

## Key results

- **Prior result** (Introduction). From the Science paper:
  "captive-born fish with two wild parents averaged 85% of the reproductive
  success of their wild-born counterparts". The 2008 review tabulates the
  same contrast as 0.70 (male) and 0.88 (female); not reconciled here.
- **Tradeoff** (Results; Fig. 2). "In 7 of the 8 F1 run-years" a tradeoff
  between success in the hatchery and in the wild; offspring of families
  with five or more returns "averaged 71% lower per capita reproductive
  success in the wild" than those of smaller families. In the Abstract's
  terms, F1 with five or more siblings "averaged 0.62 returning offspring in
  the wild", those with fewer "averaged 2.05". GLM slope P < 0.001.
- **Not inbreeding** (Results; Fig. 3A). Simulated at "11 lethal
  equivalents", inbreeding "cannot explain" the decline with family size.
- **Not relaxed selection** (Results; Fig. 3). It would give "a flat
  slope"; "relaxed natural selection cannot be the predominate cause".
- **Hatchery advantage** (Results; Fig. 4). F1 hatchery broodstock "had
  nearly double the lifetime reproductive success ... when spawned in
  captivity compared with wild fish" (Abstract), "In 4 of 5 run-years";
  paired t tests within run-years, significant with 1995 excluded, one- and
  two-sided (Materials and Methods). With 1995 included, P "equaled 0.1296
  and 0.07" for males and females.
- **The exception** (Results; Discussion). 1995 releases used "62% fewer"
  broodstock and "4,600 vs. a mean of 52,700" smolts, which "may have
  reduced the selection pressure and created less opportunity for
  selection"; those smolts were reared at low density and released larger,
  "which points to crowding as a possible selection pressure". One year.
- **What makes it worse** (Discussion). "strong selection pressures, large
  effective population sizes, high genetic diversity, and multiple
  generations in captivity". Steelhead are highly fecund; less fecund
  mammals and birds "may not adapt as quickly", and "other taxa may not
  respond in an equivalent fashion".
- **Conclusion** (Discussion). "less than one generation in captivity
  (i.e., fertilization through smolting) generated selection intensities
  necessary to produce a rapid, genetically based fitness reduction in the
  wild"; which traits are selected, and whether programmes can be changed
  to remove the pressure, is left as "the next big challenge".

## Concepts

- **Domestication selection** (unintentional adaptation to captivity),
  against **relaxed natural selection**, **inbreeding depression** and
  **environmental effects of rearing**, each with its predicted pattern.
- **Tradeoff among broodstock**: success in the supported environment
  against offspring's success in the unsupported one.
- **Lethal equivalents**: the measure of genetic load used to simulate
  inbreeding.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-tmpqa0qu](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpqa0qu.md)** (a loss favoured under the supply; one-way supply).
  Supports, and is the cleanest case read: a measured fitness gain in the
  supported environment matched by a fitness loss outside it, within one
  generation. The supply is one-way, and nothing in the stock's dynamics
  stops the loss.
- **[CLAIM-558](../claims.d/CLAIM-558.md)** (decay by disuse). Qualifies: relaxed selection, the disuse
  analogue, is rejected as the main cause here. The fast loss is selection
  against the capacity, which the claim does not cover.
- **[CLAIM-tmpoh07b](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpoh07b.md)** (the order of supply and capacity decides
  dependence). Bears in part: the harm tracks how intensely the support is
  run (broodstock numbers, smolts released, crowding), a dose within one
  generation. The claim's variable, the order in which supply and capacity
  are used, is not tested.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md)** (when a partial organization becomes independent of what
  established it). Support intensity is a lever on how much support
  degrades what it supports; withdrawal is not studied.

- **[THEORY-1048](../theory.d/THEORY-1048.md)** (Black Queen loss through a leaky good). A parallel, not

## Limitations

- **One population of one highly fecund species**; the authors warn that
  other taxa may differ.
- **The SI was not read**: the per-run-year tradeoffs (Table S1), the
  pairs analysis (Table S2) and the 1995 release details (Table S3) are
  taken from the main text's statements.
- **The hatchery-advantage test** is significant only with 1995
  excluded (one-sided P < 0.0122 females, P < 0. 0203 males as printed;
  two-sided still below 0.05); with 1995 included the paired tests give
  0.1296 (males) and 0.07 (females). Five run-years, n = 90 F1 broodstock.
- **The traits selected are not identified**; crowding rests on one year.
- **Captive success includes sea survival**, so success in captivity here is
  success of hatchery-reared offspring through their whole first life
  cycle, not of the hatchery phase alone.
