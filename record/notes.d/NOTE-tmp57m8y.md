# NOTE-tmp57m8y: Ozer, Waggoner and Kennedy (2024) — The Paradox of Algorithms and Blame on Public Decision-makers

## Contribution

A pre-registered test of the ethicists' and legal scholars' worry that algorithms let officials deflect blame by agreeing with them ("dislocation") and expose them to blame for disagreeing ("magnification"), with human and hybrid advice as comparisons.

## Key results

1. Abstract (p. 200): two experiments "conducted on three representative U.S. samples" show "no support for the hypothesized blame dynamics".
2. Study 1 (p. 205): agreeing with the algorithm, respondents "place 9% more blame upon the judge"; they "did blame the algorithms for the mistake under the agreement" condition, "but this did not reduce the culpability assigned to the judge."
3. p. 205: "We note that these effects are small and, therefore, should be interpreted with some caution." Differences by source of advice were not significant (p > 0.1, p. 207).
4. Study 2 (p. 210): the same direction, but it "is lower and does not reach standard levels of statistical significance (p > 0.05)".
5. Study 2 (p. 211): "about 66% of the effect of agreeing" with the algorithm is mediated by respondents saying the judge should have relied more on her own judgment; perceived substitution "increases," "rather than deflects, the blame placed on the judge" (p. 203).
6. Scope (p. 214): "the only counterfactual being evaluated is when the judge releases someone who goes on to" offend.

## Assumptions

Public blame of a named official in a vignette stands for democratic accountability; the judicial case stands for public decision-makers; blame ratings track consequences (the SI reports no effect on vote intention).

## Concepts

Dislocation and magnification of blame; algorithm aversion; trust in experts as moderator; abdication of responsibility as mediator; hybrid advice.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpnfjln**: marks the limit the claim's own text draws ("What it does not say ... That automation causes it"). The public did not let the algorithm take blame off the visible decision-maker; it blamed her slightly more. That fits blame landing where failure is visible, but tests nothing about organizations, upstream control or compensating effort.
- **The exchange** (A19 §9) reports the null accurately. It omits that following an algorithm slightly raised blame, partly as perceived abdication, which is the point A makes next without attributing it ("some people may hold decision-makers *more* accountable").

## Limitations

One scenario and one error type; small effects that fade in the third sample; mediation open to confounding (the authors say so); vignette blame, not real consequences; US only; judicial context only.
