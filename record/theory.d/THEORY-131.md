# THEORY-131: A corporation whose Corporate Internal Decision (CID) Structure, an organizational chart of stations plus recognition rules embedded in corporate policy, licenses redescribing its members' acts as done for corporate reasons is a non-eliminable intentional agent, unlike a mob; and if intentional agency suffices for moral personhood, which French asserts but here says he cannot further argue, such a corporation is a full-fledged moral person

## Source

French (1979), [LIT-727](../literature.d/LIT-727.md), read in [NOTE-568](../notes.d/NOTE-568.md).

## What was actually shown

French argues it in "The Corporation as a Moral Person" ([LIT-727](../literature.d/LIT-727.md)) in
three steps.

- **Moral persons are accountable agents.** A moral person is anything that
  can be a non-eliminable subject of a responsibility ascription of the
  second type, one that says the subject is accountable. Such an ascription
  asserts that the subject caused the event and that the act was intended.
  So a moral person must be a "Davidsonian agent". A mob is not: it has no
  identity beyond the sum of its members'.
- **The CID Structure makes corporate intentions.** Attributing intentions
  to a corporation must not be shorthand for attributing them to its board.
  The CID Structure, a responsibility chart plus recognition rules, works
  like a constitutive rule and licenses redescribing an event (three
  officers voting) as a corporate act. Procedure alone makes the act only
  procedurally corporate. When it also implements established policy, it
  is done for corporate reasons and is corporate intentional.
- **The conclusion.** "Corporate agency resides in the possibility of CID
  Structure licensed redescription of events as corporate intentional"
  (p. 215). Since French holds Davidsonian agency necessary and sufficient
  for moral personhood, corporations are moral persons. He says he "cannot
  further argue that position here" (p. 215).

What could have come out otherwise: the corporation–mob contrast. If a
corporation's acts could always be redescribed without loss as its
members' acts, the account would fail; French argues that policy-based
corporate reasons are not so reducible, using the bribed director whose
private reason differs from the corporation's.

## What this does not say

- **Not that members are excused.** The bribed director is responsible for
  his act, and the corporation's responsibility is a separate question.
- **Not that every organization is a moral person.** Only corporations
  with a functioning CID Structure and established policy. Informal or
  contested organizations are not treated.
- **Not a causal claim.** French says corporate and individual acts "have
  different causal ancestors though they are causally inseparable" (p.
  212), and promises to show this, but the paper does not.
- **Not about what morality requires of corporations.** The paper is about
  the criteria of moral personhood, not the content of morality.

## Connections

- **Pettit ([THEORY-139](THEORY-139.md)).** Pettit argues that corporations are fit to
  be held responsible because they are conversable agents that must
  correct their members' aggregated judgments. In the record's reading the
  two accounts agree that organizational procedure makes corporate
  attitudes, and disagree on what the conclusion is: Pettit's is fitness to
  be held responsible, French's moral personhood. No relation is declared.
- **List & Pettit ([THEORY-127](THEORY-127.md)).** Their result that robustly rational group
  judgments cannot be fixed proposition by proposition gives a reason why a
  corporation's judgment can differ from its members' on the same
  question. French reaches a similar separation by another route: policy,
  not aggregation, makes the reason corporate.
- **List & Menzies ([THEORY-133](THEORY-133.md)).** French offers no test of whether
  the corporate level is a cause in its own right. Realization-insensitivity
  is one: whether the corporate act would survive different people in the
  stations.
- **The owner's essay.** For §7.1, French gives a non-regressive ground for
  what a corporation wills: its established policy. For the unwilling
  corporation that ground is a difficulty, since in his account a radical
  change of policy makes a new corporation.
