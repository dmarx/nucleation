# NOTE-tmpxvj22: Short, Hill and Brown — What is ideological capture and how do we measure it

## Contribution

It names and operationalizes a form of regulatory capture that the
transactional literature misses: capture through the design of the
regulatory framework itself, by experts motivated by ideology rather than
by firms through lobbying. It makes the "public interest" an observable
counterfactual, measured by surveying which values drive public
judgements, and it shows a large expert–public cleavage on the antitrust
consumer welfare standard (CWS) that partisanship does not explain.

## Key insight

Capture need not divert a decision. It can set the criterion by which
every later decision is made. "Ideological capture, in other words, is not
simply about what language is put into any given standard; it is also
about the public goals that are left out of the standard." Once the
criterion excludes a value, no lobbying is needed: "If business interests
get what they want (merger approval) most of the time under the new
guidelines, we would not expect to find pervasive evidence of intense
lobbying or business influence." Whether a criterion is captured is then
an empirical question about whose values it omits, answerable from
measured public values rather than readings of legislative intent.

## Assumptions

- **Ideology** in Lukes's sense: "systems of ideas and values that shape
  the way people interpret social life, but which also establish and
  maintain power relations between social groups", not left–right
  position. Capture works through the third face of power, following Li
  (2023): interest groups "constrain and distort" how regulators define
  the public interest.
- **The public interest** is "the counterfactual state of regulation that
  would have obtained in the absence of expert influence" (after Carpenter
  and Moss), which is "in a regulatory process that broadly reflects public
  values". Note 9: "we do not equate the public interest with public
  preferences".
- On technocratic issues the public "routinely extrapolates from core
  political values", and these can be measured.
- **Samples.** Public: a nonprobability online sample of 2,092 US adults
  (Bovitz/Forthright, January 2024), quota-matched and post-stratified on
  education, partisanship and income. Lawyers: a convenience sample of 103
  antitrust lawyers, 48 recruited by Facebook advertising and 55 by email
  to merger practice groups (November 2023 to March 2024), 51.4%
  Democrats against 28.2% of the public sample.
- **Pre-registration** of hypotheses H1–H10; deviations listed in an
  appendix not read here (two are flagged in the main text, notes 33, 39
  and 40).

## Key results

- **Definition.** Ideological capture occurs "when (1) regulators create
  standards that (2) are based on an ideologically motivated (expert) view
  of the 'public interest' and (3) consistently and repeatedly marginalize
  important public values and goals". Disagreement alone is not capture:
  "so long as that framework does not marginalize important public values,
  it does not meet our definition of capture."
- **Strength, not existence, is normative.** Capture runs "from weak to
  strong" on two conditions: "(1) whether the experts promoting a given
  framework have any special claim to scientific legitimacy (a question of
  truth); and (2) whether the regulators elicited and considered public
  views before making a decision (a question of process)." The lead-pipe
  hypothetical: adopted against public opposition after comment, on
  evidence of harm, it "would not be normatively troubling".
- **History (secondary).** The CWS arrived through the Supreme Court's
  1979 adoption of Bork's revisionist reading (Reiter v. Sonotone) and the
  1982 and 1984 merger guidelines, which were exempt from notice and
  comment (note 12), not through legislation. Expert support was
  bipartisan, and party platforms did not mention antitrust (none from
  1992 to 2012, note 15). Kwoka's meta-analysis is cited: across 42 mergers
  and 119 products, prices fell in 38.6% of cases and rose in 61.3%, by
  about 9% on average (note 25).
- **Marginalized values (conjoint, H8, Fig. 1).** Effects on support for
  challenging a merger, in percentage points: a 5% price rise +11.1; price
  cuts of 5–20% −2.6 to −7.7, significant only at 10% and 20%; worse
  quality +26.6 (+26.7 later in the text); better quality −7.2; more
  lobbying +8.1; workforce cuts +14.8 to +26.3; more bailout risk +4.7
  (+4.8 later). "Just about any of these risks alone (except for increased
  bailout risk) more than offsets the public demand for a price reduction
  as large as 20%."
- **Trade-offs by group (H9).** Republicans and Democrats do not differ
  significantly in the weights they give any attribute. Lawyers are about
  30 points less likely to support a challenge overall, and a further 14
  points less likely when the merger cuts 20% of the workforce.
- **Expert–public cleavage (H4–H6, Figs. 2–3).** Support for replacing the
  CWS with a standard giving "equal weight to the impact on consumers,
  workers, small businesses, and local communities": 80.6% of Democrats,
  81.4% of independents, 74.8% of Republicans. Partisan gap 5.9 points;
  public–lawyer gap 42.6 points; lawyers' support 36.7%, more than 30
  points below strong Republicans (69.5%).
- **Values behind the cleavage.** Lawyers do not differ from the public on
  whether business regulation is needed to protect the public interest (an
  unregistered placebo, note 33), but are about 18 points less likely to
  say large corporations have too much power or that government is run by
  a few big interests. Each belief predicts public support for replacing
  the CWS.
- **Beliefs about effects (H1–H3, Figs. 4–6).** 21.8% of the public agree
  that mergers lower prices and 46.8% disagree; 72.3% agree that mergers
  give companies too much political influence. Lawyers are more sanguine
  on benefits and much more likely to disagree that mergers create social
  risks (by 0.28 to 0.33).

## Concepts

- **Ideological capture**: the three-part definition above.
- **Regulatory capture**, in the definition it widens: "the process by
  which regulation, in law or application, is consistently or repeatedly
  directed away from the public interest and toward the interests of the
  regulated industry, by the intent and action of the industry itself"
  (Carpenter and Moss 2014).
- **Marginalized values**: considerations that weigh heavily in measured
  public judgements but fall outside, or are sidelined by, the standard.
- **Expert–public cleavage**: divergence in policy preferences between a
  professional class and the public, larger than partisan divergence and
  traceable to differing values.
- **Faces of power** (Lukes): lobbying, agenda-setting, and shaping how
  interests are conceived. Ideological capture is the third.

## Connections

It extends the regulatory-capture literature (Carpenter and Moss 2014;
Carpenter 2014's demand for a public-interest counterfactual; Dal Bó 2006)
with Lukes's third face of power and Li (2023)'s account of experts
defining the public interest. Its history draws on Berman (2022), Teles
(2008), Khan (2018) and Short (2022a, 2022b). Its measure of merger effects
is Kwoka (2014). Note 4 distinguishes it from informational lobbying
models: ideologues "seek to change broad regulatory frameworks ... not by
leveraging an informational asymmetry per se, but by developing
ideological perspectives that redefine the 'public interest'."

  λ ≈ 0 on workers, lobbying and bailout risk. The claim's "What it does

  realized in at least one measured case. Note 4 is the claim's split
  stated by the authors: capture "not by leveraging an informational
  asymmetry per se" but by redefining the public interest. The claim's
  evaluator-in-the-loop part gets nothing from this paper, which has no
  dynamics.
- **TERM-tmpe6jtq.** The term requires "a standard specified apart from the
  captured process". This paper supplies one kind: measured public values,
  explicitly not public preferences and not legislative intent. It also
  differs from the term's adopted sense. Its capture is the institutional
  sense (diversion from the public interest), and its mechanism needs no
  loop and no durability through the organization's own processes, only a
  criterion. A note on the term could cite it as the measured form of the
  sense the term set aside.
- **CLAIM-348** (adequacy fixed by the evaluative target) is the formal
  side of the same point: the CWS is a target that its own descriptions
  cannot justify.
- **CLAIM-tmpj163u.** The two normative conditions (truth, and whether

## Limitations

- **Samples.** The public sample is nonprobability. The lawyer sample is a
  convenience sample of 103, recruited two ways, and skews Democratic, which
  the authors note makes the cleavage more striking but which also means it
  may not represent the antitrust bar or agency staff.
- **The counterfactual is not identified.** That the public would prefer
  another standard, and that the CWS reflects experts' values, is
  suggested by the cleavage. That the standard would differ absent expert
  influence is inferred, not shown.
- **The quality finding cuts against C5**, as the authors acknowledge: the
  CWS allows quality to be considered. Their reply, that agencies may not
  do so in practice, is not measured.
- **Lawyers as experts.** The "experts" who designed the framework were
  judges, economists and legal scholars of the 1970s and 1980s. The
  surveyed lawyers are today's practitioners. The link is assumed.
- **History from secondary sources.** The process claims rest on cited
  works (Berman, Teles, Short), not on evidence presented here, except the
  platform search and the Roper iPoll review in an appendix not read.
- **Internal inconsistency.** The introduction says the lawyer effect is
  "four times larger" than the partisan effect; the analysis says "seven
  times larger" (42.6 against 5.9). Two effect sizes are given twice with
  different values (quality 26.6 and 26.7; bailout risk 4.7 and 4.8).
- **Some tests deviate from registration** (notes 33, 39, 40).

## Open questions

- Does ideological capture occur where no expert–public cleavage is
  measurable, for instance where the public holds no values on the
  question? The definition needs marginalized public values, so it cannot
  see capture of criteria the public has no view on.
- How would the strength scale (truth, process) be measured? The paper
  gives the two conditions and no instrument.
- Does professional training cause the cleavage, or does selection into
  the profession? The data do not separate them.
