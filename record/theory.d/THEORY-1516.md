# THEORY-1516: Beuschlein and colleagues, for the European Society of Endocrinology and the Endocrine Society, hold that exogenous glucocorticoids at anti-inflammatory doses invariably suppress the HPA axis by negative feedback and that prolonged use leads to pituitary hyporesponsiveness and adrenal atrophy; that after withdrawal the axis recovers first centrally and then adrenally, over months to years and incompletely in a minority; that courses under 3–4 weeks can be stopped without taper, while longer ones are tapered, once the disease is controlled, quickly at high doses and slowly near the physiologic dose, with replacement kept low enough to leave a stimulus for recovery; and that no study has compared taper schemes

## Source

Beuschlein, Else, Bancos, Hahner, Hamidi, van Hulsteijn, Husebye,
Karavitaki, Prete, Vaidya, Yedinak and Dekkers (2024), [LIT-2218](../literature.d/LIT-2218.md); read
in [NOTE-1705](../notes.d/NOTE-1705.md). Located by recommendation number or section.

## What was actually shown

- **Suppression and mechanism** (Introduction, pathophysiology): stated
  from the literature. Feedback inhibition of CRH and ACTH; prolonged use
  "often leads to a reduction in the overall responsiveness of the
  anterior pituitary gland"; pro-apoptotic effects on the pituitary in
  rodent models, ending in "atrophy of the adrenal cortex".
- **Recovery** (Clinical question I): a systematic review restating
  Broersen et al. (38.7% to 14.9% at 4 weeks, 56.4% to 25.3% at 6 months)
  and adding three cohorts of 202 patients, recovery in 58.8% after a
  median 16 months, 60.6% after a median 24 months, and 10% after 3
  months; recovery "from a few months to up to 4 years in some cases", on
  low-quality evidence. The central-then-adrenal order and the minority
  left impaired are stated in the R 2.6 rationale with citations.
- **No taper for short courses** (R 2.1, ⊕○○○): from the review of five
  taper studies, in which abrupt stops after short high-dose courses
  produced no symptomatic insufficiency.
- **The taper and the stimulus** (R 2.2, Table 4, R 2.7 rationale): good
  clinical practice, not graded. Steps of 5–10 mg weekly above 40 mg down
  to 1 mg every 4 weeks at 5 mg prednisone; replacement to give "a
  sufficient stimulus for recovery (meaning avoiding any
  over-replacement)".
- **No comparison of schemes** (Clinical question II): "There were no
  studies identified comparing different tapering schemes."

## What this does not say

- That the taper schedule works better than any other, or than an abrupt
  stop from near-physiologic doses; that is the question TOASST is asking.
- That longer or heavier use makes recovery take longer by any stated
  amount. Exposure is to be weighed in designing a taper; no relation
  between exposure and time to recovery is given.
- That the persistent minority reflects a threshold or a second stable
  state. Recovery is read as a continuum, and the guideline's mechanism
  for persistence is atrophy, with its causal evidence from rodents.
- That recovery means clinical safety. The recovery cohorts are
  biochemical, and signs and symptoms were not reported.
- That Table 6's dose and duration figures are inhaled-only. They are
  Broersen et al.'s strata for asthma patients of all routes.
