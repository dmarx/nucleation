# THEORY-1480: Ostrom, Gibson, Shivakumar and Andersson hold that predictable aid weakens the recipient's own upkeep, as in the Samaritan's dilemma, so that assets built and repaired from outside decay and are rebuilt from outside, and that benefits outlast the aid only where beneficiaries state the demand, contribute their own assets, receive the benefits and share in deciding whether to continue

## Source

Ostrom, Gibson, Shivakumar & Andersson (2002), [LIT-2174](../literature.d/LIT-2174.md); read in [NOTE-1647](../notes.d/NOTE-1647.md).

## What was actually shown

- **The mechanism** (§2.4.4, pp. 30–31): an ordinal two-person game after
  Buchanan in which the Samaritan's "dominant strategy is always to extend
  help" and the recipient's "best response is to expend a low level of
  effort"; Appendix B argues the same outcome as an equilibrium of the
  repeated game and that a threat to withdraw is not credible. Appendix B is
  by Roy Gardner and Christopher J. Waller, not the four authors (p. 301). A
  construction, not a test.
- **Upkeep** (pp. 31–32): second-hand testimony that power stations "can last
  fifty to sixty years, if limited but constant efforts are made to maintain
  them" yet need major rehabilitation every ten to fifteen years in many
  recipient countries.
- **The pricing trap** (§3.1, pp. 47–48): while a donor will "rebuild and
  refurbish power facilities", "national leaders face high costs and few
  immediate benefits from changing their policies." Kafue's rehabilitation
  is needed "Due to underinvestment in maintenance" (p. 225).
- **Kafue Gorge** (pp. 224–228): one case, about SEK 300m over 1970–94, in
  which the government underfunded maintenance, donors stepped in, and it
  "remains completely dependent on outside funds".
- **Ownership** (§1.4.3, pp. 15–16; Ch. 11): the four conditions are argued
  from the institutional analysis and illustrated by the Conservation
  Farming Unit, whose farmers contribute, benefit and can leave; one cited
  audit (Cernea) found "all of the sustainable projects involved very
  specific efforts to enhance the institutional capacities of
  beneficiaries" (p. 12). What could have come out otherwise: projects with
  full beneficiary ownership failing, or donor-run ones lasting. The report
  cites one such comparison, "Projects that have involved the beneficiaries
  in all four processes involved in ownership – provision, production,
  consumption, and alienation – have been shown to be sustainable long
  after a project itself has ended (Shivakoti et al., 1997)" (p. 75), and
  does not test it itself.

## What this does not say

- That ownership suffices: the authors call it necessary, not sufficient.
- That government ownership suffices: vesting ownership with ministries
  "can exacerbate already acute power asymmetries in the recipient's
  political economy and, thus, diminish sustainability" (p. 57). The four
  conditions are the beneficiaries'.
- That aid always lowers upkeep: the mechanism needs help that is
  predictable and a donor unable to commit to withholding it.
- That results need the organization to survive: the CFU's results may
  outlast it, and the ERB may survive in form without function.
- Any threshold, rate or timing for handover, or any measured effect size.
- Anything beyond one donor, Sida, around 2000, and five cases in Zambia and
  India.
