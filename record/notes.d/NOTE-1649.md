# NOTE-1649: Evans & Popova — cash transfers and temptation goods

## Contribution

It gathers, for the first time by its account, the evidence on whether cash
transfers in low- and middle-income countries raise spending on alcohol
and tobacco, from a Google Scholar search of 4,290 hits narrowed to 42
papers, of which 19 report impact estimates and 11 report how many
beneficiaries said they spent transfers on such goods. It sets out why
theory is ambiguous and shows that the evidence is not.

## Key insight

Alcohol and tobacco are normal goods on regular income, so an income
effect alone predicts more of them. Against it stand a substitution effect
from conditions, a flypaper effect from the messaging that comes with the
money, and a bargaining effect from paying women. The net, in the
authors' reading, is no rise: "it seems that the flypaper effect and the
effect of women controlling more resources (the household bargaining
effect) likely compensate for the income effect" (p. 14).

## Assumptions

- **Self-reported spending is accurate enough.** Argued for (social
  desirability bias, p. 11), not validated.
- **Sign and significance stand in for effect size.** It is a count of
  estimates, not a meta-analysis. Estimates within a study (methods,
  rounds, outcomes) are counted separately, so the counts are not
  independent.
- **The comparison is with no transfer.** Not with earned income of the
  same size, apart from a few cited comparisons (p. 5).

## Key results

1. **All 44 estimates** (p. 8; Tables 5–6): 12 negative and significant,
   24 negative and insignificant, 6 positive and insignificant, 2 positive
   and significant; "82% of estimates are negative, and just 5% of
   estimates are significant and positive".
2. **The two positives** (p. 8): an Indonesian unconditional transfer's
   second disbursement, and Peru's Juntos under instrumental variables,
   where matching gave the opposite sign. They "are not consistent across
   estimates within the same study", and "one is less than a penny,
   whereas the other is 21 cents".
3. **Randomized trials only** (p. 8; Table 5 Panel C): "the 17 estimates
   from 8 randomized-control trials" give 1 negative and significant, 13
   negative and insignificant, 3 positive and insignificant, "and zero that
   are positive and significant".
4. **Other measures** (p. 9): under Oportunidades, Galárraga and Gertler's
   instrumental-variable estimates show men's smoking falling "from 30% of
   men smoking to 16%"; in Angelucci's randomized estimates beneficiaries
   are less likely to have a "habitual drinker present in the household".
5. **No heterogeneity** (pp. 9–10; Tables 7–8): "84% and 73% of estimates
   being negative" for conditional and unconditional programs; across
   regions "no clear differences"; receipt lasting "on average – 1.9 years,
   and ranges from 6 months to 5.5 years, with no clear relationship"
   between duration and the effects.
6. **Level estimates** (p. 10; Table 9): of beneficiaries reporting any
   spending of transfers on such goods, "The median proportion was 1.2%";
   the outlier is Lesotho at about 6%. The authors call this evidence
   "significantly less convincing than the impact estimates", since money
   is fungible.
7. **Robustness** (pp. 11–12). Social desirability bias would give "a much
   starker pattern of significant negative impacts"; and every study with
   a positive insignificant estimate found significant effects elsewhere
   in consumption (Table 10), so the nulls do not "derive from a lack of
   statistical power".
8. **Cash against food** (p. 12): in three trials, "In none of the cases is
   there a significant difference between the impact of cash and in-kind
   transfers".
9. **Qualitative reports** (pp. 12–13): focus groups name men who "drink
   all the money", which the authors set against Malawi's measured share
   of "0.1%" and a saliency bias: "A village drunkard stands out".
10. **Conclusion** (p. 14): "These results provide strong evidence that
    concerns that transfers will be used on alcohol and tobacco are
    unfounded."

**Two inconsistencies, found by the reader and confirmed in the text.**

- On p. 11 the text reports "just 24% of all impacts on expenditures to be
  negative and significant, and 9% for randomized-control trials". Table 6
  gives 17% and 0% for expenditure levels, or 27% and 6% for all
  estimates; neither pair is 24% and 9%.
- The same passage calls the randomized expenditure results "all eleven
  randomized trials". Table 5 Panel D counts 11 estimates from 5 studies,
  not 11 trials.

Neither changes the direction of the finding; both concern the
social-desirability argument, which rests on these figures.

## Concepts

- **Temptation goods**: after Banerjee and Mullainathan, goods that give
  utility "for the self that consumes them, but not for any previous self
  that anticipates" them.
- **Demerit goods**: Musgrave's normatively charged term for the same
  class.
- **Flypaper effect**: money sticks to the use it is labelled or messaged
  for.
- **Household bargaining effect**: paying women shifts spending away from
  alcohol and tobacco.
- **Social desirability bias and saliency bias**: the two biases the
  authors test or invoke, the first against the numbers, the second
  against the anecdotes.
- **Fungibility**: why level estimates are weaker than impact estimates.

## Bearing on the record

The record makes no claim about temptation spending. This work bears on
the character premise behind its dependency claims: that the poor turn
unconditioned help against their own interest. It is household evidence
only and does not transfer to the record's state- or project-level
mechanisms (tax learning, the fiscal contract, upkeep of donor-funded
assets). A state-level dependency claim may borrow the household
intuition rhetorically; this evidence does not back it.

- **[CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md).** Contradicts any reading of "slack effort" as
  recipients diverting help to consumption that undermines them: the
  diversion is not found, and it does not grow with duration (result 5).
  Bears on neither the flux nor the threat condition.
- **[CLAIM-603](../claims.d/CLAIM-603.md).** None on lost capability. Qualifies its premise that
  one-way supply is used against the recipient's interest; the parity of
  conditional and unconditional programs (result 5) suggests conditions
  are not what keeps use productive.
- **[CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md).** None: spending composition is not capacity learned
  by doing.
- **[CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md), [CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md), [QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md).** None; no threshold and no
  post-withdrawal outcome is measured.
- **[THEORY-1480](../theory.d/THEORY-1480.md) (Ostrom et al.).** Contradicts, weakly and indirectly,
  the Samaritan's-dilemma picture of recipients when carried down to poor
  households; its own domain, the upkeep of donor-funded infrastructure,
  is untested here.

Filed from it: [THEORY-1494](../theory.d/THEORY-1494.md). No instruction for machine-learning
practice.

## Limitations

- The 2014 working paper was read, not the 2017 journal article.
- A count of estimates by sign and significance, not a meta-analysis: no
  pooled effect size, interval or publication-bias test, and estimates
  within a study are not independent.
- Few randomized estimates: 17 of 44, from 8 studies; only 11 randomized
  estimates are of expenditure levels, from 5 studies.
- The two internal inconsistencies above.
- All outcomes are self-reported household spending; concealment within
  the household is argued away, not tested.
- No labour, asset or persistence outcome.
