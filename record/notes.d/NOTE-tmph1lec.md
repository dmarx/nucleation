# NOTE-tmph1lec: Perceptual consciousness overflows cognitive access

## Contribution

The paper restates the overflow argument and answers the objections it met
after 2007. Its new move is to make the sparse view pay for its posits: an
unconscious iconic memory detailed enough to support the tasks, and a
conscious "generic" representation. It argues that neither has independent
support, while a third memory store, fragile VSTM, gives positive evidence
for a cortical, high-capacity representation distinct from working memory.

## Key insight

A sparse account of what subjects see does not remove the information they
demonstrably use. It moves that information into an unconscious store. So
the dispute is not rich experience versus no extra posit; it is rich
experience versus a detailed unconscious image plus a conscious one with
no specific content. Asked which hypothesis is better supported, rather
than which is default, the rich view wins.

## Assumptions

- **Introspective judgments** that one sees more than one can report are
  data, used in the aspects both sides accept (p. 5).
- **Iconic memory isolates consciousness** from the retina: the neural locus
  of the high-capacity memory is in areas that are candidates for the
  basis of conscious perception (p. 1).
- **Working memory holds about four simple items** (Box 1), with capacity
  falling for complex items (Alvarez and Cavanagh: about 4 for colours,
  under 2 for cubes).

## Key results

- **Accessibility versus access (p. 1).** Overflow says necessarily most
  items are unaccessed, not that any is inaccessible.
- **Kouider et al.'s modified Sperling task (pp. 2–4, Fig. 3).** Low contrast
  plus a backward mask reduced recall to 1.47 of 4 per row (4.41 of 12; 3.9
  of 12 with a pseudo-letter present), at the level usually taken to mean
  the icon is gone. Rotated or flipped letters in uncued rows were taken
  for real letters at a rate the authors put at 10–15%. Block's reply is
  that the low error rate shows enough specific conscious information
  persisting even in a diminished icon. An isoluminant stimulus would block
  retinal persistence without diminishing the icon.
- **Fragile VSTM (pp. 5–7, Fig. 4).** With 32 items, a cue 10 ms after offset
  gives 30 correct for black/white and 20 for isoluminant red/grey; from 1 s
  on, the two are equal, and a flash before the cue affects only the early
  cue. With 8 items, capacity is 7 at 1 s, 6 at 2.5 s and 5 at 4 s,
  working memory 4. Sligte et al. 2009 locate the persisting representation
  in V4, not V1–V3. For coloured drawings, high-resolution representations
  number about 4 in fragile VSTM against slightly above 1 in working memory.
  Decreasing attention (three methods) and TMS to right dlPFC reduce working
  memory but barely fragile VSTM. Makovski and Jiang find a smaller but
  real advantage (30% for colours, 40% for odd shapes).
- **Unconscious working memory (p. 7).** Soto et al.: subjects who rated a
  masked grating invisible compared it with a later one at slightly above
  55% after 5 s (d′ = .297 for presence), too weak to explain 5 rectangles
  held for 4–5 s.
- **Box 2.** Brockmole et al.: subjects hold an imaged 12-dot partial grid for
  4–5 s at about 90% and superimpose it on a displayed one, as their
  introspective reports say they do.
- **Two fallacies (p. 8).** That a vote for overflow is a vote for
  inaccessible consciousness (Cohen and Dennett's "dissociative" grouping);
  and that unconscious images are the default (Phillips).

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Overflow does not entail that any item is inaccessible | strong (a logical point) | p. 1, lottery analogy |
| C2 | Sparse views require detailed unconscious iconic memory for which there is no independent evidence | moderate | pp. 4, 8 |
| C3 | Kouider et al.'s illusion results are compatible with rich phenomenology | moderate: rests on the diminished icon and the 10–15% error rate | pp. 2–4 |
| C4 | Fragile VSTM is a cortical store, distinct from working memory, with roughly double its capacity | moderate to strong: several Amsterdam experiments and a replication with a smaller effect | pp. 5–7 |
| C5 | Fragile VSTM is conscious | weak to moderate: inferred from the four-fold high-resolution advantage and the weakness of unconscious working memory; Block concedes it "could" be unconscious in principle | p. 7 |
| C6 | Conscious generic letter or rectangle representations are implausible | weak to moderate: appeals to crowding and peripheral vision reports | p. 5 |

## Concepts

- **overflow argument**: the capacity of phenomenal consciousness exceeds
  that of cognitive access.
- **generic illusion**: subjects mistake a conscious representation of
  letter-likeness for specific letter shapes.
- **fragment illusion**: subjects mistake conscious letter fragments for
  whole specific letters (Kouider et al.).
- **pure iconic memory**: rod-fuelled retinal persistence of a few hundred
  milliseconds.
- **fragile visual short-term memory**: a cortical persisting representation,
  intermediate in capacity, lasting 4–5 s.
- **visual working memory**: a smaller, longer-lasting store that probably
  requires categorisation.

## Limitations

- **Second-hand evidence**, as in all of Block's Opinion pieces; the
  primary Amsterdam and Kouider papers are not in the record.
- **That fragile VSTM is conscious is the contested step**, and the paper's
  case for it is plausibility, as Block says ("we will not get anywhere in
  consciousness research by accepting the less plausible account").
- **In-press copy.** Printed page numbers could not be checked.

## Open questions

- Neuroimaging could test whether attention moves from item to item during
  the long Amsterdam retention intervals, boosting each from generic to
  specific, which would revive the generic-illusion account (p. 8).
- Does fragile VSTM survive an isoluminant version of Kouider et al.'s
  pseudo-letter task?
