# NOTE-tmprv7j4: A Planning Agent's Self-Governance over Time

## Contribution

Before this essay Bratman had an account of self-governance at a time, as
guidance by a coherent, plan-infused standpoint. This essay gives the account
over time. Its new contributions are two arguments for the "plan-theoretic
model" of the cross-temporal links, the "shared agency model" behind the
second, and the end of one's own diachronic self-governance, which he uses to
explain willpower and steadfastness without making them brute tidiness or
rigidity.

## Key insight

What makes a person's agency over time hers, and self-governed rather than
merely managed, is the structure people have when they act together.
Bratman's intention now that I do A later is meant to work through my later
intention. My later intention is meant to follow through on the earlier one,
and the sub-plans are meant to mesh. That is how two people who share an
intention are related. Treating oneself across time as a partner, not a
bargaining opponent, explains why abandoning a resolution or shuffling
between options can be a lapse in self-governance even when each choice is
self-governed at its own time.

## Assumptions

- **Synchronic self-governance as standpoint-guidance.** "Self-governance
  involves guidance of thought and action by the agent's relevant practical
  standpoint ... and the standpoint itself needs to be sufficiently coherent
  to constitute a clear place where the agent stands." This avoids "a little
  person in the head who is pulling the strings". It is taken from earlier
  work.
- **Over-time/at-a-time coordination.** "An individual's self-governance
  over time needs to accommodate her self-governance at each relevant time."
  This is presented as a "metaphysical constraint", not a norm of
  rationality.
- **Bratman's theory of shared intention** (Shared Agency, 2014): (A)
  interlocking intentions, (B) intended mutual responsiveness and meshing
  sub-plans, (C) correct belief in persistence interdependence, (D)
  expectation of success, (E) common knowledge. These are offered as
  sufficient conditions for robust shared intention, not necessary ones.
- **Hierarchical, partial plans.** Continuity can hold at a higher level of
  plan while sub-plans change.
- **Ends are not intentions.** "Not all intentions are ends", and "it is
  common to have ends one knows are not co-realizable".

## Key results

The essay's model (§9), in four parts:

1. Planned temporally extended activity with a time-frame, which "will
   commonly include one's relevant later responses to one's then-earlier
   efforts".
2. Synchronic self-governance at relevant times or small intervals along
   the way.
3. Plan-theoretic interconnections of intention, which induce
   shared-agency-analogue interconnections.
4. Diachronicalized standpoints, which include a conditional, reflexive end
   in favour of one's own diachronic self-governance.

Its applications:

- **Sartre's young man (§2, §7).** Shuffling between options he judges
  non-comparable is self-governed at each time but "diminishes the extent to
  which he is diachronically self-governing". Resistance to shuffling is
  supported by the end even if the non-comparability remains.
- **Temptation (§6–7).** Two elements, a prior resolution against a present
  evaluation, cannot explain why the resolution speaks for the agent. A
  third element is needed: the end of diachronic self-governance. It
  re-shifts the standpoint first and the evaluation second. Sobel's
  "premium on steadfastness" and Rabinowicz's plan-influenced preferences
  are rejected as brute tidiness. Kelly's end of redeeming one's past
  actions is rejected as not general enough.
- **The toxin (§8).** Sticking with an intention to drink Kavka's toxin is
  excluded because at "plan's end" one would regret having followed
  through. In temptation one would regret having given in. The plan's
  temporal footprint extends to that later reflection.
- **An asymmetry (§6).** There is no parallel direct argument for an end of
  synchronic self-governance. The argument for one is indirect, through the
  diachronic end.

## Concepts

- **Shuffling**: switching between options judged non-comparable at
  successive times with no change in evaluation. The term is from Richard
  Kraut; Bratman's earlier name was "brute shuffling".
- **Plan-theoretic model**: the links characteristic of diachronic
  self-governance are those of planned temporally extended activity.
- **Shared agency model**: those links are intrapersonal analogues of the
  links in shared intention, so that the agent acts "together" with herself.
- **Diachronicalized standpoint**: a standpoint that includes the
  conditional, reflexive end of one's diachronic self-governance.
- **Plan's end**: the temporal footprint a plan specifies, commonly
  including later reflection on earlier follow-through.
- **Snowball effect**: an initial stage of executing an intention creates
  new reasons to continue, such as a non-refundable ticket. Here it is
  extended to side bets made for that purpose.

## Limitations

- The analogy is partial by Bratman's own account. There is no two-way
  causation across time, and the interdependence condition (C) holds only
  "in some cases".
- The end of diachronic self-governance is posited by inference to the best
  explanation. Nothing shows that ordinary agents have it, and it can be
  outweighed.
- The settling-function argument is conceded to be inconclusive.
- The reply to the bargaining models is two sentences long.
- The draft, not the published text, was read.

## Open questions

- Whether the conditional, reflexive end can be specified without
  presupposing the self-governance it is an end of, or whether it carries
  the regress [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md) records into the diachronic case.
- Whether empirical work on temptation and precommitment (the
  behavioral-integration literature, Ainslie) can tell the bargaining model
  and the acting-together model apart, for instance by whether resolve
  depends on anticipated later regret.
