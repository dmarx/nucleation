# CLAIM-tmpyjzi8: A moral commitment is exploitable as a resource: because a committed party reliably acts on it, whoever controls the circumstances that trigger it can capture what its fulfilment yields and leave its costs with that party, without deception, coercion or any unreasonable choice by the party, and an institution can come to run on what it so avoids paying

## The claim

The exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afa2a8d-2598-83ea-8d1a-d9ceb992a99c). The
owner, at U8: "elaborate on the resource theoretical perspective on moral
commitments". A15 opens by disclaiming: "I'm developing a
*resource-theoretic interpretation*, not describing a single established
academic theory". It names four resource-like properties of a moral
commitment (predictability, credibility, motivational force, legitimacy)
and concludes, §1: "**The exploitable resource is partly the person's
reliable willingness to act on moral reasons.**"

A15 §7:

> A moral commitment becomes a strategic vulnerability when another actor
> can control the circumstances that trigger it while avoiding the costs
> of its fulfillment.

**The institutional form.** A15 §3: a hospital needs 100 units of care,
pays for 85 and relies on employees "unwilling to let patients suffer" for
the rest. "**They've been transferred from a financial obligation of the
institution into a moral burden on its employees.** One could call this an
*implicit moral subsidy*." The loop: the work gets done, so leadership
concludes resources are adequate, so more effort is needed, and "Those
efforts conceal the inadequacy of the system." "**The institution can
become more irresponsible precisely because its employees are
responsible.**"

**When it is exploitation.** A15 §8 grants that "benefiting from someone's
conscientiousness" is not in itself objectionable, since "Every functioning
society relies on people doing things for reasons other than material
self-interest", and offers four dimensions: consent (accepted or imposed
obligations), burden (proportionate or excessive), control (influence over
the conditions of one's obligations or accountability without it) and exit
(refusal possible, or made to look like harming innocents). Exploitation
then "can consist in **appropriating the practical benefits of someone's
moral commitments while denying them control over the conditions under
which those commitments are exercised**."

## Where it sits

- It rests on CLAIM-tmpd6uxt, from the same exchange: circumstances someone
  else sets can create a genuine obligation. This claim makes the setting
  of such circumstances a standing position, not a single manoeuvre.
- TERM-123: exploitation relative to a declared
  standard. A15 §8's four dimensions are a candidate standard; the claim
  uses the term in that relative sense.
- CLAIM-690: who controls the terms sorts a dependence. A30
  §4 of the exchange asked whether a party's own moral commitment can be
  what makes its dependence a vulnerability, "the moral cost of withdrawal"
  as leverage. That question is filed with this claim, QUESTION-tmpgyn11.
- QUESTION-461 asks when an organization's
  dependence on subordinated contributors increases their authority over
  it. The subsidy is a case where it does not: the dependence is on their
  conscience, which keeps them from withdrawing.
- QUESTION-468 asks how the record
  identifies exploitation. This claim proposes one mechanism and one
  standard.

## What it does not say

- **That the institution intends it.** A15 §3: "This is a theoretical
  mechanism, not a claim that every understaffed hospital deliberately
  operates this way."
- **That the remedy is less conscientiousness.** A15's close: "**The
  challenge isn't to make people less conscientious. It's to prevent
  conscientiousness from becoming a mechanism through which the powerful
  transfer costs and responsibilities onto those least able to refuse
  them.**"
- **What Robillard and Strawser, Keller or Tronto hold.** A15 names them as
  its foundations; all three are registered unread.
