# LIT-452: Against Narrativity

Galen Strawson (2004), "Against Narrativity", *Ratio* (new series) 17(4):
428–452, December 2004 (online 17 November 2004) — DOI
10.1111/j.1467-9329.2004.00264.x

**On the citation.** The brief's details are all right: author, title,
journal, 17(4), pp. 428–452, 2004.

## Key takeaways

- **Two theses, both rejected.** The psychological Narrativity thesis is
  "a straightforwardly empirical, descriptive thesis": people experience
  their lives as a narrative. The ethical Narrativity thesis is that a
  "richly Narrative outlook is essential to a well-lived life, to true or
  full personhood". The dominant academic view holds both; Strawson holds
  neither.
- **Episodic and Diachronic.** A Diachronic "naturally figures oneself,
  considered as a self, as something that was there in the (further) past
  and will be there in the (further) future". An Episodic does not, while
  knowing perfectly well that the human being persists. Strawson counts
  himself Episodic and calls it "one normal, non-pathological form of
  life".
- **A from-the-inside memory need not be "mine*".** He remembers falling
  out of a boat from the inside, and is "strictly, literally correct" in
  thinking it did not happen to me*: "that which I now experience myself
  to be when I'm apprehending myself specifically as an inner mental
  presence or self".
- **What Narrativity is.** Form-finding (seeking unity or pattern in one's
  life), story-telling (fitting one's life to a narrative genre, which
  entails form-finding) and revision (non-conscious falsification). Being
  Diachronic is neither necessary nor sufficient. Story-telling is
  sufficient. Revision is common but not necessary.
- **Where the named authors fall.** Dennett is "[+D +F +S +R]",
  Schechtman "[+D +F +S ±R]" with accuracy required, Sartre "[+F +S +R]",
  Strawson himself "[−D −F −S −R]". On Schechtman's strong form, he is
  "not really a person".
- **Narration distorts.** Every studied recall alters the memory, so "the
  more you recall, retell, narrate yourself, the further you risk moving
  away from accurate self-understanding". Narrativity in ethics is "more
  of an affliction or a bad habit than a prerequisite of a good life".

the paper the narrative literature answers, and it is short. Its
distinctions are its lasting contribution. Its thesis rests on testimony,
the author's and that of writers he classifies, so as a claim about how
people are it is a report, not a finding.

Where it meets the record:

- **Dennett.** Strawson's Dennett is the "virtuoso novelists" passage from
  "Why everyone is a novelist" (TLS, 16–22 September 1988, p. 1029). [LIT-442](LIT-442.md)
  records that the Tufts Digital Library calls that piece a later version
  of the essay held here, and its sentence "The chief fictional character at the
  centre of that autobiography is one's self" is that essay's conclusion
  nearly word for word. Strawson reads Dennett as committed to revision.
  The essay as read in [NOTE-364](../notes.d/NOTE-364.md) makes the self a legitimate abstractum
  rather than a lie, so "revision" in Strawson's sense, falsification of
  facts, is not obviously what Dennett holds.
- **The minimal self.** Gallagher's review ([LIT-453](LIT-453.md)) presents
  Strawson's earlier "The Self" and "The Self and the SESMET" as the
  minimal-self view: a self that lasts as long as a period of experience.
  The Episodic is what that metaphysics feels like from the inside.
- **Who it agrees with.** Strawson's Shaftesbury, "let me lose self every
  hour, and be twenty successive selfs ... 'tis all one to me", is close
  to Parfit's view, as the SEP map ([LIT-454](LIT-454.md)) reports it, that
  identity is not what matters. Parfit is being filed in parallel by
  another reader. Strawson names him only once, among those who think of
  themselves as things whose persistence conditions may differ from the
  human being's.
