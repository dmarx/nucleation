# NOTE-104: Schwitzgebel — AI and Consciousness

## Contribution

The book is a skeptical overview of the literature on AI consciousness. Schwitzgebel predicts we will soon create AI systems that some influential mainstream theories count as conscious and others do not. We will not be in a position to know which theories are right, so we cannot tell whether we are surrounded by richly conscious systems or by systems as experientially blank as toasters. He argues that none of the standard arguments on either side takes us far.

What is AI?) through: ch. 3, ten possibly essential features; ch. 4, against introspective and conceptual arguments for them; ch. 5, materialism and functionalism; ch. 6, the Turing Test and Chinese Room; ch. 7, the Mimicry Argument; ch. 8, GWT and HOT; ch. 9, IIT, local recurrence, associative learning and iterative natural kinds; ch. 10, biological substrate; ch. 11, the Leapfrog Hypothesis, strange intelligence and the social semi-solution.
- Ch. 3 defines "possibly essential" features, those a reasonable theorist might take to hold in every possible conscious system, beginning with luminosity. Later chapters argue that doubt about their essentiality undermines confident theories of AI consciousness.
- Ch. 7: the best skeptical argument, the Mimicry Argument, is modelled on biological mimicry (viceroy/monarch). It shows only that superficial behavioural evidence does not justify attributing consciousness to "consciousness mimics", not that they are nonconscious.
- Ch. 8: a simple machine with modules and a central hub satisfies minimal Global Workspace criteria, so GWT settles the question only if we know it is the true universal theory, which we do not.
- Ch. 10 discusses autopoiesis (Thompson, Seth) as the leading biologicist criterion, rejects one critique of biological views (the neural replacement argument), and gives a "Copernican" argument for some substrate flexibility.
- Ch. 11: on the Leapfrog Hypothesis, the first conscious AIs may be person-like rather than insect-like. They may be "strange intelligences", and social reactions may shape our theories rather than the reverse.
