# NOTE-tmpqnndy: Davis and Weinstein — Bones, bombs and break points, read from the NBER working paper

## Contribution

Three accounts of where economic activity locates, increasing returns
(IR), random growth (RG) and locational fundamentals (LF), are set
against Japanese data: 8,000 years of regional density (archaeological
sites, then censuses), and the Allied bombing of 303 cities as a large,
variable and clearly temporary shock to relative city size. The bombing
identifies the persistence of shocks from observed innovations rather than
from low-power unit-root tests, and answers how fast and how completely a
city returns: fully, in 15–20 years, even after nuclear attack.

## Key insight

If a shock that destroys half a city cannot move its long-run relative
size, the pull back to that size is strong and the place's state is not
held up by what was destroyed. The authors put it as an inference to
policy, asking how much chance there is that "temporary subsidies of
economically relevant magnitudes will significantly alter the long run
spatial structure of an economy" (pp. 31–32). Reversion this fast leaves no
room for the hysteresis IR theories allow, at least for shocks of this
size and for aggregate city population.

## Assumptions

- **Model (§III.E, p. 21).** The log population share is s_it = Ω_i +
  ε_it with ε_it+1 = ρε_it + ν_it+1: a fixed city-specific level with
  AR(1) deviations. "If ρ = 1, then all shocks are permanent and city size
  follows a random walk"; ρ < 1 means shocks dissipate. A unique
  equilibrium is the maintained structure; multiple equilibria enter only
  as a reading of a coefficient different from −1, not as a fitted
  alternative (the companion paper, LIT-tmpgvz2v, adds that).
- **Instruments.** Deaths and buildings destroyed per capita affect
  1947–60 growth only through the 1940–47 shock. The authors concede the
  US may have "inadvertently targeted cities based on underlying growth
  rates" (p. 24), and control for 1925–40 growth.
- **Data.** City areas held constant; the 1947 census stands in for 1945,
  by which time, the authors argue, those who fled "could have returned".
- **Outcome.** Population share only; output, income and firms are not
  measured here.

## Key results

- **The shock (§III.B, pp. 17–18).** The bombing of 66 targeted cities
  "destroyed almost half of all structures in these cities – a total of
  2.2 million buildings. Two-thirds of productive capacity vanished."
  Hiroshima lost more than 20% of its population, Nagasaki about 8.5%.
  Tokyo's 50.8% of built-up area destroyed was just under the median.
- **Variation and exogeneity (§III.C–D, pp. 19–20).** About 80% of the
  cities "were virtually untouched"; variation came from learning in the
  air campaign, range, defences and luck. The shocks were temporary:
  "once the flames were out, the geographical characteristics of
  particular locations are affected little or not at all", radiation the
  possible exception.
- **First stage (Table 2, p. 23).** Deaths and destruction per capita
  "explain about 41 percent of the variance" in 1940–47 growth.
- **Main result, 1947–60 (Table 3 col. 1, p. 23).** −1.048 (s.e. 0.097):
  a coefficient of −1 "indicates that all of the shock was dissipated by
  1960"; "This means that the typical city completely recovered its former
  relative size within 15 years following the end of World War II." It
  "strongly rejects the hypothesis that growth in city size share is a
  random walk" (p. 24).
- **With the prewar trend (Table 3 col. 2, p. 24).** −0.759 (s.e. 0.094):
  "bombed cities had recovered over three-fourths of their lost growth by
  1960."
- **To 1965 (Table 3 col. 3, p. 25).** −1.027 (s.e. 0.163) with the trend
  control: "the effect of the temporary shocks vanishes completely in less
  than twenty years."
- **Reconstruction subsidies (p. 24).** Significant but "quite modest": a
  one-standard-deviation rise "would increase the size of a city in 1960
  by 2.2 percent"; in the four hardest-hit large cities they "accounted
  for less than one percentage point of their cumulative growth"; not
  significant by 1965. "the four biggest per capita recipients of
  assistance were small northern cities that were never targeted".
- **Hiroshima and Nagasaki (pp. 25–26, Figure 2).** Where recovery cannot
  be the same residents coming home, "there is a clear indication that
  they returned to their prewar growth trends. This process seems to have
  taken a little longer in Hiroshima than in other cities"; within twenty
  years "they recovered from the devastation to return to their former
  place in the constellation of cities".
- **On multiple equilibria (§IV, pp. 27–29).** IR theory has "break points
  and sustain points", and "there may be irreversibility of these changes
  even when the forces that brought about the initial change are
  reversed". The verdict: "Even nuclear bombs have little effect on
  relative city sizes over the course of a couple of decades. The
  theoretical possibility of spatial catastrophes due to temporary shocks
  is not a central tendency borne out in the data." But "we do not think
  it is appropriate to consider our exercises a test of the IR theories"
  (p. 27).
- **Mechanism, asserted (pp. 29–30).** LF holds that "there are permanent
  features of specific locations that make these locations an excellent
  site for economic activity". Fundamentals are not measured; LF is
  inferred from the reversion.
- **The level matters (footnote 20).** Sub-regions of Tokyo, or "the
  industrial mix as opposed to aggregate activity", might still be
  movable by subsidies.

## Concepts

- **Locational fundamentals.** Permanent, un-bombable features of a site
  (access to the sea, rivers, a plain) that fix its equilibrium share.
- **Break point and sustain point.** Parameter thresholds where a
  symmetric or a concentrated spatial equilibrium loses stability; the IR
  source of hysteresis.
- **ρ as persistence.** The share of a shock left after one period: 0 is
  full reversion, 1 a random walk.

## Connections

The natural foil is Kline and Moretti (LIT-2141, NOTE-1646, THEORY-1461),
already filed: both find no second stable equilibrium, but the TVA's
positive support decayed over decades while Japan's negative shock was
undone in fifteen years. A reconciliation, the reader's and not either
paper's: the bombing destroyed structures and people but on the authors'
account left fundamentals alone, while the TVA built fundamentals (dams,
navigation, power), so part of its persistence may be a moved Ω,
maintained by local users, rather than a deviation decaying; neither paper
measures fundamentals. The counter-cases are Juhász's temporary protection
(LIT-1489, THEORY-919), a temporary shock that persisted at the industry
level footnote 20 leaves open, and Bleakley and Lin's portage cities
(LIT-tmppaj4l), who read this paper's reversion as a sign of Japan's
large natural heterogeneity. Arthur's lock-in model (LIT-1314,
THEORY-863) is the IR path-dependence account found here not to be a
central tendency for city size; Balboni et al.'s household threshold
(THEORY-1465) is a multiple-equilibria finding at a smaller unit. The
companion (LIT-tmpgvz2v) tests multiple equilibria directly and extends
the result to manufacturing and eight industries.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-600 (when support is safe to withdraw): supports its caution,
  qualifies its scope.** Persistence after the event is needed to tell
  self-sustainment from reversion; here the dynamics are measured, and
  they show reversion. Read as standing inputs, locational fundamentals
  are the never-withdrawn supply that sets where the city returns, which
  fits the clause that "inputs the party cannot produce must stay
  supplied", as an interpretive mapping. Footnote 20 bears on the unit clause: aggregate size reverts;
  sub-regions and industry mix might not (the companion finds industry mix
  reverts too, more slowly where hit hardest).
- **CLAIM-604 (ignition threshold, collapse, hysteresis): qualifies.** No
  collapse or hysteresis for cities after half their structures and up to
  a fifth of their people were lost. If city upkeep has a threshold, it
  lies beyond the observed shocks. The model is not contradicted; it finds
  no field instance here.
- **CLAIM-602 (support that does the work crowds out capacity): one
  pointer.** Recovery came almost wholly without targeted support. No
  capacity measure.
- **CLAIM-597 (supplied upkeep never becomes optional): weak support, by
  analogy.** What holds the city's share is a supply the city does not
  make, its site, and it is never withdrawn; the paper cannot test
  withdrawal.
- **QUESTION-398 (independence from the founding intervention): bears, by
  analogy.** The 1947–60 coefficient measures independence from a
  transient event: −1 means the long-run state is independent of the
  shock, like A's D → 0. It speaks to shocks, not to founding support; the
  extension to temporary subsidies is the authors' argument, not their
  data.
- CLAIM-603, CLAIM-558 and CLAIM-601: no bearing. CLAIM-445 (a protective
  regime becomes self-maintaining) is untouched.

## Limitations

- **Not the version of record.** The working paper lacks the
  sufficient-condition test the companion attributes to the 2002 paper.
- **Population share only.** The fundamentals mechanism is inferred, not
  measured.
- **A special setting.** Japan 1947–65 is national reconstruction and
  rapid growth, with occupation, land reform and refugees returning to
  familiar homes; for most cities refugees moving home could explain
  recovery, and only Hiroshima and Nagasaki rule that out, read from a
  figure rather than a test.
- **Identification.** The IV relies on bombing not targeting growth; the
  prewar-trend control moves the 1960 coefficient from −1.05 to −0.76.
- **Thresholds beyond the data.** A coefficient of −1 cannot exclude
  multiple equilibria whose thresholds lie beyond the shocks observed; the
  authors' claim is "not a central tendency", not impossibility.
- **Bombs are not subsidies.** A subsidy that builds fundamentals, as the
  TVA did, is a different shock from one that destroys structures.

## Open questions

Whether the AER version's sufficient-condition test, and its tables,
match the working paper's; and whether a shock that changes fundamentals
rather than structures behaves differently.
