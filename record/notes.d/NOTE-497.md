# NOTE-497: Consciousness, accessibility, and the mesh between psychology and neuroscience

## Contribution

The paper names a circle in consciousness science and argues that it can
be broken empirically. To decide whether unreportable representations are
phenomenal, one needs the neural basis of phenomenology. To find that, one
must decide whether to include the machinery of cognitive access, which
seems to need the very answer sought. Block argues that inference to the
best explanation, applied to psychology and neuroscience together, settles
it, and that the evidence already says the access machinery is not part of
the basis of phenomenology. It replaces "reportability" with "cognitive
accessibility" and retires "phenomenal consciousness" for "phenomenology".

## Key insight

Capacity is a fingerprint of a system. If people demonstrably see more
items than their access system can hold, the machinery of seeing is at
least partly distinct from the machinery of access. If, further, the brain
contains a mechanism that would produce that surplus only on the
assumption that the access machinery is outside phenomenology, the fit
between the two sciences is evidence for the assumption.

## Assumptions

- **Physicalism about phenomenology** (§2): it is identical to its total
  neural basis. The paper's question is not physicalism versus dualism.
- **Core versus total neural basis** (§2), and **causal versus constitutive**
  conditions. The question is whether access is *constitutively* necessary
  for phenomenology (§4).
- **The global workspace model** of cognitive access (§11), shared with the
  paper's opponents.
- **Subjects' reports that they see all or almost all items** are taken at
  face value, defeasibly (§8).
- **Awareness is not cognitive access** (§6): the sense in which one is aware
  of one's experiences can be met by the deflationary or "same order"
  views, so it does not require the access underlying report.

## Key results

- **Reportability is the wrong notion (§4).** A locked-in aphasic could have
  experience and no capacity to report. Owen et al.'s "vegetative" patient
  who imagines tennis on request shows an intentional act without report.
- **Sperling (§9).** 3 × 4 arrays for 50 ms: subjects report about 4 letters
  but, cued after offset, almost all of any row. The result is the same at
  15 ms and 500 ms exposure.
- **Landman et al. 2003 (§9).** 8 rectangles for 500 ms. Capacity (Cowan's
  K) is about 4 when the cue comes with the test array, almost 8 when it
  comes with the first, and 6–7 when it comes in the blank up to 1.5 s
  after offset. Detecting a change in size or orientation is no harder than
  detecting a change in size alone.
- **Sligte et al. 2008 (§10).** With up to 32 items, capacity near 32 for an
  early cue with black/white (rod-driven) stimuli and about 17 for
  isoluminant red/grey, both falling to about 16 for a late cue and to
  just over 4 once the new array appears. A light flash just after offset
  removes the black/white advantage; at about 1 s it has no effect, while
  a pattern mask at 1 s drops capacity to working-memory level. So the
  later persistence is cortical, not retinal.
- **Engel 1970 (§10).** Monocular persistence of random-dot stereograms lasts
  about 80 ms; the experience of depth persists about 300 ms, so persistence
  is phenomenal and above early vision.
- **Working memory holds about four (§12).** Luck and Vogel (four items with
  up to four features each); Hauser et al.'s monkeys choose the larger of
  two buckets up to four apple slices and choose at random at 3 versus 8;
  infants at about three (Feigenson et al.).
- **Loftus's grid task (§11).** Subjects found the missing dot if and only if
  they rated the two half-grids as one integrated matrix, confirming their
  phenomenological reports without partial report.
- **The brain (§§13–14).** Delay-period firing in dorsolateral prefrontal
  cortex maintains working memory; prefrontal damage impairs delayed but
  not simultaneous performance. A V1–V5–V1 loop is the core basis of one
  kind of seen motion (Silvanto et al.). In Sergent and Dehaene's
  attentional blink visibility is all or none, a frontal competition.
  Kouider et al. 2007 found strong occipito-temporal activation without
  frontal activation for supraliminal words under distraction.
- **Predictions (§14).** Representations of unaccessed items in Sperling-type
  tasks will involve recurrent loops; if G.K.'s fusiform activations for
  unseen faces are recurrent, that is experience he cannot know about.

## Concepts

- **methodological puzzle**: we cannot find the basis of phenomenology
  without deciding whether it includes access, and the evidence seems to
  come through access.
- **metaphysical correlationism**: access is part of what phenomenology is.
- **epistemic correlationism**: access is part of our knowledge of
  phenomenology, so whether it is part of phenomenology is intractable.
- **cognitive accessibility, narrow and broad**: representations in the
  workspace (Dehaene and Naccache's I3), versus those plus representations
  that could enter it (I2). Overflow is overflow of the narrow sense.
- **overflow**: the capacity of phenomenology exceeds that of the workspace.
- **refrigerator light illusion**: mistaking potential phenomenology,
  available whenever attended, for actual phenomenology.
- **hyper-illusion**: a mismatch between an appearance and the appearance of
  that appearance (§11).
- **mesh**: agreement between psychological and neural results that is a
  reason to accept the theory producing it.

## Limitations

- **Overflow rests on reading subjects' reports** that they see all the items,
  which is what the illusion hypothesis denies. Block's case against the
  illusion is cumulative, not decisive.
- **Neural details are provisional** by the author's own caution (§13).
- **The 32 commentaries were not read.** They include Kouider et al.'s
  "partial awareness", Naccache and Dehaene, Grush on generic phenomenology,
  and Sergent and Rees, and Block's response; Block (2011,
  [LIT-640](../literature.d/LIT-640.md)) answers the later forms of their objections.
