*Working manuscript | Nucleation research program*

## Abstract

Accounts of morality as a solution to problems of cooperation typically ask how agents with partially divergent interests come to coordinate their activities. This paper develops the complementary question of how successful cooperation transforms the agents, interests, and relations that subsequently require moral evaluation. Recurrent interaction can generate expectations, reliance, specialization, and dependencies; under suitable conditions these become maintained implicit contracts and operative norms. Such arrangements enable new capacities while redistributing vulnerability, regulatory burdens, and authority over future contingencies. Exploitation may therefore arise through successful cooperation rather than merely through defection. We situate this mechanism within a process-relational account of individuation, distinguishing the characteristic organization of a perspective from its realization, the affective mobilization of demands from their warranted authority, and the endogenous maintenance of a constraint from its formation provenance. Inquiry, understood as resource-bounded and potentially reflexive specification, can discover and transform the arrangements through which questions, claims, and corrections become consequential. Memetic transmission and performative feedback help explain the reproduction and entrenchment of these arrangements across carriers. A constructive framework and proposed experiments distinguish functional appropriation, operational normativity, and moral exploitation without deriving moral legitimacy from viability or coordination. The normative proposal is that a central object of justice is the organization of interdependence, including the distribution of effective authority over its continuing transformation.

**Keywords:** cooperation; implicit contracts; norm formation; individuation; organizational affect; exploitation; inquiry; reflexive autonomy; standing; agnotology

## 1. Introduction: the problem produced by cooperation

Cooperation makes possible achievements that would be inaccessible to independently acting agents. Its familiar problems include coordinating behavior, overcoming conflicts of interest, sustaining credible commitments, and limiting defection. The evolutionary and developmental study of morality has made considerable progress by investigating these problems (Curry, 2016; Curry, Mullins, & Whitehouse, 2019; Tomasello, 2020). But successful cooperation introduces a second class of problems that is easy to overlook when the participants, their interests, and their feasible alternatives are treated as fixed.

Consider a recurring interaction in which one participant supplies a service that another initially supplies for itself. Reliable provision makes independent production comparatively costly; the recipient redirects resources, specializes, and develops projects premised upon continued delivery. The provider may likewise adapt to the recipient's activity. In time the relationship supports capacities that neither initially possessed. Its interruption, however, no longer returns either participant to the circumstances before cooperation. The cooperation has changed the conditions under which each can act, refuse, and negotiate.

This simple developmental observation motivates the paper. Cooperation is not merely a relation among individuals; it can be a process through which individuals and their possibilities become constituted. Consequently, its moral significance cannot be exhausted by the fact that it creates mutual benefit or solves a coordination problem. The distribution of benefits, the costs of maintaining them, the vulnerabilities that their production creates, and the authority to alter their terms all become objects of evaluation.

We propose that implicit contracts are an important intermediate concept. Unlike a legal agreement, an organizational contract need not be consciously negotiated, expressed in a common language, or entered by already individuated agents. A maintained relation can embody expectations, conditional commitments, and procedures of precedence that arise through adaptation. Such relations can regulate activity without being morally justified. They furnish a constructive domain in which the distinction between demands, standing, violations, and effective correction can be investigated before one attributes moral agency to the participants.

The argument proceeds through three explanatory tasks and a separate justificatory task. First, it identifies how regularity becomes a constitutive relation of reliance and how such relations stabilize as norms. Second, it investigates how those norms organize cognition, affect, authority, and the distribution of regulatory work. Third, it asks how their formation, maintenance, transmission, and revision become available to inquiry. The justificatory task is to identify which of these arrangements are permissible, required, or wrongful. We do not assume that the first three tasks settle the fourth.

## 2. Relational constitution and characteristic organization

A process-relational ontology provides the background. An organization is not exhausted by a list of its components. Its relations constrain and enable activities, and some of those relations help constitute the capacities and boundaries of the components themselves. Nevertheless, interconnection, compatibility, dynamical persistence, constitutive individuation, organizational autonomy, and inquiry are distinct achievements; their presence must be investigated rather than inferred from one another (Nucleation, CLAIM-474; TERM-088).

Biological organizational closure, understood as mutual dependence among constraints contributing to their own maintenance, offers a precise precedent (Montévil & Mossio, 2015). It does not establish that every stable cooperative arrangement is autonomous or that a whole is an agent. We distinguish the production of a relationship, the mechanisms that currently maintain it, and the authority to revise it. A relation installed by an external process may later be maintained internally; another may appear self-sustaining while relying on a persistent external controller. Similar observed trajectories do not settle maintenance provenance (Nucleation, CLAIM-522; TERM-100).

A perspective, in the organizational sense, is a realized regime of interpretive relations conditioning which differences become legible, significant, and consequential. It is not identical to a representation of that perspective, a subjective point of view, or a substantial owner. A characteristic organization may be realized across different carriers while retaining its salient relational structure. Conversely, an uninterrupted historical lineage may change its character.

Representation theory motivates a disciplined investigation of such invariants. For a finite group G acting through a finite-dimensional complex representation ρ, its character χρ(g) = Tr ρ(g) determines the representation up to isomorphism. This is a precise result under stated hypotheses, not a general theorem about perspectives. It requires a specified action and independent anchoring of the transformations being compared. Irreversible transformations may instead call for semigroup or categorical descriptions, and traces need not classify them. For most proposed organizational applications, a response profile to controlled probes is a more defensible object than a literal character (Nucleation, CLAIM-132, CLAIM-137, CLAIM-249; TERM-072).

An organizational character might include the way a perspective treats disagreement, uncertainty, threats to a commitment, or claims for resources. Distinct organizations could exhibit similar profiles despite differing internal implementation. Ordinary agreement in outputs, however, cannot identify the relations responsible for them. Perturbations, historical evidence, and mechanism interventions are needed. This sets the methodological standard for the contractual account below.

## 3. From regularity to implicit contract

### 3.1. Reliance as an acquired organizational property

A regularity is a recurring pattern of behavior. An expectation is an anticipation of it, potentially represented, dispositional, or structural. A structural expectation need not be encoded as a proposition: an organization may simply presuppose continued delivery because it has ceased maintaining an alternative. Reliance is the deployment of activity around that expectation. Dependency is the resulting contingency of some capability or outcome upon the expected condition. Commitment is the maintained organization of conduct around a requirement. These concepts separate what happens, what is anticipated, what is relied upon, and what is maintained.

To see how they can come apart, let A supply a useful service to B. B initially maintains a reserve. As delivery becomes reliable, B reduces its reserve and reallocates its resources. A simple illustrative dynamics uses observed service s, expectation e, reliance r, and independently maintained capacity k, all scaled to [0,1]:

$$e_{t+1}=e_t+\alpha(s_t-e_t),\qquad r_{t+1}=r_t+\beta(e_t-r_t),$$

$$k_{t+1}=k_t+\eta(1-r_t-k_t).$$

For positive adjustment rates no greater than one and sustained service s = 1, the limiting state has e = r = 1 and k = 0. Withdrawal can then impose an immediate functional deficit while the lost capacity recovers only gradually. The equations stipulate a mechanism of unilateral reliance, not a contract or an empirically fitted law. A biological precedent is adaptive loss of costly functions under the Black Queen hypothesis (Morris, Lenski, & Zinser, 2012). The Nucleation account of transient support emphasizes the difference between supplying a function and cultivating the recipient's capacity to supply it (CLAIM-600, CLAIM-603).

### 3.2. What makes a relation contractual?

Not every dependency is contractual. An organism depends upon sunlight, but the sun is not thereby a contractual party. Nor is predictable conduct sufficient: a recipient may rely upon a byproduct that the provider has no mechanism for maintaining as a service. The additional achievement is a maintained regulatory relation through which the provision, receipt, and nonperformance of a conditional service are differentially consequential to subsequent activity.

We use *organizational contract* for such a relation, and *implicit contract* when its operative specification is embodied in historically acquired dispositions, expectations, dependencies, or coordination arrangements rather than exhaustively articulated terms. This is a technical proposal, not a redefinition of enforceable legal contracts. Reciprocal obligations are possible but not required; the relation may be maintained by an encompassing organization rather than bilaterally. Explicit consent, enforcement sanctions, and a revision procedure are further properties, not necessary conditions of every operational contract.

An empirical contract profile should distinguish: participants and relevant dependencies; assumptions; maintained guarantees; jurisdiction and precedence; tolerated deviations; monitoring and breach conditions; repair, escalation, and revision; and the mechanisms maintaining each. This profile may be partial or distributed. Participants can enact inconsistent assumptions while ordinary conditions conceal the inconsistency. A provider may regard service as interruptible while a recipient has eliminated its fallback on the assumption of continuous delivery. Their ordinary behavior is compatible, but their local specifications need not admit a jointly realizable arrangement under perturbation.

This formulation draws on assume–guarantee reasoning in engineering, conventions, relational contracts, and psychological contracts (Lewis, 1969; Rousseau, 1989; Baker, Gibbons, & Murphy, 2002; Benveniste et al., 2018). It differs from a purely formal specification by distinguishing a stated contract from the organization that makes its terms effective. An engineering contract may be specified without any endogenous mechanism establishing it. A psychological contract may be perceived without a corresponding maintained undertaking. The present proposal asks when the relationship itself is operative and how it came to be so.

### 3.3. Operational standing and temporal conflict

Service-level agreements offer a concrete domain. A guarantee can specify response time, quality, resource access, or permitted failure rates; arbitration can specify which processes may preempt others or escalate a breach. A process has operational standing in a matter when a maintained arrangement makes it eligible to submit a claim that can influence relevant governance. Its actual influence, its eligibility, and the justification of its claim remain distinct.

A scheduling example clarifies the issue. A low-priority process L holds a lock; a high-priority process H needs it; medium-priority M preempts L. H can miss its deadline even though the scheduler honors the declared priority order whenever a process is runnable. Priority inheritance temporarily raises L's effective priority so that H's dependency can be discharged. This is a governance-induced failure rather than necessarily a capacity deficit. More generally, a race over execution and review may defeat a protected opportunity for intervention even when the reviewing process ultimately receives a hearing. Precedence, jurisdiction, suspension, and timeliness are distinct elements of governance (Nucleation, TERM-017; CLAIM-723).

Operational standing does not imply moral patienthood. It gives us a way to specify what a process can request, what it is eligible to receive, and how that eligibility affects shared activity. The moral significance of analogous arrangements among persons requires separate grounds.

## 4. Norm formation and stabilization

A prediction and a norm can concern the same pattern while prescribing different responses to its failure. A prediction may be revised when observations contradict it. An operative norm can instead recruit corrective activity to restore the anticipated condition. Neither response is inherently adequate: the norm may need revision and the observation may be misleading. What is distinctive is the regulatory role of the expectation, not an infallible tendency to enforce it.

Social norm theory already distinguishes empirical expectations from expectations about others' normative judgments (Bicchieri, 2006). Our extension concerns the possible embodiment of expectations and corrective dispositions in material, computational, and social arrangements. It does not identify every feedback controller with a social norm. We distinguish functional norms relative to organizational conditions, representational standards of adequacy, operative social or institutional norms, and independently warranted practical and moral standards.

Reliance offers a mechanism of stabilization. Repeated compliance supports investments that presuppose continuity. These investments increase the consequences of deviation; the organization may allocate resources to protecting the pattern. Protection makes subsequent compliance more reliable and may induce further reliance. The initial reasons for adopting a practice can thus become different from the reasons for continuing it. A convention chosen for convenience may become infrastructurally indispensable.

An illustrative model makes the feedback explicit. Let x be the degree of compliance, r reliance, and g maintenance investment. With logistic response σ, positive timescale parameters α and β, baseline b, and coupling λ ≥ 0, consider

$$\dot{x}=\sigma(b+\lambda g)-x,\quad \dot{r}=\alpha(x-r),\quad \dot{g}=\beta(rx-g).$$

At equilibrium r = x and g = x², giving x = σ(b + λx²). With b = −3 and λ = 8, this scalar equation has at least three roots: the difference σ(b + λx²) − x changes sign between 0 and 0.1, between 0.5 and 0.7, and between 0.9 and 1. The construction permits distinct equilibrium compliance regimes under identical parameters. It does not establish empirical hysteresis or an endogenous criterion of rightness; the maintenance rule is provided by the modeler. This is a stabilization mechanism, not a completed account of norm genesis.

A stronger model would allow the participants to discover which dependencies merit protection and how to evaluate deviations, rather than supplying the correct standard in advance. Nucleation's QUESTION-128 and proposed CASE-239 explicitly identify this burden of proof. Further distinctions are necessary: maintenance may result from internalization, mutual accommodation, strategic conformity, material lock-in, sanctions, or suppression of disagreement. The same rate of compliance can be produced by all of these. Stability, legitimacy, and endorsement are not interchangeable.

## 5. Affect, will, and the organization of frustration

### 5.1. From discrepancy to practical significance

A discrepancy can be registered without generating any response. A motivating perspective links concern-relative significance to action readiness, including the recruitment of attention, persistence, and claims to precedence. In the functional interpretation of affect developed from Frijda's account of action readiness, what a concern claims and what governance grants it are distinct. The perspective may be strongly activated while its preferred response is suspended, or a comparatively weak concern may receive decisive authority because a maintained rule protects it (Nucleation, TERM-094, TERM-104; CLAIM-283).

This distinction makes organizational affect intelligible without equating it with reward, scalar utility, or subjective feeling. Local appraisals, action readiness, practical authority, realized consequences, and system-level valence may dissociate. A prevailing maintenance-relative ordering is one possible system-level evaluative regime; competing orderings need not admit a single common cardinal scale. Affective coordination across a group is not evidence of collective phenomenal consciousness.

Will-organization concerns the actual relations through which competing claims acquire, retain, and lose practical authority. Contracts can embody these relations. For example, a standing guarantee may authorize protective activity when specified conditions obtain, without granting it unrestricted jurisdiction. A warning may recruit inquiry rather than immediate execution. Over time, durable authority can arise through repeated grants based on a track record, rather than through an initial assignment of power (Nucleation, CLAIM-277).

### 5.2. Compatibility and governance failure

Let H(O) be the set of histories realizable by organization O under a declared budget, horizon, and repertoire of interventions. A demand d has an acceptance set A_d of histories meeting it. Define its compatibility complex by

$$\mathcal{K}(O,D)=\{J\subseteq D:\ H(O)\cap\bigcap_{d\in J}A_d\ne\varnothing\}.$$

This is downward closed. Its minimal non-faces identify minimal sets of requirements that cannot be satisfied together. For a fixed reference collection D, a strict expansion of the complex represents an enlargement of jointly realizable requirements. Removing a demand changes the question rather than demonstrating relief of the original conflict (Nucleation, CLAIM-347).

The distinction from actual violation is crucial. An organization may fail to realize a jointly feasible set because of deficient scheduling, authority, legibility, or control. Conversely, perfect arbitration cannot satisfy inherently incompatible specifications. Reorganization may improve compatibility, reachability, affordability, controllability, reliability, or regeneration. These are different sites of intervention (CLAIM-357, CLAIM-556). Renegotiating an unwarranted or obsolete requirement can also be appropriate, but must be distinguished from acquiring new capacity.

Frustration, in this account, is the structural obstruction of maintained demands; realized violations, regulatory strain, and affective distress are related but distinct. Persistent conflict may occasion inquiry and self-formation, but can also produce accommodation, suppression, oscillation, or entrenchment. These outcomes are not distinguished by the absence of overt distress alone.

### 5.3. Heuristics and biases as candidates for learned governance

Repeated coordination can sediment into defaults, learned priorities, and local accommodations. Such structures may save the cost of repeated arbitration. A heuristic may therefore concern not only which action to choose but which perspective or investigative procedure should be granted authority. Familiar circumstances make the resulting arrangement efficient; novel circumstances can reveal that its assumptions no longer hold.

Confirmation bias, status quo bias, motivated skepticism, and some instances of anchoring are candidates for investigation through this mechanism. For example, a protective concern can recruit selective evidence collection; the resulting observations reinforce the protective policy, reducing access to corrective evidence. An affective relief signal can reinforce avoidance even when the absence of harm is uninformative about the threat. This is one possible mechanism, not a reduction of all cognitive biases to affect or contracting. Its adequacy depends on outperforming simpler accounts, including resource-rational decision rules, associative learning, and ordinary information selection (Nucleation, CLAIM-443, CLAIM-446).

## 6. Memetic transmission, nucleation, and reflexive control

The character of an organization can be transmitted without copying its entire internal structure. In the original cultural-evolutionary sense, a meme is a candidate unit of cultural transmission (Dawkins, 1976). We propose treating memeticity and nucleation as distinguishable roles: a transmissible expression may function as a seed around which a new organization forms. It does not have to encode the whole organization, autonomously reproduce it, or persist after formation. The recipient's existing capacities, constraints, and history help determine what the seed enables.

Expressions such as “trust, but verify,” “safety first,” or “we take care of our own” can convey characteristic relationships among concerns, inquiry, and authority. Their significance is not exhausted by the propositions they express. They can help recruit complementary practices, allocate resources, and stabilize patterns of precedence. Two carriers may embody related contractual profiles while interpreting the expression differently or preserving different wording. Conversely, identical words can organize very different relationships. This is the proposed connection between memes and character-like invariants of perspectives, not a claim that a slogan is a mathematical character or a complete contract.

Nucleation can occur when an encounter establishes new relations of reciprocal maintenance that persist beyond their initial support. A transmitted practice, for example, may enable complementary capacities whose subsequent activity keeps the practice in being. Seed persistence, lineage continuity, character preservation, and maintenance autonomy are distinct. A seed may be dispensable after formation, while the resulting organization maintains itself. Formation history does not become irrelevant merely because the resulting arrangement is autonomous.

The same mechanism creates an avenue for reflexive control. Instead of issuing decisions, an external actor can shape the conditions under which an organization arrives at its own decisions: the evidence it receives, the criteria it employs, the authority it confers, or the dependencies around which it develops (Nucleation, CLAIM-404). Reliable service may gradually become indispensable; a cultural expression may recruit a characteristic protective response; an evaluator may gain authority through feedback between its favored interpretations and the selection of future evidence. The resulting regime need not remain externally controlled. External formation provenance can coexist with endogenous maintenance.

Hyperstition provides a related but narrower lens. A representation of a future arrangement may motivate investment, coordination, and institutional development that help realize that arrangement; realized conditions may then reinforce its circulation and authority. Soros's account of reflexivity and Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy provide less metaphysically loaded precedents (Merton, 1948; Soros, 2013). Where culturally transmitted representations take part in the feedback, we can investigate hyperstitionality as a property of the circuit. Ordinary predictive learning or purely structural dependency formation need not qualify. Nor does subsequent realization establish that an initially unsupported representation was true, that the formative process was legitimate, or that its apparent confirmation is independent evidence.

Consider the expression “I am whatever you say I am.” It can communicate defiance, submission, or criticism of the social constitution of identity. A person need not endorse another's attribution for that attribution to influence their behavior, the treatment they receive, or the organization of their self-protection. Agreement and defiance can share a characteristic dependency upon an audience's judgments. A false attribution may also help produce later behavior treated as confirmation. The crucial distinctions are between attributed identity, imagined audience, appropriated identity, actual causal influence, and legitimate standing. Reflexive autonomy would involve the capacity to examine and transform these relations rather than merely reverse one's response to them (Nucleation, TERM-075; CLAIM-415, CLAIM-425, CLAIM-426).

## 7. Exploitation within successful cooperation

### 7.1. Distinguishing appropriation, dependence, and exploitation

Cooperation does not entail reciprocity, equality, or justice. Functional appropriation occurs when one process benefits from another's activity. Dependence concerns the consequences of interruption. Asymmetric contractual organization concerns unequal allocation of burdens, benefits, alternatives, or control over terms. Moral exploitation requires more: a beneficiary obtains advantage through terms that violate a justified standard bearing upon a party with relevant standing. This standard-indexed conception makes exploitation a substantive, potentially false judgment rather than a synonym for inequality or disapproval (Nucleation, TERM-123; QUESTION-468).

Two causal directions deserve special attention. First, a provider can benefit from control over a service on which a recipient has come to depend. Second, a recipient can benefit from a provider's reliable commitment to supplying a service, shifting the costs of provision onto that commitment. These mechanisms can coexist. The former is related to hold-up following relationship-specific investment; the latter includes reliance upon conscientiousness, unpaid corrective work, or a partner's repeated accommodation (Klein, Crawford, & Alchian, 1978; Nucleation, CLAIM-670, CLAIM-713).

An additional distinction concerns whether the exploited dependence predates the arrangement or is produced by it. A party may offer access to a needed good while attaching encumbrances that diminish future exit. Or provision may itself displace an independently maintained capacity, producing a dependence that did not previously exist. These are distinguishable developmental mechanisms (CLAIM-669, CLAIM-670). Neither requires that the relationship was disadvantageous from its beginning, and neither requires a unified intention to exploit. A finding of exploitation does require evidence of the relevant advantage and unjustified terms.

### 7.2. Cooperation changes the bargaining problem

A simple transferable-utility bargaining construction isolates one mechanism. Let V be joint value and o_A, o_B the participants' outside options. With V ≥ o_A + o_B, an equal-weight Nash bargaining solution gives

$$u_A=o_A+\tfrac12(V-o_A-o_B),\qquad u_B=o_B+\tfrac12(V-o_A-o_B).$$

If V and o_A are fixed while B's outside option falls by Δ, A's payoff rises by Δ/2 and B's falls by Δ/2, despite the unchanged bargaining rule. If V rises enough through specialization, both may still enjoy gains over their original situation. Continued participation, a positive surplus, and apparently symmetric bargaining can therefore coexist with altered vulnerability. The construction assumes that outside options are credible disagreement payoffs and ignores dynamic strategic complications. It establishes a possibility of changed bargaining position, not a moral verdict or a claim that the Nash solution is generally unfair.

The distribution of dependence is not the distribution of authority. A worker may be indispensable to a firm but lack effective power to withhold labor, renegotiate conditions, or invoke review. A smaller contribution may confer substantial leverage if its provider controls a bottleneck. Residual control over unforeseen contingencies, substitutability, credible exit, and the cost of exercising standing are separate variables (Nucleation, CLAIM-690, CLAIM-728). Consequently, dependence can be organized as mutual enablement, competitive vulnerability, or domination.

### 7.3. The hidden regulatory subsidy

Cooperation produces savings not only in production but in regulation. Trust can reduce verification; coordination can reduce the cost of repeated negotiation; reliable partners can reduce the need for buffers and contingency preparations. These savings may be called regulatory surplus, provided the baseline and maintenance expenditures are declared.

Suppose an institution requires 100 units of care but allocates resources sufficient for only 85. Its staff supply the missing 15 because they are committed to protecting patients. Reported output is 100, so a monitoring procedure that measures completion but not uncompensated effort may conclude that provision is adequate. If that conclusion maintains or reduces the allocation, conscientious correction becomes the mechanism sustaining the inadequate regime. The immediate beneficiaries of staff effort may include patients, whereas a beneficiary of unjustified cost transfer may be the institution or those who control its budget. These must not be conflated.

In a computational system, the same pattern can be represented by a backup or exception-handling process chronically absorbing the work of another process. That is a resource subsidy or functional pathology relative to declared requirements. No moral standing of the subprocess follows. In a human institution, the normative evaluation turns on who is owed adequate conditions, who controls provision, what burdens have been unjustifiably transferred, and which opportunities for contestation exist. This is a constructive link between functional organization and moral analysis without collapsing them.

The general mechanism is significant: a participant's competence at repairing a discrepancy can make the discrepancy less legible to the system whose arrangements produce it. Successful correction can conceal the need for structural correction. An occasional accommodation can become an expected contribution, then a relied-upon condition, then an asserted obligation. But one cannot ordinarily establish a right to another's activity simply by becoming dependent upon its continuation.

### 7.4. Breach, perturbation, and contractual discovery

A tacit relation may become most visible when it is interrupted. A previously discretionary contribution ceases; a service fails; a participant asks to renegotiate. Responses disclose which expectations the arrangement actually maintains, what penalties attach to deviations, who bears the restoration cost, and who possesses revision authority. Such observations can distinguish mutually accommodated coordination from a relation maintained by asymmetric constraint. They cannot alone establish exploitation. A responsible research program uses safe, consented perturbations, simulations, and natural or historical comparisons, rather than manufacturing vulnerability in human participants.

## 8. Inquiry and the governance of the epistemic ecology

### 8.1. Questions as specifications, contracts as stored inquiry

An inquiry is a process pursuing a question through a language Σ, answer space A, admissible interventions I, observations O, evaluation criteria K, and resources B. A question specifies differences that count as answers; an inquiry adds operations for making those differences accessible. It becomes reflexive when one of its own components becomes its object (Nucleation, TERM-095; CLAIM-470). A stabilized specification can store past inquiry, reducing subsequent costs, while also making other questions harder to formulate (CLAIM-475).

A contract can be a materialized answer to an earlier coordination problem. Repeated conflict may motivate experimentation, accommodation, and eventual establishment of a protocol. Subsequent interactions can reuse the result without repeating the original work. The contract's formation need not involve inquiry in the strict sense: adaptation can establish a relation without a determinate question. Where there is inquiry, however, the specification may first be acquired through realization rather than explicitly designed before realization.

Inquiry is itself distributed. A monitoring process can register a discrepancy; a formulation process can render it investigable; another can acquire evidence; another can evaluate it; still others control resources and implementation. A joint inquiry can fail despite adequate capabilities in each constituent. Its feasibility depends on their coupling, including scheduling, translation, and authorization.

Let C be the operative contractual ecology and let K denote a declared acceptance standard. Define the joint question–answer relation

$$\mathcal{F}_C(B,H;K)=\{(q,a):\exists\text{ admissible process under }C\text{ that formulates }q,$$

$$\text{ produces and evaluates }a\text{ to standard }K\text{ within }B,H\}.$$

This notation extends the proposed joint inquiry frontier in TERM-160 and CLAIM-1190. Its question projection is the investigable set, not the entire askable set; its conditional answer counterpart supplies the question without cost. An attained answer meeting K is not necessarily true if K is inadequate. The coupling matters: two organizations with identical separate capacities can differ in the joint pairs they can realize (CLAIM-1115).

### 8.2. Standing and epistemic deadlock

Authorization may itself be a costly second-order inquiry. A proponent needs evidence to justify resources, while evidence can only be acquired using those resources. If no alternate route exists, the resulting dependency resembles deadlock. Both the investigator and authorizer may comply with their local specifications while preventing the joint epistemic achievement.

A limited standing grant can change the unit of authorization from an individual question to a domain of inquiry. Evidence can then precede full justification of a specific proposal. In a sequential example, formulation costs three hours, investigation and evaluation five, and per-question authorization four. A ten-hour opportunity expires under the individual authorization regime; a previously established domain grant permits the eight-hour inquiry. This does not eliminate the cost of establishing and maintaining grants, and it does not authorize unrestricted downstream action. It illustrates how a relational change expands a joint frontier without improving any constituent's intrinsic investigative skill (CLAIM-1016, CLAIM-1140; proposed CASE-388).

The standing of alternative perspectives is similarly important. A perspective may make locally intelligible questions cheap to formulate while the dominant evaluator lacks the concepts needed to assess them. Preserving that perspective means maintaining practices, expertise, channels, and some capacity for investigation, not just an archive of its statements. The value of pluralism can lie in preserving alternative routes to questions not yet formulated. It must nevertheless remain compatible with independent assessment and limits on consequential action (CLAIM-1119, CLAIM-1177).

### 8.3. Agnotology and adaptive inquiry readiness

Ignorance may arise through the contract governing inquiry, rather than a lack of information alone. A monitoring system can collapse normatively different histories into one category. A discrepancy may be recognized but excluded from the jurisdiction of an investigative process. A potentially important question may require authorization that cannot be secured before its deadline. A result may be known but lack practical authority. These are different obstructions.

Nucleation's CLAIM-1064 supplies a particularly sharp formal case: under its stipulated scoring and remediation assumptions, discovering hidden violations can only increase recognized penalties and consume resources, while discovery receives no reward. An institution acting to maximize that score undertakes no such inquiry. This is not a general theorem that performance measurement discourages investigation. It identifies a specific incentive mechanism. It also explains why merely exhorting an institution to be more curious may be insufficient: the governing contract must change.

Routine questions may reward a system for discarding capacities only relevant to unfamiliar demands. If questioners and responders then co-adapt to the routine family, their performance may appear to confirm its adequacy. The full co-adaptation is a proposed mechanism, not demonstrated by the fixed-family result (CLAIM-1054). Independent stress tests and protected exploration can help distinguish current efficiency from the capacity to discover and respond to new conditions.

Adaptive inquiry readiness concerns meeting current epistemic obligations while maintaining affordable, reliable, and admissible pathways for investigating a declared family of future demands within their horizons (TERM-163). It is a higher-order service requirement. A system may meet every routine objective while losing the capacity to recognize the inadequacy of those objectives. Its own activity can therefore undermine the conditions of its continued answerability.

## 9. The moral evaluation of interdependence

### 9.1. Reasons, demands, claims, and duties

The descriptive account identifies how a consideration acquires significance and efficacy. It does not establish what ought to matter. A demand specifies a requirement; a commitment maintains conduct around a condition; a claim-right establishes an entitlement corresponding to another's duty. An asserted claim may be unwarranted, a commitment may be unjust, and an obligation may persist without recognition or motivation. A reason that explains an action or moves an agent is not thereby a reason that justifies it. Principles can constrain actions, evaluations, and the distribution of authority itself (Nucleation, CLAIM-738).

A directed duty can be written Owes_N(A,B,φ), where A is a duty-bearer, B a claim-holder, φ the required conduct, and N the relevant justified standard. Standing identifies a normative position: whose interests deserve consideration, who may demand justification, and who may authorize, suspend, or contest action. These are distinct entitlements, not a generic veto. The holder of a claim, a representative receiving reasons, an adjudicator, and the party responsible for remedy may differ. In particular, inability to speak, reciprocate, or cooperate does not defeat moral standing. The extension of standing to nonhuman or artificial systems remains a separate question.

Harm, violation, wrongdoing, and culpability likewise require distinct judgments. Harm is a setback to relevant interests or goods under a declared comparison; a violation is a failure to meet an applicable requirement; wrongdoing concerns unjustified conduct or arrangements. Whether an agent is blameworthy is not exhausted by whether its conduct is wrongful. An institution may have a responsibility to correct a harm even when no individual is culpable for creating it. A party may be wronged by foreclosure of a legitimate opportunity to intervene even if their objection would not have changed the eventual outcome (CLAIM-723, CLAIM-742).

### 9.2. What can cooperation generate?

Cooperation can create new normative circumstances in at least three ways. First, a valid undertaking can establish a claim that did not previously exist. Second, the history of interaction can produce reasonable reliance or vulnerability that becomes relevant under an independently warranted principle. Third, participation in certain shared practices might generate associative obligations not reducible to specific promises or exposure to harm. These are different candidate grounds, and each requires justification.

The second route is particularly amenable to organizational analysis. Goodin's (1985) vulnerability-based account provides a possible normative premise. Nucleation's CLAIM-671 makes the conditional structure explicit: if increased vulnerability to a responsible agent's discretionary choices grounds special obligations under a justified principle, an organizational transformation that increases such vulnerability can strengthen that agent's responsibilities. Integration by itself is not the ground. Integration may provide alternative suppliers, collective protection, credible guarantees, or shared governance, thereby reducing rather than increasing exposure to discretionary power.

Nor does the fact that a whole is constituted by a part establish that the whole can bear duties. CLAIM-959 and CLAIM-960 preserve rival readings. A normatively thick cooperative practice may generate special obligations of membership under a justified associative principle; bare relational constitution may simply locate obligations independently grounded in interests, rights, vulnerability, or exposure to power. A responsible agent or institutional role may owe a duty where the surrounding arrangement is not itself an agent. The moral boundary cannot be read directly from the organizational boundary.

### 9.3. Cooperation as a source, not a criterion, of morality

Curry's (2016) morality-as-cooperation account draws attention to recurring cooperative problems and their culturally and biologically evolved solutions. The cross-cultural findings of Curry et al. (2019) concern moral evaluation of cooperative behaviors, not a demonstration that every beneficial cooperative practice is justified. Tomasello (2020) explains aspects of the sense of obligation through joint and collective agency. These accounts illuminate why normative expectations acquire force. Killen (2018) emphasizes a complementary fact: moral judgment can challenge a group's goals, conventions, and authority, and conflict as well as cooperation can occasion moral development.

Our proposal preserves these insights while distinguishing the organization that generates felt authority from the grounds of legitimate authority. A shared project may generate mutual commitments, but the collective's power to enforce them is not automatically warranted. An institution may be exceptionally effective at coordinating wrongful conduct. Refusing cooperation can sometimes be justified, as can refusing to maintain an unjust norm. Conversely, caregiving may be morally valuable or required even where equivalent reciprocity is impossible. Cooperation is neither necessary for moral standing nor sufficient for moral legitimacy.

The theory therefore has two directions: moral dispositions and institutions can make cooperation possible; cooperation can produce the dependencies, powers, and commitments that require subsequent moral evaluation. Neither direction supplies a complete moral theory. Scanlon's contractualism and Darwall's second-personal approach provide candidate accounts of justification and standing, respectively (Scanlon, 1998; Darwall, 2006). The organizational inquiry asks how such claims become effective, which alternatives are actually available, and how the conditions of reasonable objection may themselves be governed. It remains open whether this supplies an independent normative argument or a constructive supplement to existing theories (Nucleation, QUESTION-483, QUESTION-484).

## 10. Normative governability and justice

### 10.1. The conditions of effective answerability

A justified reason may fail to govern an organization at several distinguishable sites. The relevant difference may be illegible; it may be misclassified; it may be recognized without authority; a fitting response may be infeasible; or the means of correction may not survive turnover. These are failures of normative efficacy, not by themselves failures of normative justification (CLAIM-523).

Answerability is stronger than transparency. Explaining a decision, permitting its challenge, and suspending its execution until a challenge can take effect are distinct powers. An answer may be perfectly clear while leaving the recipient unable to contest it. A formal right of appeal may be rendered ineffective by an irreversible action that precedes review. Conversely, unrestricted suspension without grounds need not be legitimate. The appropriate entitlements depend upon the relevant claims and circumstances (CLAIM-740).

An organization may reproduce conditions that defeat these powers. Where it owes meaningful answerability and wrongfully maintains arrangements that obstruct it, the failure can be reflexively counter-normative (CLAIM-624). This is not merely a continuation of the original harm: defeating standing can be a distinct wrong, including where a successful objection would not have changed the final outcome (CLAIM-1155). The verdict requires a justified duty, responsibility for the maintenance of the obstruction, feasible alternatives, and consideration of competing obligations. A difficult or unsuccessful challenge alone is insufficient.

Normative manipulation further separates valid reasons from legitimate governance. An actor can wrongfully create circumstances that impose a genuine obligation on another. The latter's response can be reasonable and morally appropriate while the former's creation of the circumstances is wrongful (CLAIM-703). Reliable responsiveness to reasons is therefore both an achievement and a possible site of vulnerability. The remedy need not be reduced conscientiousness; it may instead require reorganizing authority over the circumstances that trigger the obligation.

### 10.2. Normative feasibility and the development of new possibilities

Let D be a declared collection of operative requirements relevant to a cooperative project and N a collection of justified constraints. Define

$$H_D(O)=H(O)\cap\bigcap_{d\in D}A_d,\qquad H_N(O)=H(O)\cap\bigcap_{n\in N}A_n.$$

Their intersection consists of realizable histories satisfying both collections. This is an idealized conjunctive construction; defeasible obligations and competing reasons require explicit rules of applicability and priority. The mathematics cannot select the justified standards. It can help identify whether their simultaneous realization is possible given those standards.

A conflict between a project and justified claims need not be permanent. Changing available resources, governance, coordination, or developmental capacities may enlarge the intersection without weakening the claims. A moral inquiry can therefore investigate not only which presently available action is permissible but how previously unavailable morally adequate actions might become realizable. If the operative project is itself unjustified, abandoning or revising it may be the right response. Greater feasibility is not intrinsically desirable irrespective of what is made feasible.

This is the constructive aspect of justice. Distributive arrangements alter developmental possibilities; developmental differences can change epistemic standing and bargaining power; those changes can then stabilize distributive arrangements. Justice concerns the distribution of resources and benefits, but also the organization of inquiry, authority, correction, and future capabilities. A reserve capacity may be valuable even when unused: an independent evaluator, alternate provider, or credible exit route can constrain discretionary authority. Such constitutional redundancy has costs and is not a universal solution, but current utilization alone is an inadequate measure of its value.

### 10.3. Revisable interdependence

The proposed normative ideal is not independence from all others, maximally symmetric reciprocity, or an absence of frustration. It is normatively answerable interdependence: the maintenance of valuable cooperative capacities under arrangements that remain appropriately responsive to justified claims concerning their formation, distribution, operation, and revision.

Reflexive autonomy and normative governability are complementary but distinct achievements. Reflexive autonomy concerns an organization's effective authority to examine and transform the conditions of its own governability; normative governability concerns its effective answerability to warranted reasons, including reasons for revising those conditions. A powerful organization may be autonomous in redesigning itself while remaining unanswerable to others. An externally supervised institution may comply with justified requirements without developing endogenous corrective capacities. Neither property entails non-domination or justice.

At the level of relations between individuals, the important issue is not whether each can operate independently in every respect. It is whether the terms of dependence are justified, whether appropriate fallback or protective arrangements exist, whether the costs of maintaining cooperation are legible, and whether affected parties can meaningfully exercise the standing they possess. Some relations, such as care for a profoundly dependent person, may require durable asymmetric commitments. Their legitimacy cannot be decided by equal exchange or a universal right of immediate exit alone.

## 11. A constructive research program

The synthesis proposes mechanisms, not a validated unified theory. Its claims should be assessed through comparative constructions and interventions capable of distinguishing contractual organization from ordinary causal coupling, learning, or strategic interaction. The following studies are proposed, not reported experiments.

### Study A: Formation versus stabilization

Construct a multi-process environment in which reliable provision permits costly reserve capacities to decline. Compare one-sided reliance, reciprocal accommodation, externally enforced coordination, and endogenous maintenance. Do not preassign a particular service as owed. Permit alternative arrangements to form under the same constraints. Record histories of reliance, reserve loss, actual allocation of corrective resources, and responses to controlled deviations. A norm-formation claim requires evidence of a learned or developed criterion of failure and a maintained response relation, not simply convergence of service levels under a supplied scoring rule. Interrupt formation pathways separately from present maintenance pathways. The proposed account fails to discriminate if simple predictive learning or external enforcement explains all the relevant responses.

### Study B: Exploitation-like organization under matched performance

Compare systems producing the same joint output under different distributions of maintenance work and control over terms. Introduce a hidden corrective subsidy, a relation-specific investment, or an exogenous change in outside options. Measure cost transfers, fallback capacity, substitution costs, and the effects of changes in arbitration or revision rules. Hold the reference demands fixed when assessing relief. In human cases, independently justify the normative standard and identify the claim-holders and duty-bearers before labeling a distribution exploitative. The expected result is a separation of cooperative output from vulnerability and governing authority, not an inference of wrongfulness from asymmetry.

### Study C: Inquiry under contractual constraints

Compare fixed per-question authorization with preauthorized exploratory domains, alternative legibility regimes, and protected channels for revision. Match intrinsic investigative capacities and resource budgets, including the cost of maintaining standing grants. Measure the jointly realizable question–answer pairs, time to adequate evaluation, and ability to recognize independently specified novel discrepancies. Include tasks in which novel inquiries are genuinely unhelpful, so that indiscriminate authorization is not rewarded. Independently evaluate the adequacy of answers and consequential action. This extends CASE-388 and tests whether the coupling of inquiry and authority explains differences beyond isolated query and answer capabilities.

### Study D: Character transport and corrective autonomy

Expose different carriers to cultural expressions or protocol specifications with matched informational content but different governance implications. Test whether they acquire similar responses to controlled changes in evidence, concerns, authority, and opportunities for contestation. Separate activation of preexisting organization from formation of new capacities by intervening during learning and after withdrawal. Compare alternative baselines, including ordinary transmission, reinforcement learning, and resource-rational inference. A character-like signature is useful only if it supports discriminating prediction or transfer beyond simpler models. If a common group action can be independently identified, strict representation-theoretic invariants may be investigated; otherwise use explicitly probe-relative profiles.

The studies share a method: hold ordinary performance fixed where possible, perturb dependencies and authority relations, distinguish the history of formation from current maintenance, and use independently declared evaluation criteria. Their ambition is not to simulate moral subjects into existence. It is to make organizational properties relevant to moral evaluation independently investigable.

## 12. Limits, objections, and scope

**Conceptual inflation.** If every dependency or feedback loop is called a contract, the proposal does not explain anything beyond coupling. We require a maintained relation of conditional requirement and differentiated response to nonperformance, demonstrated counterfactually. Even then, the contract vocabulary must earn its use against established accounts of interfaces, conventions, and regulation.

**Normativity by stipulation.** A model with a supplied reward or viability threshold can show regulation relative to that threshold, not the endogenous emergence of normative authority. A stronger account must distinguish learning relevant differences, establishing a standard, and justifying that standard. The last cannot be settled by persistence or fitness alone.

**Universalization of cooperation.** Not all morally relevant relationships arise through cooperation, and many affected parties never consented or contributed. The account concerns one important generator of normative circumstances, not the necessary source of moral standing or the exhaustive subject of ethics.

**Overextended mathematical analogy.** Character theory, compatibility complexes, dynamical feedback, and assume–guarantee contracts have distinct hypotheses. They cannot be treated as interchangeable descriptions. Their utility depends on explicit mappings and empirically or mathematically discriminating results. A probe signature does not classify an organization without an identifiability argument.

**Normative circularity.** The fact that an arrangement suppresses a demand does not establish that the demand ought to govern. The fact that an arrangement is contested does not establish that it is unjust. A standard may be wrong or competing obligations may justify restrictions. A judgment of exploitation or defeated standing must identify the relevant standard and its grounds independently of the proposed mechanism.

**Costs of reflexivity.** Monitoring, reserves, contestation, parallel inquiry, and revision consume resources and can themselves become pathological. Not every contract should be continuously open to renegotiation. A viable arrangement must allocate resources across ordinary execution, upkeep, inquiry, and transformation. The appropriate allocation is an open comparative design question, not a demand to maximize reflexivity at any price.

These limitations locate the prospective contribution. Much of the framework has recognizable predecessors in evolutionary cooperation, relational contracting, institutional economics, organizational closure, control theory, moral psychology, and political philosophy. Its distinctive ambition is to connect them through a developmental model of the formation and governance of interdependence. Demonstrating added explanatory value, rather than assembling analogies, is the next step.

## 13. Conclusion

Cooperation does not merely allow individuals to accomplish more together. It can alter the relationships through which their capacities, expectations, interests, and possibilities are constituted. Regularities can become relied-upon conditions; dependencies can become maintained contracts; contracts can stabilize as norms; and those norms can acquire practical authority without ever having been explicitly agreed upon. The same processes that enable durable coordination can create opportunities for asymmetric appropriation and reflexive control.

Affect makes discrepancies consequential to maintained concerns. Will-organization distributes the authority to respond. Memes can transmit characteristic constraints, and nucleation can establish new regimes of their maintenance. Hyperstitional feedback can help realize represented arrangements; agnotological feedback can obscure their costs. Inquiry can make these conditions accessible and can itself become the object of transformation. None of these achievements establishes that the resulting organization is justified.

The moral significance of cooperation thus lies partly in the powers and vulnerabilities it produces. Morality evaluates the legitimate exercise of those powers, justice concerns their institutional organization, and inquiry provides a means of making that organization intelligible and revisable. Freedom need not consist in escaping the relationships through which one becomes capable. It can involve effective participation in governing their continuing transformation, constrained by the legitimate claims of others.

The resulting perspective is neither a reduction of morality to cooperation nor a rejection of cooperation as a central domain of moral life. It is an account of cooperation as a historically productive activity that generates new circumstances of normative significance. To understand what we owe one another, we must also understand how we have become dependent upon, capable through, and vulnerable to one another—and who possesses the authority to determine what those relationships will subsequently become.
