summary: >-
  Nix asks how the intolerable-suffering eligibility criterion for medical
  assistance in dying (MAID) could be operationalized in advance requests
  (ARs) by people living with dementia (PLWD) in Canada. He argues that,
  because Carter v. Canada treats MAID as a medical intervention, patient
  autonomy (and so an AR) is circumscribed by the clinician's duty of care;
  the intolerable-suffering criterion is what makes MAID concordant with
  that duty, and it must be met *at the time of provision*. But Canada's
  Supreme Court conceives suffering subjectively ("intolerable to the
  individual"), and dementia erodes both the capacity to evaluate one's own
  suffering and to communicate it. He rejects two designs: (1) ARs keyed to
  pre-specified impairments, because people cannot predict what will make
  them suffer (the disability paradox; dementia as a "cognitive
  transformative experience"); (2) ARs keyed to pre-specified behaviours
  (e.g. BPSD), because that is inconsistent with a subjective concept of
  suffering. Option 2 could work only if an objective conception of
  suffering is adopted for these cases, which needs conceptual research, and
  then validated scales, which do not exist.

# NOTE-tmp3t5qf: Nix (2024) — Operationalizing the Intolerable Suffering Criterion in Advance Requests for Medical Assistance in Dying for People Living with Dementia in Canada

## Contribution

A short conceptual analysis that locates the obstacle to advance-request MAID in dementia not in
personal identity or the authority of the earlier self but in the conjunction of (a) a
duty-of-care framing that requires occurrent intolerable suffering and (b) a subjective concept
of suffering that only the now-incapable patient can apply.

## Key results

- Abstract: "ARs that express a wish to receive MAID when the PLWD develops pre-specified
  impairments are problematic because people are unlikely to accurately predict the conditions
  that will cause intolerable suffering".
- Patient autonomy and the duty of care: "Autonomy grounds patients’ right to choose between or
  decline indicated treatments, but it does not ground a right to demand treatments that fall
  outside the scope of professionally validated treatment".
- Intolerable suffering: the argument for the criterion uses a welfare baseline, "suppose death
  represents well-being of zero"; on the subjective reading, "To some extent, patients decide when
  their suffering meets the threshold of intolerability".
- Intolerable suffering in dementia: "MAID is only concordant with the duty of care if the patient
  is suffering intolerably at the time of the provision"; "Past suffering or hypothetical future
  suffering is insufficient to justify the provision of MAID".
- Option 1: "These prevalent prejudices cast doubt on people’s ability to predict how they will
  adapt to living with dementia".
- Option 2: "If we understand suffering as subjective, then outside observers cannot, in
  principle, determine whether another person’s apparent suffering meets their personal threshold
  of intolerability"; and "None of the BPSD health measurement scales have been validated for this
  application".
- Conclusion: "But the SCC’s subjective conceptualization of suffering is incompatible with the
  operationalization of ARs for MAID for PLWD".
- Scope (Introduction): "I limit my discussion to ARs for MAID that are created when the PLWD has
  decision-making capacity and is not suffering intolerably".

## Assumptions

- MAID is a medical intervention bound by a beneficence-based duty of care (taken from Carter).
- Death as well-being zero; MAID is beneficent only when well-being is below it.
- Suffering is subjective in Cassell's sense, as the SCC's wording implies.
- The disability-paradox and caregiver-rating evidence transfers to people forecasting their own
  dementia (asserted as "some evidence suggests", not shown).

## Concepts

Advance request (AR) for MAID; intolerable-suffering criterion; duty of care vs. autonomy;
subjective vs. objective ("reasonable person") conceptions of suffering; occurrent suffering;
disability paradox; cognitive transformative experience (Walsh 2020); behavioural and
psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD).

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** A's description ("a work on operationalizing the intolerable-suffering criterion ...") is only a
restatement of the title; it is not wrong, but it says nothing of the argument. The LIT should add
that it is a Brief Report and that its conclusion is negative: neither design works under the
current subjective conception.

- **THEORY-464** (Dufner via LIT-130: in ethical questions such as advance directives the deciding
  relation is usually not numerical identity). **Supports, from outside the identity debate**: Nix
  never asks whether the PLWD is the same person as the author of the AR; the AR's authority fails
  for him because of the duty-of-care requirement of *occurrent* intolerable suffering and the
  subjectivity of suffering. That is a second relation, other than identity, doing the deciding.
  It does not engage Dworkin/Dresser or precedent autonomy at all, so it does not test the
  theory's survey of the identity literature.
- **QUESTION-364** (what makes a revision the continuation of the agent's will, and how a history
  of authorization can count). **Qualifies / leaves open**: Nix's Option 1 argument (forecasting
  failure; Walsh's "cognitive transformative experience", i.e. changed preferences, values and
  beliefs) is a concrete case where the standards for reading an earlier authorization are
  themselves revised by the agent's own process. Nix does not answer which will governs; he
  side-steps by requiring present suffering. Weak bearing.
- **Recheck, missed by the LIT: QUESTION-tmp2lvhi** (what makes one agent's authority to decide
  about another's body legitimate when the other cannot decide: welfare, earlier wishes, projected
  wishes or the relation; parent vs. advance directive). **Direct bearing**: Nix's answer for
  Canadian MAID is that the earlier wish cannot by itself authorize; welfare at the time of
  provision (occurrent intolerable suffering) is necessary, and the earlier wish only selects
  among actions the duty of care already permits. It is an instance of the "welfare" answer gating
  the "earlier wishes" answer, within one legal framework.
- **LIT-1183 / NOTE-961 / CASE-107** (Bruno et al. 2011, locked-in "happy majority"): not cited by
  Nix, but his Option 1 rests on the same disability-paradox phenomenon (he cites Albrecht and
  Devlieger 1999; Ubel et al. 2005; Smith et al. 2006 on colostomies). Bruno is held evidence of
  the same forecasting gap; the filer may cross-reference. Not a duplicate.

  branch; only LIT-tmpbnl6c). Search for "disability paradox", "transformative experience",

## Limitations

Brief, conceptual, Canada-specific; no empirical work. The dementia forecasting bias is inferred
from general disability-paradox studies and proxy-rating gaps. Option 2's "uncontroversial" severe
treatment-resistant BPSD case is asserted, not argued. It does not address precedent autonomy or
the identity literature, which is where most of the record's related entries live.
