# CASE-tmpwmg3h: The public theatres of colonial Saint-Domingue, 1764 to the Revolution, as reported: an institution of French cultural prestige that relied on free and enslaved Black performers and on enslaved labour, while racially divided seating, casting and colonial law kept those contributions from giving their makers standing in it

## The case

As A reports it, from works this record has not read.

- **The institution.** Public theatres and opera in the French slave
  colonies, Saint-Domingue above all, 1764–1789 (Powers, [LIT-tmpepraj](../literature.d/LIT-tmpepraj.md)).
  A3: Powers ties the theatre to religious, military and political power,
  and names "the maintenance of peace, the preservation of social
  hierarchy, and the promotion of French culture" as related colonial
  aims.
- **Seating.** A3: audience seating was racially stratified, with separate
  entrances and sections; the *paradis*, the upper gallery, was the
  section for nonwhite spectators, which gives the book's title its
  irony. "Someone's assigned seat communicated their position in colonial
  society before a single note was played."
- **Performers.** Free and enslaved Black musicians and singers took part
  in French opera; A3 names Minette and Lise. White critics sometimes
  diminished their success. A6 §5 cites Prest's account of Minette's
  movement across the stage's racial order as "significant but limited".
- **Labour and spectatorship.** Prest ([LIT-tmp0dw2u](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0dw2u.md)) recovers enslaved
  people as spectators, workers, performers and property, in chapters
  titled, per A6, "Mitigated Spectators" and "Concealed Contributors":
  presence as servants, hearing rather than seeing, and labour that
  built, costumed and kept the theatres and that the performance records
  hardly register. Prest's method includes runaway advertisements
  ([LIT-tmpz9o31](../literature.d/LIT-tmpz9o31.md)).
- **Creole performance.** A3, from Leichman and Bénac-Giroux
  ([LIT-tmpmbcl0](../literature.d/LIT-tmpmbcl0.md)): a performance could resist metropolitan authority while
  leaving plantation slavery intact, and a practice treated as
  entertainment could sustain identities the authorities feared.

## What it shows, if the reports hold

- **A stable, productive institution whose stability says nothing of its
  terms** ([CLAIM-546](../claims.d/CLAIM-546.md)). A6 §2: "The resulting system might be artistically
  productive and organizationally stable. But none of that establishes
  that the terms of participation were fair."
- **Recognition of an exception without change of the rule** ([CLAIM-403](../claims.d/CLAIM-403.md);
  the shape of [CASE-146](CASE-146.md)). A6 §5: "The recognition of an exception does
  not prove that the rule governing exceptions has changed."
- **A description that serves the institution and merges what its
  workers' claims need** ([CLAIM-348](../claims.d/CLAIM-348.md), [CASE-110](CASE-110.md)). A6 §3: a production
  notice "might preserve the title of an opera, the identity of a
  celebrated performer, or the date of a performance, but provide little
  information about the enslaved people whose work supported the event."
  Here the institution's description is also the historian's evidence;
  see the note of 2026-11-12 on [CLAIM-348](../claims.d/CLAIM-348.md).
- **The question it raises** is [QUESTION-tmpojwyk](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpojwyk.md): an institution that
  depended on the people it subordinated, and whether that dependence
  ever turned into their authority.

## What it does not show

- **That the theatre produced the dependence.** Slavery's coercion
  predated it. A6 §1: "What we would need to investigate is how
  theatrical specialization altered, reinforced, or occasionally
  challenged those existing relations."
- **That the theatre reproduced the conditions of its non-answerability**
  ([CLAIM-614](../claims.d/CLAIM-614.md), [CLAIM-624](../claims.d/CLAIM-624.md)). A6 §5: proving it "would require more than
  documenting segregated seating or racist dramatic representation", and
  the mechanisms may have been maintained "principally by colonial law
  and state authorities, rather than by theatrical organizations
  themselves", which is [QUESTION-449](../questions.d/QUESTION-449.md)'s question of whose the
  counter-normativity is.
- **That the theatre closed a loop of self-maintenance.** A6 §1: "The
  difficult question is whether this loop actually closes", an empirical
  question ([QUESTION-399](../questions.d/QUESTION-399.md)).
- **Anything of the performers' own view.** A3: the archive "preserves
  the voices of enslavers far more readily than those of enslaved
  performers."
