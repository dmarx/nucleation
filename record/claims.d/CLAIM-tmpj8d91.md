# CLAIM-tmpj8d91: Repeated reconstruction is attraction toward conventional regions of communicative possibility: pragmatic stances, not only lexicon and grammar, can be stabilized by transmission

## The claim

A50 §3: "Repeated retellings may converge toward culturally familiar narratives,
conventional speech acts, or highly stereotyped interpretations ... translation
drift isn't random wandering. It's an attraction toward certain regions of
communicative state space." The running case: "the criminal-world complicity
might survive several stages while the historical references disappear rapidly.
Or the poem might gradually transform from a bawdy celebration of predictable
excess into a moralizing warning about financial irresponsibility", which is
"**communicative drift along a stance dimension**, not merely lexical
degradation." A50's questions: convergence to a stationary distribution;
multiple attractors for genres and stances; how fast information about the
original decays; which properties last longest.

A52 §7.4 named the selection pressures (memorability, efficiency, genre
conformity, social acceptability, learnability), and §7.5 the hypothesis
"that cultural transmission can stabilize characteristic pragmatic stances",
extending "iterated learning from lexical and grammatical structure to speaker
footing and communicative conventions". A52 §9.4: "Distinguish fidelity to a
particular original from convergence toward broadly useful communicative
conventions."

## Where it went

The manuscript §8 keeps "A stable conventional endpoint may be far removed from
the ancestral utterance while remaining interpretable" ([CLAIM-tmppvvyq](CLAIM-tmppvvyq.md)) and the stance
example ("from speaker–audience solidarity to authoritative advice"). It does
not keep attraction, the selection pressures or the footing hypothesis. The
record's readings qualify the iterated-learning premise ([CLAIM-tmpj2kjo](CLAIM-tmpj2kjo.md)).

C1 Appendix D, the first full draft (U22), qualified it: "The long-run structure
of observed utterances is therefore a property of the specified transmission
mechanism, not a generic consequence of repetition", depending on whether the
chain has a unique stationary law or several absorbing classes. E1's contractive
chain ([CASE-tmpiueso](../cases.d/CASE-tmpiueso.md)) is the stipulated instance of convergence to a convention
([CLAIM-tmpn361s](CLAIM-tmpn361s.md)).

Proposal v5 (A82 §8.5–8.6, under U30) added two points. The first: "Distinguish
convergence of realizations from convergence of observable probability
distributions." The second: retellings of a criminal ballad "may progressively
eliminate observables associated with its original subculture", while modern
adaptation "can introduce new distinctions", so the cover itself drifts. C6 kept
"cultural attractors" as a section heading (§8). The manuscript keeps only
"change the available measurement cover" (§11).

The crystallized argument (A110 §20) gave the attractor its reading: "a shared
decoding convention rather than a preserved historical object"
([TERM-tmpranvj](../terms.d/TERM-tmpranvj.md)). The manuscript never uses the word.

## Three readings since

- **Two attractors.** Kalish et al. (LIT-tmpj7bvq) find positive and
  negative linear functions both act as endpoints.
- **Group size.** Raviv, Meyer and Lev-Ari (LIT-tmpne4ig, THEORY-tmp8h3pe)
  find that larger groups converge on more systematic and more similar
  languages, while small groups are more open to drift. This is horizontal
  interaction, not a chain.
- **Structure in the corpus.** Propp (LIT-tmppp40q, THEORY-tmpdu50e) argues
  that a teller is bound in the order of functions but free in characters,
  attributes and wording. That is a structural thesis about the corpus, not
  a measurement of retellings.
