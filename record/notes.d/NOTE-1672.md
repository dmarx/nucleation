# NOTE-1672: Underwood et al. — Hawai'i's endangered waterbirds held by perpetual wetland management

## Contribution

Twenty years of counts showing that three endangered waterbirds are
stable or increasing where their wetlands are actively managed, with the
managed refuges holding a disproportionate share of each population, and
a plain statement from the managers of what the upkeep is and that it has
no end.

## Key insight

The support counters damage that does not stop: invasive plants that grow
all year, introduced predators, hydrology that no longer works. The birds
persist where people keep doing that work, and the authors see no point at
which it could stop: "the fate of these species will be perpetually tied
to the amount of habitat that is actively managed for them"
(Conclusion).

## Assumptions

- **Counts as minima.** Semiannual statewide and monthly refuge counts,
  uncorrected for detection; the Gallinule is undercounted.
- **Comparison.** Refuges against all other wetlands, which lump managed
  and unmanaged sites; trends by log-linear Poisson models (TRIM).

## Key results

1. **The label (Introduction).** The birds "have recently been
   categorized as “conservation reliant”, which implies that they will
   require active management into perpetuity [4], [5], [6]"; [5] is Scott
   et al. (2010).
2. **Three kinds of upkeep (Introduction).** Invasive plant control "must
   be constant"; for predators, "wetlands must have a barrier of fences,
   rodenticide baited stations, and live or kill traps"; water levels are
   manipulated "to mimic natural hydrological processes that have been
   highly altered or lost". "all three management actions in concert are
   required".
3. **How fast it goes back (Introduction).** "When wetlands are not
   actively managed, waterbird habitat can rapidly degrade as weedy
   invaders tend to fill open water and mudflat areas".
4. **Trends (Results, Table 2).** Statewide multiplicative trend per
   year: Coot 1.0018 (stable), Stilt 1.0198, Gallinule 1.0686. Refuge
   against non-refuge: Gallinule 1.0546 and 1.0779, Stilt 1.0311 and
   1.0125, Coot 0.9944 and 1.0083.
5. **Disproportionate use (Results).** The refuges are about 15 % of the
   wetland area but hold on average about 36 % of Coots, 42 % of Stilts
   and 34 % of Gallinules.
6. **Unmanaged wetlands as sinks (Discussion).** Such wetlands show
   "little to no reproductive success even when population counts are in
   the hundreds".
7. **A case of withdrawal (Discussion).** At Kawainui Marsh, about 400
   ha, "growth of invasive plants and sedimentation resulting from altered
   hydrology had resulted in almost a complete loss of wetland
   functionality for waterbirds"; "one of the largest natural wetlands in
   the state provided habitat for only a handful of birds".
8. **Return with restoration (Discussion), unpublished.** On refuges,
   after restoration, populations "have increased dramatically
   (unpublished data)".
9. **No end (Discussion).** "without perpetual active management of
   wetlands in Hawai'i, the endangered waterbird species likely would
   experience population declines".
10. **Cost (Discussion).** Managing another 1,000–2,000 ha is put "to be
    at least 2–3 million USD annually".

## Concepts

- **Conservation reliant**: requiring "active management into
  perpetuity", after Scott et al. (2005, 2010) and Reed et al. (2012).
- **Active wetland management**: weed control, predator control and
  water-level manipulation, done together.
- **Sink habitat**: wetlands occupied without reproduction.

## Limitations

- Observational, with no unmanaged control: non-refuge wetlands lump
  managed and unmanaged sites.
- The decline without management is asserted from one marsh and from
  cited reproductive assessments, not measured; the return after
  restoration is unpublished.
- The authors work for the agency that runs the refuges.
- Counts are minima, uncorrected for detection.
