# NOTE-1491: Craver & Bechtel — top-down causation without top-down causes

## Contribution

It offers a deflationary reading of "top-down causation" within one sense
of level, levels of mechanisms. Whatever is intelligible in an interlevel
causal claim is a mechanistically mediated effect, "hybrids of causal and
constitutive relations, where the causal relations are exclusively
intralevel" (abstract, p. 547). An interlevel claim that cannot be
translated this way is "suspect". The paper does not argue against
interlevel causation from a theory of causation. It shows a translation
for six worked cases and lists the assumptions about causation that
interlevel causes would break.

## Key insight

A mechanism and its components are not distinct existences, so a change
in the whole is already a change in some parts, and a change in a part is
already a change in the whole. What looks like downward influence is a
change in some components, constitutive of the whole's new condition,
that then propagates through ordinary causation among components. The
symmetry of interlevel experiments, which makes interlevel causation look
puzzling, is "readily explained" by constitution. "The relation is
symmetrical precisely because the mechanism as a whole is fully
constituted by the organized activities of its parts" (p. 554).

## Assumptions

- **Higher-level causes exist,** in the sense that (1) mechanisms are
  organized collections of entities and activities, (2) mechanisms affect
  and are affected by things, and (3) "the parts of the mechanism taken
  singularly cannot be so affected or have such effects" (p. 548). This
  is assumed, not argued.
- **All higher-level causes are explained by lower-level mechanisms**
  (p. 548). Also assumed. Strong emergence, a property with no mechanistic
  explanation, is set aside: its "levels" are not levels of mechanisms
  (p. 551).
- **Causation is intralevel, between distinct relata, and typically
  asymmetrical.** The paper rests on Lewis's distinctness condition
  (p. 552) and the asymmetry of dependence (p. 553). It grants that "None
  of these arguments is decisive" (p. 554).
- **Level is relative to a mechanism.** "Levels of mechanisms are defined
  locally, within the context of a given type of mechanism" (p. 550). The
  setting is narrower than the title: it concerns levels of composition
  within one mechanism, not levels of size, of science or of ontological
  category.

## Key results

The results are a definition, an argument, and translations.

- **Levels of mechanisms (p. 548).** "An item X is at a lower level than
  an item S if and only if X is a component in the mechanism for some
  activity ψ of S. X is a component in a mechanism if and only if it is
  one of the entities or activities organized such that S ψs." The
  definition takes "component" as given. It gives no test for it.
- **Interlevel experiments (pp. 553–554).** There are four kinds:
  correlation of a component's presence with the whole's behaviour;
  lesion or inhibition; stimulation; and intervening on the whole while
  recording the parts (neuroimaging). They "share a form with Mill's
  methods". They run in both directions, which causal tests generally do
  not. Two qualifications in the footnotes matter:
  - n. 10, p. 553: a deficit after a lesion "is prima facie evidence that
    X is a component", "It is only prima facie since if the component is
    not interactively integrated into the system, it could also be
    construed as constituting a background condition on the operation of
    the mechanism, not as part of it."
  - n. 11, p. 554: neuroimaging judgments "are prima facie ones and depend
    on discovering that the components are deeply integrated into the
    system."
- **Intersize is not interlevel (pp. 550, 556).** A virus that kills a
  general is not a component of the general, so the case is ordinary
  causation between things of different sizes. But "there are pragmatic
  contexts in which a similar interlevel analysis is appropriate"
  (p. 557): when the question is how the virus killed her, its path
  through her cells is traced as part of the mechanism. What counts as
  inside the mechanism moves with the explanandum.
- **The translations.**
  - Bottom-up: the general's heart attack (p. 557). "when we reach the
    state of the mechanism that constitutes the state of death, we do not
    say ... that it causes death. It just is death."
  - Top-down, parts "carried along for the ride": Ignatius's hotdogs
    (p. 558). The hotdogs are not working parts, and "Ignatius ... is
    doing all the work" (p. 558).
  - Top-down, parts "enlisted in the ride": Hal's glucose metabolism
    (p. 559), traced through ATP use and glycolysis. Kane's memory
    (pp. 560–561) is the same, given a mechanistic account of
    intentional content, which the paper does not try to defend.
- **The general strategy (p. 561).** Every intelligible interlevel claim
  "is analyzed into a causal claim coupled with one or more constituency
  claims".

## Concepts

- **Level of mechanisms**: a level defined by the component relation
  within one mechanism. It is local, not a stratum of nature (pp. 548–550).
- **Component**: an entity or activity "organized such that S ψs"
  (p. 548). Contrasted, in n. 10, with a background condition, which is a
  part not "interactively integrated" into the system.
- **Mechanistically mediated effect**: a hybrid of intralevel causation
  and interlevel constitution, which stands in for an apparent interlevel
  cause (p. 547).
- **Carried along for the ride / enlisted in the ride**: components that
  change only because the whole moves, or components whose activity a
  change of the whole's condition recruits (pp. 558–559).

## Connections

It applies Craver's account of levels (Craver 2001, and Explaining the
Brain, [LIT-832](../literature.d/LIT-832.md), then forthcoming). The account of interlevel experiments
is Craver 2002, the source of the mutual-manipulability criterion. That
criterion is stated in Explaining the Brain and in "Constitutive
explanatory relevance" ([LIT-1930](../literature.d/LIT-1930.md)), neither of which was reached. The
paper answers Kim's (2000) argument that simultaneous upward and downward
influence makes causal circles. It anticipates the later charge
(Baumgartner and Gebharter 2016, reported in [LIT-1931](../literature.d/LIT-1931.md)) that an
interventionist reading of these experiments confuses constitution with
causation. Here the symmetry of the experiments is the evidence for
constitution. Bechtel's later paper on top-down causation through networks and
dynamics (PhilSci-Archive preprint 2016, not held) reports critics' objection
that this account, like Kim's, allows causation only at the lowest level.
That is known here from a search snippet, not from reading it.

## Limitations

- **No test of componency.** The definition of level takes "component" as
  given. The experiments supply only prima facie evidence, and the
  difference between a component and a background condition is left to an
  undefined "interactively integrated". Constitutive relevance ([LIT-832](../literature.d/LIT-832.md),
  [LIT-1930](../literature.d/LIT-1930.md)) is meant to supply the test, and this paper does not.
- **The conclusion is mostly assumed.** That all higher-level causes have
  mechanistic explanations is an assumption (p. 548). So the translation's
  success shows that the view is coherent, not that it is true.
- **Feedback is waved through.** "Even if this is made possible by the
  incorporation of complex interactions, including feedback loops, inside
  the mechanism, there is nothing mysterious" (p. 560). The paper does not
  ask whether a feedback loop that crosses the mechanism's boundary
  changes the analysis. That is the case the record needs.
- **Kane's case rests on a mechanistic account of intentional content**,
  which the paper explicitly does not defend (p. 560).

## Open questions

- What makes a part "interactively integrated" (n. 10)? Is it a matter of
  causal structure, of spatial inclusion, or of the explanandum chosen? The
  paper's virus example suggests the explanandum (p. 557).
- Does an item that is causally coupled to a system in both directions,
  but located outside it, pass the paper's four experiments? For a
  state-feedback controller it does ([CLAIM-530](../claims.d/CLAIM-530.md)). If so, what rules it
  out as a component, other than a boundary drawn beforehand?

## Bearing on the record's own argument

- **[CLAIM-522](../claims.d/CLAIM-522.md): the boundary it requires is the paper's undefined
  "component".** [CLAIM-522](../claims.d/CLAIM-522.md) says that only interventions on mechanisms,
  relative to a declared boundary, separate an internal producer from an
  outside controller that supplies the same law. Run this paper's four
  experiments (pp. 553–554) on both systems, with maintenance of c as
  the phenomenon.
  - Lesion: disabling the internal producer in A, or the controller in
    B, ends maintenance.
  - Stimulation: raising either's output raises c.
  - Intervening on the whole: perturbing c changes the activity of
    either, since each reads c.
  - Correlation: either co-varies with c.

  Every test comes out the same. By n. 10 the controller is no
  "background condition", because it is interactively integrated, reading
  c and acting on it. So by the paper's own markers the controller is a
  component of the mechanism for "c is maintained". What puts it outside
  is the declared boundary, which the phenomenon "the system maintains c"
  already names. That is filed as [CLAIM-530](../claims.d/CLAIM-530.md). It is [CLAIM-522](../claims.d/CLAIM-522.md)'s own
  closing proviso, that the boundary is not given, now traced into the
  mechanist's account of constitution.
- **[CLAIM-194](../claims.d/CLAIM-194.md): a second instance of the same gap.** [CLAIM-194](../claims.d/CLAIM-194.md) says a
  coupling criterion has "no term for who made the coupling". This paper's
  experiments have no term for which side of a boundary a component sits
  on. Interactive integration, the paper's only discriminator, is a
  coupling notion. So it inherits [CLAIM-194](../claims.d/CLAIM-194.md)'s blindness to who made the
  coupling. Same structure, not shown same mechanism: [CLAIM-194](../claims.d/CLAIM-194.md) is about
  observational statistics, and this is about interventions.
- **[CLAIM-414](../claims.d/CLAIM-414.md) and [QUESTION-086](../questions.d/QUESTION-086.md): what the experiments do separate.** Within
  a fixed boundary the lesion experiment is just [CLAIM-414](../claims.d/CLAIM-414.md)'s separating
  intervention. Disabling the inside pathway ends A's maintenance and
  leaves B's untouched. So the paper supports the record's view that an
  intervention on mechanism, not state, is what separates them. It
  supplies no intervention that finds the boundary. [QUESTION-086](../questions.d/QUESTION-086.md)'s "by what
  intervention" is answered by this paper only relative to a boundary
  already drawn.
- **[QUESTION-093](../questions.d/QUESTION-093.md) and the "higher-level agent".** On this paper's account, a
  coalition's higher-level "influence" on its members is a mechanistically
  mediated effect: members enlisted in the ride (p. 559). Nothing in
  [QUESTION-093](../questions.d/QUESTION-093.md)'s four criteria needs top-down causation in a stronger
  sense. The paper also gives no reason to think closure or
  self-individuation could be read from interlevel experiments without a
  boundary.
- **[THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md) and production.** [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md) draws the line at "constraints
  that produce one another". In this paper's vocabulary, mutual production
  is intralevel causation among components, and the organism's persistence
  is constituted by it. That is consistent with [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md). It adds nothing
  on whether production happens inside or outside the boundary.
