# NOTE-1070: Woods, Windt & Carter — Contentless experiences in meditation are neither truly contentless nor identical

## Contribution

The paper tests two claims long made in the academic study of mysticism:
that "contentless" meditative states lack all content, and that they are
therefore one identical state of pure consciousness. It does so by a
systematic evidence synthesis of the texts of the teachers of three
practices, coded feature by feature. Its answer is that, on those texts,
the states lack the familiar forms of content but keep many others, and
differ across practices. It is the first feature-by-feature comparison of
the three goal-states.

## Key insight

"Contentless" in the meditation literature means contentless "in the
limited sense that they lack well recognized forms of content such as
thoughts, perceptions, and mental images" (p. 44). What remains is not
nothing. Wakefulness, stillness, calm, bliss, a non-conceptual knowing,
freedom and unity are reported as present, and are presented as
discovered, not added. Once that is seen, the question whether all such
states are identical becomes a question about these features, and on
some of them the practices plainly differ.

## Assumptions

- **Expert texts are evidence about the experience.** The goal-states are
  reconstructed from 135 publications by eight teachers: Alan Wallace for
  Shamatha; Pearson, Rosenthal, Roth, Shear and Travis for TM; Meares and
  McKinnon for Stillness Meditation (p. 6, Figure 1). The authors list
  this as the key limitation: the experts' traditional knowledge "is not
  the same as having the skills in science or phenomenology that may be
  necessary to provide or obtain reliable reports of experience" (p. 47).
- **Majority descriptions count.** Where experts disagree, the analysis
  follows the more widespread description and notes the minority (p. 7).
  The main minority is Shear's: that the TM goal-state "lacks all or
  virtually all forms of content other than being conscious and awake"
  (p. 7).
- **"Content" is broad.** It covers any reported feature of the
  experience, including wakefulness, affective tone, and qualities such as
  simplicity or depth. The paper does not define content more narrowly
  than that.
- **A goal-state is what the practice aims at**, distinguished from the
  interim-states on the way (p. 4). Some descriptions are reclassified as
  interim states when they include perception (pp. 9, 27).

## Key results

- **Shared and absent (§3.2, pp. 9–12).** In all three practices the
  goal-states are said to have no thoughts, concepts, images or
  memories, no awareness of the meditation object, and no sense or body
  perception (only very dull perception in Stillness Meditation's
  shallower states). The experts' "no feelings" means the feelings of
  daily life: "what they actually have in mind is particular feelings that
  are experienced in daily life" (p. 10). Bliss, goodness, calm and peace
  are presented as innate, not as feelings of that kind.
- **Not flat (p. 11).** Each tradition says the emptiness has depth or
  fullness. Wallace: "This space is not an empty nothingness" (as quoted,
  p. 11).
- **Nonduality and recognition after the fact (§3.3, pp. 12–13).** All
  three report no subject–object duality. Wallace says the dichotomy
  between meditator and object "is constructed by concepts" (p. 12). And
  "the meditator only becomes aware that they have experienced the
  goal-states once they emerge from them": a thought such as "My mind is
  still" means they are not then in the goal-state (p. 13).
- **Present content (§§3.1, 3.4–3.13).** Conscious, awake and heightened
  wakefulness; stillness, silence, simplicity, naturalness; calm,
  relaxation, rest, no discomfort, no anxiety; bliss or joy; luminosity
  (Shamatha); a knowing distinct from knowledge of things; no deliberate
  doing or control, and effortlessness; loss of ego; freedom; unity,
  wholeness, security; timelessness; a spiritual aspect for some
  meditators.
- **Ground-states and the true self (§3.14, pp. 27–29).** Shamatha and TM
  call the goal-state consciousness itself. TM and Stillness Meditation
  call the deepest state pure being and the true or deepest self. Meares
  says the deepest Stillness state is somehow beyond calm. The authors
  suggest this could reconcile Shear's minority view with the majority's.
- **Interpretation (§§3.13, 3.18).** Two passages concern interpretation
  directly. Shamatha and Stillness Meditation texts say "two meditators
  may have the same experience but interpret it differently", for
  instance as spiritual (p. 26). And Wallace holds that in the absence of
  conscious concepts "certain implicit or subliminal conceptualization
  persists", and that differences in it can make experiences of what looks
  like one state "significantly different" (p. 36, citing Wallace 2000).
- **Attention, the main difference (§3.16, pp. 32–35).** Shamatha's
  goal-state has "exceptional" or "perfect" stability and vividness,
  holding for four hours or more without deviation. TM and Stillness
  practitioners cycle in and out of the state several times in a session,
  so their stability is lower, and their vividness appears lower too.
  Shamatha alone describes high arousal with deep calm ("attentional
  balance").
- **Tallies (§4.1, p. 40).** 65 features. 51 are reported or implied in
  all three traditions. The goal-states are the same on 14, different on
  4 (stability, vividness, balance, and very dull perception in
  Stillness), and unclear on 47. Of the 14 "same" features, 12 are
  absences or the bare facts of being conscious and awake.
- **Conclusions (§§4.4–4.5, pp. 42–45).** If these goal-states count as
  contentless experiences, then contentless experiences "can involve
  considerable variation" (p. 42), and in "many and perhaps most cases
  these experiences are not truly contentless" (p. 2; also pp. 44–45).
  §4.6 maps the reported losses onto Millière et al.'s dimensions of
  self, and notes that the TM and Stillness ground-states may not be
  totally selfless. §4.7 argues that TM is not a jhana practice, since its
  attention is not single-pointed.

## Concepts

- **Contentless experience**: "an absence of mental content such as
  thoughts, perceptions, and mental images" (p. 2), as the academic
  literature uses it.
- **Goal-state and interim-state**: the experience a practice aims for,
  and those passed through on the way (p. 4).
- **Everyday content**: the authors' shorthand for what is absent:
  thoughts, perceptions, images and the like. It "does not include the
  sense of being conscious or awake" (p. 11).
- **Ground-state**: a goal-state described as the essential nature of mind
  or being (§3.14).
- **Attentional stability, vividness and balance**: Wallace's constructs,
  used to compare the practices (§3.16).
- **Truly contentless**: lacking all content, or all content except being
  conscious and awake (p. 44).

## Connections

The paper answers the "pure consciousness event" literature of Stace,
Forman and Shear, and joins its critics (Katz, Gennaro, Metzinger 2020)
in finding subtle content. It is one of a pair with the authors' synthesis
of techniques and interim-states (Woods et al. 2021, cited here as 2020
and 2021, and as "Submitted"), which is not filed. It supports Metzinger's
remark that pure consciousness reports share "common phenomenological
denominators" without one single character (p. 43). It uses Millière's
dimensions of selfhood and Windt's spatial self-location. The record holds
no reading of Forman, Shear, Metzinger 2020 or Millière.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-412](../claims.d/CLAIM-412.md) (experience constituted by interpretive organization).**
  The exchange cited this paper for the point that apparently contentless
  states keep features such as wakefulness and calm, so that they may
  still carry interpretive structure. The paper supports the first half
  directly. It does not argue the second: it never uses interpretation as
  a category of analysis. Two of its passages bear on the claim more
  directly than the exchange's description did. Wallace's view that
  "implicit or subliminal conceptualization persists" and shapes the
  experience (p. 36) is a teacher's statement that organization below
  report fixes the character of a state with no conscious concepts. That
  is the claim's pattern, though on one source. Against it, the texts'
  claim that two meditators "may have the same experience but interpret it
  differently" (p. 26) puts interpretation after the experience, as a
  label laid on it. That is the production-or-description reading the
  claim must distinguish itself from, and the texts give no way to tell
  which is right. In the claim's terms the paper's chief lesson is
  limiting: the residual content (calm, stillness, knowing) is not
  conceptual, so if the claim needs interpretation here, it is the broad
  sense of [TERM-073](../terms.d/TERM-073.md), and the case does not distinguish that from
  bare organization.
- **[CLAIM-411](../claims.d/CLAIM-411.md) (much interpretation not shown to be experiential).**
  It gives the counter a mirror case: states reported as experience while
  explicit interpretation is reported absent. If the claim keeps such
  states as experiential, what does the work must be organization below
  the level of concepts, which is where the counter says the unconscious
  cases also sit.
- **[QUESTION-254](../questions.d/QUESTION-254.md).** The features said to survive when everyday
  content goes (wakefulness, unity, a non-conceptual knowing, no
  subject–object duality) are candidate marks of what keeps an
  organization experiential. They come from texts, not from tests.
- **[TERM-072](../terms.d/TERM-072.md) (perspective, three senses).** The reported loss of
  subject–object duality and of ego, with consciousness persisting, is
  the case where the represented perspective (sense 2) fails and a
  phenomenal one (sense 3) is still claimed. The TM and Stillness
  "true self" reports complicate that: some self-content may survive
  (§4.6).
- **[THEORY-063](../theory.d/THEORY-063.md) (unity without a self-model).** Reports of unity and
  wholeness with ego gone fit Metzinger's claim that unity does not need a
  self-model. This is report-level consonance, not support.
- **[LIT-1354](../literature.d/LIT-1354.md) (Chandia-Jorquera et al.).** The paper's §4.4 asks
  imaging studies to allow for variation among contentless states. That
  study's scale defines TM's state as "free from content", the very
  description this paper finds the TM texts do not support. Its
  practitioners came from the TM organisation, whose teachers are among
  the sources here.
- **THEORY filed.** [THEORY-866](../theory.d/THEORY-866.md) states the finding, scoped to the
  texts.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- **The evidence is texts by teachers.** Some passages describe specific
  experiences, others are generalisations (p. 47). Several are teaching
  materials or popular books by people who run or promote the practice.
  No meditator was asked anything.
- **"Unclear" dominates.** 47 of 65 features could not be judged the same
  or different. The finding of non-identity rests on four differences,
  three of them about attention.
- **Content is undefined.** Counting calm, simplicity or depth as content
  is what makes the states non-contentless. A reader who holds that only
  intentional or representational content counts may grant the data and
  deny the conclusion. The paper does not argue the point.
- **Coding by one author.** The first author coded the texts, and has
  practised Stillness Meditation for over 20 years. Samples were checked
  by the third author (p. 47). No inter-rater agreement is reported.
- **The submitted version was read.** The cover says the final version is
  likely to differ. The version of record (2022, 52 pp.) may revise
  features, counts or wording.

## Open questions

- Do meditators' own reports, gathered by micro-phenomenology or similar
  methods, show the features the texts name, and the same four
  differences? The paper proposes this (p. 43).
- Is the residual content (calm, knowing, unity) a feature of the
  experience, or a description applied after emerging from it, since the
  state is said to be recognised only on emerging (p. 13)?
- Does "implicit conceptualization" (p. 36) name anything testable? A
  study that varied meditators' doctrinal training and compared their
  reports of the same practice would bear on it.
- Are there practices whose goal-states are contentless in the fuller
  sense the paper leaves open (p. 45)?
