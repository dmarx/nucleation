# LIT-1066: How a Cockpit Remembers Its Speeds

Hutchins, E. (1995). How a cockpit remembers its speeds. *Cognitive Science* 19(3), 265–288. DOI 10.1207/s15516709cog1903_1

Read in full (NOTE-867). A task analysis, not a measurement: the approach speeds are computed 25 to 30 minutes ahead, in a low-workload phase, from a speed card selected by the displayed gross weight, and set as bugs on the airspeed indicators. At the moment of use, recalling a number and reading a scale become a judgement of whether the needle is next to a bug. The cockpit remembers the speeds without either pilot's memory being improved. The argument rests on mandated procedure for an anticipated, invariant task; nothing is measured.

## Why this is here

The owner's EMT case ([CASE-085](../cases.d/CASE-085.md)) is a team failing to deploy a resource
in time though its presence was known. This paper is the classic study of
a crew and its artifacts holding what no member holds alone, which is
[QUESTION-101](../questions.d/QUESTION-101.md)'s subject and bears on [CLAIM-296](../claims.d/CLAIM-296.md) and [TERM-055](../terms.d/TERM-055.md).
Closer to the case than *Cognition in the Wild*, which is Unavailable.
