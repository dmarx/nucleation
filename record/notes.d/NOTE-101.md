# NOTE-101: Luque — Philosophical analysis of the Price equation

## Contribution

The paper imports Kuhn's (1970) notion of a "generalization-sketch" into the dispute over the Price equation. It argues that critics and users are both right. The critics are right that the equation is not a model and predicts nothing on its own. The users are right that it is fruitful, because its role is that of a schema from which specific models are derived. Luque supports this with a derivation (§3), a survey of results the equation recovers and of its extensions across fields (§4, Tables 2–3), an invariance argument (§5), two worked case studies (§6.1–6.2), and replies to the tautology and predictive-power objections (§7).

## Key insight

Price's equation stands to evolutionary biology as f = ma stands to mechanics. It is a law-sketch that is "empty in [its] predictive content" (p. 31) until a model supplies the entities, the characters, the time step and the causes. Its value lies in unification and in heuristic guidance, not in prediction. Demanding dynamic sufficiency or predictions from it is a category mistake.

## Assumptions

Premises and authorities the argument rests on:

- **Derivation (Frank's standard form, §3):** the equation assumes only a mapping from ancestors i (frequency q_i, character z_i, fitness w_i) to descendants (q′_i = q_i w_i / w̄, z′_i). The one restriction stated is a closed population in which every entity at t + 1 descends from, or is itself, an entity present at t, which excludes migration (p. 8). The equation holds over any time interval.
- **Kuhn's account** of generalization-sketches (Kuhn 1970, p. 465; Kuhn 2000) and of exemplars as the way scientists learn to specialise the sketch.
- **Unification as explanatory value** (Kitcher 1993), and "buy generality at the expense of predictive power" (Birch 2014, p. 400).
- **Sober's (1984) distinction** between consequence laws and source laws.
- **Cartwright's (1999) "fundamentalism"** and Rice & Papadopoulos's (2009) "axiomatic theories", taken as descriptions of the style of theorizing the equation embodies.
- **Ramsey–Quine theoretical holism** (via Sklar 2013): theoretical terms get their meaning from their role in a theory. Used in §7.1 to say that both F = ma and the Price equation acquire empirical meaning only through interpretation.
- **A premise from the philosophy of mathematics:** mathematical theorems are not tautologies, which he supports with Gödel's incompleteness theorems and Putnam (1975) (p. 28, fn 17).

## Key results

What it argues:

- **Eq. (1), p. 7:** Δz̄ = Cov(w, z) + E(wΔz). Eq. (2): Δz̄ = Cov(ω, z) + E_ω(Δz), with relative fitness ω. Eq. (3), continuous time (Page & Nowak 2002). Eq. (4): Δz̄ = Cov(ω, z′) + E(Δz), Okasha's preferred causal partition. Eq. (5): Δz̄ = Cov(ω, z) + Cov(ω, Δz) + E(Δz), after Godfrey-Smith 2007, for when selection and transmission interact non-additively. All are collected in Table 1.
- **Unification (§4):** the diallelic viability-selection result Δp = p(w*_A − w̄)/w̄ is re-derived from the covariance term (pp. 10–11). So are Robertson's secondary theorem, Δz̄ = Cov_A(w, z), and the "classical interpretation" of Fisher's fundamental theorem, Δw̄ = Var_A(w). Footnote 7 notes that the exact theorem concerns only the partial change caused by selection. The paper also lists applications in selection theory, stochastic evolution, ecology, epidemiology, non-genetic inheritance and culture, and mentions economics and cosmology. Tables 2 and 3 collect about 25 derived identities and extensions.
- **Invariance (§5):** following Frank (2012a), selection can be written as Δ_s z̄ = β_zw F(Δq̃), with F the Fisher information in the frequency fluctuations. Infinitely many combinations of frequency change and character value give the same total selection. Against an objection he credits to Jun Otsuka, Luque argues that invariances always stem from a model's mathematical form, as Galilean invariance does for Newtonian models, and that they can still have empirical consequences (pp. 15–16).
- **Generalization-sketch (§6):** the equation provides a common language (covariances, expectations, regressions). It works as a consequence law and a "promise", and needs exemplars to learn. Hamilton's 1979 course notes are offered as evidence that it was taught that way (pp. 22–23).
- **Case studies:** Day & Bonduriansky (2011) derive eqs. (6a–b), overlapping-generation versions that separate selection, "reproductive transmission" and "survival transmission", and then eqs. (7a–b) under a first-order fitness approximation. These are specialised into models of environmental noise, maternal effects, indirect genetic effects and transgenerational epigenetic inheritance (§6.1). Rice's stochastic Price equation shows that all moments of the fitness distribution, not just the mean, contribute to directional evolution; the second and third terms pull the population toward minimum variance and maximum skewness in fitness (§6.2).
- **Tautology (§7.1):** the equation is "not a tautology" in the logician's sense (p. 28). The critics' real complaint, that it follows from notational definitions, has the same shape as the 18th–20th century dispute over whether F = ma is a definition (d'Alembert, Mach, Poincaré, Wilczek). Both acquire meaning only by interpretation within a theory. The biological interpretation, "change over time, ancestor/descendant relations, and phenotype" (Rice 2004), is empirically grounded (p. 30).
- **Predictive power (§7.2):** dynamic sufficiency belongs to particular models, not to the equation, and van Veelen et al. (2012) concede as much. On van Veelen's claim that the equation cannot answer Type 1 (top-down) questions, Luque replies that predictive power is not the only explanatory value and that generalization-sketches are predictively empty by nature (pp. 31–32).

## Concepts

- **generalization-sketch** — after Kuhn (1970): a schematic law whose symbolic form changes from one application to the next, such as f = ma becoming mg = m d²s/dt² for free fall. Kuhn also called it a "law-sketch"; structuralists call it a "guiding principle" (fn 1).
- **abstractness / unification / invariance** — the three features Luque says the Price equation shares with other scientific principles. Invariance here means Frank's invariance of total selection across combinations of frequency change and character value.
- **consequence law vs source law** — Sober (1984): a consequence law says how forces, once present, change a system; a source law says what produces the forces.
- **fundamentalist / axiomatic theorizing** — starting from postulates believed true and deriving a system's mathematical rules, as opposed to idealised models built on simplifying assumptions (Cartwright 1999; Rice & Papadopoulos 2009).
- **dynamic sufficiency** — a model's capacity to be iterated to predict any future state (Lewontin 1974). Luque, following Frank and Gardner, calls it a property of models, not of the equation.
- **Type 1 / Type 2 questions** — van Veelen (2005): top-down (from assumptions, deduce implications) vs bottom-up (from data, find the model that fits).

## Connections

The paper builds on Frank's (1995, 2012a, 2012b) and Rice's (2004, 2008) pro-Price programme, on Gardner's "meta-model" reading (2008), and on Okasha's remark that the equation is "a schema that may be used to understand all other evolutionary models" (2010, p. 426). It argues against van Veelen (2005), van Veelen et al. (2012) and Nowak & Highfield (2011). Birch (2014) appears only for the two versions of Hamilton's rule and the generality-for-predictive-power trade.

In the record, it pairs with the units-of-selection material: [LIT-160](../literature.d/LIT-160.md) (Units and Levels of Selection, read) and [LIT-201](../literature.d/LIT-201.md) (Alternative formulations of multilevel selection, still a seed). There the multilevel Price partition (Table 2, "multi-level Price equation (Okasha 2006)") underwrites decomposing selection by level. Luque touches multilevel selection only in the survey (p. 12).

**philosophy-of-science:** the paper addresses the epistemic and semantic status of a fundamental equation. Is it an empirical law, a definition or a schema? Does unification count as explanation without prediction? How does a mathematical identity acquire empirical content? Luque's positions are: a schema (a Kuhnian generalization-sketch and a Soberian consequence law); unification is an explanatory value in its own right (Kitcher); and empirical content comes from interpretation within a theory (Ramsey–Quine holism). The tag is justified and should lead.

## Bearing on the record

It supplies a standard reference for the "just an identity" objection to general equations. The move it makes, granting that a general identity is predictively empty and locating its value in unification and model-construction, is the reusable content, and it transfers to any record discussion of fundamental equations in a science. No THEORY document in this record is sourced to it.

It carries nothing for ML practice. The Price equation has been used to analyse learning dynamics elsewhere, but this paper does not mention machine learning, and no instruction for the Anthology of the SOTA follows from it.

## Limitations

- The tautology argument (§7.1) is the weakest part. Establishing that theorems are not logical tautologies does not answer the critics' substantive point that the equation adds no empirical content, and Luque concedes that point in §7.2 anyway. The abstract's "some recent disputes about it could be diverted" is fair; any stronger reading, that the disputes are resolved, is not supported.
- The invariance section borrows its only substantive result from Frank (2012a) and gives no derivation. The claim that this invariance is of a kind with physical symmetries rests on analogy.
- The prescriptive claim (researchers should adopt the equation) is asserted with no comparison against other routes to the same models. Luque concedes that his case-study authors "found previous results", such as Lande 2007 and Gillespie 1974/1977, that were already derivable by other means (p. 27).
- Descriptive coverage stops at about 2015, and the survey is explicitly non-exhaustive (fn 8).
- The version read is the PhilSci manuscript, not the typeset article. Wording and pagination may differ.

## Open questions

- Does the generalization-sketch reading survive the known non-uniqueness of Price partitions, such as eq. (2) vs (4) vs (5), and the dependence on how ancestor–descendant maps are drawn? Luque notes the partitions (§3) but not the problem this poses for the invariance claim. A treatment showing which partitions a given causal model licenses would settle it. Otsuka (2015), cited in passing, points that way.
- Is there a principled difference between the Price equation and f = ma, given that force has independent source laws (gravitation, Coulomb) while selection "forces" arguably do not? The paper gestures at Sober's source/consequence distinction but does not say what the biological source laws are.
