# LIT-455: Defending the Bounds of Cognition

Fred Adams, Ken Aizawa (2010), "Defending the Bounds of Cognition", in
Richard Menary (ed.), *The Extended Mind*, Cambridge, MA: MIT Press,
pp. 67–80 — DOI-10.7551/mitpress/9780262014038.003.0004. Read from a draft
that predates it (see the history note).

## Key takeaways

- **The fallacy, named.** "When Clark makes an object cognitive when it is
  connected to a cognitive agent, he is committing an instance of a
  'coupling-constitution fallacy.'" The pattern: point to a case where an
  object or process is coupled to a cognitive agent, then "slide to the
  conclusion that the object or process constitutes part of the agent's
  cognitive apparatus". Coupling is not constitution: the neurons into a
  neuromuscular junction are coupled to the muscle but are not part of it.
  The authors call it "the most common mistake that extended mind theorists
  make" and charge Van Gelder and Port, Clark and Chalmers ([LIT-097](LIT-097.md)), Clark
  2001, Gibbs and Haugeland with it.
- **What is needed instead is a mark of the cognitive.** The 2001 proposal
  is restated: cognition is "constituted by certain sorts of causal
  processes that involve non-derived content", and as a contingent
  empirical fact some processes that are recognisably cognitive occur only
  in the brain.
- **The non-derived-content condition is not all-or-nothing.** Cognitive
  states must *involve* non-derived content, not consist of it throughout.
  A process with *no* intrinsic content is ruled non-cognitive.
- **Euler circles.** Clark's case of an imagined Venn or Euler diagram,
  whose meaning is conventional, confuses how a diagram on paper gets its
  meaning with how a mental image of it does. That a convention had to
  exist first is a historical dependence, like a mental image of a car
  depending on the car's invention, not a fact about what constitutes the
  image's content. And conventions need access to the syntactic items they
  attach meanings to, which nobody has for brain states.
- **No brain-tool science.** Even if information processing crosses the
  skull, brains process information by different principles from
  consumer electronics. Tools are artifacts, an open-ended set, so a
  science of brain-plus-tool systems would have to cover a "motley", not
  just a multiplicity. Intracranial processes may be many, but the brain's
  development bounds them.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, from a text fetched while the
critics of [LIT-097](LIT-097.md) were filed. [NOTE-380](../notes.d/NOTE-380.md) is the close reading, and
placed it: **Active**, with the caveat that what was read is a draft.

**Which text this is.** The chapter in Menary's volume, in a draft written
for it ("Clark does not refer to this argument in this volume", n. 4). It
replies to a manuscript of Clark's "Memento's Revenge: The Extended Mind,
Extended", which the volume prints as the chapter before it (pp. 43–66,
DOI-10.7551/mitpress/9780262014038.003.0003, per Crossref; not filed here).
The draft is not a reply to Clark 2005 ([LIT-403](LIT-403.md)), though the two
Clark texts share material: [LIT-403](LIT-403.md) imagines "the partially overlapping
circles of a certain Venn diagram" and Martians who store bit-mapped text,
and the manuscript quoted here has both. But the draft's only Clark
reference of the period is "Memento's Revenge" (forthcoming), and it quotes
from it three things [LIT-403](LIT-403.md) does not contain: the three coupling conditions
(reliably available, automatically endorsed, easily accessible), a passage
on complexity theory and power laws, and the Euler-circle sentence in
different words ("imaging a set of Venn Diagrams/ Euler Circles in our
mind's eye"). Its
reference list cites "Defending Non-Derived Content" as under review; that
paper appeared in *Philosophical Psychology* 18(6) in December 2005
(DOI-10.1080/09515080500355186, per Crossref), so the draft is earlier than
that.

**The "coupling-constitution fallacy".** This is where the record first
meets the name. [LIT-438](LIT-438.md) (2001) argues the point, with the
thermostat's bimetallic strip, but does not name it, and its status note
says the name "comes from the authors' later work". This text is that later
work, or the earliest piece of it the record holds: it introduces the name
as something "we might call" the fallacy and defers "a less quick and dirty
treatment" to a paper then "in progress". [NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md) and Chalmers's 2019
chapter ([LIT-415](LIT-415.md)) attribute the name to the authors' 2008 book. The book is
the earlier *print* appearance (the chapter came out in 2010), but this
draft shows the name in circulation before the end of 2005. Whether any
earlier published text uses it is not settled here.

**How it bears on [LIT-097](LIT-097.md) and [LIT-403](LIT-403.md).** It is the second round of the
exchange that [LIT-438](LIT-438.md) opened and [LIT-403](LIT-403.md) answered. Against [LIT-403](LIT-403.md)'s
Venn-diagram argument it gives the record's one direct reply: the
conventional content belongs to the diagram on paper, and nothing Clark
says shows the mental image gets its content the same way. It does not
answer [LIT-403](LIT-403.md)'s other moves (silicon could carry intrinsic content; it
would not matter). Its new charge against [LIT-097](LIT-097.md) is the one [NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md) found
unargued in the 1998 paper: the step from coupling to parthood.

**Mereology.** The fallacy is a claim about parthood: being coupled to Y
does not make X a part of Y, for objects or for processes. That is why
`mereology` is tagged. The text gives no criterion of parthood in return;
it relocates the question to the nature of X ("The nature of X, of
course").

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.
