# NOTE-070: Fridman, the Russian perspective on information warfare

## Contribution

Over the preceding decade, "information warfare" had become a heavily politicised term in Russian domestic and foreign affairs. The article traces its conceptual roots in Russian academic, political and public discourse, and identifies the main actors who promoted narratives of a Western information war against Russia. The author argues that, given Russia's bid to reassert itself as a global power and Western anxiety about Russian information activities, understanding these narratives and how they were politicised is essential.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- p. 62: Russian literature on information warfare is split into two groups. One treats it as neutral, dual-use techniques of influence ("just like nuclear energy"). The other is an ideological strand that sees it as a technique the West uses to undermine Russia.
- pp. 63–70, Messner: the life of the émigré White Army officer, and his "subversion-war" (myatezhevoyna) theory that future war is fought for "the souls" of the enemy population, with "propaganda by word" and "propaganda by deed". His ideas went unknown in the USSR and were revived after 1991 and reinterpreted as the West's Cold War method.
- pp. 70–73, Dugin: a Eurasianist civilisational frame, in which US "net-centric war" seeks total informational control. His prescription is a symmetric "Eurasian network" of officials, special services, intellectuals and "patriotically-oriented journalists".
- pp. 73–76, Panarin: politics as an "informational struggle" over elite and mass minds. In his view the sea powers (Britain, then the US) mastered information warfare and used it to bring down the Russian Empire (1917) and the USSR (1991).
- pp. 76–79, politicisation: the narratives are echoed in Kremlin rhetoric, the 2015 National Security Strategy, the Information Security Doctrine and media and NGO laws (e.g. the 2014 Federal Law No. 305-FZ). Levada Center polling from December 2014 is cited: 87% saw the West as hostile, and 42% said the hostility took the form of information war.
- pp. 80–81, conclusion: scholars, government and public reinforce one another. Following Tulchinsky, the author treats the politicisation of "information war" as itself a form of information war, with the truth "somewhere in the middle" between top-down manipulation and bottom-up demand.

## Open questions

- The survey pairs it with the held *Russian Hybrid Warfare*, which appears to be Fridman's own book: a Crossref search returned a chapter of *Russian "Hybrid Warfare"* by Fridman, DOI 10.1093/oso/9780190877378.003.0007, online 2019-02-21. This article reads as a precursor to that book's argument, and a deeper reading should check how much it overlaps before both are filed.
- The sources are mostly Russian-language, and the argument is interpretive intellectual history. The citation of Western analysts (p. 80, n. 78) is where the author positions himself against claims that the Kremlin simply orchestrates the narrative.
- It complements c02 (the EEAS view of FIMI) by giving the Russian doctrinal framing of the same phenomenon.
