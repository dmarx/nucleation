# THEORY-tmpe97rb: Rubin and Hewstone report, from a count of experiments on social identity theory's self-esteem hypothesis, that intergroup discrimination tends to raise self-esteem but is not in general motivated by low self-esteem, so the hypothesis is not supported in its full and unqualified form, and that most tests measured global personal self-esteem where the theory implies specific social state self-esteem

## The account

The theory says members discriminate "as ingroup members, rather than
as individuals" (pp. 5–6), so testing it with personal self-esteem
"would be to recast social identity theory as an individualistic theory
of intergroup behavior" (p. 6). Most tests did. Counted, the evidence
says "intergroup discrimination leads to an increase in self-esteem but
is not motivated by a need for self-esteem" (p. 22). Nine studies find
high personal self-esteem with the most discrimination. The authors
propose a narrower version in specific social state self-esteem, and
warn that identification and esteem measures, and some bias and esteem
measures, overlap (pp. 23–24).

## Evidence

A vote count without effect sizes ([NOTE-1278](../notes.d/NOTE-1278.md)); only one of eight full
tests supports both corollaries. Some esteem gains only restore what
categorization lowered.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-300](../questions.d/QUESTION-300.md) asks whether apparently selfless action is "just
self-interest on an enlarged self". The self-esteem hypothesis is social
identity theory's version of that claim for group-serving action. The
review finds the benefit to the self more often than the motive, and
finds the group-level motive nearly untested.
