# CASE-tmp15jvz: Joking relationships: one kind among the Crow and in Africa with no route between them, descended between Crow and Hidatsa, and a transported tale beside them

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for: a documented case for
the three relations [CLAIM-tmp7qr7y](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp7qr7y.md) separates. Two instances share a kind with
no transport between them. A pair shares it by descent. A rendering shares it
by transport. The case is partial on independence and on recognition.

## The case

**The kind.** Radcliffe-Brown, "On Joking Relationships" (1940), as reprinted
in *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952), p. 90: "What is meant
by the term 'joking relationship' is a relation between two persons in which
one is by custom permitted, and in some instances required, to tease or make
fun of the other, who in turn is required to take no offence." He separates
"symmetrical" and "asymmetrical" varieties on the same page, and calls such
relationships "widespread, not only in Africa but also in Asia, Oceania and
North America".

**Instances with no route between them.**

- **Crow.** Lowie, *Social Life of the Crow Indians* (1912), p. 204: the
  *i'watkucé*, the child of a man of one's father's clan, "might twit him with
  it and publicly disgrace him, yet such action must not be resented." The
  twits are recorded verbatim: to a man long with one wife, "You are next to a
  dead thing"; to a woman, "you are exceedingly lazy, you never do beadwork or
  make moccasins for your husband" (pp. 204–205). Radcliffe-Brown (1949,
  reprinted 1952, p. 109) puts the Crow in his class: with such men "he has a
  joking relationship; he may make offensive remarks to them or receive such
  from them without offence."
- **Dogon.** Radcliffe-Brown (1952, p. 91), citing Paulme: "among the Dogon a
  man stands in a joking relationship to his wife's sisters and their
  daughters."
- **Thonga.** Radcliffe-Brown (1952, pp. 97–98), citing Junod: the sister's
  son's "privileged disrespect" to the mother's brother; "The uterine nephew
  is a chief! He takes any liberty he likes with his maternal uncle".

The positions differ: father's clansmen's children among the Crow, wife's
sisters among the Dogon, mother's brother among the Thonga. The relation is
the one Radcliffe-Brown defines: licensed disrespect, with offence not to be
taken. That is [TERM-040](../terms.d/TERM-040.md)'s split of one kind with different positions. The Crow
recorded twits have the shape of [CASE-018](CASE-018.md)'s sentence: a public remark on the
hearer's conduct that the hearer must not resent.

**A descended pair.** Lowie, *Notes on the Social Organization and Customs of
the Mandan, Hidatsa, and Crow Indians* (1917), p. 42: "As among the Crow,
individuals whose fathers belonged to the same clan were 'joking-relatives'
(maku'tsatsi)", and "it was not the function of his fellow-clansmen but of his
maku'tsati to reprove him or make fun of him." On the clan system on which the
relation is defined, he concludes (pp. 90–91): "There can be no doubt that we
are dealing with a unique clan concept developed by the parent tribe", and he
calls independent origin of the shared kin terms "absurd in the light of known
tribal relations". On another shared custom, teknonymy, he leaves the question
open on the page (p. 92): "Does the Crow-Hidatsa custom represent a feature
independently evolved by the parent tribe or … borrowed by Hidatsa and Crow
from different directions since their separation?" So descent against
independent evolution is a question the source itself asks of Crow–Hidatsa
customs, and answers for the clan concept. He does not say that the joking
relation in particular was inherited. He also records it among the Mandan,
neighbours of long contact (p. 15: "As among the Hidatsa and Crow, there was a
joking-relationship"), so spread between neighbours is possible too.

**A transport.** Tehrani, "The Phylogeny of Little Red Riding Hood" (2013):
"an Ibo tale, which grouped with European variants of Little Red Riding Hood,
thus endorsing the collector's belief that the story is not of local origin,
but an Ibo oral translation of the western fairy tale." The same paper tests
convergence elsewhere and rejects it for the East Asian tales: "it seems
unlikely that they could have evolved independently."

## What it can show

The three relations of [CLAIM-tmp7qr7y](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp7qr7y.md) on documented material.

- **Kind-sharing without transport.** The Crow joking-relative and Dogon or
  Thonga joking are one kind on a structural definition, with no route by which
  one could be a rendering of the other. Neither has fidelity to the other: not
  high, not low, none.
- **Kind-sharing by descent of an institution.** Crow and Hidatsa share it
  through a parent tribe. This is [CLAIM-002](../claims.d/CLAIM-002.md)'s continuity of transmission, the
  identity of an institution across its branches. It is not a transport of any
  token, so it shows that descent and transport are also different facts.
- **A transport.** The Ibo tale is a rendering of a source, with a phylogeny
  that places it.

It supports the claim's point that descent is not similarity. Lowie treats
"independently evolved" against "borrowed" as an empirical question that a
shared custom alone cannot settle, and he settles it for the clan concept on
other evidence, from tribal history.

## What it cannot show

**The condition met only partly: documented independence.** The Crow and the
Dogon or Thonga had no contact before 1492, and there is no historical route
by which the institution could pass between them. But no source states their
independence. Radcliffe-Brown's explanation is structural, that the same "kind
of structural situation" (p. 91) yields the custom. That is a theory of
convergence, but he does not argue against diffusion, and he declines
historical conjecture: "conjecture gives us neither scientific nor historical
knowledge" (p. 104).

**The second condition met only partly: recognition of the kind.** The kind is
an analyst's. The insiders' categories (*i'watkucé*, *maku'tsati*, the Dogon
*mangou*) are local. Griaule, on the Dogon–Bozo exchange of insults, rejects
the comparative class: "Nous adoptons, vis-à-vis travaux parus sur cette
question, une attitude négative" (quoted by Radcliffe-Brown, 1952, p. 105).
There is no evidence that a Crow would recognise a Dogon joking exchange as
the same thing. That bears on [CLAIM-tmpawn12](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpawn12.md), which takes competent
participants' uptake as the standard.

**The strongest reply.** A defender of reading fidelity off kind-sharing says
that analysts' kinds are cheap: put the Crow and the Dogon in one class and
of course nothing transports between them, because the class was drawn after
the fact. The reply has force against condition (c). It does not reach the
claim, which needs only that kind-sharing and transport can come apart, and
the Crow–Hidatsa and Ibo halves show transport and descent where the kind is
also shared.

**Judged fidelity.** No one asks whether Thonga joking is a "rendering" of
Crow joking. So the case leaves [CLAIM-tmp7qr7y](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp7qr7y.md)'s empirical residue untouched:
do judges track descent? That is [CLAIM-137](../claims.d/CLAIM-137.md)'s second defeat disjunct.

**What would close the gap.** A judgement test on these texts. Present raters
with a Crow twit from Lowie, a Hidatsa reproof, and a constructed English
"rendering" of the Crow twit. Ask which pairs are "the same kind of remark" and
which is "a version of" which, telling one group the provenance and not the
other. If kind judgements do not move with provenance and version judgements
do, judges keep the two relations apart, as the claim says the thesis does.
The items could serve [CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md).
