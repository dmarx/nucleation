# LIT-tmp3t040: Making It Explicit: Reasoning, Representing, and Discursive Commitment

Robert B. Brandom (1994), Cambridge, Mass.: Harvard University Press,
xxv + 741 pp. — https://lccn.loc.gov/93050631

## Key takeaways

From the parts read (the Preface and chapter 3, pp. 141–157 and
172–193):

- **Assertion as a doing.** Asserting authorizes further assertions:
  one's own inferential consequences, and the audience's reassertions.
  It also undertakes a conditional task-responsibility to show
  entitlement to the claim if it is appropriately challenged. Entitlement
  is inherited in two ways: intrapersonally and across contents by
  justification, and interpersonally within one content by deferral to
  the asserter ("the buck is passed to A") (§III.3).
- **Default and challenge.** Many claims are treated as entitled until
  appropriately challenged, which stops the regress. A challenge is
  itself an assertion of an incompatible claim, and its entitlement is
  assessed in the same way. The sanction for an unentitled claim is
  internal: the claim loses its authority to entitle anyone to its
  consequences (§III.4–5).
- **Deontic scorekeeping.** Adapting Lewis's "Scorekeeping in a language
  game", the score is a constellation of commitments and entitlements.
  It is doubly perspectival: scores are kept for each interlocutor and
  by each interlocutor. A speech act's significance is how it changes
  the scores. Its content is its role in commitment-preserving
  inferences, entitlement-preserving inferences and incompatibilities,
  from which that change follows "according to a uniform pattern"
  (Dummett) (§IV.1–4).
- **Force and content.** Deferrals, disavowals, queries and challenges
  attach different force to the same assertible contents (§IV.5).
  Orders and promises are parasitic on assertion: "Asserting is the
  fundamental speech act" (§III.3).

**What a full reading should check.** Chapter 4's practical commitments
(intentions), which would bear on non-assertive speech acts. Chapter 8's
de re ascription and the "social-perspectival character of conceptual
contents", which is where communication between scorekeepers with
different commitments is treated, and so where any bearing on
translation would lie.
