# NOTE-tmp1mo2x: Flayelle et al. — A taxonomy of technology design features that promote potentially addictive online behaviours

## Contribution

A review across six online domains (video gaming, gambling, cybersexual
activities, shopping, social networking, on-demand streaming) of the
features said to promote uncontrolled use. It then classifies them by the
learning process they act on, drawing on model-free and model-based
reinforcement learning, so that features in different domains can be
compared by mechanism (Table 2, p. 8).

## Key insight

A design feature can weaken control by working on cached values, which
makes cues pull and habits form. Or it can work on the user's own
computation of costs and benefits, inflating the activity's value,
hiding its costs, cutting deliberation short, or never letting a goal be
fully reached. Providers need no theory of either: "massive deployment
allows learning by trial and error" (p. 6).

## Assumptions

- **Problematic use is defined clinically.** Loss of control and continued
  use despite harm, after ICD-11's gaming disorder: "impaired control;
  increasing priority given to gaming over other life interests and daily
  activities; and continuation or escalation of gaming despite the
  occurrence of negative consequences" (p. 2).
- **Model-free and model-based control run together.** They "are not
  mutually exclusive, but can operate in parallel" (p. 6), unlike
  automatic-against-willed dual-system models.
- **Engagement is always chosen.** "we posit that engaging in a
  potentially problematic online activity is always a choice" (p. 10).
  It is model-free or model-based, not compelled.
- **Industry motives are taken as given.** Such systems "clearly point to
  economic motives for exploiting these features" (p. 6). This is
  supported by citation, not by any analysis of firms.

## Key results

By section. Nothing here is a new study.

- **Domain reviews (pp. 2–6).**
  - *Gaming.* Variable and fixed ratio schedules, avatars and owned
    virtual goods, loot boxes and "predatory" monetization (p. 3).
  - *Gambling.* Time-limited inducements, constant availability of sports
    betting and live betting (p. 4).
  - *Cybersex.* Algorithmic recommendation and personalized notifications
    (pp. 4–5).
  - *Shopping.* Countdown timers "(which often provide false
    information)", sell counts and scarcity alerts (p. 5).
  - *Social networking.* Infinite scrolling, interest-matched feeds,
    likes, the "double tick", ephemeral stories, push notifications
    (p. 5).
  - *Streaming.* Autoplay, "generally reported as the biggest challenge to
    self-control in binge-watching", together with smart download and
    skipping of recaps and credits (p. 5).

  Gaming research is "limited by its reliance on cross-sectional data"
  (p. 4); for cybersex "empirical research is scarce and supporting
  evidence is mainly indirect" (p. 5).
- **Convergence (p. 6).** Domains interpenetrate, with loot boxes in games
  and shopping in social feeds. Behavioural tracking is proposed as the
  common feature: it "allows service providers to maintain users within
  ‘online interactive loops’ to maximize data collection and refine these
  profiling analyses".
- **Provider-side selection (p. 6).** "Online product development requires
  little knowledge of underlying psychological processes because massive
  deployment allows learning by trial and error: design features that
  increase the frequency of online behaviour, time-on-device or spending
  (and therefore revenue) will be maintained, whereas those that do not
  will be abandoned". Users and researchers, meanwhile, lack "an
  understanding of how it enables the manipulation of their behaviour in a
  progressively personalized manner".
- **Model-free features (pp. 6–7).** Habit and incentive sensitization are
  the model-free routes. "Incentive sensitization (intense cue-driven
  ‘wanting’) is hypothesized to be caused by the repeated overstimulation
  of mesolimbic and mesocortical dopaminergic pathways", by drugs "as well
  as other reinforcers when their delivery is highly unpredictable"
  (p. 6). Random-ratio schedules make uncertainty irreducible and are
  found in gambling and, the authors say, in games and social media
  "where non-monetary rewards such as ‘likes’, ‘retweets’ or notifications
  are unpredictable" (p. 6). But "we are unaware of any formal analysis of
  design features related to reinforcement schedules in domains other than
  gambling" (p. 7). Personalized notifications and ads are
  "individualized or personalized cues that might generate even more
  craving" (p. 7).
- **Model-based features (pp. 7–10).**
  - *Overvaluation.* Immersion, social reward and fear of missing out
    (FOMO).
  - *Interference with deliberation.* Autoplay, countdowns and cash-out
    offers "interfere with the deliberative and controlled processes
    needed to overcome present-oriented decision-making" (p. 8).
  - *Partial goal fulfilment.* "Features that reflect moving goalposts
    are ubiquitous in video gaming, social networking and on-demand TV
    streaming, and they keep individuals engaged for longer than planned
    after initiating the activity" (p. 8), because "online environments
    often allow only partial satisfaction" (p. 8). Infinite scrolling
    "reveals some desired information, but also offers the never-ending
    promise of new rewards" (pp. 8–9).
  - *Cognitive biases.* Stop buttons foster an illusion of control.
    "Losses disguised as wins" and near-misses "contribute to the false
    belief that one is progressing or learning something valuable about
    the game, or artificially reduce perceived losses" (p. 9).
- **The synthesis (p. 10).** "manipulations that facilitate model-free
  control have the potential to turn an instrumental behaviour into an
  addictive one". Equally, an activity can become problematic "simply
  because their consequences are computed to be more valuable than
  consequences of abstaining", where "technology enables online activities
  to be adapted to match personal motivations, which might exploit
  vulnerabilities".
- **Box 1 (p. 9).** Prevention through self-control strategies, built on
  habits rather than resisting temptation. It also proposes regulation,
  including "legislation that prohibits web-based applications and
  interfaces from exploiting nudge techniques".
- **A caveat against its own field (p. 10).** Applying substance-use
  models to online behaviour "is highly reductionistic and tends to
  pathologize common behaviours".

## Concepts

- **technology design features**: structural characteristics of a site or
  application, as against general features of the internet such as
  accessibility, affordability and anonymity (the Triple A model).
- **model-free / model-based control**: action driven by values cached from
  past reinforcement, against action driven by a model of consequences
  and a comparison of expected utilities.
- **random-ratio schedule**: reward for each response with a probability
  that does not depend on history, so its uncertainty cannot be reduced by
  learning.
- **partial goal fulfilment**: goal attainment that reveals a further goal
  (levels, infinite scroll, cliffhangers, points).
- **dark nudges**: design that steers choice against the user's interest.
- **informational asymmetry**: providers know users' behaviour, and users
  and researchers do not know how that knowledge is used.

## Connections

It rests on the incentive-sensitization theory ([LIT-tmpotzm0](../literature.d/LIT-tmpotzm0.md),
[THEORY-tmp7r9j4](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp7r9j4.md)), which it cites as one model-free route, and on the
model-based and model-free framework of Daw, Niv and Dayan ([LIT-580](../literature.d/LIT-580.md)). Its
provider-side selection is the user-facing half of what Doctorow calls
twiddling ([LIT-033](../literature.d/LIT-033.md), [NOTE-041](NOTE-041.md)): back-end adjustment of "recommendation
criteria" and ranking at low cost and high frequency. Doctorow argues from
the firm's incentives, these authors from users' psychology, and neither
studies a firm.

## Bearing on the record

**[QUESTION-tmprsrxs](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmprsrxs.md): designed proxy-keyed ascendancy in users. Yes, as
description, and with a sharper map than the question has.** Each of the
paper's model-based families acts on a different part of [TERM-tmp8gbrt](../terms.d/TERM-tmp8gbrt.md)'s
regime, and the model-free family on its strength. The mapping is the
record's, not the paper's.
- **Strength of a cue-keyed ranking (model-free).** Random-ratio rewards
  and personalized triggers raise the pull of the service's cues. Read
  with [THEORY-tmp7r9j4](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp7r9j4.md), this re-keys a ranking's strength to cues, the
  personal case the question names. The paper itself flags this route as
  the least shown outside gambling (p. 7).
- **The ranks themselves (overvaluation).** Immersion and social reward
  raise where the activity sits in the ordering.
- **The reference course (partial goal fulfilment).** Moving goalposts move
  the reference course along the ascendant ordering. On the sign clause
  the user is kept just behind course, which keeps the ordering engaged
  without letting it settle at neutral. That fits [CLAIM-tmpz8lli](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpz8lli.md)'s
  Carver-and-Scheier reading of sign as progress against a reference
  rate. It would also predict that these features yield mildly negative
  goal-conduciveness valence, not positive, during use. That is testable,
  and not tested here.
- **The system's reading of its own progress (cognitive biases).** Near-
  misses and losses disguised as wins "contribute to the false belief that
  one is progressing". On [CLAIM-tmptd5us](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmptd5us.md) this falsifies the system's model
  of where it stands along the ascendant ordering, which is mis-felt
  valence, not unfelt. It is the user-side counterpart of [CASE-151](../cases.d/CASE-151.md)'s board
  receiving a model of the bank's ascendancy that misrepresented it.
- **The negotiation itself (interference with deliberation).** Autoplay and
  countdowns do not outweigh the other perspectives. They pre-empt them:
  the next episode starts before any perspective that would stop it is
  consulted. Ascendancy is then won by default, not in conflict. This
  bears on [QUESTION-tmpsqwu5](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpsqwu5.md). A conflict record will show no conflict
  where a feature has removed the occasion for one, so the measurement
  must count pre-empted decisions as well as decided ones.

  The question's criterion is still owed. The paper does not say when an
  ordering is "by their bearing on its maintenance" and when it is a proxy. Its
  clinical marks, "increasing priority" and "continuation or escalation
  ... despite the occurrence of negative consequences", are priority and
  persistence against harm, which is the question's proxy-ascendancy
  described from outside. They are not a criterion.

**The organizational side: a firm's metric installing ascendancy in users.**
"design features that increase the frequency of online behaviour,
time-on-device or spending (and therefore revenue) will be maintained,
whereas those that do not will be abandoned" (p. 6). Read with the
record's account, this is two proxy-keyed ascendancies coupled. The
firm's ascendant ordering, ranked by engagement and revenue, a proxy for
its viability, selects features. The selected features make a cue-keyed
ranking ascendant in users, a proxy for their good.
- **[CLAIM-432](../claims.d/CLAIM-432.md).** The coupling fits threat without remorse. The harm to users
  need not be represented anywhere in the firm, since selection by the
  metric needs "little knowledge of underlying psychological processes".
  So the remorse that is functionally absent is a concern absent from the
  ordering, not one overridden. The paper's call for regulation is the
  claim's remedy, a redesign of the firm's concerns from outside, though
  the paper does not put it that way.
- **[NOTE-041](NOTE-041.md).** Doctorow's twiddling is the firm-side mechanism and this is
  its user-side effect. Doctorow's third stage, in which value is drawn
  from users, would be the firm's metric prevailing over the user-facing
  concerns that held it in the first stage. That connection is the
  record's.
- **[CASE-151](../cases.d/CASE-151.md)'s parallel is partial.** There, signals of harm stayed inside
  the firm, held by people who could not act. Here the asymmetry is
  between the firm and its users and researchers, and the firm holds the
  signals.

**A case worth filing, from another source.** This paper documents no
particular firm, so it cannot ground a documented CASE. A specimen of a
platform whose own records show features kept for their effect on
engagement against internal signals of harm to users would test the
coupling directly. Public candidates are the internal research disclosed
from Meta in 2021 and the complaints state attorneys general later filed
against platforms over youth design features. Neither was read here, and
both are allegations or leaks rather than findings, so the source and its
standing would need to be settled first.

**Not an instruction for machine-learning practice.** Recommendation
algorithms appear as features whose effects on users are reviewed, not as
methods.

## Limitations

- A narrative review. Most of the evidence is cross-sectional, qualitative
  or indirect, and the authors say so domain by domain.
- The taxonomy is theoretical. No feature is shown to act on the process
  it is filed under, and some features appear under both headings.
  Infinite scrolling, for example, is a reinforcement schedule for
  streaming but partial goal fulfilment for social networking (Table 2).
- The claim that unpredictable non-drug rewards produce sensitization
  rests on cited animal and laboratory work not analysed here, and the
  authors know of no formal analysis outside gambling.
- Provider motives are asserted from citations. No firm's practice is
  examined.

## Open questions

- Do moving-goalpost features produce negative or positive momentary
  affect during use, measured apart from choice? The record's sign clause
  predicts negative.
- Can pre-empted decisions (autoplay) be distinguished from decisions won
  by the ascendant ranking in a conflict record, as [QUESTION-tmpsqwu5](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpsqwu5.md)
  would need?
- Is there a documented case of a firm keeping a feature for its
  engagement effect against its own evidence of harm?
