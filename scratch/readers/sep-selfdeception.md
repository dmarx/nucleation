### Summary
A survey. Accounts split into intentionalist ones (model self-deception on
interpersonal deception; escape the static and dynamic paradoxes by temporal
or psychological partition, §2) and revisionist ones, which revise the
intention requirement (non-intentionalist / deflationary, Mele chief, §3.1)
or the belief requirement (proxies for belief, second-order belief,
in-between belief, shifting degrees, §3.2). Then twisted self-deception
(§4), responsibility and morality (§5), adaptation vs spandrel (§6), and
collective self-deception (§7). The author gives no verdict, but his framing
leans to the view that deflationary accounts are viable for garden-variety
cases and can keep responsibility.

### Contribution
A map of the field with the deflationary account's objections (wishful
thinking, selectivity, tension, self-knowledge, agency) and its replies set
side by side; an affective turn (Galeotti, Lauria) flagged as where theory
is going.

### Key results
1. Minimal definition (preamble): self-deception involves a person who "as a
   consequence of some motivation or emotion, seems to acquire and maintain
   some false belief despite evidence to the contrary".
2. §1: the two camps: "those that maintain that the paradigmatic cases of
   self-deception are intentional and those that deny this".
3. §3.1: deflationists treat it "as a species of motivationally biased
   belief" (§3 intro); to be deceived is "nothing more than believing
   falsely" (Johnston 1988; Mele 2001), so Sam deceives Joan "albeit
   unintentionally".
4. §3.1, motivation, not accident: Allison's belief "fulfills her desire,
   quells her fear, or reduces her anxiety, and it's this function—not an
   intention—that explains why her belief formation process is biased"; "not
   an innocent mistake but a consequence of her motivational states".
5. §3.1, Mele's jointly sufficient conditions: S "treats data relevant ... to
   the truth value of p in a motivationally biased way"; plus (2012) S's
   belief is a product of "reflective, critical reasoning," and S "is wrong in
   regarding that reasoning as properly directed".
6. §3.1, selectivity by error cost (Friedrich 1993; Trope and Lieberman 1996, as the entry spells it):
   "the minimization of costly errors is the central principle guiding
   hypothesis testing"; Josh: "no other chocolate gives him the same
   pleasure". Bermúdez's complaint stands: "self-deception needn't follow".
7. §3.1 affect: "affect plays a key role in triggering the conditions under
   which motivation leads to self-deception"; "dopamine regulation and
   negative somatic markers" (Lauria et al. 2016) as empirical support.
8. §3.1 tension / deep conflict: critics say deflationary self-deceivers are
   "self-delusion" cases; defenders reply deep-conflict cases are "escapism"
   (Longeway; Lynch 2012).
9. §3.1 agency: Lynch's "agentism": intentions like "find any p-supporting
   evidence", held "with a view of determining whether p is true".
10. §4 twisted: "a belief that is neither warranted nor wanted"; motives
   offered: anxiety reduction (Barnes), error cost (Mele), desire to believe
   (Nelkin 2002: "the self-deceptive belief is motivated by a desire to
   believe that p"); Galeotti: such a desire "seems absurd even at an
   unconscious level".
11. §5.2 remedies: "the cultivation of one's epistemic virtues and the
   cultivation of one's epistemic community (Galeotti 2016b)".
12. §6: "Just because 'positive illusions' make us 'feel good,' of course, it
   does not follow that they are adaptive"; von Hippel and Trivers: evolved
   to deceive others; Van Leeuwen: a "spandrel".
13. §7.1: social self-deception when "others are a means used in each
   individual's self-deception" (Dings 2017); "The collective entrenches
   self-deceptive beliefs by providing positive reinforcement".

### Assumptions
- Folk-psychological categories (belief, desire, intention) frame the
  debate; §3.2 notes they may be too coarse.
- Motivation is synchronic: desire, fear or error cost biases a given
  episode of belief formation. Nothing in the entry models self-deception as
  learned or shaped by repeated reward over time ("episodic or habitual" is
  raised only as a moral consideration, §5.2).
- The motive is directed at the content (p's being true) except on Nelkin's
  desire-to-believe view.

### Verdict on the LIT summary
Accurate in substance, narrow in frame. The entry does put "can a mind
deceive itself without intending to" at its centre (§1, §3.1), and
deflationary accounts do say yes. But: (a) deflationism is one of two
revisionist branches; the other (revision of belief, §3.2) is about the
product, not the process, and is not mentioned; (b) the entry insists the
non-intentional case is still motivated, not an innocent error, which is
the feature that matters for the record's use (see bearing below); (c) the
entry does not settle the question. Metadata: `published: 2006-10-17`
confirmed; add the substantive revision of 2023-03-13; `first_author`
Deweese-Boyd confirmed (the entry also writes DeWeese-Boyd).

### Bearing on the codes
- CLAIM-tmpu6fff (counter): QUALIFIES. Deflationism removes the intending
  deceiver but not the selecting process: the bias has a "function—not an
  intention" (§3.1) and is tuned by error costs. That is a third option
  between the counter's two (a cue misfiring vs an optimizer). The entry
  also supplies the counter's best line: a false belief from no motivation
  is "some sort of innocent error" (§3.1), not self-deception; so a false
  insight from fatigue or a drug with no motive is error on the entry's
  terms, as the counter says. Silent on insight itself.
- CLAIM-tmpcv6i6 (thesis): QUALIFIES. The inside mechanism the entry
  describes is a second standard (what is welcome or costly to get wrong)
  biasing the evaluator, not a process learning the evaluator's cheap
  signal; nothing about the evaluator's bound or a capacity ratio.
  Nearest match: Mele's 2012 condition that S is "wrong in regarding that
  reasoning as properly directed" (§3.1), the self-check passing biased
  reasoning. Supports two of its three remedies (§5.2: epistemic virtues =
  raise capacity; epistemic community = an evaluator not selected against).
  §7.1 (Dings) fits its chatbot meeting point: others "are a means used in
  each individual's self-deception", and seeking like-minded company is
  the social version. Twisted self-deception (§4) is a hard case for any
  reading where what is selected is a pleasing signal.
- CLAIM-tmpu6c7h (alternative): QUALIFIES, the best fit of the three but
  not favoured by the entry. Its case-by-case line matches the entry's line
  between motivated self-deception and innocent error (§3.1), and its
  "seeking" matches agentism ("find any p-supporting evidence", §3.1) and
  Dings's seeking out like-minded people (§7.1, with "positive
  reinforcement"). But the comfort that selects belief in the entry is the
  comfort of p's being true (Allison, Josh), or anxiety reduction (Barnes),
  not a feeling of insight; the nearest to seeking a felt state is Nelkin's
  desire to believe (§4), which Galeotti calls near-incoherent. No account
  in the entry makes reward-learning over repetitions the mechanism, so
  the claim's "frequency rises with reinforcement" test gets no support
  here. Lauria et al.'s dopamine evidence (§3.1, §4) is cited, not shown.
- Which is favoured: none; the entry never discusses insight, feelings of
  rightness, or selection over time. If forced, CLAIM-tmpu6c7h, on §3.1's
  motivated/innocent line plus §7.1.
- Q1 aside (deception without intent): §3.1 non-intentionalists take
  interpersonal deception itself to need no intent (Sam and Joan), and Smith
  2014 models non-intentionalism on "the purposive mechanisms of deception
  operating in non-human organisms". Bears on LIT-tmpjnbi0's question.

### Limitations
- A survey: positions reported, few adjudicated; the author's own view
  (DeWeese-Boyd 2007, on control) shows only in §5.1.
- Selectivity is left open (§3.1): error cost explains some cases, and
  "self-deception needn't follow".
- Empirical support (Bargh et al. on unconscious goal pursuit, Lauria et al.
  on dopamine; Lamba and Nityananda) is cited, not assessed.
- Pleasure, comfort and reward appear only as examples and as one strand
  (anxiety reduction); no account of how a mind comes to self-deceive more
  over time, which is the record's live question.
- §7.2's NASA case rests on Trivers 2000's interpretation.
