# CASE-tmpabax8: Simplified texts for restricted and for full readers: a gain and a loss documented on different texts, documented nulls and a reversal, and a design that tests both on the same renderings

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for: a rendering made from
the source alone that measurably helps a named restricted receiver and
measurably loses information for a full-access one, for [CLAIM-tmp0jq5k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp0jq5k.md).
No single study was found that measures both on the same renderings. This
case is therefore **stipulated**: a constructed design, whose two halves
and whose nulls rest on documented studies, each cited below.

## The case

**What is documented.** Text simplification, by hand or by algorithm,
makes a rendering from the source text alone, without the situation the
text is about. In [CLAIM-tmp0jq5k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp0jq5k.md)'s terms it is a garbling.

- *A gain for named restricted readers.* A. Cerga-Pashoja, J. Gaete,
  A. Shishkova and V. Jordanova, *Frontiers in Psychiatry* 10:546, 2019,
  doi:10.3389/fpsyt.2019.00546: 243 adolescents and adults with
  high-functioning autism, in a cross-over design, read texts simplified
  with the OpenBook tool and the originals; "both adults and adolescents
  with ASD gave more correct answers for the simplified" texts (mean 11.2
  against 10.0, p < .001; the Bulgarian adolescents' gain, 7.4 against
  6.8, was not significant). There was no neurotypical arm.
  L. Javourey-Drevet, S. Dufau, T. François, N. Gala, J. Ginestié and
  J. C. Ziegler, *Applied Psycholinguistics* 43(2):485–512, 2022,
  doi:10.1017/S014271642100062X: 149 French second-graders read 20 texts
  in normal and hand-simplified versions; comprehension was "significantly
  better for simplified than for normal texts", and gains "were greater
  for participants with weaker reading performance" (scientific texts
  only; p = .051 for the interaction). The authors concede that "some
  semantic nuances might be lost" but did not measure it: the same
  questions served both versions. G. Leroy et al., *Journal of Medical
  Internet Research* 15(7):e144, 2013, doi:10.2196/jmir.2569: 99 lay
  readers answered correctly on 63% of items for an algorithmically
  simplified health text against 52% for the original (p < .001).
- *A loss for full readers.* S. Agrawal and M. Carpuat, "Do Text
  Simplification Systems Preserve Meaning? A Human Evaluation via Reading
  Comprehension", *TACL* 2024, doi:10.1162/tacl_a_00653,
  arXiv:2312.10126, Table 2: 112 native English readers answered
  reading-comprehension questions at 78.33% on the originals, 77.22% on a
  human "Elementary" simplification, and from 76.11% down to 67.78% on
  system simplifications (20.50% for one system); "over-deletion is the
  key culprit that makes questions unanswerable".
- *Nulls for restricted readers.* A. Szarkowska, I. Krejtz,
  O. Pilipczuk, Ł. Dutka and J.-L. Kruger, *Across Languages and Cultures*
  17(2):183–204, 2016, doi:10.1556/084.2016.17.2.3: with 44 deaf, 33 hard
  of hearing and 60 hearing viewers, "no benefit of editing down the text
  of subtitles, particularly in the case of intralingual subtitling and
  deaf viewers. Verbatim subtitles displayed with the higher presentation
  rate yielded slightly better comprehension results".
- *A reversal.* D. Burnham et al., *Journal of Deaf Studies and Deaf
  Education* 13(3):391–404, 2008, doi:10.1093/deafed/enn003, abstract:
  "more proficient readers show better comprehension than poor readers
  and are better able to benefit from caption rate and, to some extent,
  text reduction modifications". The gain from the garbling went to the
  *less* restricted readers.

**What is stipulated.** The design that would put both halves on one set of
renderings:

1. Take matched passages, each with an original and a simplified version.
   OneStopEnglish, which Agrawal and Carpuat use, already gives each
   article at three levels, so the materials exist.
2. Fix probe items in advance, by annotators who see both versions, in two
   kinds: **P-kept**, answerable from both versions, and **P-lost**,
   answerable from the original only.
3. Draw readers from a named restricted population (for instance L2
   readers at CEFR A2, or readers with autism) and from fluent native
   readers. Each reads one version of each passage.
4. Add probes on the interpersonal relation (stance, politeness) as well
   as content, so the study reaches [CLAIM-087](../claims.d/CLAIM-087.md).

[CLAIM-tmp0jq5k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp0jq5k.md) predicts that restricted readers gain on P-kept items, that
full readers lose on P-lost items, and that no restricted reader of the
simplified version beats full readers of the original on any item.

## What it can show

On its documented halves, both parts of [CLAIM-tmp0jq5k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp0jq5k.md)'s picture, apart.
A garbling raised usable information for restricted receivers whose rules
could not extract it as easily from the source (the autism and grade-2
studies, Leroy). A garbling lost information that full readers could have
used (Agrawal and Carpuat, for system output). Neither contradicts
Blackwell's order ([THEORY-156](../theory.d/THEORY-156.md)): a gain for a bounded receiver is the
claim's gap between what a rendering makes possible and what a receiver
can use ([THEORY-176](../theory.d/THEORY-176.md)'s move for restricted rules).

The nulls show the defeat condition being tested in the field, and
sometimes coming out on the null side. That fits the claim's own hedge:
garblings "help a receiver only when they do work the receiver cannot".

As stipulated, the design is built to test the claim's ceiling directly:
whether any restricted reader does better with the rendering than a full
reader does with the source, on the same items.

## What it cannot show

- **The central condition is met only across studies.** No documented
  study gives one rendering a gain for a restricted reader and a loss for
  a full reader. The gain studies re-use questions answerable from both
  versions, so they cannot see a loss; the loss study has no restricted
  arm. The ceiling is never checked within one study, and the design that
  would check it has not been run.
- **Content, not relation.** Every documented probe is a content question.
  None touches the interpersonal relation that [CLAIM-087](../claims.d/CLAIM-087.md) is about, so the
  case supports [CLAIM-087](../claims.d/CLAIM-087.md)'s improvement only by analogy.
- **The strongest reply.** The full readers' loss may be noise where it
  matters most: 78.33% against 77.22% for the human simplification, a
  point. And the restricted readers' gains may be gains in speed or effort,
  not in information their rules could not extract. A defender of reading
  the access gap as effort, not rule class, would say the documented
  studies cannot tell those apart, and that is right: none controls
  reading time.
- **The reversal.** Burnham's result runs the other way from the pattern
  the claim's gloss suggests: within a restricted population, the better
  readers gained more from the garbling. It does not defeat the claim,
  which says a garbling can help, not that it helps the most restricted
  most. It does show that the gain is not automatic, and it is known here
  only from the abstract.
- **What would close the gap.** The stipulated design above. Its P-kept and
  P-lost items, with the two populations, could serve as items in the
  discriminating study ([CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md)), whose named populations and stated
  tasks it shares. The thresholds, sample sizes and rates are for its
  preregistration and are not fixed here.
