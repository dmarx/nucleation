# LIT-103: Closing the Cartesian Bureau de Change

Keith Frankish (2025), *Adaptive Behavior 34(1), 17–21 (2026 issue; special issue on Walter Veit's A Philosophy for the Science of Animal Consciousness)* — DOI-10.1177/10597123251359198

## Key takeaways

- Veit's Darwinian, function-first account of animal consciousness belongs to the post-Cartesian (illusionist) paradigm. Frankish argues it slips back into Cartesianism by keeping "qualia" talk and by letting hedonic valence motivate "in virtue of its feel", which posits a private "bureau de change" where value is felt. Since the paradigms are incommensurable, he says, irreducible subjectivity must be dropped entirely.

*Seeded from the abstract and a skim, not a reading. What follows is what the work says about itself.*

In *A Philosophy for the Science of Animal Consciousness*, Walter Veit gives a Darwinian framework for consciousness. He separates its dimensions, maps their distribution across species, and traces them to an ancient system of hedonic valence that evolved for action selection in early animals. Frankish places this within a "post-Cartesian" paradigm that treats consciousness as a complex functional state open to ordinary science, as opposed to the Cartesian focus on irreducibly subjective qualia and the hard problem. He detects residual Cartesianism in Veit's use of "qualia" and in the suggestion that valenced states do their work through their "feel", which implies a private "Cartesian bureau de change" where intrinsic value is felt. He urges Veit to be thoroughly functionalist, since the two paradigms are incommensurable.
