# NOTE-826: Turvey — Preliminaries to a theory of action

## Contribution

The essay sets out, in Bernstein's line and largely after Greene (1971),
a set of principles for how coordinated action is produced, and an outline
of how vision enters it. It names the units of action "coordinative
structures", and makes tuning, the setting of parameters of autonomous
lower units, the means by which higher levels control them. It is
explicitly "preliminary and speculative" (p. 2). The empirical content is a
review of Soviet and Western reflex-probe studies showing that the spinal
segmental apparatus is reorganized before a voluntary movement.

## Key insight

The executive cannot know or command the details. There are too many
degrees of freedom, and the same impulse to a muscle gives different
movements in different conditions. So an act is assembled from units that
regulate themselves, and the higher level controls them the way one
controls a spring: not by moving it, but by setting its stiffness and its
rest length, after which the spring finds its own way to equilibrium
whatever the starting point.

## Assumptions

- **Bernstein's problem.** The relation of innervation to movement is
  equivocal, and the controlling system has far fewer degrees of freedom
  than the controlled one (pp. 5–6).
- **A state-like executive.** Coordinated activity "is under the management
  of an 'executive system'" (p. 5), understood as "a set of procedures
  engaging a number of neuroanatomical structures" (p. 14), not one site.
- **Reflexes as a basis.** The set of reflexes is assumed to span all acts,
  as a basis spans a vector space (p. 8, after Easton 1972a).
- **Small systems model large ones.** "We shall proceed on the assumption
  that the regulatory principles for large systems follow very much the
  pattern of small systems" (p. 30).
- **Movement-related evidence only.** "There are no experiments on
  segmental pretuning for acts that depend on the detection of
  environmental properties" (p. 22). The environmental account is
  extrapolated.

## Key results

- **Constancy in action (pp. 2–4).** The letter A can be written with the
  fingers, the whole arm, the teeth or the toes. "A required result can be
  attained by an indefinitely large class of movement patterns", which
  cannot be stored, so A-writing is "creative in the sense that language
  is creative" and rests on abstract, generative procedures.
- **Against the keyboard executive (pp. 5–6).** Three counts.
  - Reciprocal innervation makes instructing muscles singly "arduous and
    wasteful".
  - For one limb segment under one muscle, I·d²α/dt² = F(E, α, dα/dt) +
    G(α), with E the innervation. If E = E(t, α, dα/dt), "the same impulses
    may produce different movements and different impulses the same
    movement". With a hammer, a baton or a can of beer the same rhythmic
    movement needs different impulse sequences, which "cannot maintain
    even a remote correspondence" to the movement (Bernstein).
  - "The number of degrees of freedom of the system controlling action is
    much less than the number of mechanical degrees of freedom of the
    controlled system" (Kots et al. 1971). Gunkel: two hands moving at
    different rhythms modulate each other's amplitude.
- **The coordinative structure (pp. 7–9).** Spinal pattern generators fix
  the form of stepping, and afferent information fits it to the external
  forces, so a "relatively simple executive instruction" sets the whole
  segmental apparatus going. "We shall refer to reflexes and functional
  combinations of reflexes as 'coordinative structures' [a term borrowed
  from Easton (1972a) but used here with greater latitude]." "A closely
  knit functional combination of reflexes performs as a relatively
  autonomous unit; by this assumption, relative autonomy is a fundamental
  property of coordinative structures, whether large or small." Skill
  acquisition is "a problem of reducing the degrees of freedom" (fn. 1):
  in Kots and Syrovegin's wrist–elbow task the joints moved in one of
  "three to seven constant ratios", set by functional, not mechanical,
  links.
- **Action concepts as operators (p. 9).** The elements of an action
  concept are "the operations defined over the set of coordinative
  structures"; an action concept is "analogous to a mathematical operator".
- **Heterarchy (pp. 10–13).** Whether one structure is executive to another
  is not fixed; "either may treat the other as a relatum, or subprocedure,
  depending on the problem". Internal feedback, from spinal centres to
  cerebellum, checks "predictions of how functional groupings of muscles
  (coordinative structures) will behave". An act "evolves as the mapping by
  a heterarchically organized system of an intention onto successively
  larger collections of increasingly smaller and less complex coordinative
  structures".
- **Executive ignorance (pp. 14–15).** After Greene, the executive does not
  know which state a lower level will reach. "The units of information
  that behave deterministically are not functions but equivalence classes
  of functions": "ballparks into which it is sufficient for the executive
  to transfer the state".
- **The plan as a projection of the environment (pp. 16–17).** The action
  concept of A and the perception concept of A both depend on
  topological, not metric, properties. Evarts's motor-cortex units code
  force, which Turvey reads as a late, "surface" stage. "The action plan
  unfolds as a series of progressively less abstract projections of the
  environment."
- **Tuning (pp. 18–22).** The γ system sets the equilibrium (static) and
  damping (dynamic) of the spindle servomechanism, so "the servomechanism
  is not only informed of what it has done, but … can be informed of what
  it must do". Higher-level signals to the γ system and to interneurons
  act "less as instigators of movement than as modulators of the gain of
  the feedback loops". "The control of movement is in many respects the
  reorganization or tuning of the system of segmental interactions", and
  "this attunement precedes the transmission of activating instructions".
- **Evidence of pretuning (pp. 22–26).**
  - Gurfinkel et al.: knee extension follows the command at 160–180 msec;
    a patellar reflex evoked after about the first 100 msec is enhanced,
    more so the nearer to movement.
  - Gottlieb et al.: the H-reflex is augmented in the 60 msec before
    plantar flexion and generally inhibited before dorsiflexion.
  - Hip flexion enhances the reflex only of the quadriceps head involved;
    knee extension enhances both heads; elbow flexion also enhances the
    patellar reflex, less. "Both specific and nonspecific changes …
    precede voluntary motor unit activation."
  - Kots and Zhukov: in the final 30 msec before dorsiflexion reciprocal
    inhibition of the antagonist is enhanced without any change in its
    motor neurons' excitability.
  - The reorganization goes from "initially diffuse" to "more localized"
    closer to the movement (Gelfand et al.).
- **Tuning as parameter specification (pp. 26–30).** A spring,
  F = −S₀(l − λ₀), is controlled by setting S₀ and λ₀. Asatryan and
  Fel'dman's unloading experiments show that a joint has an "invariant
  characteristic" M(φ) fixed by its initial setting, independent of the
  external load. In Fel'dman's experiments the arm went to the angle that
  characteristic defined even when spring and weight were removed
  unpredictably before movement: "the equilibrium position is independent
  of the initial conditions", and correction comes only after the new
  steady state. Turvey generalizes: coordinative structures are "biasable,
  self-regulating vibratory systems", illustrated (not modelled) by
  m·x″ + k·x′ + s·x = 0, tuned by setting s (stiffness, so equilibrium)
  and k (damping). In Gottlieb et al.'s data the H-reflex changes do not
  track the EMG, so "the mechanisms of tuning and activating coordinative
  structures are largely separate" (p. 30).
- **Vision and tuning (pp. 32–38).**
  - After Gibson (1958), properties of the optic flow that change control
    locomotion (outflow to go forward, symmetric expansion for collision,
    "maximize its optical size" for pursuit); properties that do not
    change specify what surfaces and objects afford.
  - Tuning reflexes (tonic neck, labyrinthine, eye-muscle stretch) bias the
    segmental apparatus so that a crude "jump" lands in the direction of
    gaze. But neck angle is "ambiguous with respect to distance", so the
    tuning for a jump's trajectory must come from optical properties that
    specify distance and height, "realizable as segmental tunings without
    the intervention of executive procedures".
  - "In control reactions the relation is between changing properties of
    stimulation and patterns of tuning, and in identifying reactions it is
    between nonchanging properties of stimulation and action plans."
    "Different properties of stimulation enter into the unfolding act at
    different levels."
- **Duality (pp. 40–43).** Perceiving and producing may be "dual
  representations of common neural events": "the structure that affords
  perception of a speech sound also affords its production". The only
  evidence offered is hybrid crickets, whose females prefer the hybrid
  males' song (Hoy and Paul 1973), and Turvey grants the principle may hold
  "only in the communication mode".

## Concepts

- **Coordinative structure.** A reflex or a functional combination of
  reflexes that acts as a relatively autonomous unit; borrowed from Easton
  (1972a), "used here with greater latitude". Turvey's examples are spinal
  and within one body: stepping, the α–γ servomechanism ("the fundamental
  coordinative structure"), the locomotor synergism.
- **Executive.** Not a structure but whichever procedure is, "at the
  moment, in respect to the task", acting as executive to another (Greene).
- **Heterarchy, coalition.** Organization in which the roles of higher and
  lower are not fixed and domains negotiate.
- **Equivalence class (ballpark).** The set of states or functions a lower
  system can interconvert, which the executive specifies instead of a
  single state.
- **Tuning.** Biasing the parameters of an autonomous system (gain,
  equilibrium, damping) without disrupting its self-regulation;
  movement-related or environment-related.
- **Tuning reflex.** A reflex whose effect is to bias the segmental
  apparatus (tonic neck, labyrinthine) rather than to move a limb.

## Connections

The essay works from Bernstein's *Co-ordination and Regulation of
Movements* (1967, [LIT-907](../literature.d/LIT-907.md), held unread), from Greene (1971), from the
Gelfand–Tsetlin group's papers, and from Gibson (1958, 1966). It takes
Koestler's holon ([LIT-908](../literature.d/LIT-908.md), unread) for the whole-part character of
substructures and then questions its fixed direction. Its later lineage in
the record: Kugler, Kelso and Turvey (1980, [LIT-913](../literature.d/LIT-913.md), unread) recast
coordinative structures as dissipative structures; Kelso et al. (1984,
[LIT-902](../literature.d/LIT-902.md), unread) supply the jaw-perturbation evidence; and the
Haken–Kelso–Bunz line is [THEORY-069](../theory.d/THEORY-069.md). Its mass-spring tuning is Fel'dman's
equilibrium-point idea, before that name.

## Bearing on the record

- **[LIT-904](../literature.d/LIT-904.md)'s summary is half right.** It says the chapter is "usually
  credited with introducing the coordinative structure". Turvey says the
  term is "borrowed from Easton (1972a) but used here with greater
  latitude" (p. 8). The chapter is where the term was widened from
  reflexes to functional combinations of them and made the unit of action
  control, not where it was coined. The 2026-10-11 curation entry's "the
  usual source for the term" should say so.
- **The coined sense, for [CLAIM-175](../claims.d/CLAIM-175.md) and [CASE-046](../cases.d/CASE-046.md).** [CLAIM-175](../claims.d/CLAIM-175.md) records
  "coordinative structure" among the names discussed for the dancing
  couple. In Turvey's sense a coordinative structure is built from
  reflexes and lies within one body; nothing in the essay extends it
  across two. What does carry over is relative autonomy, control by
  tuning, and the executive's ignorance of the details. [CASE-046](../cases.d/CASE-046.md) calls
  compensation that preserves the dance "the signature of a coordinative
  structure in motor control". That signature, reciprocal compensation, is
  Kelso et al. 1984 ([LIT-902](../literature.d/LIT-902.md)), not this essay. Turvey's nearest evidence is
  Kots and Syrovegin's fixed joint ratios and Fel'dman's equilibrium that
  does not depend on initial conditions, which is equifinality, not
  compensation. An extended use would be the record's widening, as
  Turvey's was a widening of Easton's.
- **[THEORY-069](../theory.d/THEORY-069.md).** The HKB account models coordination as relative-phase
  dynamics. This essay is its predecessor in the same programme but
  models units as tuned springs and says nothing of phase transitions.
- **Nucleating-argument line.** The executive that specifies only a
  "ballpark" and leaves its realization to self-regulating parts is the
  structure of an argument whose owner states a direction and leaves the
  filing to an agent. This is a reading-across, and the record has no
  claim it would support or defeat.
- **Filed from this reading:** [THEORY-302](../theory.d/THEORY-302.md), control by tuning of
  autonomous units, at the strength the reviewed experiments give it for
  the spinal level and no more.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- Preliminary and speculative by the author's statement (p. 2), with no
  experiments of its own.
- All pretuning evidence is for simple, movement-related acts; the
  environment-related account is extrapolation (p. 22).
- The vibratory-system equation is "given only to illustrate a principle"
  (fn. 7).
- The reading is of the 1975 report; the 1977 chapter may differ.

## Open questions

- Whether environment-related pretuning occurs, that is, whether optical
  information sets segmental parameters before an act without executive
  involvement; experiments of the reflex-probe kind during visually guided
  jumping or catching would answer it.
- Whether tuning and activation are separate mechanisms beyond the one
  dissociation reported.
- Whether the 1977 chapter keeps the attribution to Easton and the
  examples unchanged.
