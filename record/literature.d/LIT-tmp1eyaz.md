# LIT-tmp1eyaz: Translation and Relevance

Ernst-August Gutt (1989), *Translation and Relevance*, PhD thesis,
University College London (University of London). UCL Discovery eprint
1317504.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmpfykfi](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpfykfi.md), which skimmed the thesis and read the
chapters named in the status note.*

- **No separate theory of translation.** Most kinds of translation are
  interpretive use across languages, and relevance theory accounts for
  them without a typology of texts or functions (abstract; pp. 186, 190).
- **Resemblance between utterances is resemblance in what they
  communicate.** Two utterances interpretively resemble each other "to the
  degree that the sets of assumptions which each conveys in its own
  cognitive environment overlap", that is, in their explicatures and
  implicatures (pp. 74–75). Identity of propositional form is not
  sufficient for identity of interpretation (p. 72).
- **Faithfulness is optimal resemblance.** A translation is presumed to
  resemble the original "closely enough in relevant respects": in the
  assumptions that make it adequately relevant to its audience, expressed
  so that they are recovered without unnecessary effort (pp. 162–164).
  What counts as relevant depends on the audience's cognitive environment.
- **Direct translation is the limiting case.** A receptor-language
  utterance is a direct translation "if and only if it purports to
  interpretively resemble the original completely in the cognitive
  environment envisaged for the original" (p. 254). The communicative clues
  it preserves (semantic representation, syntax, pragmatic connectives,
  formulaic expressions, sound) are identified by their effect on the
  interpretation, not by their form (pp. 199–201, 253–255).
- **Against equivalence.** Functional equivalence gives a basis for
  comparison but not for evaluation (pp. 22–27), and dynamic equivalence's
  "equivalence of response" is unrealistic where the receptors' cognitive
  environment differs from the original audience's (pp. 122–127).
