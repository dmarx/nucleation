# NOTE-996: Nichols & Bruno — Intuitions about personal identity

## Contribution

The paper turns Williams's (1970) claim that personal-identity intuitions
depend on how a case is told into survey data. It shows that the
divergence Williams described can be produced in ordinary participants,
that his own explanation of it (first against third person) does not
account for it, and that abstract questions and a forced reflective choice
come down on the psychological side. It also states a method: when
intuitions conflict, find which are robust across frames and weigh those
more.

## Key insight

The conflict among personal-identity intuitions is a framing effect, and
framing effects can be studied instead of only lamented. Which frame to
trust is not fixed by the frame type (abstract is better for the Linda
problem, concrete for grammaticality), so the authors use convergence and
reflective preference as the tie-breaker. Everything they conclude about
persistence itself is conditional on intuitions being the right evidence.

## Assumptions

- **Intuitions bear on the theory.** Stated as a condition, not defended:
  "Insofar as our philosophical theory of personal identity should be
  based on our intuitions" (abstract, p. 293); "We have not attempted to
  defend the role of intuitions" (p. 306).
- **More robust means more weight.** "If intuitions are supposed to guide
  theory building, then, ceteris paribus, intuitions that are more robust
  ought to be given more weight" (p. 307).
- **A liberal "psychological theory".** Any view on which "persistence or
  continuity of distinctive psychological factors is necessary for
  persistence of self" counts (pp. 298–299; n. 12 notes the usual usage is
  necessary and sufficient). Only necessity is tested; sufficiency was
  dropped because pilot participants found Williams's Lockean case "very
  difficult ... to comprehend" (p. 298; n. 11).
- **"Self" and "person" are used interchangeably.** "Persistence of self"
  and "personal identity" name one question throughout. A person is "a
  more-or-less autonomous agent that thinks, acts on the basis of reasons,
  and is subject of perceptual experiences" (p. 293).
- **The population.** Introductory philosophy students at the University
  of Arizona, before personal identity was taught, in class (pp. 300, 304,
  306; n. 18). The authors call it "homogenous on several important
  factors, including age, culture, and socioeconomic status" (p. 307).
- **Yes/no and free-response measures.** No scales in the Pain and
  abstract studies; a 0–9 scale in the Blok replications (nn. 14–15).

## Key results

- **The two frames (§1, pp. 294–295).** The authors label Williams's
  third-person body exchange "the Lockean frame" and his first-person
  torture series "the Pain frame". Williams's two explanations of the
  divergence are set out (§3, pp. 297–298): the Lockean case is "the
  product of the will of the experimenter" (Williams p. 179), and the
  mentalistic verdict came from a third-person argument, the bodily one
  from a first-person one.
- **Lockean side, replicating Blok, Newman & Rips (2005) (§4,
  pp. 299–300).** Jim's brain is placed in a stock body and his original
  body destroyed; in one version the memories are intact, in the other
  the scan finds "no memories". Third person: agreement that it is "still
  Jim" is higher with memories, t(28) = 3.17, p < 0.01; means 5.45 against
  3.24 on 0–9 (n. 14). First person, the names replaced by "I": t(42) =
  3.33, reported p = 0.01; means 5.95 against 4.09 (n. 15). No first- against
  third-person difference, t(70) = 0.69 (memory) and 1.09 (no memory). The
  design was within-subject, both versions to each participant (n. 16).
  The authors note their means are "generally closer to midline" than
  Blok et al.'s.
- **Against the experimenter's-will explanation (p. 300).** In the Blok
  case "all that is specified is that the memories are erased. There is no
  hint that the memories are recreated somewhere else", and the
  psychological answer still comes.
- **Pain frame, first person (§5, pp. 300–301).** "Helpful doctors" treat a
  lethal brain infection with a procedure that "will permanently eliminate
  your distinctive mental states (including your thoughts, memories, and
  personality traits)"; days later they "administer a series of painful
  shots". Four yes/no items, the first a comprehension screen, the fourth
  the target: "When the doctors administer the series of shots, you will
  feel the pain." 68% agree, χ²(1, N = 50) = 6.48, p = 0.01; among those who
  passed the screen and gave adequate explanations, 75%, χ²(1, N = 28) =
  7.000, p = 0.0082. One adequate explanation from an agreeing participant:
  "the doctors stated that you will lose your thoughts, memories, and
  personal thoughts, not your sense of feeling" (p. 301).
- **Pain frame, third person (pp. 302–303).** The same with "Jerry": 72%
  agree, χ²(1, N = 54) = 10.67, p < 0.01; comprehending subgroup 72%,
  χ²(1, N = 36) = 7.111. No difference from the first person, χ²(1, N = 64)
  = 0.062, p = 0.80 (Fig. 1). So the anti-psychological response "is not
  restricted, as Williams suggested, to the first-person rendering"
  (p. 302).
- **The authors' diagnosis (§6, pp. 303–304).** The demand is in the Pain
  frame: "if I am not going to feel it then who is? Similarly for the
  third-person version, if Jerry isn't going to feel the pain, then who?"
  This is offered as a hypothesis, not tested directly. Note 20 adds Emily
  Esch's alternative, that pain's vividness drives it and "a less vivid
  mental state, like itch, might generate different responses".
- **Abstract question (§6, pp. 304–305).** Free response to "what is
  required for some person in the future to be the same person as you?",
  with a request for plain words added after pilots "often triggered
  religious or spiritual responses" (n. 21). Over 70% named psychological
  factors, χ²(1, N = 53) = 9.98, p < 0.01 (by my recomputation, 38 of 53).
  To the statement "In order for some person in the future to be you, that
  person doesn't need to have any of your memories", more than 80%
  disagreed, χ²(1, N = 54) = 21.41, p < 0.001 (44 of 54 by recomputation).
  Against the Pain frame, χ²(1, N = 88) = 25.018, p < 0.001 (Fig. 2). The
  cell counts behind that N are not given, and I could not reconstruct
  them from the reported samples.
- **Abstract is not always better (pp. 305–306).** The Linda conjunction
  fallacy is a case where the abstract answer is right; grammaticality
  judgements ("Before his band broke up, Milo went to college") a case
  where the concrete one is.
- **Reflective choice (§7, p. 306).** Each participant got both the Pain
  case and the abstract question, order counterbalanced, then: "it
  wouldn't really be consistent to say both that you would feel the pain
  of the shots and also that in order for a person to be you, that person
  must have some of your memories. In light of this, which one are you more
  inclined to agree with?" 64% chose memory, χ²(1, N = 45) = 3.76, p =
  0.053, reported as "greater than what one would predict by chance alone"
  (Fig. 3). A "sizable minority" of 36% did not (n. 23). In a comparable
  condition on free will, Nichols and Knobe (2007) found a roughly 50/50
  split (n. 24).
- **Conclusion (§8, pp. 306–308).** Psychological intuitions "are resilient
  across significant changes in the cases" and converge with abstract
  judgement; Williams's anti-psychological intuitions "don't seem to stand
  to scrutiny when examined in a broader and aggregative context". "We think
  all of this points in favor of resolving Williams' intuitive conflict in
  favor of a psychological view of personal identity (assuming that
  intuitions should inform our theories of personal identity)." And:
  "Obviously we don't think that in general the way to solve philosophical
  problems is to ask undergraduates."

## Concepts

- **Lockean frame / Pain frame** — the authors' names for Williams's
  third-person body exchange and his first-person torture series.
- **Psychological theory** (liberal) — any view on which continuity of
  distinctive psychology is necessary for persistence (n. 12).
- **Thought experimenter demand** — pressure a case's construction puts on
  the answer, by analogy with experimenter demand (p. 303).
- **Robustness** — persistence of an intuition across changes of frame,
  used as its weight in theory-building.
- **Reflective equilibrium (folk)** — the authors' label for asking people
  to choose between two of their own conflicting judgements.

## Connections

The paper sits in the program of Nichols and Knobe (2007) on free will,
using framing (Tversky and Kahneman 1981) and the abstract/concrete
contrast (Sinnott-Armstrong 2008). Its Lockean data come from Blok,
Newman and Rips (2005) and Rips, Blok and Newman (2006). Bartels and Rips,
and Bartels and Urminsky, are cited for behavioural evidence that expected
psychological change lowers willingness to wait for a later reward (n. 17).
Nichols (2008), "Imagination and the I", had explained the Pain verdict by
the flexibility of "I" in imagination and had assumed the first-person
telling drove it; this paper's third-person result undercuts that
assumption, as note 19 concedes. On the philosophical side it answers
Rovane's and Sider's pessimism about the method of cases (§3, p. 297).
None of these was read here except Williams.

## Bearing on the record

- **The rubber hand THEORYs: no bearing.** [THEORY-766](../theory.d/THEORY-766.md) (the report
  difference), [THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md) (ownership as graded common-cause belief),
  [THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md) (interoceptive signals) and [THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) (minimal selfhood
  as whole-body identification) are about the felt ownership and location
  of a body now. This study is about judgements of a person's persistence
  over time, with no bodily stimulation and no ownership or location
  measure. It does not meet, partly meet or bear against any of their
  promote_when or refutation conditions. Nor does it touch [THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md)
  (ownership against agency). The only common ground is method: the
  authors' "thought experimenter demand" is the vignette counterpart of
  the expectation problem [THEORY-766](../theory.d/THEORY-766.md) names, and here too it is
  hypothesised and not controlled.
- **[LIT-462](../literature.d/LIT-462.md), [NOTE-387](NOTE-387.md), [THEORY-531](../theory.d/THEORY-531.md) (Williams).** [THEORY-531](../theory.d/THEORY-531.md)'s claim is about what
  fear can rationally follow, and its promote_when says "Reports of what
  people would in fact fear cannot settle it". This study collects reports
  of a different thing, whether one "will feel the pain", and asks nothing
  about fear, so it neither meets nor refutes [THEORY-531](../theory.d/THEORY-531.md). Its vignette is
  also not Williams's series: total elimination of distinctive psychology
  in one step, no torturer, no copied "memories", no second person. What
  it does test are two empirical remarks in Williams (pp. 179–180).
  First, that the mentalistic verdict comes from a third-person telling
  and the bodily one from a first-person telling: these participants give
  the same answers in either person (C3). Second, that the swap verdict is
  produced by the experimenter's choice of where to stop: memory still
  matters with no recipient (C4, weakly, since a full exchange was not
  tested). [THEORY-531](../theory.d/THEORY-531.md)'s title says the two tellings "support opposite
  criteria". As a fact about responses, the opposition is reproduced here,
  but it follows the case, not the grammatical person. A proposal only:
  [THEORY-531](../theory.d/THEORY-531.md)'s "What this does not say" could add that the link between
  person of telling and verdict is Williams's observation about his own
  two arguments, and that among these participants it does not hold.
- **The record's body/psychology split ([THEORY-546](../theory.d/THEORY-546.md), [LIT-448](../literature.d/LIT-448.md), [NOTE-374](NOTE-374.md)
  §§3–7).** The study sets a psychological criterion against a
  non-psychological one, and lumps bodily, organismic and soul views
  together on the latter side (pp. 296–297). Its two concrete cases do not
  separate brain from body from bare sentience. In the Blok case the brain
  survives in a new body and the original body is destroyed; erase the
  memories and participants say it is not Jim. In the Pain case the same
  brain (cured: the procedure "works exactly as expected") and body
  survive with distinctive psychology gone; participants
  say "you" will feel the pain. So same brain, different verdicts. The
  difference between the cases is whether the original body survives and
  whether the question is identity ("still Jim") or feeling ("will feel the
  pain"). The paper does not discuss this; the observation is mine. What
  it suggests is that the Pain item may be answered as a question about
  whether a subject of experience continues in that body, which a
  psychological theorist who counts bare sentience as psychology need not
  deny. One participant's explanation says so outright ("not your sense of
  feeling"). The study therefore bears on the split as a matter of folk
  judgement, and favours psychological necessity in the abstract, but the
  concrete case that is supposed to oppose it does not cleanly test the
  bodily view.
- **[THEORY-417](../theory.d/THEORY-417.md), [NOTE-374](NOTE-374.md) and [TERM-045](../terms.d/TERM-045.md): which question.** By Olson's map the
  study asks the persistence question, but its abstract probe ("what makes
  a person the same person") invites the characterization reading that
  [THEORY-417](../theory.d/THEORY-417.md) separates from it, and several free answers quoted ("that you
  have the same beliefs, values, and memories") would fit either.
  The Pain item reads more naturally in [TERM-045](../terms.d/TERM-045.md)'s sense 1 (a subject
  that feels) than in sense 3 (persisting); the Blok item asks sense 3
  directly. The paper's "persistence of self" runs these together, which
  is the conflation [ADR-024](../decisions.d/ADR-024.md) exists to prevent; in this record's terms the
  work is about personhood, with one item that may be about the self.
- **Can folk judgement settle the metaphysical question?** Not by itself,
  and the authors do not claim it does: their conclusion is conditional on
  intuitions informing the theory, and they say it is "hardly a decisive
  finale". [NOTE-374](NOTE-374.md) records Olson's view (§2) that the persistence
  question is not about the meaning of words and so not answerable a
  priori; and lists "thought experiments are evidence" among the debate's
  assumptions. On that view, a survey establishes how a population applies
  a concept under various frames, and reaches the persistence condition
  only through a further premise that the concept is reliable about the
  fact. What it can do without that premise is narrower and still useful:
  show that an intuition philosophers cite is or is not widely shared
  (Williams's Pain verdict is: about 70%), and test a proposed explanation
  of an intuition (Williams's first/third-person explanation fails). That
  is the distinction [QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md) keeps for reports of experience: a report
  establishes what is reported, and reaching what is reported about needs
  a further warrant. The parallel is mine; [QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md) concerns
  experience, not identity.
- **[LIT-451](../literature.d/LIT-451.md) (Hume).** Hume held that the "nice and subtile questions"
  of personal identity "can never possibly be decided" and are
  "grammatical rather than philosophical". Frame-dependent verdicts with a
  36% reflective minority are what that view predicts; the authors think
  aggregated, reflective judgement can still tip the balance. The pairing
  is mine.
- **[THEORY-447](../theory.d/THEORY-447.md) and [THEORY-476](../theory.d/THEORY-476.md)** (Parfit, Lewis) concern fission and what
  matters. The authors explicitly did not test fission or teletransport,
  since those concern sufficiency (n. 11), so nothing here bears on them.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **One population, small samples.** Arizona introductory philosophy
  students; Ns from 28 to 54 per condition. The authors flag the
  homogeneity themselves (p. 307).
- **The reflective result is marginal.** p = 0.053, described as greater
  than chance, two-tailed by the authors' own label. The forced-choice
  wording names "some of your memories" as the alternative and tells
  participants their answers are inconsistent, which is its own demand.
- **The demand explanation is untested.** No condition removes the "who
  else would feel it?" pressure while keeping the case concrete; the
  abstract question differs from the Pain case in many ways at once.
- **Necessity only.** Sufficiency of psychology, the half of Williams's
  Lockean case that drives the body-swap verdict, is not tested.
- **Construct of the Pain item.** "Distinctive mental states" leaves
  sensation intact by wording, and the procedure "works exactly as
  expected", so the brain is cured, not destroyed (item 2 asks whether
  "the infected brain will continue to function"). Agreement that "you will feel the pain" may express continuity
  of sentience, not rejection of psychological criteria. The adequate
  explanation quoted on p. 301 reads exactly that way.
- **Within-subject Blok replication.** Each participant saw both memory and
  no-memory versions, which invites contrast; the authors say a
  between-subject design "might well" differ (n. 16).
- **Reporting.** The cell counts for the N = 88 comparison are not given
  and do not follow from the reported samples; t(42) = 3.33 is given as
  p = 0.01, which is conservative. Figures were not viewed here.
- **No measure of religious belief,** though pilots drew religious answers
  and soul views are among the non-psychological options (n. 21).

## Open questions

- Does the Pain verdict survive a vignette that eliminates all
  psychology, sensation included, or asks "will it be you" rather than
  "will you feel"? That would separate identity from bare sentience.
- Does Esch's itch variant (n. 20) lower agreement? That would test
  vividness against demand.
- Is the 36% minority a stable individual difference (religiosity,
  personality), as note 23 asks?
- How do the results stand against later folk work: Berniūnas and
  Dranseika's (2016) reply in the same journal, which argues that the folk
  do not take psychological continuity as necessary for personhood or
  identity (known here only from its PhilPapers record and a search
  snippet, not read), and Nichols's later work with Strohminger on the
  moral self? Neither is held.
