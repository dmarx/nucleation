# THEORY-444: To mean something by an utterance, in the non-natural sense, is to intend it to produce an effect in an audience by means of the audience's recognition of that intention, where the recognition is meant to be a reason for the effect and not merely its cause; an intention to produce the effect, or to have it recognised, is not enough

## Source

Grice (1957), [LIT-1042](../literature.d/LIT-1042.md) (*The Philosophical Review* 66(3):377–388), read
in full in [NOTE-735](../notes.d/NOTE-735.md).

## What was actually shown

**Two senses of "mean" (pp. 377–378).** Natural meaning ("Those spots mean
measles") entails its content and has no one who meant it.
Non-natural meaning ("Those three rings on the bell mean that the bus is
full") reverses all five of Grice's linguistic tests.

**Against the causal theory (pp. 379–381).** Stevenson's tendency to
produce an attitude, "dependent on an elaborate process of conditioning",
either lets the tail coat mean a dance or is circular. It also gives no
account of what a speaker means on one occasion, which Grice takes as
basic.

**The analysis, reached by counterexample (pp. 381–384).**
- An intention to induce a belief is not enough: the handkerchief left at
  the scene of a murder means nothing.
- An intention that the audience also recognise that intention is not
  enough: Herod showing Salome the head, the child letting its mother see
  how pale it is.
- The recognition must do work. Showing Mr. X a photograph means nothing,
  because it would convince him if left by accident. Drawing him a
  picture means something. A deliberate frown has its effect "only
  provided the audience takes it as intended to convey displeasure"
  (p. 383).
- Hence "A uttered x with the intention of inducing a belief by means of
  the recognition of this intention", and generally (p. 385): "A meantNN
  something by x" is roughly "A intended the utterance of x to produce
  some effect in an audience by means of the recognition of this
  intention". The effect can be an action: pointing to the door.

**Reason, not cause (p. 385).** The grunt meant to make someone blush shows
the intended effect "must be something which in some sense is within the
control of the audience". The recognition is "a reason and not merely a
cause". Of cutting someone in the street: "one can refuse to be
offended".

**What could have come out otherwise.** A case with recognition doing no
work could have counted as meaning, or one with recognition doing the
work could have counted as not meaning. Grice's cases sort the other way.

## What this does not say

- **What act was performed.** The paper analyses meaning, not
  illocutionary force. It says nothing about what separates a warning, a
  request or a reproach when the same thing is meant. That step is
  Strawson's ([THEORY-511](THEORY-511.md)), and [NOTE-735](../notes.d/NOTE-735.md) says so.
- **That the three intentions are sufficient.** Strawson's watcher case
  satisfies them without communication, and needs a fourth intention
  ([LIT-1043](../literature.d/LIT-1043.md), pp. 446–448).
- **That sentence meaning reduces to speaker meaning.** Grice is
  "sympathetic" to that and "aware that the point is controversial". The
  reduction of timeless meaning is "a first shot", and the red light, which
  means stop without anyone meaning it, is admitted as awkward (C5, weak).
- **That speakers run complicated psychological occurrences.** Grice
  disclaims "peopling all our talking life" with them. Intentions are read
  by public criteria: "an utterer is held to intend to convey what is
  normally conveyed", and context decides between candidates (pp.
  386–388).
- **That the analysis is exact.** It is stated "roughly" throughout, and
  the condition on reasons (C4) rests on one case.
