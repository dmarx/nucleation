# NOTE-1238: Imachi et al. — Isolation of an Asgard archaeon at the prokaryote–eukaryote interface

## Contribution

Before it, Asgard archaea were known only from metagenomes. Their
physiology and cell biology were inferred from gene content. This paper
grows one. It shows what the cell looks like, what it eats and that it
cannot grow without partners that remove its hydrogen and formate. It
also gives a closed genome, which removes the contamination worries that
attend metagenome-assembled genomes. From these it proposes a model of
eukaryogenesis.

## Key insight

The organism most closely related to eukaryotes that anyone has grown
is a small, internally simple cell whose way of life is dependence on
other microbes. It ferments amino acids and can do so only while a
partner consumes the hydrogen and formate it makes. Its biosynthesis is
also incomplete. The authors' model makes this dependence the starting
point of eukaryogenesis: a host already "entangled" with partners by
metabolism and by its protrusions, which later engulfs one of them and
then "endogenizes" it, handing over its own energy metabolism.

## Assumptions

- **A modern relative stands in for an ancient host.** The authors
  stress that "roughly 2 billion years" separate MK-D1 from the lineage
  that led to LECA. The model rests on "reasoned assumptions" and
  "extrapolations".
- **The Asgard ancestor resembled MK-D1.** The model assumes it was
  "syntrophic, internally simple (that is, similar to MK-D1)" and lived in
  anaerobic marine sediment.
- **Gene presence stands for capacity.** The ancestral reconstruction
  rests on which catabolic genes are present across metagenome-assembled
  Asgard genomes and form monophyletic clusters.
- **Lipids by subtraction.** Lipids were measured on the co-culture, and
  MK-D1's composition is inferred using a reference Methanogenium
  isolate.
- **Phylogeny among cultured organisms.** The ribosomal-protein trees
  were built from cultured organisms only. Metagenome-derived sequences
  were used for the alignment and then removed "to avoid any influence of
  contamination", except for Ca. Korarchaeum.

## Key results

- **Cultivation (Fig. 1, Extended Data Fig. 1, Extended Data Table 1).**
  - A methane-fed bioreactor ran for more than 2,000 days on Nankai
    Trough seep sediment. Subcultures on casamino acids with four
    antibiotics gave faint turbidity after about a year.
  - Growth is slow: a lag of 30–60 days, more than 3 months to full
    growth, about 10⁵ 16S rRNA gene copies per ml, and a doubling time of
    about 14–25 days. Temperature and substrate changes did not improve
    it.
  - After six transfers MK-D1 was 13% of a tri-culture with
    Halodesulfovibrio (85%) and Methanogenium (2%). Later transfers
    removed Halodesulfovibrio, giving "a pure co-culture" with
    Methanogenium.
- **Syntrophy (Fig. 2, Extended Data Tables 2–3).**
  - MK-D1 degrades ten amino acids. Inhibitors of its partners
    (molybdate for sulfate reducers, 2-bromoethanesulfonate for
    methanogens) "significantly impaired" its growth.
  - High hydrogen or formate "completely suppressed" growth, as
    thermodynamic dependence on a partner predicts.
  - ¹³C labelling of methane and CO₂ varied with the partner, so MK-D1
    makes both H₂ and formate. The genome has one hydrogenase
    (MvhADG–HdrABC) and one formate dehydrogenase (FdhA).
  - The partner is replaceable: MK-D1 also grows with Methanobacterium
    sp. MO-MB1.
  - NanoSIMS shows uptake of amino-acid nitrogen into MK-D1 aggregates,
    "performed without replicates", on two kinds of culture.
  - Fourteen culture conditions did not raise yield.
  - The metabolic reconstruction (Fig. 2a) marks vitamin B12, biotin,
    lipoate and thiamine pyrophosphate among compounds predicted to come
    from the partners.
- **Cells (Fig. 3, Extended Data Fig. 3).**
  - Cocci 300–750 nm across (average 550 nm), in aggregates with
    extracellular polymer.
  - Cryo-EM and TEM show "no visible organelle-like inclusions, in
    contrast to previous suggestions". The cryo-EM rests on 14 images from
    two observations, TEM on 131 from six. DNA and ribosomes could not be
    told apart.
  - Membrane vesicles (50–280 nm), chains of blebs, and protrusions
    80–100 nm wide, of various lengths and often branching. They form
    neither networks nor connections between cells.
  - Archaeal ether lipids (C20-phytane, C40-biphytanes with 0–2 rings),
    and no genes for ester lipids.
- **Genome and expression (Fig. 4a, Extended Data Figs 2, 4).**
  - A closed genome of 4.46 Mb.
  - Ribosomal-protein trees (31 proteins, maximum likelihood and Bayesian)
    show MK-D1 as sister to eukaryotes among the cultured taxa. "Thus,
    MK-D1 represents the closest cultured archaeal relative of
    eukaryotes."
  - 80 eukaryotic signature proteins. 23 are among the 500 most highly
    expressed genes, including actin-, gelsolin-, ubiquitin- and
    ESCRT-III-related proteins. Three possible division systems (FtsZ,
    actin, ESCRT) are expressed together.
- **The Asgard ancestor (Fig. 4b, Extended Data Figs 5–7).** Most Asgard
  genomes encode amino-acid catabolism and NiFe hydrogenases and have
  restricted biosynthesis. Catabolic genes for histidine, serine and
  threonine degradation and for butyrate and propionate fermentation are
  conserved across phyla including Heimdallarchaeota. The authors
  propose that the last Asgard common ancestor "was an amino-acid-degrading
  anaerobe that produced H2 and fatty acids as by-products ... and
  depended on metabolic partners, although we do not reject the
  possibility of other additional lifestyles".
- **The E3 model (Fig. 5).** "a hypothetical model", "not definitive".
  - Entangle: the host degrades amino acids with H₂-scavenging sulfate
    reducers, then interacts with a facultatively aerobic partner that
    scavenges O₂ (the future mitochondrion), as oxygen rises.
  - Engulf: protrusions wrap the partner, and a membrane around the
    chromosome forms at the same time. Phagocytosis is judged unlikely,
    given the host's small size and lack of machinery and energy.
    "further evidence is required to support this conjecture".
  - Endogenize: the host shares 2-oxoacids with the endosymbiont. It then
    loses its own anaerobic 2-oxoacid catabolism, which eukaryotes lack,
    resolving "catabolic redundancy" and O₂ sensitivity. An ADP/ATP
    carrier makes the symbiont the cell's ATP supply.
  - The authors depart from hydrogen-transfer models: "Although previous
    models propose that H2 transfer was a key interaction that drove
    endosymbiosis ..., we believe that current data favours the above
    interaction" (Supplementary Note 8, not read).

## Concepts

- **Syntrophy**: growth that depends on a partner consuming one's
  products, here H₂ and formate, so that degradation stays
  thermodynamically favourable.
- **Eukaryotic signature proteins**: proteins otherwise found only in
  eukaryotes. MK-D1 has 80.
- **Pure co-culture**: MK-D1 with one partner and nothing else. It is
  not an axenic culture, which is why the name keeps Candidatus
  (Supplementary Note 3, not read).
- **E3 (entangle–engulf–endogenize)**: the authors' three-stage model,
  described above.
- **Endogenization**: the host delegating catabolism and ATP generation
  to the endosymbiont, and losing its own.

## Connections

The paper builds on the metagenomic discovery of Lokiarchaeota and the
Asgard superphylum, and on metagenomic proposals of Asgard hydrogen
metabolism, some of which it contradicts in detail. Its engulfment step
takes up a "previously proposed alternative route" in which external
structures enclose a partner. It sets aside hydrogen-transfer models,
the hydrogen hypothesis among them ([LIT-1597](../literature.d/LIT-1597.md), unavailable). Kay et al.
([LIT-1566](../literature.d/LIT-1566.md), read in [NOTE-1234](NOTE-1234.md)) cite this isolate's protrusions and
its prokaryote-like size, and class E3 as "mitochondria intermediate".
Kay et al. place nFECA as sister to Heimdallarchaeia, not to the Loki
lineage that MK-D1 belongs to, which agrees with this paper's narrower
claim of "closest cultured" relative. The paper dates the TACK–Asgard
lineage to "approximately 2.1–2.4 billion years ago", citing other work.
Kay et al. date nFECA, a younger node, to 3.05–2.79 Ga. The two
timescales do not fit together. The comparison is mine.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-277](../questions.d/QUESTION-277.md).** The question registered this work as "the empirical
  case for" syntrophy. The reading supports that for the host lineage's
  way of life, with corrections and one new point.
  - **Dependence before endosymbiosis (D).** The host lineage, on this
    evidence, was already obligately dependent on other microbes, for
    removing H₂ and formate and probably for some vitamins. But the
    dependence is on a service, not on a partner. Methanogenium,
    Methanobacterium and Halodesulfovibrio each serve. That is high D
    with no partner specificity, no cohesion (R) and no control (C). It
    is a case of the question's "interdependence that is not
    individuality", at the start of the most famous transition. The
    inference is mine.
  - **A loss route inside the endosymbiosis (G).** E3's endogenization is
    a host-side loss of a redundant function, its own 2-oxoacid
    catabolism, made affordable because the symbiont supplies the
    product. That resembles the Black Queen route ([LIT-1568](../literature.d/LIT-1568.md), [NOTE-1212](NOTE-1212.md),
    [THEORY-1048](../theory.d/THEORY-1048.md)), but inside a partnership and with a non-leaky product
    passed through a dedicated ATP carrier. The authors propose it; it
    is not shown. The comparison is mine.
  - **Not the hydrogen hypothesis.** The question's later note groups
    this work with Martin & Müller ([LIT-1597](../literature.d/LIT-1597.md)) under syntrophy. Imachi et
    al. make the host the H₂ producer and say current data favour an
    interaction other than H₂ transfer as the driver of endosymbiosis.
    The two works share the word "syntrophy" and differ on who feeds
    whom and why the partner entered.
  - **Weak bearing on host complexity.** The LIT says the isolate
    "bears on" whether host complexity preceded the mitochondrion,
    since it "shows no internal complexity". The authors themselves set
    2 billion years between MK-D1 and the host lineage, and Kay et al.
    say the same of cultivated Asgard archaea. One modern relative
    without internal membranes neither shows nor refutes an ancient
    complex host.
- **[NOTE-159](NOTE-159.md).** O'Malley and Parke cite Asgard inferences to doubt that
  eukaryogenesis was primarily a transition in individuality. A host
  whose ancestors lived by dependence on a guild of partners is
  consistent with that doubt. The inference is mine.
- Filed as [THEORY-1108](../theory.d/THEORY-1108.md). No instruction for machine-learning
  practice.

## Limitations

- **One organism, not axenic.** All characterization is of MK-D1 with
  partners. Lipids were inferred by subtracting a reference strain.
- **"Closest relative" is among cultured taxa.** The trees omit
  metagenome-derived Asgard genomes, including the Heimdallarchaeota
  that other work places closer to eukaryotes.
- **The ancestral state is inferred from gene presence** in incomplete
  metagenome-assembled genomes. The authors keep other lifestyles open.
- **Small imaging samples.** The cryo-EM rests on 14 images, and NanoSIMS
  had no replicates, as the authors say.
- **The E3 model is a hypothesis.** Its steps (an SRB stage, engulfment
  by protrusions, a protonuclear membrane formed during engulfment) are
  not tested, and the supporting arguments sit in supplementary notes
  not read here.
- **Inconsistent timescale.** The cited 2.1–2.4 Ga for the TACK–Asgard
  lineage conflicts with later dating of nFECA ([LIT-1566](../literature.d/LIT-1566.md)).

## Open questions

- Do cultured Heimdallarchaeia, or other lineages closer to eukaryotes,
  share MK-D1's obligate syntrophy and internal simplicity?
- Is the partner dependence obligate in principle, or does MK-D1 grow
  alone under H₂-removing conditions (a gas-sparged culture, an
  electrode)? That would separate thermodynamic dependence on a service
  from dependence on organisms.
- What are the protrusions for? The authors suggest physical interaction
  with partners; whether they wrap or exchange with partners is not
  shown.
- Can a symbiont-to-host ATP supply evolve before the host loses its own
  catabolism, as E3 needs?
