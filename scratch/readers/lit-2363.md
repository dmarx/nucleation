## Summary

A researcher wants welfare preferences from choices of people who are not
utility maximizers and does not know their behavioural model. Bernheim and
Rangel's (2009) Pareto relation (x over y iff y is never chosen when x is
available) avoids false positives but is coarse, more so as data grow.
Caliari proposes an axiom, Informational Responsiveness (IR): if x is judged
indifferent to y, one more choice of x with y available breaks the tie toward
x. With Neutrality and Choice non-negativeness, IR characterizes
choice-frequency methods that recover the true preference exactly for
preference-monotonic models (MON) on single-menu data (Prop. 1); on
homogeneous datasets it guarantees less (APU models, Prop. 2); adding
Connection (an IIA-like axiom) characterizes weighted counting refinements of
the Pareto relation (WCRP+) that recover preferences under all MON models
(Prop. 3). An experiment (145 St Andrews students, 2019; 25 lottery menus,
25 delayed-payment menus; then a non-incentivized ranking of four main
alternatives) shows most participants violate WARP (63% Time, 94% Risk) and
that IR methods, especially the Prop. 3 family, cut expected false positives
against the reported ranking compared with Bernheim–Rangel.

## Contribution

An axiomatic middle way between model-based (Manzini–Mariotti) and
"model-less" (Bernheim–Rangel) welfare analysis: properties of the welfare
method fix the family of behavioural models under which it is correct; plus an
experimental test against stated rankings with a coarseness/false-positive
trade-off and a "completeness" measure after Fudenberg et al. (2022).

## Key results, with section and quote

1. **The trade-off.** §1: the researcher "faces a trade-off between the
   uncertainty of a coarse welfare preference and the mistakes of an
   incorrectly elicited one."
2. **"Model-less" is a misnomer.** §1: "a better description of this approach
   is 'implicitly model-based,' as each welfare method, being an inverse
   mapping, naturally imposes constraints on the models of decision-making."
3. **IR defined.** §2.1, Axiom 1: x I_D y and x, y ∈ A ⇒ x P_{D+(x,A)} y; "an
   indifference should always be broken by welfare-relevant choices."
   Footnote 1: first introduced by Goodin and List (2006) as "One Vote
   Responsiveness".
4. **Prop. 1.** §2.2: on single-menu data, a method satisfies IR, NEU and CNN
   iff it correctly elicits for exactly the MON models. Remark 1: the BR method
   "fails to elicit the underlying welfare preference more and more often as
   N grows", the characterized methods succeed more often.
5. **Prop. 2 (negative).** IR, NEU, CNN and Transitivity guarantee only APU
   models on homogeneous datasets; "in most cases a negative answer to whether
   IR can generalize the Pareto relation" (Remark 2).
6. **Prop. 3.** IR, NEU, CON, CNN ⇔ correct for all MON models on any dataset
   ⇔ WCRP+ (weighted counts with strictly positive menu weights).
7. **Normative vs descriptive IR.** §2.3.1: the number of observations needed
   to break an indifference "is a decision variable for the researcher"; "the
   BR method, violating IR, is equivalent to unanimity. This implies, in our
   choice setting, giving veto power to irrationalities."
8. **Attention-type models fall outside.** Fn. 8: models based on attention,
   salience, perception, reference points or context "violate IR and CNN
   because they induce a misalignment between choice probabilities and the
   underlying deterministic preference."
9. **Results rest on aligned data.** §2.3.2: "If we allow for sampling
   variation ... our proofs do not follow through."
10. **Inconsistency is common.** §4.1: "In Time, 63% of the participants
    violate WARP at least once, while in Risk this percentage grows to 94%";
    correlation between the two +0.06.
11. **Time.** §4.2.1: BR uniquely identifies the top alternative for 55%,
    Prop. 3 methods 87%; expected false positives for the top alternative 26%
    (BR) vs 12% (CRP), for the whole preference 44% vs 35%.
12. **Risk.** §4.2.2: BR uniquely identifies 12% but set-identifies 88%; CRP
    about 60% unique but 36% false positives vs 12% for BR; expected false
    positives 57% (BR) vs 39% (CRP). Hypothesis 3 (Prop. 3 beats Prop. 2
    methods) "seems less clear" in Risk.
13. **Completeness and direct test of IR.** §4.3: the Prop. 3 family comes
    "surprisingly close" to an oracle method (OW) fitted to the reported
    rankings; optimal weights are strictly positive for every menu type except
    asymmetric-dominance menus in Time ([−0.4, −0.1], Table 5), "a potential
    source of false positives".
14. **Attention model welfare is nearly empty in practice.** App. D: the
    Masatlioglu et al. (2012) method "uniquely identifies the best alternative
    only for 1 out of 145 participants" in Time and 14 in Risk; completed with
    BR or MS it does no better than they do.

## Assumptions

- Each decision-maker has a transitive, complete welfare preference that a
  stochastic choice model maps to choice probabilities (§2).
- Datasets are aligned with the generating model (choice frequencies order as
  probabilities do), as if sampled many times (§2, §2.3.2); the experiment has
  one observation per menu.
- The reported ranking (non-incentivized, no indifference allowed) is the
  welfare preference against which methods are scored (§1, §3, fn. 4); the
  paper treats this as a mitigated, not solved, misalignment and estimates an
  irreducible error.
- Neutrality: no prior information about alternatives or models (§2.1).

## Verdict on the source's characterization

A's account ("recovering welfare-relevant preferences from inconsistent
choices without knowing the behavioural model, with an
informational-responsiveness criterion and experiments") is **accurate in
outline, with one correction**: the methods do not work "without knowing the
behavioural model" in general. They require no explicit model, but the paper
insists they are "implicitly model-based" (result 2) and are guaranteed
correct only for preference-monotonic models; attention, salience and
reference-point models are outside (result 8). "Recovering" is also stronger
than shown: the experiment reduces expected false positives against stated
rankings; it does not recover preferences for everyone (Risk: 39% expected
false positives on the top alternative).

## Bearing on each named code

- **QUESTION-456, failure mode 2** ("behavioral observations are evidence about
  a demand, not automatically the demand itself"; Â_i ≠ A_i; behaviour under
  selective exposure can hide the error). **Supports, and sharpens.** (a) Any
  rule from choices to preferences embeds a model of behaviour (result 2), so
  inferring A_i from behaviour always imports assumptions about how behaviour
  was produced, which is the question's worry put formally. (b) The
  inference is reliable only when choice frequencies are monotone in the
  preference; attention and salience models, the formal counterpart of
  selective exposure, break exactly that condition (result 8), and under the
  attention model choices reveal almost nothing (result 14). So the error the
  question fears is precisely the case the paper's tools exclude. (c) The
  choice of how many observations break an indifference is the researcher's
  normative decision, a trade between false positives and coarseness (result
  7): the analyst's, not the data's. That is a point at which a
  governance-shaped choice enters the inferred set. (d) It does **not** give a
  procedure for fixing D or A_i independently of the inferring party; its
  benchmark is the subjects' own stated ranking, which QUESTION-456's
  governance concern would also call into question.
- **LIT-2458** (Caliari and Petri, NOTE-1746, the neighbour): the target is now
  read, so the "in part" stand-in is superseded. The two agree in theme
  (behaviour underdetermines welfare-relevant preferences) at different levels:
  LIT-2458 aggregate stochastic choice, LIT-2363 individual choice with a
  remedy and experiments. LIT-2363 does carry what the neighbour lacked:
  individual-level recovery, the IR criterion and the experiments.
- **LEAD-158 / QUESTION-128** (objectivity of relational norms; normativity
  from an organization's own dynamics). **Weak bearing.** The only route is
  result 7: whether an inconsistent agent's choices fix its preference depends
  on a threshold the observer sets, so "what the agent prefers" is partly
  observer-relative when choices conflict. Nothing on organizations or
  constitutivism.

## Limitations, including gaps in the argument

- **Benchmark is stated, non-incentivized rankings** (fn. 4); a "false
  positive" is disagreement with what subjects say afterwards, not with
  welfare. The paper concedes misalignment and estimates an irreducible error
  but does not argue that the reported ranking is the welfare-relevant one;
  that is assumed (citing Nielsen and Rehbeck 2022).
- **Theory assumes aligned data; the experiment has one observation per
  menu**, which "is therefore unlikely to satisfy the alignment property"
  (§4.3.1). The match between theorems and experiment is by analogy.
- **The OW method is fitted to the reported rankings** and is "purely
  diagnostic"; completeness scores are relative to it.
- **MON is assumed, not tested directly**; Hypothesis 3 is unclear in Risk,
  which the paper attributes to non-monotone models or noise without
  deciding (§4.2.2, fn. 21).
- **Sample**: 145 students at one university, 2019; two domains with almost
  uncorrelated inconsistency (+0.06), so the results may be
  domain-specific.
- **Missing case**: welfare-relevant preferences that differ from both choice
  and report (e.g. after learning) are outside the framework.
