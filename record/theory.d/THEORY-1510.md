# THEORY-1510: On Suding, Gross and Houseman, where degradation has built biotic–abiotic feedbacks that hold the degraded state, such as invader-driven fire and nitrogen cycles, herbivory on regenerating plants, lost seed sources or bare soil that sheds rain, restoring the historical disturbance regime or abiotic conditions does not return the community; restoration must first disrupt those feedbacks or move the state directly, often by several actions together, before restored conditions suffice

## Source

Suding, Gross & Houseman (2004), [LIT-2169](../literature.d/LIT-2169.md); read in full in [NOTE-1700](../notes.d/NOTE-1700.md),
from the typeset.io (SciSpace) copy of the published version. Page numbers
are the journal's.

## What was actually shown

A review, so what is shown is the authors' synthesis of cited cases:

- **Restored conditions failing** (pp. 46–47): fire put back after a
  25-year hiatus "shifted community composition but did not return it to a
  barren community"; in semi-arid rangeland "reduction of grazing intensity
  does not restore the grass dominance"; on Hudson Bay "the reduction of
  herbivory does not facilitate recovery" (p. 50).
- **The feedbacks named** (pp. 47–50): Hawaiian grasses "alter nitrogen
  cycling and promote fire, which further benefits introduced grasses";
  deer "selectively feed on regenerating saplings"; in desertification
  "less vegetation holds less water"; invader-dominated seed rain, so that
  management ends up "facilitating the spread of these species".
- **Intervening on the feedback succeeding** (pp. 47, 50): salt marshes
  "can be restored when plugs of Puccinellia phryganodes, a former dominant
  species, are transplanted into the mudflats"; once micro-catchments hold
  water, "grasses regenerate spontaneously, creating a structure that
  catches more water, thereby reversing desertification"; carbon addition
  "reduced weed biomass by 50% and increased the abundance of desirable
  prairie plants sevenfold".
- **Several actions together** (p. 50): actions on multiple constraints
  "(e.g. burning and adding native seeds) often prove more successful than
  would either approach alone", and those on strong feedbacks "might need
  to be implemented before other tools will be effective".
- **When conditions then suffice** (Fig. 2c, p. 49): with the biotic
  constraints addressed, "disturbance/abiotic re-establishment might be
  sufficient".

Each cited case could have come out the other way, with restored
conditions alone bringing recovery, as the paper says they do in prairie
potholes "If hydrology can be restored and the seed bank has persisted".

## What this does not say

- That any of these degraded systems has alternative stable states. The
  authors say such tests "are beyond the scope of most restoration
  efforts" and that persistence may be "slow transient successional
  change"; their reason for assuming alternative states is that it "might
  be more costly" not to. [THEORY-136](THEORY-136.md)'s bar is not met, and Schröder et
  al.'s count ([THEORY-1485](THEORY-1485.md)) suggests field cases rarely meet it.
- That the intervention can then stop. Only with a single constraint does
  the paper expect "little or no further management intervention"; its
  practice cycle ends in maintenance by "very different actions than those
  required to shift states", and no case measures a withdrawal. This
  bears on [CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md) and leaves its persistence test open.
- How often restoration by conditions fails, or by how much combined
  actions beat single ones. The cases are chosen to illustrate, the effect
  sizes come from single cited studies without designs or sample sizes,
  and comparisons of sequence are, in the authors' word, "limited".
- That the paper measures the threshold or hysteresis [CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md) describes.
  Box 2 draws one; no case estimates it.
