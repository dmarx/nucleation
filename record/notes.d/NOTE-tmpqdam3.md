# NOTE-tmpqdam3: Roberts — Talkative AI and the fiction of artificial minds

## Contribution

Chatbots produce fast, fluent, context-sensitive text that sustains a convincing impression of intelligent agency. Realists take this linguistic skill as evidence of underlying cognitive capacities and treat the systems as subjects of mental states. Roberts first challenges realism from embodied cognition: how could a digital system have the interoceptive, affective, perceptual and action-oriented states that seem tied to an animate body? He then argues that anti-realism is compatible with a fictionalist attitude. In conversation we imagine or make-believe that the AI is an autonomous agent with its own thoughts and experiences, as we do with traditional fictional characters. These fictions are distinctive in being interactive, co-constructed and self-involving, and in blending the real with the imaginary.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §2 "Realism and anti-realism" (pp. 2355–2357): realism comes in degrees, from minimal beliefs (Godfrey-Smith) to a full human catalogue of mental life. Standard blockers are syntax without semantics (Searle) and the symbol-grounding problem.
- §2.1 "Embodied cognition" (pp. 2356–2359): taking the moderate thesis that a substantial class of mental phenomena is tied to the body (Gallagher, Noë, Colombetti, Clark), Roberts cites interoception (thirst, nausea), affect, perception and skilled action as states a disembodied chatbot cannot have.
- §3 "Fictionalism about artificial minds" (pp. 2359–2360): chatbot utterances routinely assert or imply embodied states, so they are "systematically untrue", which makes the systems unreliable testifiers. Anti-realists may simply disengage, or may engage fictionally. The relation to interpretationism (Dennett's "yes and no") is flagged as unsettled.
- §3.1 (pp. 2360–2361): beyond ordinary AI-told stories, chatbots support a new practice of imagining real-time reciprocal dialogue with an independent agent that is itself the fictional character.
- §4 (pp. 2362–2366): the character is interactive and co-constructed (the user's prompts help build it), self-involving, and "blended" (the world-inheritance or reality assumption is strengthened by live data about the actual world). §5 draws three practical upshots, beginning with the point that realist and fictionalist stances answer to different rational norms.
