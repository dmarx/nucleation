# CLAIM-709: Equality of wrongdoing and equality of power are separate questions: two parties can each act wrongly, even as often, while only one holds control over the terms of the other's life, so a tally of wrongful acts does not settle who is responsible for the relationship's coercive structure, and a finding of that control does not excuse the controlled party's own wrongful acts

## The claim

The owner opened the exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afa2a8d-2598-83ea-8d1a-d9ceb992a99c) at U1
by asking what is understood of "what actually happened and who's the
asshole (including the possibility that the answer is both of them)". At
U2, quoting A2's "Domestic-violence researchers distinguish between two
people using violence and two people exercising equivalent power and
control", the owner asked A to "elaborate on this distinction in general".
A3's answer, "Where I land":

> **Equality of wrongdoing and equality of power are separate questions.**
> You can recognize someone's harmful actions without pretending the
> relationship was symmetrical. Conversely, recognizing a power imbalance
> shouldn't mean denying their agency or excusing every harmful thing they
> did.

A3 keeps three questions apart: what each person did, why and in what
circumstances, and what overall pattern the behaviour created. "Two people
can both be guilty under question 1, while one bears substantially greater
responsibility under question 3."

The support is the stipulated pair [CASE-279](../cases.d/CASE-279.md), in which the more frequent striker is the controlled party;
the argument is [ARG-113](../arguments.d/ARG-113.md).

## Why the two separate

From the continuation's filing of the same turns (share
https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b, whose U1–U17
coincide with this share's). A3 §7 puts the two as questions of different
objects: "**Question 1: Who harmed whom?** This focuses on individual
actions and consequences." "**Question 2: Who had power over whom?** This
focuses on the ongoing relationship. Who could set the rules? Who had to
accommodate the other's moods? Who could impose consequences? Whose
autonomy was being restricted?" Under the first, "Both people can be
guilty"; under the second, "the answer may be profoundly unequal, even
when both people have done harmful things."

In the record's terms the separation holds because [TERM-124](../terms.d/TERM-124.md) makes
domination a capacity, "used or not", to set the terms on which another
can act, exit, contest or renegotiate, while a count of acts, however
complete, counts uses. A3 §3's six questions (can each say no, keep
friends, spend money, leave the room, disagree, leave the relationship)
ask about that capacity, not about acts. The separation runs both ways:
each act keeps its own verdict, which a finding of domination neither
erases nor fixes. A3: "Neither method should replace the other. Both the
specific physical acts and the larger pattern matter." The senses of
responsibility at stake (for a particular act, for the pattern or its
conditions) are [TERM-127](../terms.d/TERM-127.md)'s; a verdict in one does not carry to
another.

## Where it sits

- [CLAIM-690](CLAIM-690.md): whichever way a whole and its part depend on
  each other, what sorts the dependence is who controls its terms. This
  claim is the interpersonal version, with wrongful acts in place of
  dependence as the quantity that does not settle it.
- [TERM-124](../terms.d/TERM-124.md), domination relative to a declared
  standard: Dan's position in the case is domination in that sense.
- [CLAIM-545](CLAIM-545.md): domination is another's capacity to govern
  the relations through which one's demands become effective, whether or
  not it is used.
- The same exchange's claim that responsibility for one's conduct and for
  the conditions of others' conduct are distinct ([CLAIM-708](CLAIM-708.md)) says what
  the second question is responsibility for.

## What it does not say

- **That the controlled party's wrongs are excused.** A3: "recognizing a
  power imbalance shouldn't mean denying their agency".
- **That mutual abuse is impossible.** A3 rejects that reading of the
  National Domestic Violence Hotline's position: Johnson's typology
  "explicitly allows for relationships in which both partners are violent
  and controlling".
- **Who is "the worse person".** A7 §9 argues that "acts of domination,
  acts of defense, failures of care, and failures of honesty aren't
  straightforwardly commensurable", so there may be no answer to that
  question even where these two are determinate: responsibility as a
  profile without a total order, the shape [CLAIM-354](CLAIM-354.md) gives freedom (a
  partial order, "not a scalar"). The claim does not need it and does not
  assert it; it is left in the curation entry as A's, not filed.
- **Anything about any real couple.** The exchange began from the Depp and
  Heard dispute (U1, A2); the claim needs only the stipulated case, and the
  record files no case naming them.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Cunha and colleagues, read

This systematic review of how bidirectional partner violence is measured supports the claim's first half from measurement practice. Thirty-four of 50 studies used the Conflict Tactics Scale, which counts acts, and "the CTS does not capture information about who initiates the violence, making it difficult to distinguish between acts committed in self-defense or retaliation and those initiated without provocation". As a result, "comparing the frequency of violence can lead to couples being perceived as having BV, even when one partner is merely acting in self-defense". A tally of acts, as the field takes it, therefore cannot show who holds control. The review says nothing about the claim's second half, responsibility for the structure. The authors also counsel "avoiding the reinforcement of unilateral blame", consistent with the claim's refusal to excuse the controlled party. Relation: corroborates. The reading was filed as a stand-in for [LIT-2671](../literature.d/LIT-2671.md).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Lohmann, read

Lohmann and colleagues' meta-analysis of coercive control and mental health does not address who is responsible for a relationship's structure, and it measures control as a set of behaviours, not as control over the terms of another's life. What it supports, from outside the exchange, is the premise behind the claim's case: one act can belong to different patterns. "Verbal threats can thus reflect situational couple violence or they can occur within the enduring pattern of domination that characterizes coercive control." It also qualifies how far the distinction is measurable: the correlates of coercive control were indistinguishable from those of broader psychological IPV, and the authors suspect "construct overlap". Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Mazinani and O'Connor, read

IPV researchers outside the exchange state the separation this claim makes. They analyse violence as arising between two partners, and they keep that analysis apart from responsibility: "This relational framing does not imply equivalent agency, mutuality, or shared responsibility; responsibility for a violent or coercive act lies with the person who commits it". And: "a victim’s resistance or constrained responses should not be treated as equivalent to the perpetrator’s controlling violence". They also give the measurement reason a tally misleads: "Effect sizes based primarily on the occurrence of physical acts may not capture motive, coercive control, fear, chronicity, severity, injury, sexual violence, or constraints on safety and leaving." This is the authors' stated framing, not an empirical result, so it supports the claim's coherence and standing, not its truth. Relation: corroborates (THEORY on this reading).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Johnson, read

Johnson's 2008 book, which A3 cites for the typology, has no lawful copy; his 2006 article "Conflict and Control"
([LIT-2703](../literature.d/LIT-2703.md)) was read in its place. It supports the claim's first half from evidence independent of the stipulated case:
Johnson's types of partner violence are defined by control and not by the violent acts ("The distinction lies in the
degree of control present"; the classification "most certainly is not based in the nature of the violent acts
themselves"), and in violent resistance one partner is violent without being controlling while the other controls.
So the separation between who acts violently and who controls is not only coherent ([CASE-279](../cases.d/CASE-279.md)) but found in data,
with consequences for frequency, escalation and injury. It does not support the second half, that responsibility for
the relationship's structure goes with control: Johnson's typology is descriptive. It also bears on the claim's "What
it does not say": mutual abuse is possible in the typology (mutual violent control), but was rare in Johnson's data
("only five such couples (10 individuals)"). Relation: corroborates (THEORY on Johnson 2006).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Stark and Hester, read

Stark's 2007 book, which A3 cites, has no lawful copy; Stark and Hester's 2019 review ([LIT-2712](../literature.d/LIT-2712.md)), which restates its
definitions with page references, was read in its place. It supports the claim's separation of acts from power from
outside the stipulated case: "Coercive control is not a type of violence"; in "perhaps as many as a third of all cases"
abuse produces entrapment "without any notable incidents of" violence; and an act-based definition let abusive men
"disconnect their violent acts from one another in time and space". So a tally of violent acts can miss the party who
holds control. The review is about harm and its mechanism; it does not argue that responsibility for the
relationship's structure goes with control, which is the claim's second half. Relation: corroborates (THEORY on
Stark and Hester 2019).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Long, read

Christie's "The Ideal Victim", cited for the exchange's A5 §3, has no lawful copy; Long (2021), [LIT-2667](../literature.d/LIT-2667.md), which quotes
his definition, was read in its place. It bears on this claim's "What it does not say", that recognizing a power
imbalance should not mean denying the controlled party's agency or excusing her wrongs. Long describes the
victim-offender dichotomy that works against holding both: it "presupposes that a person is either a victim or an
offender, but they cannot be both", and in one partner-violence case the police "assumed that he was the aggressor".
That is a reason the claim's separation of questions is needed in practice; it is not evidence for the claim.
Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Freyd, read

Freyd (1997), registered with this claim from the exchange, was read. It frames abuse as a question of power ("A focus
on betrayal is thus implicitly a focus on power", p. 28) but says nothing about tallies of wrongful acts or about who
controls the terms of a relationship. It neither supports nor qualifies the claim. Relation: none. (Its registration
named this claim only because the exchange's section on the topic cited it.)

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tognazzini and Coates, read

The SEP entry "Blame" reports Scanlon's account: blaming is taking "your
relationship with him or her to be modified in a way that [a judgment of
blameworthiness] holds to be appropriate" (Scanlon 2008: 128–29). That fits
A7 §4's use: one may withdraw trust on the impairment without a verdict on
who is worse. The entry also reports Radzik's view that standing to blame
wrongs "committed within special relationships, such as romantic
partnerships, familial bonds, and friendships" is restricted. That bears on
third parties judging a couple. Relation: none.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Anderson, read

The SEP entry on coercion ([LIT-2673](../literature.d/LIT-2673.md)) reports a legal proposal built on
the same separation between acts and control. Galoob and Sheley (2021)
would give a coercion-based defence to a defendant over whom the coercer has
"coercive control", "such as in a long-term violent and abusive
relationship", where the coercer "has usurped the target’s decisional
authority". The entry also argues that coercion generally "requires some
significant differential in power between coercer and coercee" (§3.3). It
does not discuss tallies of wrongful acts. Relation: none on the claim; the
reading bears on its case (below).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Nagel, read

The controlled partner in this claim's case acts under circumstances the
other partner sets. In Nagel's terms ([LIT-2662](../literature.d/LIT-2662.md)) that is circumstantial
luck produced by another agent, not by fate, which his essay does not treat
separately. Nagel's resultant luck bears on the tally: "overall culpability
corresponds to the product of mental or intentional fault and the
seriousness of the outcome", which a count of acts ignores. Relation: none.
