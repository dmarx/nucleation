# NOTE-tmptxas9: Multigenerational legacies of trauma: Modeling the what and how of transmission

## Contribution

The first attempt to turn Danieli's framework, Trauma and the Continuity
of Self (TCMI), set out in her 1998 handbook, into a testable model. It
puts in one multivariate model what earlier studies examined one at a
time: the survivor's age at the time, what they went through, where and
how they resettled, and the family they built after. It asks how much of
the child's state runs through the parent's way of living after the
trauma, and how much runs past it.

## Key insight

What passes to the child is the parent's way of living after the trauma,
not the trauma itself. In the framework's words, "the parent's fixity
becomes the child's biopsychosocial milieu" (p. 640). The data give that
claim a shape. Internment matters to the child only through the parent's
victim style. The post-war family matters more than the war. And the one
factor that reaches the child directly is not an injury but a broken
link: the child's sense that the murdered grandparents are not their own.

## Assumptions

- **TCMI.** Identity spans many spheres: biological, intrapsychic,
  familial, communal, cultural, national. Trauma ruptures their flow
  through time, a state Danieli calls fixity. The conspiracy of silence,
  "societal indifference to, avoidance, and denial" of survivors'
  experiences (p. 640), deepens it.
- **Survival strategies become styles.** In the extreme they "generalize
  to a way of life" (p. 640) and shape how survivors raise children.
- **The child is a good enough witness.** Parents' styles, the child's
  impacts and the family history are all reported by the child. The
  authors cite Yehuda et al. (2006) for children as reliable informants on
  parental PTSD.
- **The child's state is reparative.** The outcome is named for "the core,
  perhaps unconscious, motivation of the second generation to undo and
  repair the past and heal their parents and themselves" (pp. 640–641).
- **Missing data are missing at random.** Part 3 completers and
  non-completers did not differ on any parent or child measure, which the
  authors take to justify mean substitution (p. 644).

## Key results

- **The sample** (p. 642).
  - The website had 7,222 visitors; 2,809 viewed the survey and 789
    completed Part 1. The analysis kept 422 adult children with complete
    Parts 1 and 2 and some of Part 3. Grandchildren were excluded.
  - 72% daughters, 78% North American and 22% Israeli, mean age 60
    (SD = 8.2, range 38–84), 55% with graduate degrees.
  - Anonymous, collected 16 June to 24 December 2012, in English and
    Hebrew, through survivors', offspring and general Jewish organizations
    and word of mouth.
- **The measures** (pp. 642–643), from the three-part Danieli Inventory.
  The style scales were derived by exploratory factor analysis of the
  survey's own Part 1 data.
  - Victim style, 30 items (α = .92–.93): stuck in the loss and trauma
    rupture, overprotectiveness, emotional volatility.
  - Numb style, 18 items (α = .89): emotional barrenness, conspiracy of
    silence in the family, intolerance of weakness.
  - Fighter style, 12 items (α = .69–.70): valuing mastery and justice,
    valuing and maintaining Jewish (group) identity.
  - Reparative adaptational impacts, 36 items (α = .91–.92), six
    subscales: insecurity about one's competence, reparative
    protectiveness, need for power or control, obsession with the
    Holocaust, defensive psychosocial constriction, immature dependency.
    Linked to diagnoses of major depression, PTSD and generalized anxiety
    at ds = 0.48–0.89, in an earlier paper.
  - Broken generational linkages: the mean of two items, "I rarely think
    of my parent's murdered parents as my own grandparents" and remembering
    the family's Holocaust experiences "only in bits and pieces"
    (p. 643).
- **Descriptives** (pp. 643–644). Impacts averaged 3.00 (SD .66) on a
  1–5 scale, spanning nearly the whole range. Mothers' victim, numb and
  fighter styles averaged 2.85, 2.79 and 3.58; fathers' 2.68, 2.87 and
  3.55. Of those with data, 84.7% had two survivor parents, and 47.4% of
  mothers and 45.5% of fathers had been interned without hiding or escape.
- **Phase 1: what predicts the parents' styles** (pp. 644–646; Tables 3
  and 4).
  - Family history explained little: 6%, 4% and 5% of mothers' victim,
    numb and fighter styles; 3%, 1% (ns) and 3% (ns) of fathers'.
    Internment without hiding or escape went with stronger victim style
    (mothers β = .22, fathers .28). Adult survivor mothers scored higher
    on all three styles than child survivors (victim β = .18).
  - Family milieu explained more: 16%, 11% and 25% for mothers; 12%, 11%
    and 19% for fathers. Settling in Israel went with weaker victim style
    (β = −.22 for each parent) and much weaker fighter style (−.43, −.37).
    Broken generational linkages went with stronger victim (.17, .16) and
    numb style (.25, .25).
- **Phase 1: what predicts the child** (p. 646).
  - Without the parents' styles, Holocaust experiences (R²Δ = .026),
    setting and status (.009) and family configuration (.003) had no
    effect on the child. Social support (.054, p < .05) and continuity
    (.055, p < .001) did; broken linkages β = .22.
  - On the six parent styles: mothers' and fathers' victim styles,
    β = .31 and .33; mothers' numb style .15 (p < .05), fathers' .04 (ns);
    fighter styles .10 and −.07 (ns). Daughters reported more impacts
    than sons. R² = 52%.
- **Phase 2: the path model** (pp. 646–647; Table 5). Numb and fighter
  styles were dropped, as uneven or negligible.
  - All-indirect model: χ²(22) = 31.30, p = .09, RMSEA = .032,
    CFI = .988, with one significant residual, between broken linkages
    and the child (z = 2.43). Freeing that path: χ²(21) = 25.01,
    p = .247, RMSEA = .021, CFI = .995. R² = 15% and 13% for mothers' and
    fathers' victim styles, 51% for the child.
  - Victim style to child: mothers .38, fathers .34.
  - Indirect effects of internment without hiding or escape: mothers'
    .05, fathers' .06. Mother being an adult survivor: .00.
  - Total effects of milieu: Israel −.16, surviving maternal grandmother
    −.11, SES −.07, family size −.07, religious continuity −.07. Mother's
    survivor-group affiliation −.02 (ns).
  - Broken linkages: direct .09, indirect .12, total .21, the largest of
    any independent variable.
- **The double dose** (pp. 646–647). Having two survivor parents had no
  direct or indirect effect on the child. Each parent's victim style had
  an independent, additive one. The authors read the double dose as
  psychosocial, not a matter of survivor status.
- **The authors' conclusion** (p. 646). The study "demonstrated
  empirically" that survivors' experiences during and after the Holocaust
  affect their children. Their limitations section grants that the
  effects are "necessary but not sufficient for establishing causation"
  (p. 648).

## Concepts

- **TCMI** (Trauma and the Continuity of Self: A Multidimensional,
  Multidisciplinary, Integrative Framework): Danieli's 1998 account of
  identity as many spheres continuous through time, and of trauma as a
  rupture in that continuity.
- **fixity**: being "stuck" at the rupture; its severity depends on the
  trauma's timing, duration, extent and meaning, and on later
  victimization.
- **conspiracy of silence**: survivors' response to society's
  indifference, avoidance and denial; here also a factor of numb style
  inside the family.
- **posttrauma adaptational styles**: victim, numb and fighter, survival
  strategies generalized to a way of life, as the child sees them.
- **reparative adaptational impacts**: the child's state, read as a drive
  to repair the past and heal the parents and oneself.
- **broken generational linkages**: the child's distance from the murdered
  generation, in identity and in memory.
- **continuity**: links that keep identity whole across the rupture,
  measured as broken linkages and shared religious practice across four
  generations.

## Connections

- **Fridman ([NOTE-tmpi101n](NOTE-tmpi101n.md)).** The two designs answer different
  questions.
  - Fridman compares survivors' daughters with others and finds no
    difference. Danieli et al. have no comparison group, so they say
    nothing about whether survivors' children differ from others. They
    show which survivors' children report more. The authors do not cite
    the meta-analyses Fridman restates ([LIT-tmpd29uc](../literature.d/LIT-tmpd29uc.md), [LIT-tmppmeni](../literature.d/LIT-tmppmeni.md)); they
    cite Fridman et al. 2011 only on age cohort.
  - Fridman's sample is Israeli, and here Israel is protective (total
    −.16). This is an inference: the null may be partly an Israeli
    result.
  - On silence they diverge. Fridman's Proposition 8 holds that survivors
    setting the trauma aside may protect their children. Here numb style,
    which includes the conspiracy of silence in the family, had a modest
    effect through mothers only (β = .15) and none through fathers. The
    stronger channel was the parent stuck in the loss.
- **A group's story buffers, the third time.** Costa et al.'s "grateful
  nation" ([NOTE-tmp3firt](NOTE-tmp3firt.md)), Fridman's state-building, and here Solomon's
  remark, quoted by the authors, that in Israel the Holocaust was the
  "legacy of all" (p. 647). This is the first time the record has an
  association for it, but it is a country dichotomy, not a measure of
  meaning.
- **Volkan ([NOTE-tmp16of2](NOTE-tmp16of2.md)).** His depositing of a trauma in children by
  parents is this paper's channel too: children absorbed the Holocaust
  through "osmosis" (p. 648). Fighter style includes keeping up Jewish
  group identity, and it had no effect on the child.
- **Eyerman ([NOTE-tmp0goia](NOTE-tmp0goia.md)).** Cultural trauma passes by category. This
  sample is defined by descent and recruited through survivor
  organizations, so it cannot separate the category from the family.
- **Hirst, Cyr and Merck ([NOTE-tmpsbl9z](NOTE-tmpsbl9z.md)).** There, knowing a parent's
  memory went with stronger identification. Here, half the broken-linkage
  measure is remembering the family's history only in fragments, and that
  goes with more impacts.
- **The germline (Horsthemke, [NOTE-tmpahh4j](NOTE-tmpahh4j.md)).** The authors propose
  adding genotype and methylation data to the inventory (p. 648). That
  would join the routes in one sample, not separate them.

## Bearing on the record

- **The intergenerational trauma question ([QUESTION-tmp8bttd](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmp8bttd.md)).** The
  paper bears on three of its routes. What follows are inferences; the
  authors do not frame their results as routes.
  - **Parenting.** This is the clinical tradition's own quantitative test
    of the route, and it places the route within survivor families. What
    reaches the child runs through how the parent lived after (victim
    style), not through what the parent went through. With Fridman, both
    can hold: no general harm to survivors' children, and variation among
    them that tracks the parent's way of living. But nothing here shows
    harm against a baseline, so it cannot answer Fridman's null.
  - **Silence.** The question calls this route "parenting and silence".
    On this evidence silence is the weaker part. Two works now fail to
    make it the main channel: Fridman, who argues that setting the
    trauma aside may have protected the children, and this paper, where
    numb style, which includes silence, had a mother-only effect and did not enter the final
    model.
  - **Identity.** The question asks whether an ancestor's injury becomes
    part of the descendant's self. "I rarely think of my parent's murdered
    parents as my own grandparents" is the nearest the record has to a
    measure of whether the ancestors are part of the descendant's self.
    It is a family self, not a group's. Descendants who count them as
    their own reported fewer impacts, and the effect was partly direct.
    Meanwhile the outcome scale counts "obsession with the Holocaust" as
    harm. So the question's phrase covers two things that point opposite
    ways here: the ancestor becoming part of the self, which went with
    less harm, and the injury becoming part of it, which is part of the
    harm. The data are correlational and the direction is open: a
    distressed child may also be one who has pulled away from the family
    past.
  - **The group.** Fighter style's group-identity component and mothers'
    survivor-group affiliation had no effect on the child. Israel, where
    the trauma was the nation's, protected. On this sample, a shared
    group story did not carry harm to the child, and may have buffered
    it.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** Indirectly. The outcome is
  named for a child's drive to "heal their parents and themselves", and
  its subscales include reparative protectiveness. The clinicians score a
  child's care for a parent as part of the child's own condition. That
  fits a self extended to the family, Brewer's relational self
  ([NOTE-tmphttin](NOTE-tmphttin.md)), but the paper does not separate the child's caring from
  any extension of the self, and is not built to.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** The grandparents item
  asks whether the dead are counted "as my own", not how much one cares
  about them. It is a candidate criterion of the self's extent backward in
  time. But it is averaged with a memory item and never set against a
  measure of caring, so it shows nothing yet.
- **Batson's objection ([CLAIM-tmpybc4k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpybc4k.md)).** Not addressed.
- **A dated note is proposed for [QUESTION-tmp8bttd](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmp8bttd.md)**, not for the claim
  (section 4).
- **No THEORY filed.**
- **No ML instruction.**

## Limitations

- **No comparison group.** The design cannot show that survivors'
  children differ from anyone's (p. 648).
- **One informant for everything.** The child rated the parents' styles,
  their own impacts and the family history, retrospectively. A distressed
  child may see their parents as more victim-like, which the authors grant
  could inflate the links (p. 648).
- **Convenience sample.** Volunteers reached through survivor and Jewish
  organizations, highly educated, mostly daughters.
- **Attrition and imputation.** Of 789 who completed Part 1, 422 were
  analysed. Missing values were replaced by the mean; among them, the
  nature of the Holocaust experience was unknown for 32.0% of mothers and
  34.8% of fathers, and displaced-person camp for 45.0% and 47.4%
  (Table 2).
- **Measures built and tested on the same data.** The style scales came
  from factor analysis of this survey's Part 1. Fighter style's α was
  .69–.70.
- **Model building.** Variables were kept or dropped by significance in
  Phase 1, and the direct path for broken linkages was added after the
  first model's residual.
- **The conclusion outruns the method.** "Demonstrated empirically"
  (p. 646) sits beside the limitations' "necessary but not sufficient for
  establishing causation" (p. 648).
- **Not independent of the handbook.** The first author edited the
  handbook whose framework is tested; the third reviewed the handbook for
  ISTSS.
- **Typos.**
  - The in-text citation "Fridman, Fridman, Bakermans-Kranenburg,
    Sagi-Schwartz, & van IJzendoorn, 2011" (p. 641) doubles the first
    author; the reference list has one.
  - Religious continuity is coded "0 = no continuity, 1 = some
    continuity, and 3 = complete continuity" (p. 643); 2 is probably
    meant.
  - Cuthbert and Insel (2013) is given the DOI of Cuthbert (2014),
    10.1002/wps.20087 (p. 649).
  - "Mahway, NJ" for Mahwah (p. 650).
  - Table 2 gives no n for fathers' number of places before resettlement
    (p. 644).

## Open questions

- Do the two broken-linkage items act alike? Counting the murdered as
  one's own and remembering their history are different things, and only
  the first bears on the self.
- Does the linkage effect hold with a second informant, or over time? If
  linkage predicts later impacts, the self-inclusion reading gains; if
  impacts predict later withdrawal, it loses.
- Would non-survivor families of the same origin show the same pattern?
  Without them, the paper's within-group result cannot be set against
  Fridman's between-group null.
