# NOTE-tmphzb5j: Lenggenhager, Tadi, Metzinger & Blanke — Video ergo sum

## Contribution

It moved the rubber hand illusion from a body part to the whole body. Before
it, experiments manipulated the ownership and felt location of a hand, which
the authors call "a part-to-whole relationship" that "did not experimentally
manipulate selfhood per se" (p. 1097). Here a video of one's own back,
projected 2 m ahead in a head-mounted display and stroked in time with one's
real back, was rated as one's body and pulled felt self-location toward it.
It gave the study of bodily self-consciousness a laboratory procedure and two
measures, a questionnaire and a walking-back drift, that later full-body
illusion work builds on.

## Key insight

Where I feel I am, and which body I feel is mine, can be moved by the same
temporal match of seen and felt touch that moves ownership of a rubber hand,
but only part of the way, and only for something shaped like a body. The
self did not leave the body: it was located a little toward a seen body,
without any change in the place it seemed to see from.

## Assumptions

- **Bodily self-consciousness is the route to a model of the self.** It is
  defined as "the nonconceptual and prereflective processing and
  representation of body-related information" (p. 1096), citing Gallagher,
  Metzinger, Damasio and Legrand.
- **Selfhood is global ownership.** The "fundamental sense of selfhood" is
  "experienced as the transparent content of a single, coherent whole-body
  representation, rather than as multiple representations of separate body
  parts", and "whole-body representations or global ownership are directly
  associated with the sense of selfhood" (p. 1097). Both are cited to
  Metzinger's *Being No One* (ref. 2); neither is tested.
- **The drift measures felt self-location.** Blindfolded participants were
  walked backwards about 20 cm in small steps, then asked to walk back to
  where they had stood; the error toward the seen body is the measure (SOM
  pp. 1–2). That this error reports where the self was felt to be, and not
  something else, is assumed.
- **The questionnaire measures self-attribution.** Seven items adapted from
  Botvinick and Cohen's rubber hand questionnaire, scored −3 to 3 (SOM p. 2).
- **Out-of-body experience and heautoscopy are the clinical models.** The
  design is "based on clinical data in neurological patients with
  out-of-body experiences", in which "the spatial unity between self and
  body may be disrupted" (p. 1097).

## Key results

- **Setup (p. 1097; SOM pp. 1–2).** Participants stood with their back to a
  camera 2 m behind them, saw the live image in stereoscopic 3D in a
  head-mounted display, heard white noise, and had their back stroked with
  a pen for 60 s. Study I: n = 14 (9 women, mean age 23.1). Study II: an
  independent n = 14 (7 women). Asynchrony in study I replayed the video
  recorded in the training session; in study II it was a constant 200 ms
  video delay.
- **Study I, self-location (p. 1097; SOM p. 3).** Drift toward the seen body
  was 24.1 ± 9.0 cm (mean ± SEM) after synchronous stroking, different from
  the starting position (p = 0.02, t = 2.67), and 12.5 ± 8.5 cm after
  asynchronous stroking (p = 0.17). The paired difference was significant
  (p = 0.01, t = 2.92). There was no left–right drift. (The printed text
  gives the asynchronous figure as "12. ± 8.5 cm"; the SOM gives 12.5.)
- **Study I, self-attribution (p. 1097; SOM p. 3).** After synchronous
  stroking Q1 (touch felt where the seen body was touched), Q2 (the touch
  was caused by the pen on the seen body) and Q3 (the seen body felt like
  one's body) were positive: 2.6, 2.2 and 2.1 on the −3 to 3 scale. Every
  other item, and every item after asynchronous stroking, was negative.
  "None of the participants reported sensations of overt disembodiment or
  a change in visuospatial perspective"; some reported "weirdness" or
  "strangeness", and "some found the experiment irritating" (p. 1097).
- **Study II, self-location (pp. 1097–1098, Fig. 3; SOM p. 4).** Relative to a
  motor-control condition (displacement with no prior visual input or
  stroking), synchronous stroking gave drifts of 17.1 ± 6.1 cm toward one's
  own filmed body (p = 0.02) and 25.4 ± 8.4 cm toward a mannequin
  (p = 0.01), but 14.3 ± 7.3 cm toward a white rectangular object, not
  significant (p = 0.07). An empty room gave 1.12 cm. No asynchronous
  condition gave a significant drift. The Synchrony × Condition interaction
  was significant (p = 0.04). Only the mannequin differed between
  synchronous and asynchronous stroking (p = 0.0004); for one's own body
  that difference was not significant. In synchronous stroking only the
  mannequin and the object differed (p = 0.04).
- **Study II, self-attribution (p. 1098; SOM pp. 4–5, table S1).** Q1 and Q2
  were positive after synchronous stroking for all three, the object
  included. Q3 was positive for one's own body (2.1) and the mannequin
  (0.6 ± 0.6) and negative for the object (−0.4), where synchronous and
  asynchronous did not differ (t = 1.55). So "Q3 is important to evaluate
  self-identification ..., whereas the first two questions seem more related
  to the feeling and location of touch" (p. 1098).
- **Smaller synchrony effects in study II (p. 1098; SOM p. 5)** are put down to
  the more predictable 200 ms delay, which may have made seen and felt
  touch seem more related in the asynchronous condition.
- **Interpretation (p. 1098).** Self-attribution and localization of the
  entire body "rely, at least partly, on similar visual-somatosensory
  integrative mechanisms to those of body parts". The weaker drift and the
  absent Q3 for the object "speak in favor of bottom-up mechanisms as well
  as cognitive constraint, rejecting a pure Bayesian account" (the account
  attributed to Armel and Ramachandran, that correlation suffices).
  "Bodily self-consciousness and selfhood can be dissociated from one's
  physical body position."
- **Not an out-of-body experience (p. 1099).** Since there was no
  disembodiment and no change in perspective, "we have induced only some
  aspects of out-of-body experiences or rather the closely related
  experience of heautoscopy". Full out-of-body experiences need "other
  mechanisms in addition to conflicting visual-somatosensory information,
  such as visual-vestibular disintegration".
- **Brain (p. 1099).** "We speculate that humans' daily experience of an
  embodied self and selfhood, as well as the illusion reported here, relies
  on brain mechanisms at the temporoparietal junction." This rests on cited
  lesion, stimulation and imagery studies; nothing was imaged here.
- **The e-letters (June 2008).** Meyer argues that "self-localization depends
  foremost on the visual perception of the application of somatosensory
  stimuli", since in this study the self went where the stroke was seen, not
  to the camera's viewpoint. Ehrsson and Petkova reply that the rubber hand
  illusion fails with a hand rotated or more than 30–40 cm away, that they
  could not induce a whole-body illusion with a mannequin a few metres away
  "in 10 naïve participants", and propose that participants were
  recognising "a video reconstruction of their own body", as one recognises
  oneself on a shop's CCTV monitor. They add that the questions "are
  somewhat ambiguous when applied to the virtual reality set-up", that the
  study does not say whether participants "ever stopped feeling the touch on
  their on real back", and that the drift for one's own body hardly differed
  from that for the object. Blanke, Metzinger and Lenggenhager reply that
  under asynchronous stroking participants seemed to locate the self where
  the touch was felt, that "visual dominance is an important, but not
  exclusive, mechanism", and that a condition with no stroking at all,
  never tested, is needed to know where participants located themselves to
  begin with.

## Concepts

- **Bodily self-consciousness** — "the nonconceptual and prereflective
  processing and representation of body-related information" (p. 1096).
- **Selfhood** — the "fundamental sense" of being a self, tied to bodily
  self-consciousness rather than to its cognitive or emotional layers, and
  experienced as the content of one whole-body representation (p. 1097).
- **Body-part ownership against global ownership** — the sense that a part
  is mine, as in the rubber hand illusion, against ownership of the whole
  body, which the authors associate directly with selfhood (p. 1097).
- **Self-attribution and self-localization** — taking a seen body (or part)
  as one's own, and where one feels oneself to be; measured here by
  questionnaire and by drift.
- **Out-of-body experience** — "a disembodiment of the self to an
  extracorporeal location, an extracorporeal visuospatial perspective, and
  the sight of one's own body from this self-location" (p. 1099).
- **Heautoscopy** — seeing an illusory body and feeling located at it, at
  one's physical body or in between, often identifying with it, without the
  overt disembodiment of an out-of-body experience (p. 1099).

## Connections

It is built on the rubber hand illusion (Botvinick and Cohen 1998, ref. 6),
its proprioceptive drift and its premotor and insular correlates (Ehrsson
et al.; Tsakiris et al.), and on Blanke's clinical work on out-of-body
experience and heautoscopy (refs. 15, 17, 27). Its use of Tsakiris and
Haggard (2005) for a cognitive constraint on ownership is the comparison
behind the mannequin and object conditions. Its conceptual frame is
Metzinger's, a co-author: selfhood as the transparent content of a
whole-body model, and global ownership as its core. Ehrsson's companion
Brevia in the same issue induced a different illusion, self-location at the
camera's viewpoint with chest stroking, and the e-letters set the two
side by side. Botvinick and Cohen 1998 and Blanke and Metzinger 2009 are
being filed alongside this reading.

  answered positively for a box, so the touch items do not discriminate
  self-identification; Q3 does. The behavioural drift, the measure that
  needs no report, did not significantly separate one's own body from the
  box in synchronous stroking. Report and behaviour diverge in a way that
  limits each.
- **[LIT-1186](../literature.d/LIT-1186.md).** That study's participants attempted out-of-body perception.
  This paper's distinction between illusory self-location and the

  laboratory illusion does not cross it.
- **[THEORY-tmpuehzc](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpuehzc.md)** (filed 2026-10-20) takes this study, with Blanke and

## Limitations

- **Small samples.** Two groups of 14, with standard errors of 6 to 9 cm on
  drifts of 12 to 25 cm.
- **Inconsistency between studies.** The own-body synchrony effect,
  significant in study I, was not significant in study II, explained after
  the fact by the different asynchrony method.
- **Slips in the text and supplement.** The printed asynchronous drift is
  "12. ± 8.5 cm". The SOM says the empty-room and motor-control drifts were
  negative ("Indeed in these conditions the drift was negative"), but
  reports the empty-room drift as 1.12 cm. It refers to a "Fig 2C" the
  paper does not have, and gives "t<3.24" where a lower bound is meant.
- **The measures.** The drift was measured once per condition after
  displacement and walking, so motor and memory error enter it; the
  motor-control subtraction in study II addresses only a constant bias.
  The critics' point that the questionnaire cannot tell ownership from
  self-recognition in a video stands, and the design has no condition
  that separates them.
- **What the authors do not claim.** They do not claim to have induced an
  out-of-body experience, and the temporo-parietal claim is labelled
  speculation.

## Open questions

- Is this an illusion of ownership or recognition of oneself in a video
  system? Ehrsson and Petkova's alternative predicts no effect without the
  camera and display; a direct test within one design, comparing first- and
  third-person views, is what they ask for.
- Where do participants locate themselves with the display on and no
  stroking at all? The authors' reply says this baseline was not tested.
- Does visual-vestibular conflict, added to visuo-tactile conflict, produce
  disembodiment and a shifted perspective, as the authors predict?
