# LIT-tmpyozog: Intention and Convention in Speech Acts

P. F. Strawson (1964), *The Philosophical Review* 73(4):439–460 —
DOI-10.2307/2183301

## Key takeaways

- **Not every illocution is conventional.** A verdict, an umpire giving a
  batsman out, an introduction and a surrender are acts done as
  conforming to a convention. A warning to a skater ("The ice over there
  is very thin"), an entreaty ("Don't go") and an objection in debate
  need no convention beyond the linguistic.
- **Grice's analysis, strengthened.** A watcher case satisfies Grice's
  three intentions without being communication. Strawson adds a fourth
  intention: that the audience recognise the intention that it recognise
  the first.
- **Uptake is understanding.** The audience's understanding is taking the
  speaker to have that complex intention. Strawson identifies it,
  tentatively, with Austin's uptake. The aim of securing uptake is "a
  standard, if not an invariable, element" of the illocutionary act.
- **What this explains.**
  - Explicit performatives make an overt intention explicit.
  - Showing off is not an illocution, because recognition of the
    intention would defeat its effect.
  - Insinuating is not an illocution, because its intention is
    "essentially nonavowable".
- **Where it strains.**
  - Request and entreaty are separated by recognition of how the wish is
    held.
  - An order needs recognition of a social context with rules and
    sanctions, which the scheme holds "with some strain".
  - Institutional acts drop the intended response and the single
    audience altogether.
- **What is common.** "The illocutionary force of an utterance is
  essentially something that is intended to be understood."
