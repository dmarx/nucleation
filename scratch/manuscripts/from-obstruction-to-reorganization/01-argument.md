## 2. The argument: arguments that drift, records that anchor, inquiries that respecify

This section sets out the account of inquiry that the record holds, and that the predecessor manuscript, "luria: nucleating an argument", began. It is a report of the record's argument, not a new one. Each step is cited to the entry that holds it, with that entry's standing. *Active* means the record accepts it. *Proposed* means it is filed and argued but not adopted. Where this paper joins steps the record keeps apart, it says so in §2.7. The record's own accounts of these lines are SYNTHESIS-013 to SYNTHESIS-017 and SYNTHESIS-012.

### 2.1 What fails: an argument loses its identity, not its structure

The predecessor manuscript started from an observation about the record's own exchanges. An argument developed across many sessions with collaborators who keep no memory between them does not, characteristically, fail by lacking structure. Each session's output is well organised. It fails by losing the *identity* of what was said. Concepts are redefined silently. Objections and readings come back as though new. The thesis changes with no turn arguing for the change. CLAIM-177 (Active, empirical) records this across six exchanges between one owner and one family of assistants. CLAIM-324 (Active, empirical) is the documented instance. In the exchange "What Survives Translation?", the manuscript drafted straight from the argument's matured state changed the thesis "with no turn arguing the move" and dropped 24 of that argument's items, none on request.

Two refinements keep the observation honest. First, maturing legitimately drops things. The owner's term for an argument's current terminal state, the *crystallized argument* (TERM-052), comes with the warning that "maturing drops things legitimately". An absent item is therefore either dissolution or loss, and the record must be able to tell which. Second, drift has more than one object. Besides concept drift, an inquiry can drift in its question: a provisional answer can become part of what is being asked (TERM-056, *question drift*). That is different from query drift, in which the queries move away from a question that stays fixed.

The record holds three readings of *why* identity is lost. Each is Proposed, and they are not yet discriminated by any test:

- **Retrieval outside a critical distance** (CLAIM-180). A restatement close enough to an idea retrieves it. One that has drifted too far is taken for new. A code bypasses retrieval by content altogether.
- **Iterated transport** (CLAIM-191). Each restatement passes the argument through a channel with only a weak link back to its source, so restatements drift toward the collaborators' own framings rather than at random.
- **A whole's course coming apart from its members'** (CLAIM-192). The thesis moves without any participant arguing for the move, as an organised whole's trajectory can diverge from its members' intentions.

The generalisation, that such arguments fail mainly at identity beyond this one owner and assistant family, is CLAIM-483, still Proposed.

### 2.2 What helps: anchors, and what makes something an anchor

The predecessor's title metaphor is crystal nucleation. A solution does not crystallise until some structure persists long enough for further structure to grow on it. CLAIM-185 (Proposed) states the analogue: an argument's nucleation threshold is whatever makes a structure persist, because more complex structure can grow only on anchors. Three further steps say what persistence means and what produces it.

- **Persistence is relative to stipulated identity.** What counts as "the same claim persisting" is not discovered. It is fixed by an amendable decision (CLAIM-195, Active): the record stipulates the identity criteria. This makes the choice explicit without making it non-arbitrary.
- **Storage is not anchoring; addressability is** (CLAIM-182, Proposed, empirical). A transcript stores everything and anchors nothing. A structure that is kept, but cannot be pointed at part by part, does not stop re-derivation. The same content becomes an anchor once its parts get addresses.
- **Recurrence is a trigger.** Coding a thing the second time it turns up catches the drift of §2.1 the first time it could occur, and it keeps capture from flooding the record (CLAIM-176, Proposed). This was the record's original filing rule (ADR-032). ADR-072 later confined it to the record's *process*, so that an insight a source surfaces once is still filed when it has content of its own.

A demonstrated result then limits what anchoring can be expected to do. **Persistence is not productivity.** CLAIM-595 (Active, demonstrated) shows, using committor analysis of assembly processes, that two intermediates can have the same expected residence time and different chances of leading to the target. A state's committor depends on where its exits lead, not how fast it leaves. An intermediate can persist while *lowering* the chance that the target forms. TERM-117 accordingly defines a *productive nucleus* against a declared target, horizon and comparator, and the record's note on CLAIM-185 draws the moral: "a persisting trap is still built on". The record has counted how often its codes are cited and found almost every code cited (CASE-183). But being built on is not being productive, and the record does not declare a target, comparator or horizon for an argument against which productivity could be judged (SYNTHESIS-013).

### 2.3 What anchoring costs, and when it pays

Anchors have costs, and the record holds an account of them (SYNTHESIS-015).

- **Entrenchment.** An early anchor can lock in its framing, so that later structure grows on it and alternatives become hard to reach (CLAIM-184, Proposed). One route is iterative retrieval turning a provisional interpretation into a premise (CLAIM-301, Proposed). Against this, the record grants that adaptive search is not biased in itself (CLAIM-292, Active, demonstrated). Retirement by status is the record's remedy only if superseded anchors stop attracting new structure. A count on 6 November found that on this record they do.
- **Who pays.** Design-rationale systems were largely abandoned, Grudin argued, because capture cost fell on one person and the benefit on another (LIT-757). When an agent with no memory does the filing, capture and benefit fall to the same party. It pays the rediscovery tax every session, and capture is cheap because its context is loaded at the moment of filing (CLAIM-179, Proposed). "The disparity does not disappear. It moves." The owner now pays to read and correct the record instead of to write it. That is why the failure mode becomes flooding, and why a throttle matters.
- **The price of filing.** Filing moves work across time. It is paid for now (filing, indexing, tracking dependencies, refreshing) against reconstruction later, so it is worth its cost only when expected reuse covers it (CLAIM-303, Active). Where reuse cannot be forecast, the rent-or-buy result of competitive analysis (THEORY-348, from LIT-1096) gives a rule. File at the first use at which reconstructing again would take the overpayment past the cost of filing. That rule is within a factor of two of the hindsight optimum on every sequence of uses (CLAIM-308, Active, demonstrated). The bound assumes that the cost of recognising what a filed argument answers is fixed. CLAIM-314 (Proposed) objects that this cost grows with what is stored, so per-item filing decisions are not independent.
- **Where the two justifications part.** The second-occurrence rule thus has two justifications on the record: catching drift (CLAIM-176) and cost-competitiveness (CLAIM-308). CLAIM-630 (Proposed, demonstrated) shows they coincide only while filing costs between one and two reconstructions' overpayment. When filing is dearer, the cost rule files later. Every reconstruction it pays for in between is a restatement with nothing filed to check it against, and the competitive guarantee prices none of that drift. SYNTHESIS-016 identifies this as the one thing the nucleating-argument line says beyond its parts.

### 2.4 What a record of inquiry can secure

If maturing legitimately drops and changes things, a record cannot aim at preserving an argument. CLAIM-189 (Proposed) holds that fidelity is the wrong measure. What a record can secure is *traceability*: change that is announced and located. CLAIM-250 (Proposed) gives traceability an object, the argument's *accountability structure*: what was asserted, why, how it was challenged and what justified its revision. A manuscript is then a view over that structure, checked against the record, and not the argument's only copy (CLAIM-178, Proposed). Drift shows only against a record that keeps a claim's status and its inclusion in a work apart.

A run of demonstrated results, made exact in an exchange of 14 October, fixes what such a record is and is not (SYNTHESIS-017):

- **Conclusions do not fix revisions.** Two inquiry histories can agree on every current conclusion and still call for different revisions when evidence arrives about what a conclusion depended on (CLAIM-294, Active, demonstrated).
- **Support topology does not fix revisions.** Two records with the same support graph can call for different revisions when one support fails, because the graph records that support exists, not how much (CLAIM-293, Active, demonstrated). The record notes that its own `rests_on` and `grounds` relations have exactly this limit.
- **Sufficiency is relative.** A state sufficient for a family of decisions under a model makes the rest of the history worthless for those decisions. It stops being sufficient when a source is discredited, the model is revised or the decisions change (CLAIM-300, Active, demonstrated).
- **Three properties, separable.** Decision sufficiency, historical accountability and correctness can each hold while the other two fail (CLAIM-299, Active, demonstrated). An accountable record can be wrong.

Hence CLAIM-295 (Proposed). A kept record of justifications adds nothing for decisions the current state already suffices for. What it can add is a *wider family of decisions* for which what is kept suffices, and decisions a *bounded* agent could make that it otherwise could not afford. That real inquiries change their questions in ways that make this purchase worth its cost is, the claim says, not shown.

### 2.5 Inquiry as specification

The record's most general account of inquiry is the owner's: "inquiry is a form of specification" (CLAIM-470, Active). A question fixes which differences would count as an answer. An inquiry adds the operations that make those differences accessible. What it finds can stabilise into a specification that constrains later inquiry. Inquiry stands to its findings as a specification stands to its realisations, with one difference: **an inquiry can revise what an answer could be.**

TERM-095 writes an inquiry as a tuple (Σ, 𝒜, 𝓘, 𝓞, 𝓚, 𝓑). The components are a language Σ in which the question is posed; an answer space 𝒜 that says which differences would count as an answer; interventions 𝓘 and observations 𝓞 that make those differences accessible; criteria 𝓚 for judging candidate answers; and the resources 𝓑 it runs on. **An inquiry is *reflexive* when any of these becomes what it is pursuing.** Related terms place the tuple. An effective-description regime (TERM-065) keeps a description apart from the regime under which its adequacy is judged, and CLAIM-470 adds that the regime is itself a product of inquiry and can become its object. A relational vocabulary (TERM-091) is the arities and kinds of relation a class of descriptions can state. It lets the record say that a constraint is *inexpressible* in a vocabulary, which is different from saying it is false or unknown.

CLAIM-475 (Active) adds the resource bound. Specifying is itself a resource-bounded realisation. "I can't interrogate a question that takes me more time than I have to even ask it." The questions an agent can ask are therefore a product of what it has already realised: its *inquiry frontier* (TERM-092), as distinct from the questions its language can express and those that have answers. A stabilised specification is past inquiry stored so that later questions become cheaper to ask, and by the same token it can make other questions harder. This is entrenchment (§2.3) reached through cost rather than dependence.

The record's notes on CLAIM-470 name Dewey's theory of inquiry (LIT-1645) and Collingwood's logic of question and answer (LIT-1651) as the nearest predecessors. The record has not read either first-hand, and both are Unavailable. They name counterexample-guided abstraction refinement (LIT-1652) as the nearest formal case: an abstraction is refined exactly where a spurious counterexample shows it too coarse. The record's version differs from CEGAR in one respect that matters below. CEGAR only splits, whereas the record's grammar has also grown by *adding* types beside old ones and, once, by withdrawing one (§6.6). CLAIM-477 (Proposed) carries the analogy further, holding that relata stand to relations as realisations stand to specifications. This paper does not need it.

### 2.6 The record as an apparatus of its own inquiry

The last step turns the account on the record itself (SYNTHESIS-012). The record fixes the grammar in which its argument is expressed. CLAIM-181 (Proposed) was the predecessor manuscript's own objection to itself. The record's closed vocabulary of schemes, roles and relations formalises early, so a move that fits no CLAIM, TERM, ARG or declared relation "is forced into one or lost until the misfit is noticed and the grammar amended to hold it". In TERM-091's terms, a constraint the inquiry needs can be inexpressible in the record's relational vocabulary.

The record's answer is not to deny the objection but to observe what happened. CLAIM-419 (Active, empirical) records that the grammar is fixed at any moment but amendable by decision. In practice, a move that fit no scheme or relation has repeatedly been treated as a defect of the grammar and answered by amending it. Whether every such move is caught is not shown. CLAIM-479 (Active, demonstrated) adds an asymmetry. A typed relation is more constrained than either of its ends: a claim's content is open prose, while `rests_on` fixes the scheme at both ends and declares its converse. A relation's rigidity is therefore a fact about the grammar, not evidence that the dependency it records is sound. CASE-131 is the documented instance. A fixed converse made the record assert an inference nobody had made, and the grammar gained a relation in answer.

These are integrated in the definition of an apparatus given in §3: TERM-089's four functions (differentiate, retain, enforce, revise) and CLAIM-469's thesis (Active) that luria is an apparatus in that operational sense for the record's objects. The stronger reading, that the record *constitutes* its objects in Barad's sense, is CLAIM-476. It is Proposed, and its first defeater, that "constituted" adds nothing to the operational facts, is unanswered. Two further entries stay open. CLAIM-472 (Proposed) holds that an apparatus's choice of what to make determinate is open to evaluation and not only to verification. QUESTION-072 (Open) asks whether the record distributes credibility among contributors by making some contributors' moves addressable and leaving others' as unfiled turns. QUESTION-366 (Open) asks whether an inquiry needs the record of its own development to *know its object*, as Simondon's readers hold (LIT-1911), or only to revise its conclusions, which is what the record has shown (CLAIM-294, CLAIM-475).

TERM-089's own gloss is the caution this paper carries: "A self-modifying apparatus is not necessarily an epistemically self-correcting apparatus."

### 2.7 What this paper adds to the argument

The predecessor manuscript asked what makes an argument developed with memoryless collaborators settle into stable structure, and what a project memory contributes (QUESTION-037). Its answer was anchors: addressable, persistent structure. It also recorded, as CLAIM-181, that the anchors are coded in a grammar fixed early. This paper joins the steps above into four theses. They are integrations stated here for the first time. The record holds their components but not the theses themselves, and Appendix C asks whether they should be filed as claims.

- **T1. Anchors are expressed in a language, and the language is part of the inquiry.** What can persist, be addressed and be traced is fixed by the record's grammar (§§2.2, 2.4, 2.6). In TERM-095's terms, the grammar is Σ, together with much of 𝒜 and 𝓚.
- **T2. Revising the grammar is the record's reflexive inquiry.** By CLAIM-470, an inquiry can revise what an answer could be. When the record amends a scheme, field, vocabulary or relation, its inquiry has taken a component of its own tuple as its object. Editing entries is working *within* the inquiry regime. Amending `luria.yaml` is investigating the regime. (The distinction is A16's, quoted in CLAIM-470.)
- **T3. Traceability must reach the grammar.** If what a record can secure is announced and located change (§2.4), the same must hold for changes to the grammar. Otherwise drift recurs one level up: a vocabulary word silently changes sense, or a scheme's boundary moves without its entries being re-read. The record's decision records, migrations, supersessions and history notes are, on this reading, the accountability structure of its grammar.
- **T4. The economics of anchoring apply to the grammar too.** A grammar change has a construction cost, a transition cost and a maintenance cost (CLAIM-569). A stabilised grammar is stored inquiry that makes later filing cheap and can make other distinctions hard to reach (CLAIM-475, CLAIM-184). And a grammar can persist, being used and populated, without being productive (CLAIM-595).

Section 3 shows how luria realises the tuple and these theses. Sections 6 to 8 examine what happened when Nucleation's inquiry took its own grammar as its object.
