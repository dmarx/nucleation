# QUESTION-432: Do aid-funded projects whose beneficiaries stated the demand, contributed their own resources, received the benefits and shared the decision to continue keep their benefits after donor support ends more often than projects funded and controlled by the donor, and does a recipient's own spending on maintaining donor-funded assets fall as its expectation of donor repair rises?

## Why it is a question

[THEORY-1480](../theory.d/THEORY-1480.md) reports Ostrom and colleagues' account. Predictable help sets
up a Samaritan's dilemma that lowers the recipient's own upkeep. Benefits
outlast the aid only where beneficiaries hold all four dimensions of
ownership: provision, production, consumption and alienation. The report
argues both from an incentive analysis and five short illustrative cases
with no counterfactual ([NOTE-1647](../notes.d/NOTE-1647.md)). It cites one cross-project claim, that
projects with all four kinds of ownership "have been shown to be
sustainable long after a project itself has ended (Shivakoti et al.,
1997)" (p. 75). That volume is not registered here. This question is a
sharpening of [QUESTION-398](QUESTION-398.md), asked of aid.

## What would answer it

[THEORY-1480](../theory.d/THEORY-1480.md)'s former promote_when, verbatim:

> A study comparing aid-funded projects or assets finds that those whose
> beneficiaries stated the demand, contributed their own resources,
> received the benefits and held a say over continuation kept their
> benefits after donor support ended more often than those funded and
> controlled by the donor; and a study finds that a recipient's own
> spending or effort on maintaining donor-funded assets falls as its
> expectation of donor repair or rehabilitation rises.

The outcomes must be observed after exit. The authors put the time
needed to judge sustainability at "at least 10 to 20 years" (p. 121).
Either part could come out otherwise. Projects with full beneficiary
ownership could fail as often as donor-run ones, or maintenance effort
could be unrelated to expected repair.
