# NOTE-995: Starmans & Bloom — Windows to the soul

## Contribution

It gave the question "where in the body is a person?" an indirect method
that works with 4-year-olds: which of two equally distant objects is closer
to someone. Before it, the evidence was adults' direct answers, open to the
objection that they repeat what they learned about the brain. After it,
there is a result that preschoolers and adults, judging cartoon characters,
treat objects near the eyes as closest to the person, and track the eyes
when they are moved to the chest.

## Key insight

Ask which fly is nearer to someone and people answer as if the person were
at the eyes, not spread over the body and not at the head as a whole. The
authors take this to show where people think the self is. What it shows
directly is where people think *someone else* is.

## Assumptions

- **Closeness tracks the self.** "If people have an intuition that the self
  is located in a particular part of the body, then they should judge that
  objects nearer to that part of the body are closest to the person"
  (p. 314). That the converse holds, that a closeness preference reveals
  an intuition about the self rather than about salience, attention or
  social engagement, is assumed.
- **Person, self and soul are one target.** The question asks about Mary;
  the conclusion is about "the self"; the title about "the soul". The
  paper does not separate them.
- **An indirect question escapes learned theory.** "The indirect nature of
  our method, and the fact that these judgments are shared by adults and
  preschoolers, suggests that our results do not reflect a culturally
  learned understanding of the role of the brain" (p. 317). The children's
  limited grasp of the brain is cited (Johnson & Wellman 1982 and others),
  not measured.
- **Judgements about a cartoon generalise to persons.** All stimuli are
  drawings: Mary, the alien Zafi, the profile Erika (Fig. 1).

## Key results

- **Experiment 1 (pp. 314–315).** 50 children aged 4–5 (mean 4;8, 23 girls),
  from local preschools; 52 adults on Amazon Mechanical Turk (35 women,
  18–64, mean 33), paid $0.50. A fly was superimposed on Mary at the eyes,
  mouth, chest, stomach or feet; all ten pairs once; "Which fly is closer to
  Mary?" Each position scored out of 4. Choices rose toward the eyes
  (Spearman rs = 1.0 for both groups). On the six head-against-lower-body
  trials, children chose the head 70% and adults 76% (binomial, both
  p < .0001). Within the head, adults chose eyes (M = 2.85) and mouth
  (2.69) equally (t(51) = .84, p = .40); children chose eyes (2.84) over
  mouth (2.42; t(49) = 2.04, p = .05). By most frequent choice, eyes led in
  both groups (adults 46% against mouth 37%, Fisher p = .43; children 60%
  against 40%, p = .07).
- **Experiment 2 (pp. 315–316).** 41 children (mean 4;11, 20 girls) and 51
  adults (35 women, 20–70, mean 37); three children and four adults were
  excluded for not pointing to Zafi's face on its chest and head on top.
  Zafi is Mary recoloured green with the face on the chest: Mary's eyes and
  mouth positions become Zafi's head, chest becomes eyes, stomach becomes
  mouth. Adults chose the chest-eyes position over every other (M = 3.45,
  all p < .0001), chose the position nearer the eyes 86% of the time
  (t(46) = 12.20, p < .0001), and chose lower head and mouth, equidistant
  from the eyes, equally (t(46) = .95, p = .35); 77% chose eyes most often.
  Children chose eyes (M = 2.68) over every position (all p < .03) except
  the upper head (2.41; t(40) = .95, p = .35); chose the position nearer
  the eyes 63% of the time (t(39) = 3.79, printed "p < .0001"; that t gives
  p ≈ .0005); and chose lower head over mouth (2.20 against 1.58,
  t(39) = 2.38, p = .02). Most frequent choice: eyes 49%, upper head 39%
  (Fisher p = .50). The authors read the children's split as difficulty
  "to refrain from thinking of the eyes as being in the usual spot".
  They name two alternatives: a fly at the eyes is irritating, or "Which
  fly is closer?" was read as "Which fly will this person see as closer?"
- **Experiment 3 (pp. 316–317).** 42 children (mean 4;11, 28 girls) and 53
  adults (31 women, 19–67, mean 34). A snowflake beside Erika in profile,
  in front of the head (eyes), stomach and feet, and behind the head and
  the feet; head and eyes drawn smaller to be less salient. Choices rose
  toward the eyes (all rs > .9). Eyes chosen over every position (adults
  M = 3.23, all p < .001; children 2.70, all p < .02). Stomach and back of
  head were chosen equally (binomial, adults p = .20, children p = .14);
  back of head over front of feet (adults p = .05, children p = .01). Most
  frequent: eyes for 64% of adults and 55% of children; back of head 9%
  and 31%; stomach 9% and 29%. So participants "are not using what a person
  can see as a measure of what is close to her" (p. 317).
- **Association check (footnote 1, p. 317).** Separate Mechanical Turk
  samples asked which part of the character they associated most with the
  fly (N = 19) or the snowflake (N = 22) chose "randomly", with the nose
  most common (N = 4 each).
- **Conclusion (p. 317).** "Together, these studies provide converging
  evidence that children and adults intuitively think of the self as
  occupying a physical location within the body, close to the eyes." The
  results "might instead be rooted in a more intuitive or phenomenological
  sense of where in our bodies we reside".
- **Hiding (pp. 317–318).** Following Neisser (1997), young children who say
  an adult "cannot see me" when their own eyes are covered may mean "cannot
  see my self"; "the eyes are the windows to the soul".

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Children and adults judge an object near a drawn person's head closer to her than one near her lower body | strong for this task | Exp. 1, about 50 per group, 70–76% of head-against-body trials, p < .0001 |
| C2 | The preference follows the eyes, not the face or head as such | moderate for adults, weak for children | Exp. 2 adults 77% eyes, lower head = mouth; children split eyes 49% / upper head 39%; Exp. 1 adults eyes = mouth |
| C3 | The preference is not for what the person can see | moderate | Exp. 3: back of head over front of feet, p = .05 and .01; stomach = back of head |
| C4 | Children and adults think of the self as located at or near the eyes | weak | rests on C1–C3 plus the untested assumption that closeness to a person is closeness to her self |
| C5 | The intuition is not learned from ideas about the brain | weak | inference from the indirect method and the children's age; children's beliefs not measured |
| C6 | It rests on a phenomenological sense of where we reside | speculation | nothing first-person was measured |
| C7 | Children's "you can't see me" with eyes covered reflects locating the self at the eyes | speculation | reinterpretation of other studies |

## Concepts

- **Location of the self** — as operationalised: the part of a drawn body
  such that objects near it are judged closest to the person.
- **Projected self-location** — Alsmith & Longo's term ([NOTE-1008](NOTE-1008.md)) for
  this kind of judgement, made about a depicted figure rather than one's
  own body.

## Connections

It answers Bertossa et al. 2008 (adults, by interview, locate themselves
behind the eyes, blind participants in the head too) and Limanowski & Hecht
2011 (head and chest on an outline) with a method meant to bypass learned
theory. It is in the line of Bloom's *Descartes' Baby*, on intuitive dualism
in children. Alsmith & Longo 2014 ([LIT-1266](../literature.d/LIT-1266.md)), read alongside, calls its
claim "provocative", classes it as projected self-location, and, with people
pointing at their own bodies, finds the torso as well as the face. The two
results differ in person (third against first), in object (cartoon against
own body), and in resolution (eyes against a face region that includes
them).

  test. Blanke & Metzinger ([NOTE-1002](NOTE-1002.md)) place the geometrical origin of
  the weak first-person perspective, usually visuospatial, inside the volume
  of self-location. That ordinary judges place a person at her eyes fits
  that picture. But Experiment 3 shows the judgement is not about the
  person's visual field, and it is a judgement about someone else. It says
  nothing about where anyone feels themselves to be, which is what the
  THEORY's self-location dimension is.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md): what a folk judgement can establish.** It establishes how
  people attribute location to a pictured person under one question
  ("closer to"), and that 4-year-olds do it as adults do. That is a fact
  about the attribution. It does not establish where a self is, since no
  folk consensus fixes that, nor where people experience themselves to be,
  since no one reported on their own case. The authors' last step, to a
  "phenomenological sense of where in our bodies we reside", is the move
  from attribution to experience that [QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md) asks evidence to earn.
  Alsmith & Longo's first-person task, run on adults' own bodies, gave a
  different answer, which is a reason not to read the third-person result
  as a report of experience.
- **[TERM-045](../terms.d/TERM-045.md) and [ADR-024](../decisions.d/ADR-024.md).** The question is about a person (Mary), the
  conclusion about "the self", the title about "the soul". [ADR-024](../decisions.d/ADR-024.md) keeps
  self (being a subject) apart from person, and [TERM-045](../terms.d/TERM-045.md) asks which sense
  of self a claim uses. The nearest is sense 1, the subject with a point of
  view, located; the paper does not say, and its evidence cannot choose.
- **[THEORY-417](../theory.d/THEORY-417.md), [NOTE-374](NOTE-374.md), [LIT-451](../literature.d/LIT-451.md).** No bearing on the questions of personal
  identity: nothing here concerns persistence, what a person is, or Hume's
  missing impression of a self. It is about where ordinary thought puts a
  person in her body, not about what she is or what makes her persist.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **The inference to the self is built in.** No condition separates
  "closer to the self" from closer to what is socially salient in a face
  (gaze, attention, where one would look at someone). Experiment 3 removes
  "what she can see", not "where one looks at her".
- **Experiment 1's flies sit on the body.** Each fly is superimposed on
  Mary, not beside her (Fig. 1), so every position touches her and
  "closer" must be read in some other way; how children read it is not
  known.
- **Adults did not prefer eyes to mouth in Experiment 1**, and children did
  not separate eyes from the upper head in Experiment 2. The eyes-not-head
  conclusion for children rests on Experiment 3.
- **Small slips.** t(39) = 3.79 is printed "p < .0001" (p ≈ .0005). In
  Experiment 2 the children's tests have 40 and 39 degrees of freedom with
  41 children, and the adults' 46 with 51 adults, so the analysed numbers
  are not stated. The abstract's "closet" is a typo for "closest".
- **Samples.** Preschoolers from local schools and US Mechanical Turk
  adults paid $0.50; no other culture. The claim that this is not cultural
  rests on age, not on cross-cultural data.
- **Pictures, not people.** Every judgement concerns a cartoon.

## Open questions

- Does the eye preference hold when the judge locates herself rather than a
  cartoon? Alsmith & Longo's task suggests not wholly; a design asking
  both questions of the same participants would tell.
- Does it hold across cultures whose ideas of the self's seat differ (the
  heart, the belly), which is the test the "not learned" claim needs?
- Is it about the self or about the face as the seat of attention? A
  character whose eyes are closed or covered, or one looking away, would
  separate them.
