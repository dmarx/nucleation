# LIT-tmper0q2: Rediscovering the Social Group: A Self-Categorization Theory

Turner, J. C., Hogg, M. A., Oakes, P. J., Reicher, S. D. & Wetherell, M. S. (1987). *Rediscovering the Social Group: A Self-Categorization Theory*. Oxford: Basil Blackwell.

Not read. The summary is drawn from what the work is standardly cited
for. A reading should check it first.

## Why this is here

The record held social identity work only as unread fandom papers ([LIT-1138](LIT-1138.md), [LIT-1133](LIT-1133.md), [LIT-1147](LIT-1147.md), [LIT-1140](LIT-1140.md)) and Pettigrew's review ([LIT-1408](LIT-1408.md)), whose recategorization strategy presupposes this theory, without the theory's own source. [CASE-104](../cases.d/CASE-104.md), a fanbase said to hold one view of its owner, turns on when fans act as a group and when as individuals, which is the shift from personal to social identity the book defines. Smith's intergroup emotions, which [NOTE-552](../notes.d/NOTE-552.md) cites behind [THEORY-126](../theory.d/THEORY-126.md), also build on it. The book's claim that the psychological group is a real level of self-perception bears on social-ontology's question of what a group is.

## Read in its place

The book cannot be read here, so the record holds it at second hand:

- **Its own words.** Skorich (LIT-tmpmzum3, NOTE-tmplcvy8) quotes its
  definitions, assumptions and hypotheses from chapter 3 (pp. 3, 42–56),
  and León (LIT-tmpe36at, NOTE-tmpuba7m) quotes pp. 29, 42–51, 66, 103 and
  204. Between them they give the self-concept, the three levels,
  meta-contrast, accessibility × fit, functional antagonism,
  depersonalization as "the basic process underlying group phenomena",
  and the claim that group size is no theoretical restriction.
- **Its authors' later statement.** Turner et al. 1994 (LIT-tmpzgybs,
  NOTE-tmpf9mzh).
- **Summaries.** Hornsey 2008 (LIT-tmpi8o30, NOTE-tmp3dav9) and Reimer et
  al. 2020 (LIT-tmpxgtr8, NOTE-tmp222eb).

Missing still: the book's argument for each step; its sub-theories of
cohesion, attraction, ethnocentrism, self-esteem and cooperation, which
Skorich declines to cover (p. 7); chapter 5 on cohesion, which León
cites; and the chapters that Hornsey's references credit to Oakes
(salience, pp. 117–141) and Reicher (crowds, pp. 171–202). A reading of
chapters 3 and 6 would close most of the gap.
