# LIT-1278: Putting pain in its proper place

Reuter, K., Sienhold, M. & Sytsma, J. (2019). Putting pain in its proper place. *Analysis* 79(1), 72–82 (online 11 June 2018). DOI 10.1093/analys/any030. Read from the accepted manuscript, Victoria University of Wellington repository, DOI 10.26686/wgtn.14234279.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-1007](../notes.d/NOTE-1007.md).*

- **The puzzle.** "The pain is in my fingertip. The fingertip is in my
  mouth. Therefore, the pain is in my mouth" seems invalid (Block 1983).
  Tye explains this by making "pain" intensional, which supports
  representationalism about pain. Noordhof explains it by a
  state-attributing, non-spatial "in".
- **The rival account.** The "in" is spatial and "pain" is not
  intensional. The conclusion fails because it implicates that something
  is wrong with the mouth, and the premises do not.
- **Study 1 (174 MTurk participants).** The conclusion was rejected for
  pain, tissue damage and inflammation, with no significant difference
  between pain and the two bodily terms, and accepted for a paper-clip.
  Neither Tye's nor Noordhof's account predicts the rejection of the
  bodily terms.
- **Study 2 (70 raters, 560 judges, 14 items).** Mean acceptance of the
  conclusion tracked mean rated misleadingness across the items (R²
  0.897; R² 0.702 for the seven "in" items).
- **Study 3 (244 MTurk participants).** Rewording the conclusion as
  "technically speaking there is a pain located in her mouth" raised
  agreement for every item tested (F(1, 236) = 48.71, η² .17), but pain
  reached only the midpoint (3.79).
- **The authors' conclusion.** If the account is right, the argument no
  longer supports representationalism, and "the results suggest that
  pain is a state of the body, not of the mind".

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-20 at the owner's request, in a round of experimental
work near the rubber hand illusion. It is experimental philosophy in the
strict sense: vignettes and rating scales put to lay participants about
how they read sentences about pain. Read in full from the accepted
manuscript: **Active**.

It does not build on the rubber hand illusion and **does not bear on**
[THEORY-766](../theory.d/THEORY-766.md), [THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md), [THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md), [THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) or [THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md). It
studies how people judge an inference about where a pain is. It does not
study where a sensation is felt, or who owns a limb. The NOTE says why
its likeness to referred touch is a likeness of topic only.

Its place is with the record's holdings on pain and phenomenal
consciousness. Block's "Mental Paint" ([LIT-635](LIT-635.md), [NOTE-511](../notes.d/NOTE-511.md)) presses
representationalists on bodily sensations; this paper removes one
argument that Tye used for representationalism about pain, if its
diagnosis is right. Chalmers's meta-problem ([LIT-425](LIT-425.md), [NOTE-354](../notes.d/NOTE-354.md)) treats
problem intuitions as widely shared; this paper belongs to the programme
(Sytsma, Reuter and colleagues) that tests whether such intuitions are
the folk's. For [QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md)'s distinction, it gives evidence about a
concept and about how a sentence is read, not about the experience of
pain. The step to "pain is a state of the body" goes beyond the data,
and the NOTE says how far.

Read against Borg, Harrison, Stazicker & Salomons (2019), filed in the
same round ([LIT-1270](LIT-1270.md)), who argue that the folk concept is not
univocally bodily. They list this paper's authors among the bodily
view's advocates but do not discuss its argument.
