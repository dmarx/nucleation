## What was actually shown

**The report difference replicates.** Botvinick & Cohen's ten subjects
affirmed referred touch and ownership after synchronous brushing. Longo et
al. found ownership and location crossing from negative to positive with
synchrony, within subjects. Kalckert & Ehrsson found the same with a
moved, not brushed, model hand: a delay of about 500 ms removed it.
Abdulkarim & Ehrsson found ownership depended on synchrony in two
experiments of 18 (d 0.58–1.76). In Lush et al.'s 353 participants, as
reanalysed by Ehrsson et al., the synchronous-minus-asynchronous difference
in illusion ratings was significant in every hypnotisability quartile and
unrelated to hypnotisability (rho = −0.066, BF01 = 5.7).

**The ownership report is the weak one.** In Lush et al.'s sample the
ownership item averaged 0.7 on a −3 to +3 scale, against 1.9 and 1.2 for
the two referred-touch items. Agreement with it rose 0.76 per point of
hypnotisability and reached minimal agreement only in the top 31% (Lush &
Seth). In Roseboom & Lush's resampling of the synchronous reports into
studies of 20, samples from below the median of hypnotisability agreed with
the ownership item about 4% of the time. They agreed with the referred-touch
item 95% of the time. The ownership result held at n = 50.

**Drift comes apart from the reports.** In Rohde et al., drift toward the
rubber hand was as large when the hand was only seen, with no brushing, as
under synchronous brushing (about 6 cm). It was as large under asynchronous
brushing interrupted every 10 s. Only continuous asynchrony for 120 s kept
it near zero, while ownership by questionnaire needed synchrony. In
Abdulkarim & Ehrsson, a motor moved the hidden hand 8 cm toward or away
from the rubber hand. Pointing followed it by about 5–6 cm, and ownership
did not change with the direction of movement. This meets the second
refutation condition of [THEORY-tmpef8lf](THEORY-tmpef8lf.md), which this THEORY corrects.

**The standard controls do not equalise expectations.** People shown the
procedure but never stimulated expected the illusion statements more than
the control statements (d = 1.40). They also expected them more under
synchronous than asynchronous brushing (d = 1.05, n = 20; Lush 2020). In
Lush et al.'s sample, expectations were higher for synchrony, and that gap
did not vary with hypnotisability (Lush & Seth). An instruction that
asynchrony works best did not change expectations. Both sides call that
manipulation a failure.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say the stimulation causes the report difference.** Ehrsson
  et al. read the difference's independence from hypnotisability as
  evidence that it is perceptual. Lush & Seth show that uniform unequal
  expectations predict the same null. Neither side has run the experiment
  that would decide it, with expectations matched in stimulated
  participants. That is the promotion test.
- **It does not say the reports are suggestion.** Lush (2020) says plainly
  that his result is not evidence that the illusion is suggestion. Lush et
  al.'s pre-registered test of hypnotisability against the difference
  score was inconclusive.
- **It does not say drift is unrelated to ownership.** With the hand still,
  the classic drift and its correlation with ownership reports came back
  (Abdulkarim & Ehrsson, r = .562). Longo et al. found ownership and
  location each predicted drift. The claim is that drift is not a measure
  of the reports: it can be produced without them and moved without
  moving them. Both drift studies are small (groups of 10 and 18), and
  Abdulkarim & Ehrsson's null has no equivalence test.
- **It does not settle what the ownership item measures.** Longo et al.'s
  components and Kalckert & Ehrsson's dissociations rest on the same
  questionnaire logic the suggestion papers question.
- **It is about a hand.** Whole-body ownership is [THEORY-tmpuehzc](THEORY-tmpuehzc.md)'s
  question. The suggestion papers predict, without testing, the same
  confound for the full-body illusion.
