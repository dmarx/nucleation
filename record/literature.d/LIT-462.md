# LIT-462: The Self and the Future

Bernard Williams (1970), "The Self and the Future", *The Philosophical
Review* 79(2), April 1970, pp. 161–180 — DOI-10.2307/2183946

**On the citation.** The brief's details are all right: author, title,
journal, 1970, volume 79, issue 2, pages 161–180. Crossref gives the first
page and the month; the JSTOR cover page gives the range. `published:` is
the issue month, since no source gives a day. The paper was reprinted as
ch. 4 of Williams's *Problems of the Self* (Cambridge University Press,
1973), which was not seen here.

## Key takeaways

- **The third-person telling says the persons swap bodies.** A and B, told
  that one emerging person gets $100,000 and the other torture, choose
  selfishly. Whatever they choose, the reactions afterwards (complaints,
  satisfaction, recalled choices) make "a strong case for saying that the
  B-body-person really is A, and the A-body-person really is B" (p. 164).
  "The only rational thing to do … would be to identify oneself with one's
  memories, and so forth, and not with one's body" (p. 167).
- **The first-person telling says I am the one tortured.** Told that I will
  be tortured, then that I will first forget, then have my character
  changed, then receive another's "memories", fear stays the proper
  reaction at each step. "My undergoing physical pain in the future is not
  excluded by any psychological state I may be in at the time" (p. 169).
- **The step that completes the swap changes nothing for me.** In the
  series (i)–(vi), the last step, putting A's information into B, "is
  absolutely no difference at all in what happens to him, the only
  difference being in what happens to someone else" (p. 174).
- **Indeterminacy does not help.** "To be told that a future situation is a
  borderline one for its being myself that is hurt … is something which, it
  seems, I can do nothing with" (p. 175). Fear "seems neither appropriate,
  nor inappropriate, nor appropriately equivocal" (pp. 176–177).
  Conventionalist decision, fit for lawyers, "seems to have no sense at all"
  as a guide to one's own fears (p. 178).
- **The first-person argument supports the body, the third-person
  argument the mind**, against the expectation that the first person goes
  with the mental and the third person with the bodily (p. 179).
- **A shaky resolution.** The neat swap is artificial, produced by the
  experimenter's will, while the principle that fear reaches through
  psychological change "seems positively straightforward". So, deciding
  selfishly, A "should pass the pain to the B-body-person. It would be
  risky" (p. 180).

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, to fill out the record's
coverage of personal identity. [NOTE-387](../notes.d/NOTE-387.md) is the reading, and it placed
the work: **Active**. It is the standard first-person objection to the
psychological criterion and the source of the "risk" that Parfit's
"Personal Identity" sets out to dissolve. Read it as an argument about what
fear and expectation can follow, not as a theory of identity: Williams
claims no criterion, ends "not in the least clear which option it would be
wise to take", and calls his resolution "rather shaky".

No anthology topic holds it, it carries no instruction for machine-learning
practice, and the anthology does not hold it.

Where it meets the record:

- **Birch's teletransporter "with a twist"** ([LIT-111](LIT-111.md), [NOTE-183](../notes.d/NOTE-183.md)) recreates a
  person's states from a generative model without scanning, and Birch
  argues that this breaks the causal continuity Relation R needs. That is
  Williams's case (iv), "memory" impressions modelled on a real person but
  not caused by him. Williams argues that from the subject's side the
  causal link added in case (v) makes no difference to what he should
  expect (p. 173). The two readings meet on the same case and weigh the
  causal condition oppositely: Birch makes it decide who persists, Williams
  doubts that it can decide what one should fear. The comparison is mine.
- **Dennett's "Where Am I?"** ([LIT-405](LIT-405.md), [NOTE-338](../notes.d/NOTE-338.md)) is also told in the first
  person, and reaches the opposite verdict: the narrator follows his point
  of view, not his body. Williams's torture series is the reply a reader of
  Dennett could make, that a point of view can be changed by changing what
  is in it while the body goes on being hurt.
- **Damasio and Carvalho** ([LIT-388](LIT-388.md)). Williams chooses physical pain
  because it is "absolutely minimally dependent on character or belief"
  (p. 170). Their account of feelings as experiences of body states that
  report homeostatic value, not memory or character, is a scientific
  version of what makes pain the right test. The pairing is mine.
- **The survey** ([LIT-130](LIT-130.md), [NOTE-166](../notes.d/NOTE-166.md)) gives the biological criterion and
  animalism as the rival to the psychological one. This paper leans to the
  body from the first person, from what one may fear, not from biology.
  Olson's animalism, being filed in parallel by another agent, argues that
  we are animals on metaphysical grounds; the two reach the body by
  different routes.

Parfit's "Personal Identity" (1971), filed beside it, answers it in its
footnote 11. Lewis's "Survival and Identity" does not discuss it.
