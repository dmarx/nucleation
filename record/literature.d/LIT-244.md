# LIT-244: Natural Kinds (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

Alexander Bird, Emma Tobin (2008), *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (ed. Edward N. Zalta)* — <https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/natural-kinds/>

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, NOTE-tmp7mv59.*

- **Four questions, several commitments.** Naturalism (some
  classifications are natural), strong realism (kinds are entities) and
  essentialism come apart; "Intermediate views tend to be more popular".
- **Usefulness is not enough.** Shared natural properties, support for
  induction and a role in laws are each necessary but not sufficient:
  white things, charged things and Newton's law are the counterexamples.
  Inductions "often concern relations", but entering a natural relation
  does not make things a kind.
- **Cluster kinds.** Boyd's homeostatic property clusters have vague
  boundaries and no necessary property; Slater recasts them as clusters
  stable enough for inference. Critics say the mechanisms are
  undelineated or conventional.
- **Constructed but real.** Hacking: whether a constructed category is
  real is "subject to empirical investigation", as for child abuse,
  whose real instances were found, against satanic ritual abuse, whose
  were not. Social kinds are interactive: classified people change.
- **Epistemology first, and its limit.** Kinds judged by their role in
  scientific inference, against Lemeire's reply that epistemic success
  constrains conventional classifications too, and so cannot separate
  them from natural ones.
- **Essences.** The Kripke–Putnam arguments give at most partial
  essences; Salmon argues that essentialism follows from the semantics
  only with an essentialist premise. Bird (2020) holds that the semantics
  carries those assumptions innocently.
