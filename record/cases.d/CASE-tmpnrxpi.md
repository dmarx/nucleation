# CASE-tmpnrxpi: Tempe, Arizona, 18 March 2018: an Uber test vehicle whose developmental automated driving system relied on its sole operator to brake killed a pedestrian while the operator streamed video; the NTSB made her distraction the probable cause and the company's safety culture, which had removed the second operator and left automation complacency unaddressed, a contributing one

## The case

From the NTSB's Highway Accident Report NTSB/HAR-19/03, adopted 19
November 2019 (LIT-tmpmlje2, read for responsibility in NOTE-tmpokcvk).
Page numbers are the report's.

- **What happened.** At night on N. Mill Avenue, Tempe, an Uber Advanced
  Technologies Group test SUV under its developmental automated driving
  system struck and killed a pedestrian pushing a bicycle across the road
  outside a crosswalk. The system detected her 5.6 seconds before impact
  but "never accurately classified her as a pedestrian" or predicted her
  path (p. v).
- **The design.** It "precluded activation of emergency braking for
  collision mitigation, relying instead on the operator's intervention"
  (p. v). Uber had deactivated the Volvo's forward collision warning and
  automatic emergency braking, which "removed a layer of safety
  redundancy" (findings).
- **The organization.** Uber had removed the second vehicle operator,
  which "increased the task demands on the sole operator and also reduced
  the safety redundancies"; it "had the means to retroactively monitor the
  behavior of vehicle operators", but "rarely did so" (p. 45). It "did not
  adequately recognize the risk of automation complacency and develop
  effective countermeasures" (p. 44). It had no corporate safety plan
  (p. 46).
- **The operator.** "the operator was streaming a video for the entire
  trip, including the moments before the crash" (p. 42), against a
  prohibition she knew of (Landsberg, p. 62). Attentive, she "would likely
  have had sufficient time to detect and react" (p. 43).
- **The finding.** Probable cause: her distraction. Contributing: Uber
  ATG's risk assessment, operator oversight and lack of mechanisms for
  automation complacency, "all a consequence of its inadequate safety
  culture"; further, the impaired pedestrian's crossing and the Arizona
  Department of Transportation's oversight (p. v; p. 59). The culture
  contributed "specifically to the vehicle operator's extended
  distraction" (p. 46).

## What it can show

- **Control and duty apart.** The organization set a task "humans are
  very poor at" (p. 44), made the sole operator the only brake, and did
  not make the task feasible. That is the control-and-duty part of
  CLAIM-tmpb47ul, in an official finding.
- **A frontline failure that was partly her own.** The operator really did
  fail a duty she knew. The claim allows this, and A17 makes the point
  from this case: "A moral crumple zone can contain someone who is partly
  responsible for the failure."
- **A no-blame investigation that keeps upstream provenance**, for
  QUESTION-tmp2t6ve: the NTSB "does not assign fault or blame", and still
  names the company's culture and the state's oversight as contributing.

## What it cannot show

- **That blame landed on the operator.** The NTSB carried cause upstream
  itself. Whether she became a moral crumple zone is a question about
  prosecution and the press, which the report does not cover and the
  record has not read. As an allocation of cause, the report sits nearer
  the second branch of CLAIM-tmpb47ul's `defeated_if` than a crumple zone.
- **Elish's earlier reading** (LIT-tmpnmwlk, NOTE-tmps5u6y, p. 53) rested on
  the preliminary report, which left open that the operator was "looking
  down at the touch-screen"; the final report found her streaming video.
