# NOTE-tmpfj8cp: Group-level emotions

## Contribution

A five-page review of intergroup emotions theory and the work it has
generated. It states the theory's core in a paragraph, lists four
established facts, surveys consequences for out-groups and the in-group,
and points to emotion regulation as the new direction.

## Key insight

Appraisal theory said an emotion needs an object appraised as "affecting
me personally as an individual" (Arnold 1960, quoted p. 3). Intergroup
emotions theory replaces "me" with the group, by way of the self: "when
people identify with a group, the group identity becomes an aspect of the
self through self-categorization. Like any aspect of the self, the group
then becomes imbued with affective significance" (p. 3). The group's
fortunes are then appraised as one's own, and emotions follow.

## Assumptions

- **The social identity perspective** (p. 3): self-categorization makes a
  group membership part of the self.
- **Appraisal theory of emotion**: an emotion follows from appraising an
  event's implications, and the group can stand where the individual did.
- **Self-categorization and identification are distinct.** Categorization
  "sets the stage"; identification ("the importance and centrality of a
  group membership to the individual") moderates (p. 4).

## Key results

- **Definition** (p. 3): a group-based emotion is "experienced by
  individuals on behalf of a group to which they belong and with which
  they identify" (Niedenthal and Brauer 2012).
- **Four facts** (pp. 3–4).
  - Emotions follow the salient membership: more pride and less disgust
    thinking of oneself as a student of an admired university than as a
    citizen of a disliked country.
  - Members tend to share a profile, which acts as a norm they converge
    on; but sharing is not part of the definition.
  - Appraisals are group-level: people feel guilt for in-group misdeeds
    they did not commit (Doosje et al. 1998).
  - Identification moderates. The highly identified converge more on
    group norms, but may reappraise to avoid guilt, disappointment or
    fear about their group.
- **Toward out-groups** (pp. 5–6).
  - Group-based emotions predict collective action better than cognitive
    perceptions do.
  - Anger is the most studied and predicts aggression; contempt predicts
    it as strongly, and more often extreme, violent action, where anger
    predicts protest and exclusionary policy.
  - Positive emotions matter: a composite of positive emotions mediated
    contact's effect on prejudice more than negative ones (Miller et al.
    2004); admiration and respect mediated it more than anger in a US
    representative sample (Seger et al., unpublished).
  - Group-based emotions mediated apology's effect on forgiveness.
- **Toward the in-group** (pp. 6–8).
  - Members feel anger, fear or guilt when they disagree with the group's
    action, and these dissipate when the group acts as they wished.
  - The highly identified appraise in-group aggression as more justified
    and feel less guilt (Maitner et al. 2007).
  - Identification is multidimensional (Leach et al. 2008; Roccas et al.
    2008). Centrality should encourage group appraisals of all kinds;
    superiority or idealization should encourage positive in-group
    emotions and avoidance of negative ones.
  - Ties may be to the group as a whole or to its members (Brewer and
    Gardner 1996; Prentice et al. 1994), with possibly different emotions
    and actions; untested.
- **Regulation** (pp. 8–10).
  - Emotion-regulation interventions reduced support for punitive
    policies in the Israeli–Palestinian conflict (Halperin et al.).
  - Ideal levels of group anger shaped later experienced anger and policy
    support (Porat et al.).
  - Told that other in-group members felt little guilt for an in-group
    misdeed, participants felt more (Goldenberg et al. 2014).
  - Misattribution reduced group anger; manipulated norms moved members'
    emotions toward them.

## Concepts

- **group-based (group-level) emotion**: an emotion felt on behalf of a
  group one belongs to and identifies with.
- **group-based appraisal**: appraising an event for its implications
  for the group rather than for oneself.
- **identification**: the importance and centrality of a membership,
  distinct from being self-categorized in it.
- **emotional self-stereotyping**: converging on the group's typical
  emotion profile as a norm.
- **ties to the group versus ties to members**: a bond with the group's
  symbolic meaning, or through relationships with members.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** These are inferences; the
  paper does not discuss selflessness.
  - The theory's core is the thesis's mechanism stated for emotion. The
    group is "an aspect of the self", and so its fortunes are appraised as
    one's own. Group guilt for misdeeds one did not commit is the clearest
    case: a self-directed emotion follows the group, not the individual.
  - It separates two things the thesis runs together. Self-categorization
    fixes whose fortunes are appraised; identification, how much they
    matter. Both are needed for the emotion. The thesis's "larger self" is
    the first; caring about it is the second.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** The paper offers the
  separation the objection asks for: salient self-categorization as the
  self's extent, identification as caring, with distinct effects. The
  salience result is the useful one: the same person feels different
  emotions as the frame changes, with no change in what they care about.
  But the highly identified reappraise to avoid negative group emotions,
  so caring shapes the appraisal too, and the two are not independent.
- **A dated note could be added to [CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)** (or to
  [CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)). Proposed text: "Intergroup emotions theory, as Smith
  and Mackie (NOTE-tmpfj8cp) restate it, keeps self-categorization and
  identification apart: categorization makes the group 'an aspect of the
  self' and sets which events are appraised for it; identification, the
  membership's importance, moderates how strongly. Emotions shift with the
  salient category, which is a criterion of the self's extent that moves
  without a change in caring. But the highly identified reappraise group
  misdeeds to avoid guilt, so caring also feeds back into the appraisal."
- **No THEORY filed.** Intergroup emotions theory could be one, but the
  primary studies (Smith, Seger and Mackie 2007 on four criteria) are not
  held.

## Open questions

- Is a group-based emotion felt when a membership is salient but the
  person does not identify with it? The four facts imply yes, weakly; a
  study holding salience fixed and varying identification would show how
  far the two come apart.
- Do people feel group-based emotions for a close-relationship "we" in
  the same way, or only for categories?
