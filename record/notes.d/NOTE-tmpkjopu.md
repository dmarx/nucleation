# NOTE-tmpkjopu: Frankish — Closing the Cartesian Bureau de Change

## Contribution

In *A Philosophy for the Science of Animal Consciousness*, Walter Veit gives a Darwinian framework for consciousness. He separates its dimensions, maps their distribution across species, and traces them to an ancient system of hedonic valence that evolved for action selection in early animals. Frankish places this within a "post-Cartesian" paradigm that treats consciousness as a complex functional state open to ordinary science, as opposed to the Cartesian focus on irreducibly subjective qualia and the hard problem. He detects residual Cartesianism in Veit's use of "qualia" and in the suggestion that valenced states do their work through their "feel", which implies a private "Cartesian bureau de change" where intrinsic value is felt. He urges Veit to be thoroughly functionalist, since the two paradigms are incommensurable.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- "The Cartesian Paradigm" (pp. 17–18): Block, Chalmers and Nagel treat qualia as intrinsic and non-functional. This produces a hard problem and an explanatory gap, rules out evolutionary explanation (anything causally efficacious is a functional posit), and forces a human-centred extrapolation to animals with no scientific way to settle it.
- "The Post-Cartesian Paradigm" (p. 18): illusionism or qualia eliminativism (Dennett; Frankish 2017). Experiences are functional states, the question is "what consciousness does" (Dennett's "hard question"), introspection gets no special authority, and experiences are real but not irreducibly subjective.
- "Veit's Darwinian Standard" (pp. 18–19): Veit's evolutionary-biology standard, decentring of introspection and functional questions put him in the new paradigm.
- "The Pull of Cartesian Gravity" (pp. 19–20): the terminological charge is that "qualia" and "phenomenal" are philosophers' terms defined against function and treated as introspectively fixed (the role they play in IIT). Frankish proposes coining a neutral term ("quasi-phenomenal properties") instead of repurposing them.
- "A Cartesian Bureau de Change?" (p. 20): Veit's minimal hedonic valence ("a proximate common currency") is described as a "commanding sensation" and a "felt common currency". If valence motivates through intrinsic feel, then "a tiny one-dimensional Cartesian bureau de change" leaves the hard problem as hard as ever. Frankish suspects Veit of "fudging" to appeal to Cartesians, since the feel of a physical state is not needed for its functional role. If "feel" is metaphorical, Veit should say so and give a positive naturalistic account of how valence motivates. Conclusion (pp. 20–21): "The Cartesian and post-Cartesian paradigms are incommensurable".

## Open questions

- A crisp statement of the illusionist, functionalist pole, useful as a foil to [LIT-tmpasglk](../literature.d/LIT-tmpasglk.md) (Seth's biological naturalism, which keeps a real explanatory gap in play) and to [LIT-tmprityc](../literature.d/LIT-tmprityc.md) (IIT, whose axioms Frankish says rest on introspection).
- The "bureau de change" argument generalizes to any account on which valence motivates by its feel, including proposals about AI welfare that turn on valenced experience ([LIT-tmp75cds](../literature.d/LIT-tmp75cds.md), [LIT-tmp4tk05](../literature.d/LIT-tmp4tk05.md)).
