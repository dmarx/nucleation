# Article 8. What We Owe Through Cooperation

## Rights, demands, commitments, and the legitimate governance of relational becoming

**Abstract.** Cooperation changes its participants. It creates capacities, dependencies and relations of authority that alter the conditions on which they act next, and it gives some of them power over others. This article argues that what cooperation owes its participants cannot be read off its success, and that much of it cannot be read off its outcomes either. Rights, demands and commitments are distinct, and none establishes another; so neither the joint satisfiability of a scheme's demands nor its viability certifies that its terms are owed. A right that protects a choice constrains how a decision may be reached, and two arrangements can reach the same endpoint while one respects such a right and the other defeats it. On that basis the article examines the proposal that defeating a party's standing to contest is a wrong of its own, even where the objection would have failed, and the instrumentalist objection that standing matters only for the harm it prevents. It shows that the argument for the wrong can be built without any difference in actual or expected harm, but that it carries a legal and moral judgement the objection does not share, so the dispute is located rather than settled. It then bounds answerability: influence does not ground it, it does not require direct authority, and responsibility for a dependence does not confer authority over the dependent. It sorts the grounds proposed for special obligation (membership, induced reliance, vulnerability, entrusted authority, control over essential conditions, associative principles), shows how matched cases tell them apart, and finds that none is established. It argues that repair does not wait on culpability, and that a regime which penalizes the recognition of violations can, in a model, make an institution stop looking for them. Last, it argues that morality evaluates the terms of cooperation without being confined to cooperators. Most of these steps are interpretive proposals; the article says where.

## 8.1 Introduction

A cooperative scheme that works produces a great deal: joint output, mutual reliance, specialized roles, people who could not now do without it. It is tempting to think that it also produces its own justification, that the scheme's continuing to work shows that its terms are acceptable to those who keep it working. The tempting thought has a respectable form. If everyone's demands can be met together, and the arrangement persists, what further question is there about what it owes?

This article argues that there are several further questions, and that they have a common structure. Each concerns a relation between a party and an arrangement that the arrangement's outcomes do not reveal: whether a choice the party is entitled to make would have been respected had it been made; whether a party could have contested an act before it became irreversible; whether a party's claim had any route by which to count; whether a party's dependence gives anyone an obligation toward it. None of these is settled by the scheme's working. Some are not settled by any record of what happened.

The argument runs in the order its steps depend on one another. §8.2 separates rights, demands and commitments, and draws the consequence that success does not certify terms. §8.3 shows what an evaluation of outcomes cannot see, using a construction on the right to refuse treatment. §8.4 applies that to the proposed wrong of defeated standing, in two forms: by timing, where an act outruns the objection to it, and by representation, where the objection arrives and has no category to arrive in. §8.5 sets out the instrumentalist objection and says how far the reply goes. §8.6 treats standing and answerability as directed relations and sets three limits on what they imply. §8.7 examines the grounds proposed for special obligation. §8.8 separates repair from blame. §8.9 places morality relative to cooperation. §8.10 says what the argument does not establish and states the strongest objection; §8.11 concludes.

Two features of the material should be said at the start. First, the argument is normative, and most of its constructions are stipulated: they isolate the case on which a verdict must be given, and none shows that an institution behaves so. Where a result comes from a model, the article says so. Second, the positive steps beyond the frame of §8.2 are proposals, several of them contested by objections that have not been answered; the text marks them as it goes.

## 8.2 Rights, demands and commitments

Begin with three things that are often run together. A right is a normative position a party holds. A demand is something asserted, maintained or recognized. A commitment is an undertaking. They are distinct, and none establishes another: "a right does not depend on being demanded or on anyone's commitment to it, a demand's being asserted, maintained or recognized gives it no warrant, and a commitment is neither an obligation nor an authorization, while an obligation persists without commitment; each passage between them, a right generating a demand or a valid undertaking generating a right, needs its own warrant" (CLAIM-738). The claim is not that the three never give rise to one another. A promise can create a claim, and a right can ground a demand. The claim is that each such passage needs a warrant of its own, and that none follows from the bare presence of the other term.

Several consequences sort what may pass between the three.

A demand owed to a party "can cease to apply legitimately only by the exercise of a valid normative power over it"; an institution that stops counting a demand "changes only the set it recognizes" (CLAIM-743). Likewise, succeeding in making a remedy unreachable "gains a wrongdoer no normative power over what is owed": the claim persists while the correctability of its remedy falls to zero, and a settlement reached under the conditions the violation created does not by that fact authorize it (CLAIM-722; it rests on CLAIM-464).

There are also limits on what a holder of power can confer. "No delegation, contract, collective decision or success in imposing an arrangement thereby confers authority to determine whether that person keeps the standing from which claims against the arrangement arise" (CLAIM-720). It bears on whether rights that no institution creates or can extinguish can serve as the criteria such judgements need (QUESTION-486). The reading of Locke on which this draws supports the claim's form but not its scope (ARG-117), and whether standing can be forfeited, as Locke allows, is open (SYNTHESIS-102).

Rights may come first in the order of institutions even where principles come first in the order of justification: "rights can be prior in organization to later exercises of authority even where principles are prior to them in justification" (CLAIM-735).

Duties toward institutions divide in the same way. The duties to comply with a just arrangement, to support it and to help establish one are proposed as distinct, the third "bounded by whether the needed arrangement can be reached from the present one by permissible, resource-bounded transitions" (CLAIM-729). The first separation is contested: the duties to comply and to support "may be one duty, as Rawls's natural duty treats them, and are not shown distinct until a case separates them" (CLAIM-752). No case separating them has been built.

The step the rest of the article needs most follows from the first distinction. If a demand's being maintained gives it no warrant, then a scheme in which every maintained demand is met has not thereby shown that any of them deserves to be met. Put exactly, "the joint satisfiability of demands cannot certify that any deserves fulfilment, and an institution's maintained requirements cannot certify their own authority" (CLAIM-725). Two companion results make the same point at other levels. "Viability does not establish what ought to persist": an unjust institution may be highly viable (CLAIM-075). And "an encompassing organization's success does not establish the well-being, viability or autonomy of its constituents" (CLAIM-060). Each holds in its own scope: demands and maintained requirements, viability, success at one scale against well-being at another. Taken together they say what this article's opening thought denied: successful cooperation cannot establish the legitimacy of its own terms. That sentence adds nothing to the three; it restates them.

The same structure, a passage that needs its own warrant, governs reliance. Being expected, relied on, committed and owing are distinct, and "reliance makes something owed only under a further principle" (CLAIM-tmp0i6c0; Article 3 develops it for implicit contracts). Reliance returns in §8.7 as one of the proposed grounds of special obligation.

## 8.3 What an evaluation of outcomes cannot see

The second step concerns rights of a particular kind: those that protect a choice rather than secure an outcome. A fishing quota or a water allocation is an outcome requirement, and it fits a set of acceptable outcomes. A right of refusal does not. Whether an arrangement respects a right that protects a choice "is a property of its policy over the protected choices, not of any realized history" (CLAIM-741).

The claim rests on two separations, each shown by a construction in which everything but the feature at issue is held fixed.

The first separates endpoints from histories. Two routes can end in the same arrangement, one by proposal, authorization and change, the other by unilateral change, foreclosed alternatives and acceptance. "The substantive outcome may be identical. The provenance of authority is not." An evaluation of endpoints cannot tell them apart; an evaluation of histories can (CLAIM-741). The point is not new to this argument: judgements about an organization can depend on a history its end state does not show (ARG-043; CLAIM-608).

The second separates histories from policies, and it is the distinctive one. Take a competent patient with an applicable right of refusal and no justified exception (CASE-283). Arrangement A treats on consent and refrains on refusal. Arrangement B treats either way. On the history in which the patient consents, both produce the same history, consent followed by treatment, and "any evaluation that is a function of the realized history gives the same verdict to both". Yet A respects the right at both values of the patient's choice, and B fails it on refusal. "If the patient always consents, no observed history ever separates them" (CASE-283). Respecting such a right "may require preserving a conditional response to choices that the holder never actually makes" (CLAIM-741).

Three limits are part of the result. It covers only rights that protect choices, and it does not say which rights those are: "What it cannot show: which rights protect choices rather than outcomes, or that any actual institution behaves like B" (CASE-283). Observation runs one way: "An observed refusal overridden would show B's policy; consent observed, however often, cannot show A's" (CASE-283). And the demonstration is formal. It is a fact about two stipulated policies, not a finding about any institution. The construction has been checked only by the one who built it, and no objection to the claim has been filed; the natural one would be that rights of refusal protect only outcomes. The rest of the article leans on the claim's first separation more than its second, and the first is the less contestable of the two.

The right's defeater names "a right of refusal (or of appeal, or an easement)" among those it covers (CLAIM-741). A right of appeal is a standing to contest. That brings the result to the next section.

## 8.4 Defeated standing

### 8.4.1 The criterion

Consider an actor who proceeds with an act while a party still has standing to object, and who makes the act's consequences irreversible before the objection can take effect. The proposal under examination is that this is a wrong of its own, apart from acting without consent: "where a party has standing to authorize, suspend or contest an act, an actor who without adequate justification makes the act's consequences irreversible before that standing can be exercised defeats it, even where the objection would have failed" (CLAIM-723). The wrong has four conditions, and needs all four: the party held a protected standing; "the act made that standing ineffective before it could be exercised"; the defeat is attributable to "the actor's conduct, timing, withholding of notice or control over execution"; and "nothing justified proceeding without it" (CLAIM-723). Irreversibility alone does not make a wrong.

The causal half of this is accepted on independent grounds: "An intervention can change what it would take to correct it", which "says nothing about intent" (CLAIM-464). What the criterion adds is the wrong. Its nearest legal analogue is Fuentes v. Shevin (1972), on which the right to be heard "does not depend upon an advance showing that one will surely prevail" (THEORY-1972). The analogue has two weaknesses that matter here. The opinion supports the criterion "as a legal analogue, not as a moral principle" (CLAIM-723); and the Court called the decision a narrow one, whose later doctrinal standing has not been checked (CLAIM-723; THEORY-1972). So the criterion is an interpretive proposal with one legal analogue, not a settled principle.

### 8.4.2 The occasion of the wrong, by timing

The second condition, that the act became irreversible before the standing could be exercised, has a mechanism that can be modelled, and the modelling shows how readily the occasion of the wrong arises when one side can act faster than the other can respond.

The test was set before it was run: "Oversight that loses effect as speed rises, with authority and resources fixed, supports CLAIM-710 and CLAIM-464; no effect of speed defeats them" (LEAD-068). The run is a queueing model in which an overseer keeps "the same authority, information and reviewers at every speed" and can suspend an act until it becomes irreversible (CASE-316). Over 20 seeds, "at ten times the speed the share of objectionable actions stopped before irreversibility fell from 0.994 to 0.396, already 0.711 at five times"; with volume multiplied as well, it fell to 0.043 (CLAIM-879). In that model, then, the criterion set beforehand was met. The rates are invented, and the result is a model result.

An analytic result gives the same shape in general form. When challengeable decisions arrive faster than a corrective process can resolve them, "the formal right to challenge stays with everyone while its preventive effect cannot": with valid challenges arriving at rate λ and resolved at rate μ < λ, "at most a share μ/λ can ever be resolved in time, whatever the order of review" (CLAIM-705). Within that bound, "the order of review decides whose right to challenge still prevents anything", so that under insufficient capacity, "scheduling distributes preventive rights" (CLAIM-704). Both are derivations in idealized processes.

The general thesis these instantiate is that an arrangement "can keep, or increase, its capacity for effective action while its capacity to be effectively corrected by others degrades, because the two are maintained by different relations, so the first is no evidence of the second" (CLAIM-462). The model illustrates it and its finding corroborates it (CASE-316; CLAIM-879). The criterion of defeated standing and the proposed study from which the model descends are already treated together as remedies foreclosed before review (SYNTHESIS-098).

What the model measures must be stated exactly. It counts how often an objectionable act becomes irreversible before an objection can take effect. That is the second condition of the wrong, for objectionable acts only, met by timing alone. The model has no party harmed by the act: the party with standing is the overseer, whose standing is its power to suspend. It does not count objections that would have failed, which are the cases the criterion is distinctive for; it does not model attribution, and lists "Intent to outrun review" among what it cannot show; and it does not model justification (CASE-316; CLAIM-723). It therefore measures how often the occasion of the wrong arises as speed rises, and which remedies, such as precommitted limits and automatic suspension, win it back. It does not measure the wrong. An extension that adds an affected party who petitions to revise a precommitted limit, and measures the harm done to that party before the limit can be revised, is specified and not yet run (LEAD-216).

### 8.4.3 The wrong, by representation

The second form of defeat involves no race. An organization that harms, and also reproduces the representations that make its harm illegible, is proposed to commit "a counter-normativity distinct from the harm" (CLAIM-614). The question this raises is what exactly that wrong is, what makes it reproductive, and when it is the organization's own rather than its participants' (QUESTION-441). One answer is given from the side of duty: the wrong is a breach of "a second-order duty of answerability", and "a verdict only given an independently justified standard and a feasible alternative" (CLAIM-624). More of this part of the argument relies on it than on any other proposal. Its line of argument is drawn together with two other routes by which an organization can lose sight of what it owes, the exit of the harmed and discovery it is penalized for, routes kept apart (SYNTHESIS-094).

The answer this article examines is given from the side of the claimant, as a statement of the wrong apart from the harm (LEAD-097). "Where those an organization harms have standing to contest the harm with it, reproducing the projections that keep their claims from registering wrongs them beyond the harm, in that standing", and so the wrong "holds where contesting would not have changed the harm's course" (CLAIM-1155). The claim keeps three things apart: H, the harm; C, "the further harm that follows because the harm goes unrecognized and so uncorrected"; and D, the defeat of standing. It holds only under three conditions. Those affected have standing, which the claim takes as a condition and does not derive from the harm's being a wrong, since a right does not by itself generate a warranted demand (CLAIM-738). The organization reproduces the projection as part of its self-maintenance. And a feasible alternative representation exists; where none does, "the claim says nothing" (CLAIM-1155). It rests on three accepted premises: that a contribution's admission, recognition, credibility, uptake and part in revising standards "are separable" (CLAIM-403); that adequacy of a description "is fixed by the evaluative target", so a description adequate for an institution's performance can merge states its members' claims must distinguish (CLAIM-348); and that normative efficacy needs separate achievements of legibility, volitional efficacy and correctability (CLAIM-523).

The inference is a matched-outcome construction (ARG-228). Two water utilities have both contaminated a town's supply in an incident that is over. A regulator testing "on its own schedule and not on complaints" triggers a compensation scheme that pays "whatever either utility does". The first utility's complaint taxonomy registers contamination-related illness. The second's "merges contamination-related illness with pre-existing conditions, so the same complaint is filed as a non-incident". The harm H is matched, and so is the further harm C, actual and expected: "Here the match holds ex ante." That closes the gap left by an earlier construction, in which one party's missing procedure "would raise the risk of later harm" (CASE-229; ARG-055), so that the difference could be put down to expected harm. Here only D differs. The new element is the mechanism: "the objection arrives and has no category to arrive in" (CLAIM-1155). The construction's last step then carries over the judgement of §8.4.1, from the criterion and from Fuentes, to this case (ARG-228).

Two limits are written into the claim. If being unheard is counted as a setback of its own, so that D is a harm, the claim that hiding the harm is a wrong beyond it still survives, because what would defeat that claim is reduction to the harm's continuation, not to harm in general (CLAIM-1155; CLAIM-614). And who bears the wrong, the agents who control an arrangement or an institution that is itself an agent, is left open between two alternatives (CLAIM-876; CLAIM-877; LEAD-131). Whether CLAIM-1155 is a thesis of its own, or CLAIM-624 stated from the claimant's side, is also open.

### 8.4.4 Why the defeat is invisible in outcomes, and how far that goes

That defeated standing can matter even where its proper exercise would not have changed the result can look like a puzzle. §8.3 dissolves the puzzle, though not in the way one might first expect.

Where the objection would have failed, the arrangement that respects a standing to contest and the one that defeats it reach the same endpoint. They do so by different histories: in one the objection is heard and refused, in the other the act is made irreversible first; in the utilities, one taxonomy registers the complaint and the other files it as a non-incident. So the case is one of the first separation, not the second. "Where the objection would have failed, respecting and defeating a standing to contest reach the same endpoint by different histories (CLAIM-741's first separation), so an evaluation of endpoints cannot represent the defeat and one of histories can; only where no objection is made does the difference lie in policy alone (its second separation)" (CLAIM-741). A standard over histories can represent the defeat directly, by requiring that no step be made irreversible before the window for objection closes (CLAIM-523).

This is an application of CLAIM-741, not a new inference. A parallel application, that a categorical gate fails a right to individualized consideration at the level of policy (CLAIM-745), was tested as a candidate claim and declined for that reason, as "a substitution instance of CLAIM-741's second separation … an inference CLAIM-741 already holds" (SYNTHESIS-102). The present application is of the same kind, and is stated as such.

What follows is limited. The phrase "even where the objection would have failed" names what a constraint on histories looks like when the endpoint is fixed, and what a constraint on policy looks like when no objection is made. It is not a paradox. But the result is about representation: what an evaluation over endpoints or histories can see. It does not say that failing such a constraint wrongs anyone beyond the harm it is expected to cause. An instrumentalist can accept all of §8.3 and still evaluate histories and policies by their expected harm. So the separations explain why the defeat is invisible in outcomes. They do not settle whether it is a wrong. That is the work of the next section.

## 8.5 The instrumentalist objection

The strongest objection to the wrong of defeated standing is this: "Standing to contest a harm matters only as a means of preventing or remedying harm, so defeating it wrongs those affected only through the harm it lets continue or risks, and where the harm's course is fixed by causes contest could not touch, reproducing the projections that defeat it adds no wrong beyond the harm" (CLAIM-1184). It is stated in four premises and at its strongest. The second already meets the reply that the outcome was unchanged: "ex ante, a missing channel raises expected harm". Its best support is the practice it generalizes: "Courts let an error stand when it made no difference to the outcome, and the cost of a procedure is weighed against the harm it averts" (CLAIM-1184).

The construction of §8.4.3 is the reply (ARG-228). It concedes the case the objection's third premise describes, a harm whose course, actual and expected, is fixed by causes contest could not touch, and builds it. It denies the first premise, that a procedure for contest is valuable "because, and to the extent that, it prevents harm, stops it, or remedies it". Harmless-error practice does not carry that premise: "it governs whether a judgement is reversed, not whether the party was wronged, and an error found harmless is still found to be an error" (CLAIM-1184). The objection says what would revive it, where "the record's judgements" are the ones the construction carries over: "a showing that the record's judgements in CLAIM-723 and THEORY-1972 are themselves only judgements of expected harm under uncertainty, so that once the uncertainty is removed, as the construction removes it, the judgement goes with it" (CLAIM-1184).

That revival condition aims at the reply's weak point. The construction does not argue from premises the instrumentalist shares that D is a wrong. Its last step carries over a judgement already made, and that judgement rests on the criterion of §8.4.1, itself a proposal, and on one reading of one narrow decision. What the construction achieves is real but specific: it removes every instrumental difference, so that anyone who still judges the second utility to have wronged the townspeople cannot be judging expected harm. Whether that judgement survives the removal is exactly what the objection denies. The objection is therefore neither answered by an argument in the series nor shown sound. The dispute has been located, at the question whether the judgement in Fuentes and in the criterion is a judgement about harm under uncertainty, and not settled.

The structure of the reply is not peculiar to standing. A general form is derived for matched constructions of this kind: they discriminate between an instrumental and a non-instrumental account "only when they hold fixed what a power's holder would do with it", and there "the verdict turns on whether a reason to hold or share a power, apart from its use, is an input" the account admits (CLAIM-909). The construction of §8.4.3 is one of that form, for standing to contest. The other instance comes from the contractualist side: variants of a set of matched constructions fix a decider "known to be error-free" or a sole reviser "known to enact exactly what the subjects would vote for", so that "only the allocation differs" (CASE-336; CLAIM-909). Of those variants, the one that varies who controls review is nearer to standing to contest than the one that varies who holds the power to revise rules. Three readings of them are kept as alternatives: that the subjects' lacking a share "is no less legitimate", the sense of a defect being "a residue of protective reasons that no longer apply" (CLAIM-913); that there is "a personal reason of status, not to live under another's sole say" (CLAIM-911); and that the verdict comes only "by importing a principle of standing" (CLAIM-912). The two instrumentalisms, CLAIM-1184 and CLAIM-913, are of one form; whether they stand or fall together is not established, since standing to be heard on a harm is not a share of a power to revise.

## 8.6 Standing and answerability as directed relations

If a party can be wronged in its standing, then standing must be something a party has toward someone, and answerability must be owed by someone to someone. This section sets out what such directed relations require and what they do not imply.

The requirements separate. For a normatively relevant discrepancy to change what an organization does, "three distinct achievements are needed and any can fail while the others hold: legibility …; volitional efficacy …; and correctability", and "none establishes that the demand or the revision is justified" (CLAIM-523). Applied to domination, these are "needed, not … sufficient", and they meet its third condition "only together with a failure of exit" (CLAIM-686; CLAIM-682). For an affected party, explanation, contestation and interruption "are separate entitlements", with "contestation without interruption a right that arrives too late" (CLAIM-740). An objection is proposed to be effective only if six achievements hold together, from its becoming formulable to its correction being maintained, so that it can fail at any of them "while its formal standing is unchanged" (CLAIM-732). And "authority over a decision does not carry authority over the conditions on which it may be challenged" (CLAIM-727).

What a standing is, positively, is proposed as a constraint rather than an input: "to have standing toward an agent is to have claims that can constrain what it is entitled to optimize, not only to enter its objective" (CLAIM-tmpa61rf). An objective that admits other parties only through their contribution to it excludes their standing, whatever it knows of them.

When answerability is required of an authority, to whom and why (QUESTION-480), is answered by a proposal that it is directed and role-specific. "An actor owes an answer to a party with a claim concerning the matter, on one of several grounds that need not presuppose the actor's legitimate authority, among them interference with a protected interest, entrusted authority, collective rule, consequential dependence and control over recourse", and "what is owed is indexed to the claim, not to how much the party is affected" (CLAIM-739; TERM-126). What is protected is contestability, "not a guarantee of success, a veto or costless adjudication" (TERM-130). The proposal lists grounds without saying why anyone is owed an answer at all, and that root question is open (SYNTHESIS-103; QUESTION-485, which asks from where a relationship, capability or restriction derives its legitimacy).

Three limits bound the power that cooperation gives its participants over one another. They are separate, and each is a denial that one thing implies another; their conjunction adds nothing to them.

- **Influence does not ground answerability.** "Influence over others does not by itself ground a duty to answer to them" (CLAIM-749). A claim that the accountability of those who govern an arrangement "should rise with how indispensable the arrangement has become" concedes it (CLAIM-687).
- **Responsibility does not confer authority.** A qualification on the vulnerability proposal of §8.7 says it: "Creating a dependency can generate or strengthen responsibilities without granting legitimate authority over the dependent"; otherwise "an actor could gain moral authority simply by making others need it" (CLAIM-671).
- **Standing does not require direct authority.** An institution "can still respect the standing and claims of its constituents without giving them direct authority to effect change within the institution (e.g. effective representative governance vs. captured)" (CLAIM-tmpsh3og).

The third limit corrects a formulation that ran the two together. On that formulation, the profile the word sociopathy points at is an institution that makes others' circumstances legible to itself and uses that legibility for its own objective "while their justified claims gain no authority over what it does" (CLAIM-tmptrn75). Read as direct authority, that describes every well-run representative institution. The correction separates three things: direct authority, where the constituents can themselves change what the institution does; effective weight, where their claims constrain what it does through their own authority or through processes that represent them; and legibility (CLAIM-tmpsh3og). It then splits the term. Asymmetric legibility, the institution reading those it affects while they hold no direct authority, is "a structural condition that makes institutional sociopathy possible" and does not constitute it (TERM-tmp383al). The vice is the condition in which the claims of those it affects "carry no effective weight in what it does by any route, direct or representative; typically arising where legibility is asymmetric and the processes meant to carry the claims are captured" (TERM-tmp72sq3). The contrast of effective and captured representative governance is the separating pair: alike in asymmetric legibility and in the constituents' lack of direct authority, different only in whether the representing processes carry their claims (CLAIM-tmpsh3og).

For this article the consequence is a constraint on how it may speak. That those affected have no direct say does not by itself show a defect in an arrangement of power. What has to be shown is that their claims carry no weight by any route. The distinction is testable in principle, and it fails if "'effective weight' cannot be told apart from direct authority by any observation" (CLAIM-tmpsh3og). The vice itself is the subject of a later article.

## 8.7 The grounds of special obligation

Cooperation is thought to generate special obligations, owed to participants and not to outsiders. On what ground? The question has two parts that must be kept apart: whether an organized whole grounds norms concerning its parts, and if so who bears the obligations, "the whole, where it is the kind of thing that can owe, or the agents and roles that maintain, control or benefit from the arrangement" (QUESTION-460). A whole "can be the ground or object of a normative judgment without necessarily being the bearer of the corresponding obligation" (QUESTION-460). Six grounds are candidates: membership, induced reliance, vulnerability, entrusted authority, control over essential conditions and associative principles. They stand very differently.

**Membership.** The question whether membership adds anything can be put to a matched pair. Hold fixed "a person's vulnerability, the controlling actors' capacities and the institutional consequences", and let the person be a constituent in one case and not in the other, "to test whether membership adds an obligation" (CASE-256). Any of three results was to count as an answer (LEAD-110). Worked through by argument, the pair yields two readings, kept as alternatives. On the first, "for a whole that is a bare relational arrangement, being a constituent adds no obligation beyond those grounded in the constituent's vulnerability, interests, rights and exposure to power … because such an arrangement is not an agent the obligation falls on the agents or roles that maintain, control or benefit from it" (CLAIM-960; ARG-207). Its bearer step rests on the accepted claim that enacting a perspective does not make an independent agent (CLAIM-418), and is supported in demonstrated form by the result that no single institution's inability to bring about a remedy implies that no combination of them can (CLAIM-457). The first reading says only that bare constitution does not ground an obligation; it does not say what does. On the second, "where membership in a whole is normatively thick … constitution can ground an obligation the whole owes a constituent that it owes no outsider, under an independently justified principle of associative obligation" (CLAIM-959). No such principle has been adopted, and the second reading is conditional on one.

The two readings are scoped to different wholes, so part of the contest between them is a difference of scope. The readings that bear on it point both ways. Thompson (2022; THEORY-1814) holds that the obligations of group agents persist through changes of membership and pass to successors, and Fineman (2010; THEORY-1818) that a state which brings institutions into legal existence is responsible for monitoring them; both concern collective agents. Kittay (THEORY-1823) holds that "nested relations of dependency are the basis of society" and that support for carers is owed by the wider society. Against them, Lovett and Riedener (2021; THEORY-1819) argue that organizations "have no moral status", and that what we seem to owe them "we owe to their members". The prior question, whether a whole that is not an agent can owe anything, is untouched, and nothing establishes "that any whole, in any sense, owes its parts anything" (SYNTHESIS-092).

**Induced reliance.** Reliance makes something owed only under a further principle (CLAIM-tmp0i6c0), and the conditions under which it does, "only when the reliance was knowingly induced, when it was reasonable …", are an open question (QUESTION-tmprudey). Article 3 treats them.

**Vulnerability and control.** The proposal here is conditional by design: "Where an organizational transformation increases a constituent's vulnerability to the discretionary choices of a responsible agent, and that vulnerability grounds special obligations under an independently justified principle, the transformation can strengthen that agent's responsibilities" (CLAIM-671). Integration need not increase vulnerability, and an argument that it does so in general has been rejected (ARG-099). The principle the claim needs is Goodin's, whose sources could not be obtained (LIT-2531). What sorts a dependence, in a companion proposal, is not its magnitude but "who controls the terms on which it is met" (CLAIM-690). Its ground is a fictional bargaining model in which the part's share "responds to the whole's need of P by one half in A and not at all in B" (CASE-264). And the proposal keeps the bearer apart from the ground: "control fixes on whom a strengthened responsibility falls, not whether the principle that grounds it holds" (CLAIM-690). Control can also erode the capacity to govern what is controlled: where the capacities a collective needs are learned by exercise and lost with disuse, and the arrangement does the work through which they are exercised, "whether a collective can correct an arrangement is not independent of the arrangement" (CLAIM-673).

**Entrusted authority and control over essential conditions.** Each appears in the argument, but not as a ground of obligation. Entrusted authority is one of the grounds of answerability in §8.6 (CLAIM-739). Control over essential conditions is a condition of domination (CLAIM-682) and, as above, what sorts where a strengthened responsibility falls (CLAIM-690). Whether either grounds obligations to those affected in their own right, beyond those their vulnerability, interests and rights ground, is an open question (QUESTION-tmpeiurf).

**Associative principles.** These are the supposition of the second membership reading (CLAIM-959), and they are not read. Neither is the principle of fair play, on which those who accept the benefits of a cooperative scheme owe their share of its burdens (Hart 1955; LIT-tmpfhd8f; Rawls 1964; LIT-tmp4lk5u), nor its standard critique (Simmons 1979, which is not registered). A reading of them, for whether membership in a cooperative scheme grounds obligations of its own, is specified and not done (LEAD-tmphsmng). The nearest text read is Rawls's natural duty as Scanlon cites it, which appears as an objection to the separation of the duties to comply and to support (CLAIM-752), not as a ground of special obligation.

The result of this section is therefore a method and a map, not a ground. It would be false to say that constitutive participation has been shown to ground duties: that requires normative justification, not evidence of dependence. What the argument holds is how to tell the candidate grounds apart, by matched cases that fix all but one (CASE-256; CASE-264), and where the bearer falls once a ground is supplied (CLAIM-960; CLAIM-690). It does not hold any ground. Two of the candidates (CLAIM-959; CLAIM-671) wait on principles that have not been read.

## 8.8 Repair without blame

A scheme that harms owes repair. Does it owe it only where someone is to blame? The proposal here is that it does not: "an agent's diminished culpability for the reasons it acts on does not diminish the wrongfulness of what it does, the claims of those it harms, or the need for correction, because the grounds of correction are not exhausted by the grounds of blame" (CLAIM-742). The distinction behind it is that blame and accountability are not the same: an agent may not be at fault for holding the reasons it holds and still be accountable for the effects of acting on them. The claim leaves open "that the non-culpable agent is not the one who owes the correction", and its use of a five-way distinction among senses of responsibility depends on a term not yet adopted (TERM-127).

Two proposals rest on it. Blame and repair "do not substitute for each other", and their failures are blame displacement and remedial scapegoating (CLAIM-717). And blame "gives us provenance for holding upstream actors accountable as well" (CLAIM-733). The same thesis holds from the side of incapacity: a present incapacity to recognize a violation does not show that the incapacitated party is not responsible for it, and "where no one is culpable for the incapacity the duty to repair still stands" (CLAIM-714). A matched construction shows the separation of duty from path: two institutions produce the same wrongful outcome through the same defective procedure with blameless decision-makers, and "the duty to address the wrong is the same and the corrective paths differ, assigned without inventing blame" (CASE-284). Who owes the correction in any given case is not settled, and neither is the general question under what conditions responsibility to repair "survive[s] the absence, uncertainty or dispersion of blameworthiness" (SYNTHESIS-104; QUESTION-470). The sentence that obligations of repair and correction need not depend on individual culpability restates CLAIM-742 with CLAIM-717; it does not say who owes the repair.

If blame supplies provenance, does giving up blame give up the trace upstream? That is the question blameless post-incident practice in engineering poses (QUESTION-476). One specimen shows that provenance can be carried without blame. An investigator barred from assigning blame or liability traced a test-vehicle operator's distraction to her company's safety culture and the state's oversight, and addressed its recommendations to them, "so blame is one carrier of upstream provenance and not its only source" (CLAIM-751; CASE-292). The same crash also shows where a blaming process stopped: the criminal process stopped at the operator while the safety investigation named the company and the state (CLAIM-1005). But the contrast there "is between the criminal law and a safety investigation, not between blame and its absence" (CLAIM-1033), so it shows where criminal law stopped, not where blame as such stops.

The converse risk, that a blameless review stops its trace at "the system" and so drops those who set budgets, staffing and deadlines along with the operator it shields, is a proposed empirical claim (CLAIM-750). The test was committed before it was run: "Blameless reviews that rarely reach above the team's authority while independent ones do support CLAIM-750" (LEAD-119), with the codebook, sampling rule and decision rule fixed before coding. A pilot coded 30 public postmortems drawn at random and the 10 most recent completed Chemical Safety Board reports (CASE-335). Causes naming a decision above the engineering team appeared in "2 of 30 postmortems (7%) against 7 of 10 reports", and action items above it in 4 of 30 against 9 of 10. The finding is "the direction a stopped blameless trace predicts, not yet separated from domain, severity or publication", corroborating the claim weakly (CLAIM-905). The pilot is not independent: its one coder was of the same model family as the claim's author and knew the prediction. And its unit is contested. A single postmortem's causes and action items "are the wrong unit for testing where a blameless practice's trace stops", because at least one major practice routes questions about priorities set above the team to reviews of repeated incidents and to reviews with organizational leaders, so an organization "could keep upstream provenance across incidents while each postmortem stops at the team" (CLAIM-1062; THEORY-2294). An independent second coder, a further arm and a design that separates the commissioning body from the domain remain to be done (LEAD-119).

A model bears on the blameless rationale, though not on blame. Consider an institution "each of whose recognized violations is penalized, newly recognized ones included, whose discovery of requirements earns nothing, and whose remediation lags recognition". In such an institution, inquiry that only reveals requirements "never raises its score": on every path it adds only penalized violations and spent budget, "so an institution acting on that score makes no inquiry" (CLAIM-1064). The static form is that expanding the recognized demand set cannot decrease the count of recognized violations, so that such governance gives an incentive "not to improve its identification of its obligations" (CLAIM-662). Run as a simulation with visible and hidden requirements under five regimes, the penalized institution "learns to make no inquiry in every setting while its internal record shows almost no violations", against half its budget spent on inquiry when an independent evaluator scores it (CASE-396). Discovery penalized in this way is one of the routes by which an organization loses sight of what it owes (SYNTHESIS-094). A regime that penalizes recognized violations is not blame, and the model has no culpability in it. Its bearing on repair without blame is that it shows, in a model, how a regime that charges whoever finds a failure can suppress the discovery that correction needs; whether blaming practices behave so is not shown.

## 8.9 Morality and cooperation

The last question is how morality stands to cooperation. One influential answer identifies morality with solutions to cooperation problems (Curry 2016; THEORY-468). The account developed here has been said to clarify that relationship (CLAIM-tmp1fj5p), and the claim is defeated if the account adds nothing to morality-as-cooperation, to Tomasello's shared-agency account (THEORY-2109) and to Killen's reply (THEORY-2110), "so that it relabels them". Its positive form is that "morality evaluates the terms on which cooperation is formed, maintained and transformed" (CLAIM-tmpreipd).

That claim was put forward in five steps, and one was rejected: the step that set it against morality-as-cooperation on the ground that cooperation creates new problems. A "sequence of cooperation problems is still cooperation problems", and the step "passes from where moral dispositions come from to what makes verdicts valid" (ARG-tmpn6xou). The claim therefore stands, if it does, on two steps, with a third as an important case and a fourth as a research direction (CLAIM-tmpreipd):

- "Cooperation is not necessarily morally good, and noncooperation is not necessarily morally bad";
- "moral consideration cannot be restricted to cooperative participants";
- exploitation within successful cooperation, as the important case;
- how cooperation creates relationships that make morality necessary, as the direction.

Morality-as-cooperation is not refuted by this. Only the second step "does tell against THEORY-468's closing clause that behaviour unrelated to cooperation is morally neutral" (ARG-tmpn6xou). The contrast must run through wrongful cooperation, justified refusal and consideration owed to those who do not or cannot cooperate, not through the claim that cooperation creates new problems. There is a further rival to be faced: a mutualistic account on which, where people can choose their partners and have equal outside options, the stable division of joint gains is proportional to contribution, and partner choice selects for "a genuine sense of fairness as proportionality" (Baumard, André and Sperber 2013; THEORY-457). The present argument has not been set against it, and where the two part is open.

One read case bears on consideration owed to those who cannot cooperate. Climate change, on Gardiner's argument (2006; THEORY-1569), joins a global commons problem to "an intergenerational one that institutions and reciprocity cannot solve, because the generations do not coexist". If later generations are owed restraint, and reciprocity with them is impossible, then moral consideration extends to parties who cannot be partners in any scheme. That bears directly on the second step.

Two results keep cooperation apart from its moral assessment, at different levels. Coexistence is not cooperation: in Hawk–Dove with the cost of fighting above the value of the resource, the types coexist "with Hawks still taking the whole resource from Doves", so "whether types persist together and whether they cooperate are separate questions" (CLAIM-546). And "cooperation local to a group, the persistence of several groups whose interests are opposed, and concern for those outside one's group are three achievements, and none of them secures the next" (CLAIM-547). On the empirical side, morality-as-cooperation's items form seven factors "when rated for relevance … but not when rated as agreement", which is "evidence about the instrument's structure, not about what morality contains" (THEORY-505); and on Killen's reply, "cooperation and interdependence are a basis for morality but not sufficient for it" (THEORY-2110).

This places morality differently from the formula that it is situated within the legitimate governance of the power that interdependent individuals acquire over one another through cooperation. Read literally, that formula confines morality to the powers cooperation creates. The second step says the opposite, that moral consideration is not earned by cooperating, and the third of CLAIM-547's achievements, concern for those outside one's group, points the same way. The weaker statement is the one the argument supports: morality evaluates the terms of cooperation, among other things.

The contractualist alternative is a different contrast (QUESTION-483). Matched constructions were proposed to test whether reasonable rejection explains them without a principle of who holds a power (LEAD-033). As stated, each is explained by reasonable rejection, on personal reasons of protection, "without a principle of who holds a power" (CLAIM-908). Only variants that fix what the holder would do discriminate, and their verdict is the three readings of §8.5 (CLAIM-909; CLAIM-911; CLAIM-912; CLAIM-913). Contractualism is read here through Scanlon's replies and an encyclopedia entry; the book itself could not be obtained (LIT-2721).

## 8.10 Discussion: what the argument does not establish

The argument has a firm frame and an open interior, and the line between them should be clear.

The frame is this. Rights, demands and commitments are distinct, and none establishes another (CLAIM-738); maintained requirements cannot certify their own authority, and viability does not establish what ought to persist (CLAIM-725; CLAIM-075). So success does not certify a scheme's terms. The requirements of normative efficacy are separable (CLAIM-523), and so are the entitlements of an affected party (CLAIM-740). Influence does not ground answerability (CLAIM-749). Coexistence is not cooperation, and concern for outsiders is not secured by cooperation within a group (CLAIM-546; CLAIM-547). A choice-protecting right is not represented by an evaluation of endpoints, and in the distinctive case not by one of histories (CLAIM-741), though this is a construction not yet adopted, and the second separation has had no check beyond its builder's.

The interior is open in five places.

First, the wrong of defeated standing is proposed, not established. The criterion is an interpretive proposal with one legal analogue whose later standing is unchecked (CLAIM-723; THEORY-1972). The matched construction strips out every instrumental difference, but its last step carries a judgement the instrumentalist rejects (ARG-228). This is the strongest objection to the article, and the article has not answered it. Its best statement is that the judgement the construction carries over is a judgement of expected harm under uncertainty, which the construction removes by design (CLAIM-1184). To meet it, one would need an argument from premises the instrumentalist shares, or a case in which the instrumentalist's own verdicts betray a non-instrumental reason, perhaps a reason of status of the kind one reading of the contractualist variants finds (CLAIM-911). Neither is on hand.

Second, no ground of special obligation is established. The method for telling them apart is in place; the principles two of them need are unread (LEAD-tmphsmng); and whether entrusted authority or control grounds obligation in its own right is an open question (QUESTION-tmpeiurf).

Third, the empirical support is thin. The constructions are stipulated, the queueing model's rates are invented, and the inquiry-suppression result is a model result (CASE-283; ARG-228; CASE-256; CASE-264; CASE-336; CASE-316; CASE-396). The one measurement of real organizations is a pilot that is not independent and whose unit of analysis is under objection (CASE-335; CLAIM-1062).

Fourth, the root of answerability, why anyone is owed an answer at all, is not given (SYNTHESIS-103; QUESTION-485). The article lists grounds and bounds, not a foundation.

Fifth, the article treats morality and cooperation but not moral development: how participation in cooperation shapes the moral capacities of those who take part. The fourth step of CLAIM-tmpreipd points toward it, as a direction; this article does not take it.

What would change the picture is clear in each case. An independent recheck of CASE-283 and an answered objection to CLAIM-741 would firm up §8.3. A reading of fair play and associative obligation would either supply the principle CLAIM-959 and CLAIM-671 need or show that it is not to be had. A second, independent coder and a unit that follows reviews across incidents would make the blameless-trace test a test. A model with an affected party would let the occasion of the wrong of defeated standing be counted with a party who is wronged (LEAD-216). None of these is a further simulation of the norm itself; the questions at the centre are philosophical, and they wait on argument.

## 8.11 Conclusion

Cooperation's success does not certify its terms, because a demand's being maintained gives it no warrant, and because what a scheme owes its participants is fixed partly by relations its outcomes do not show. A right that protects a choice is respected or defeated in how a decision is reached; two arrangements can end in the same place and differ in it. Defeated standing is the clearest instance: where the objection would have failed, the defeat is invisible in the endpoint and visible in the history, and where no objection is made it lies in policy alone. That explains why the wrong of defeated standing can be present where nothing turned on it. It does not show that it is a wrong; the instrumentalist objection that it is not has been located precisely and not answered.

Around that centre the argument draws limits. Influence does not ground answerability; responsibility for a dependence does not confer authority over the dependent; and respecting a party's standing does not require giving it direct authority, so that what marks an institution's indifference to those it affects is that their claims carry no weight by any route. The grounds proposed for special obligation can be told apart by matched cases, and none of them is yet established. On the proposals of §§8.8–8.9, repair does not wait on culpability, and morality evaluates the terms of cooperation and is not confined to cooperators; in a model, a regime that penalizes the discovery of violations stops discovery. What the article owes its reader, in turn, is the line between these: the frame is firm, and the interior is a set of proposals whose remaining work is argument and reading, not new studies.
