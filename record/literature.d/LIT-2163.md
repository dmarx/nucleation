# LIT-2163: Factors affecting success of conservation translocations of terrestrial vertebrates: A global systematic review

Morris, S. D., Brook, B. W., Moseby, K. E. & Johnson, C. N. (2021). Factors affecting success of conservation translocations of terrestrial vertebrates: A global systematic review. *Global Ecology and Conservation* 28: e01630. doi:10.1016/j.gecco.2021.e01630. Read as the preprint: "Lessons from a century of conservation translocations", EcoEvoRxiv, 12 February 2021, doi:10.32942/osf.io/hnzqk.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading of the preprint, [NOTE-1657](../notes.d/NOTE-1657.md). Locations are section numbers; the preprint has no pages.*

- **Reported success hides a captive deficit.** Wild stock "did not
  result in higher success rates (wild 70.2%, captive 76.0%)", but growth
  after release was "wild +2.2, sd 5.7; captive -2.8, sd 12.2" (3.4), and
  captive sourcing ranks first or second among predictors of growth
  (Table 3).
- **Soft release shows no population-level benefit.** "soft 79.8%, hard
  75.0%" on reported success, and "hard +5.6, sd 9.8; soft -1.3, sd 6.9"
  on growth (3.4), while soft release grew more common over time
  (r = 0.27, 3.3).
- **Independence from augmentation is the top of the outcome scale.** A
  viable population is one "likely to persist without further
  augmentation" (Table 2); of the IUCN reports' successes, 24.8% were
  viable, and some reported only "high initial survival (10.1%)" (2.4).
- **Numbers released matter most**, with returns diminishing above about
  20–50 individuals (3.1), and longer release programmes go with fewer
  negative outcomes: "79% (38/48) of translocations with negative
  population growth rate had a duration of two years or less"
  (Discussion).

## Standing in the record

The record's first reading on reintroduction and translocation. Read as
the preprint; the published version is unread, and the record should not
cite a figure here as the journal version's until it is. On soft release
it disagrees with Resende et al. (2021), [LIT-2173](LIT-2173.md), unread, whose
abstract reports soft release about 40% better, mostly for reptiles and on
movement metrics; on captive stock it agrees in direction with Gross et
al. (2023), [LIT-2176](LIT-2176.md), unread. It bears on [CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md) (supply that never
yields independence), which it qualifies; on [CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md) (support that
does the work crowds out capacity), for which the captive deficit is
suggestive but confounded; on [CLAIM-566](../claims.d/CLAIM-566.md) (assembling, persisting and
regenerating come apart), which it supports; and on [QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md) and
[QUESTION-399](../questions.d/QUESTION-399.md) (when an established partial organization stops needing the
intervention, and how large the nucleus must be). Filed from it:
