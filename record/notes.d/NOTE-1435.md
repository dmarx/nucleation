# NOTE-1435: Gutt — Translation and Relevance (thesis, 1989)

## Contribution

The thesis argues that translation needs no general theory of its own.
Relevance theory already explains it: most translation is interpretive use
of language across a language boundary, and the principle of relevance
supplies a notion of faithfulness that is general and yet fitted to each
text and audience (pp. 3–4, 190). It distinguishes indirect translation,
which may resemble the original loosely, from direct translation, which
aims at complete resemblance, and shows the second to be the limiting case
of the first (pp. 254–255). It is the doctoral version of the account
published in 1991 as *Translation and Relevance: Cognition and Context*
([LIT-784](../literature.d/LIT-784.md)).

## Key insight

A translation is not judged by how much of the original's wording or
propositional content it keeps. It is judged by how far the assumptions it
communicates to its audience overlap with those the original communicated,
and in which respects that overlap matters to the audience. Wording
matters only as a clue to interpretation: a translation that changes the
words and keeps the interpretation keeps what counts.

## Assumptions

- **Relevance theory, as in Sperber and Wilson (1986).** Communication is
  ostensive-inferential. Every act of ostensive communication carries a
  presumption of its own optimal relevance: adequate contextual effects for
  no unjustifiable processing effort (pp. 50–52).
- **Thoughts and utterances have propositional forms**, and a
  representation can be used descriptively, as true of a state of affairs,
  or interpretively, in virtue of its resemblance to another representation
  (pp. 56, 60–61).
- **Concepts have logical, encyclopaedic and lexical entries** (pp.
  209–211). Connotation, register, the force of greetings and proverbs are
  placed in the encyclopaedic entries of the words themselves (pp. 229–240).
- **The translator has no access to the audience's cognitive environment**,
  only beliefs about it, and the account predicts failure where those beliefs
  are wrong (p. 180).

## Key results

- **Interpretive resemblance between propositional forms (p. 57).** After
  Wilson and Sperber (1988), two propositional forms "interpretively
  resemble one another in a context C to the extent that they share their
  analytic and contextual implications in the context C." Resemblance
  comes in degrees, and two thoughts with no analytic implication in common
  can resemble each other through a shared contextual implication (pp.
  59–60).
- **Resemblance between utterances is a matter of what is communicated
  (pp. 70, 74–75).** Between utterances, resemblance "is generally a matter
  of what overlap there is in the sets of assumptions that are communicated
  by each utterance rather than merely a matter of sharing analytic and
  contextual implications" (p. 70). "Since the linguistic form of an
  utterance can influence its contextual effects, identity in propositional
  form between two utterances is not a sufficient condition for identity in
  their interpretations" (p. 72): "So Charles has lost his car keys" and
  "After all, Charles has lost his car keys" share a propositional form and
  differ in interpretation. Gutt amends the definition so that each
  utterance is interpreted in its own context: "two utterances
  interpretively resemble one another to the degree that the sets of
  assumptions which each conveys in its own cognitive environment overlap,
  that is, interpretive resemblance between utterances depends on the
  degree to which they share their explicatures and/or implicatures" (pp.
  74–75). The worked case is a report of "The back door is open". In a new
  context the report "We should close the back door" resembles the original
  more closely than a word-for-word repetition does (pp. 73–74).
- **Faithfulness as optimal resemblance (pp. 162–164).** "In interpretive
  use the principle of relevance comes across as a presumption of optimal
  resemblance": what the reporter conveys is presumed to resemble the
  original, and to do so with adequate contextual effects and no gratuitous
  processing effort. The translator's goal is "to produce a receptor
  language text such that its interpretation resembles the interpretation
  of the original in those assumptions that make it adequately relevant to
  the receptor language audience", expressed so that "these assumptions
  are recoverable by the receptors without unnecessary processing effort"
  (p. 164).
- **Closer is better, other things equal (pp. 178–180).** Of Knight's
  rhyming translation of Morgenstern against four alternatives, "the more
  closely it resembles the original, the better" when processing cost is
  equal (p. 179). Where more resemblance costs relevance, the less
  resembling rendering "will usually be the one required for successful
  communication". Scott-Moncrieff's "thou" for Stendhal's "tu" kept the
  pronoun's number and lost the intimacy it implied. To English readers it
  conveyed archaic or church language, at extra processing cost (pp.
  165–170, 179–180).
- **Translation principles are applications of relevance to audiences
  (pp. 181–190).** Beekman and Callow's guidelines, Newmark's rules for
  text types, Namy's for interpreters and Savory's twelve contradictory
  principles are each the principle of relevance applied to a class of
  audience. They are resolved by qualifying each "when required for
  consistency with the principle of relevance" (p. 190). The account is
  "context-specific in that the search for consistency with the principle
  of relevance always brings in the particular cognitive environment of the
  audience addressed. All this is achieved without recourse to typologies of
  texts, communication acts and the like" (p. 190).
- **Direct translation by analogy with direct quotation (pp. 197–202).**
  Indirect quotation depends on resemblance in cognitive effects, direct
  quotation on resemblance in linguistic properties (p. 198). Linguistic
  properties cannot be kept across languages, but "the point of preserving
  stylistic properties lies not in their intrinsic value, but rather in
  the fact that they provide clues that guide the audience to the
  interpretation intended by the communicator" (p. 199). Silt'i clefting
  can do what English focal stress does (pp. 200–201). Direct translation
  "calls for the preservation of all communicative clues" and lets the
  receptors reach the intended interpretation "provided they used the
  contextual assumptions envisaged by the original author" (p. 201).
- **Communicative clues, by source (chapter 6).** Clues come from the
  semantic representation (pp. 202–213), from syntax (Dickens's
  juxtaposed clauses carry an echoic, ironic reading that conjoined clauses
  lose, pp. 215–217), from proper names (pp. 222–224), from pragmatic
  connectives (an English translation of Hebel turns an anaphoric German
  *so* into a consequential English *so*, pp. 224–229), from formulaic
  expressions and proverbs, from onomatopoeia, from register, and from
  rhyme and rhythm (pp. 229–246). A formulaic expression is translated by
  one that carries the same encyclopaedic information: the Amharic
  greeting, literally "May he give (you) health on my behalf", by
  "hello", and German *Frisch gestrichen* by "Wet paint" (pp. 231–235).
  Explicating weak implicatures is not direct translation, because those
  implicatures depend on context (p. 246).
- **Direct translation defined (p. 254).** "A receptor language utterance
  is a direct translation of a source language utterance if and only if it
  purports to interpretively resemble the original completely in the
  cognitive environment envisaged for the original." Whether two utterances
  share their clues is checked "by checking whether they give rise to the
  same interpretation when processed in the same cognitive environment"
  (p. 253). So direct translation depends on interpretive use, and
  "communicative clue" has no theoretical standing of its own (p. 255).
  Direct and indirect translation are one continuum of interpretive
  resemblance, of which complete resemblance is the limit (pp. 254–255).
- **Against equivalence (pp. 22–27).** "Function" is now the most widely
  accepted frame for translation equivalence (Levý, House, de Waard and
  Nida) (pp. 22–23). On House's model it supports comparison but not
  evaluation: "the notion of 'equivalence' itself may not be truly
  evaluative in nature but merely comparative" (p. 27).
- **Against dynamic equivalence (pp. 122–129).** Nida and Taber aim at the
  original's "message", but their constraint to "linguistic translation"
  forbids supplying the contextual assumptions or implications that
  receptors lack (pp. 123–124). Achieving "a high degree of equivalence of
  response" may be possible in near-primary communication situations "but
  it seems unrealistic for secondary communication situations with
  significant differences in cognitive environment" (pp. 126–127).

## Concepts

- **Interpretive use**: entertaining or uttering a representation in
  virtue of its resemblance to another representation, not as true of a
  state of affairs (p. 60).
- **Interpretive resemblance**: between propositional forms, shared
  analytic and contextual implications in a context (p. 57). Between
  utterances, overlap of the explicatures and implicatures each conveys in
  its own cognitive environment (pp. 74–75).
- **Optimal resemblance**: the form the presumption of relevance takes in
  interpretive use; Gutt's notion of faithfulness (p. 162).
- **Primary and secondary communication situations**: whether the
  audience uses the contextual assumptions the communicator envisaged
  (pp. 118–120).
- **Communicative clue**: a property of the original stimulus that guides
  the audience to the intended interpretation (p. 199). It is defined by
  its effect, and derived from interpretive use (p. 255).
- **Direct and indirect translation**: complete interpretive resemblance
  in the original's envisaged cognitive environment, against resemblance in
  relevant respects for the receptors' (pp. 202, 254).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-501](../claims.d/CLAIM-501.md): met in substance, by the thesis.** Its defeat condition is
  "a reading of Gutt on which interpretive resemblance is judged by
  closeness of wording or of propositional content". The thesis says the
  opposite in so many words. Resemblance between utterances is overlap in
  the explicatures and implicatures each communicates in its own context
  (pp. 74–75), and "identity in propositional form ... is not a sufficient
  condition for identity in their interpretations" (p. 72). It counts
  irony (pp. 215–217), intimacy (pp. 165–167), register and formality
  (pp. 238–240) and the force of a greeting (pp. 229–232) as parts of the
  interpretation a translation is to keep, and these are of the kind the
  line calls stance and footing. Even direct translation, the strictest
  notion, fixes its clues by their effect on interpretation, not their
  form (pp. 253–255). So on this reading the account predicts that judged
  pragmatic observables beat text similarity, and the claim's
  extrapolation holds. Three cautions for the status review:
  - the thesis makes no prediction about human fidelity ratings, so the
    prediction is still the record's inference from the account;
  - fidelity is relative to a cognitive environment, the receptors' for
    indirect translation and the original's for direct, so raters' context
    enters what the account predicts;
  - this is the 1989 thesis, and the book ([LIT-784](../literature.d/LIT-784.md)) stays unread.
- **[CLAIM-205](../claims.d/CLAIM-205.md): partly, for its relevance-theoretic half.** The claim says
  a relevance-theoretic account "need not be committed to only one context
  or a static stance profile". For indirect translation, which Gutt calls
  the general case, the thesis agrees outright. Faithfulness is "determined
  for each occasion by the principle of relevance and the cognitive
  environment mutually shared" (p. 63). The account is "context-specific
  ... without recourse to typologies" (p. 190), and the same original calls
  for different translations for different audiences (pp. 161–164,
  181–190). Direct translation does fix one context, the one envisaged for
  the original (p. 254). The thesis proposes no operationalization, so it
  does not bear on the "rich contextual baseline" [CLAIM-205](../claims.d/CLAIM-205.md) proposes.
- **Not for the anthology.** The thesis carries no instruction for
  machine-learning practice. Its footnote on simulating translation in
  artificial intelligence (p. 256, n. 2) is a remark about what such work
  would need.

## Limitations

- **OCR.** The text layer has errors (for example "Lev?" and "LevI" for

- **No empirical test.** The account explains translators' and critics'
  judgements after the fact. The thesis tests none of its predictions on
  readers.
