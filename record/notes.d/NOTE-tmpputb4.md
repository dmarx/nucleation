# NOTE-tmpputb4: Stark — Coercive Control (Vermont testimony, 2015)

## Contribution

Stark's own summary of the account the 2007 book develops: partner abuse
is typically a course of conduct aimed at a partner's liberty, not a series
of assaults, and the law should treat it so.

## Key results

- **Opening** (p. 1): battered women have long insisted that "violence
  wasn’t the worst part" of their abuse.
- **Definition** (p. 4): "Coercive control is a strategic course of
  oppressive behavior in which some combination of physical and sexual
  violence, intimidation, isolation and control are used to dominate,
  exploit and/or subjugate a partner and deprive them of basic rights and
  liberties."
- **Against an incident focus** (p. 3): "Interventions focused on discrete
  assaults (rather than course of conduct) miss the significance of abuse
  in the vast majority of cases". "If you wait for injury to identify,
  intervene or apply sanctions, you miss 95%-98% of abuse cases."
- **Low-level violence** (p. 4): "The violence in CC typically consists of
  repeated, frequent or routine but low-level assaults."
- **Prevalence** (p. 3): "In 60% to 80% of cases, partner abuse includes
  some combination of physical violence, sexual violence and coercion,
  stalking and other forms of intimidation" (unsourced).
- **Pattern over parts** (p. 5): "many of the tactics, taken alone, may
  not rise to the level of abuse (e.g. making rules for how someone dresses
  or spends their money), in combination they contribute to the pattern of
  coercive control."
- **Liberty** (p. 6): "Coercive control not only violates women’s rights
  to physical safety/integrity, but also their basic rights to autonomy,
  dignity and liberty."
- **Gender** (p. 2): "Although women as well as men commit large numbers
  of partner assaults, 53% of the men arrested for domestic violence have
  >3 cases, whereas this is true of only 3% of women."

## Assumptions

That frequency and pattern, not the severity of single acts, carry abuse's
significance; that its harm is to liberty and autonomy as well as safety.
Figures are asserted with few citations.

## Concepts

- **Coercive control**: a strategic course of oppressive behaviour that
  deprives a partner of rights and liberties.
- **Course of conduct** as against discrete incidents.
- **Isolation and control** of daily life (dress, money, movement).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-709](../claims.d/CLAIM-709.md)**: corroborates its view that a tally of acts does not
  settle domination: responses aimed at "discrete assaults" miss the
  pattern, and tactics that are not abuse "taken alone" make it "in
  combination". Stark speaks of a perpetrator and a victim and does not
  take up the claim's other half, that the dominated party's own acts keep
  their verdict.
- **[CASE-279](../cases.d/CASE-279.md)**: Dan's control of money, monitoring, isolation and
  threats are Stark's "isolation and control".
- **[CLAIM-545](../claims.d/CLAIM-545.md)**: Stark's harm to "autonomy, dignity and liberty" is the
  record's coercive control of "The person's continuing practical freedom".
- **[TERM-124](../terms.d/TERM-124.md)**: what Stark calls subjugation is domination in the term's
  capacity sense; the link is the filer's, not Stark's.

## Connections

Stands in for Stark (2007), [LIT-2675](../literature.d/LIT-2675.md), for the book's core thesis:
abuse as a strategic course of conduct; harm to liberty, autonomy and
dignity as well as safety; incident-focused responses miss it. It does not
restate the book's history of the battered women's movement, its critique
of the battered-woman-syndrome defence, its case studies, or its argument
about gender and entrapment.

## Limitations

Advocacy testimony, not research. Statistics are given without sources.
Typographical errors ("serous disservice") and a repeated heading
("CONCLUSION III") suggest an unrevised text.
