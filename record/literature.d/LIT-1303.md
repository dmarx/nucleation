# LIT-1303: Worth a Thousand Words: The Images of Copyright

Tushnet, R. (2012). Worth a Thousand Words: The Images of Copyright. *Harvard Law Review*, 125(3), 683–759.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-1016](../notes.d/NOTE-1016.md).*

- **Transparent or opaque.** Courts treat an image either as a window on
  reality whose meaning admits no debate, or as ineffable and beyond
  analysis. Both refuse to treat the image as a thing in its own right
  with a complicated relation to the real (pp. 686–687, 702).
- **The double standard in copyrightability.** Any photograph is
  protected for the photographer's choices, while a new typeface for a
  literary text is not (pp. 713–716). Courts protect "realism" weakly and
  "style" strongly, though realism is itself a style (pp. 724–733).
- **Describing changes seeing.** Verbal overshadowing: putting a nonverbal
  stimulus into words lowers the accuracy of its memory and shifts
  judgments of it, without lowering confidence. Lawyers' descriptions in
  litigation therefore change what factfinders see (pp. 733–738).
- **The reform.** Abandon substantial similarity. Keep a reproduction
  right against exact or near-exact copies, judged by differences, and send
  the rest to the derivative-works right with a market test (pp. 738–740).
- **Words win.** In comic-book authorship (*Gaiman v. McFarlane*) the
  writer of a stock description is made co-author of the drawn character
  (pp. 741–751). Fair use, built on textual quotation, handles whole-image
  copying badly (pp. 751–755).
