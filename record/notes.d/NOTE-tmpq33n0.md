# NOTE-tmpq33n0: Khan and Dhar — The effect of future choices on self-control

## Contribution

It shows that a broader choice frame does not by itself raise
self-control. Knowing that a choice recurs lowers self-control in the
present choice, because people expect to be virtuous next time. Seeing the
present choice as the best indicator of the next one reverses most of
that.

## Key insight

What a series does to the present choice depends on what the chooser
predicts about the later choices. Predicted inconsistency (optimism)
licenses the vice now; predicted consistency removes the licence.

## Assumptions

- **Vice and virtue by pretest.** A vice is "something tempting that may
  have fewer long-term benefits", a virtue "not very tempting now but may
  be more beneficial in the long-run" (p. 280); the stimuli were chosen by
  pretest ratings on these scales.
- **Future choices announced, not imposed.** In the repeated-choice
  conditions participants were told they would choose again; in
  Experiments 1 and 4 and the link study they were debriefed that "there
  would be no further choice" (p. 280).
- **Anticipated guilt is measured before choice** on a three-item scale,
  for the vice and the virtue in turn (Experiment 3).

## Key results

- **Experiment 1 (p. 280).** Movies, n = 80: lowbrow chosen by 80% in the
  repeated-choice condition against 57.5% in isolation, χ²(1) = 4.71,
  p < .05.
- **Experiment 2 (p. 281).** Magazines, n = 60 women: first choice vice
  83% against 53%, p = .01; second-week vice 53% against 40%, n.s., so
  not variety seeking or balancing. Switching was more common in the
  isolated condition (67% against 37%, footnote 1).
- **Prediction study (p. 281).** n = 66: "67% of the participants
  predicted that they would choose a virtue the following week but only
  36% actually chose a virtuous option that week", p < .01.
- **Experiment 3 (pp. 282-283).** Snacks, n = 90 women: vice 83.3% with a
  similar future choice, 57.0% in isolation, 63.0% with a future choice
  in another domain (n.s.). Anticipated guilt for the vice 3.16 against
  4.60, t(58) = 3.04; with guilt in the model the condition effect falls
  to B = -0.67, p = .38 (Baron and Kenny). Mood does not explain it.
- **Experiment 4 (pp. 283-284).** n = 160: vice 47.5% isolated, 70%
  repeated, 90% when next week's snack was fixed as the virtue, 42.5%
  when fixed as the vice. No adding-up effect was found (footnote 4).
- **Experiment 5 (p. 285).** n = 132 MBA students, 100 rupees now or 175
  in two weeks: smaller sooner taken by 64.5% when a similar choice was
  due next month, 37.1% in isolation, χ²(1) = 9.85, p < .01. Only the
  high-self-control half moved (21.3% to 64.4%); the low half did not
  (69.6% against 64.7%).
- **Link study (p. 286).** Using "a procedure described by Kirby and
  Guastello (2001)", participants were told before choosing "that their
  current choice was the best indicator of how they would choose next
  week". Vice: 90% repeated, 46.6% isolated, 53% repeated-with-link;
  linking "significantly reduced the share of participants choosing the
  vice in the immediate period from 90% to 53%", χ²(1, N = 60) = 10.00,
  p < .01.

## Concepts

- **Repeated-choice versus isolated-choice condition**: the same present
  choice, with or without knowledge of a similar later choice.
- **Choice linking**: telling the chooser that the present choice predicts
  the later one, Kirby and Guastello's suggestion.
- **Guilt-reducing justification**: an optimistic belief about a future
  virtuous choice that lowers the anticipated guilt of the present vice.

## Bearing on the record

- **THEORY-067, `promote_when` (1).** The link study varies only whether
  the present choice is presented as a precedent for the next one, with
  the rewards and the choice set unchanged, and choice shifts toward the
  virtue (90% to 53% vice). That is the direction the condition asks for,
  in a food choice rather than money. But the link is an experimenter's
  assertion, not the chooser's own prediction; the shift only brings
  choice back to the isolated rate (53% against 46.6%), not beyond it; and
  the study is reported in a paragraph, with no method section, n, or
  test against isolation. It meets the condition in part.
- **What the paper adds that the précis does not.** Without a perceived
  link, a series lowers self-control: the chooser treats the present
  choice as an exception and predicts virtue later. On Ainslie's account
  that is a short-range interest finding an exception (NOTE-443, §6.1),
  so the finding is
  compatible with THEORY-067, and it says the precedent has to be
  perceived, not given by the series.
- **CLAIM-169 and CLAIM-198.** Both wait on the mechanism (bundling by
  self-prediction). This reading is evidence that perceived precedent
  moves present choice; it does not show that the person's own
  prediction does, which is what CLAIM-198 asserts.
- **CLAIM-156.** No bearing on discounting or mindfulness.

## Limitations

- Vice and virtue choices are hedonic goods, not dated rewards, except in
  Experiment 5, where the delayed option is larger and the effect still
  appears.
- The link study is reported only in the General Discussion, with its
  conditions and percentages and one N; its sample, setting and the test
  of link against isolation are not given.
- The mediation test is Baron and Kenny's, with one measured mediator.
- Several samples are women only, or MBA students in South Asia; the
  self-control split in Experiment 5 is a median split.
