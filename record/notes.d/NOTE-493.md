# NOTE-493: On a confusion about a function of consciousness

## Contribution

The paper names and separates two things the word "consciousness" runs
together: phenomenal consciousness (experience) and access consciousness
(availability of a content for reasoning and the rational control of
action and speech). It sets both apart from monitoring consciousness and
self-consciousness. With the distinction in hand it diagnoses one argument
form in the literature, from a deficit in "consciousness" to a function of
experience, as a conflation. It also says what survives of that argument.

## Key insight

When "consciousness" and some capacity go missing together, ask which
consciousness is missing. In the standard cases both P and A are gone, and
the capacity lost, using information to guide action, is just what access
*is*. Crediting the loss to experience slides from an obvious function of
A's machinery to a non-obvious function of P. That P causes A is a
coherent hypothesis. It is the slide that is the fallacy.

## Assumptions

- **Anti-functionalism about P (§3).** "I take P-conscious properties to be
  distinct from any cognitive, intentional, or functional property." Block
  says limited versions of his points survive for the functionalist (n. 16).
- **Blindsight patients lack P in the blind field.** Taken from the target
  reasoning, not argued. Block notes his argument does not depend on it:
  if it is false, the case reduces to the Searle–Penfield one (§6).
- **Introspection as "the first word" on P (§4.2)**, though not the last.
- **A is stipulated to be demanding enough** that blindsight "guesses" do
  not count as access (§4). Block says there is "no right or wrong here".

## Key results

- **A-consciousness (§4).** A state is A-conscious if, in virtue of having
  it, a representation of its content is (1) inferentially promiscuous,
  poised for use as a premise in reasoning, (2) poised for rational control
  of action, and (3) poised for rational control of speech. The three are
  jointly sufficient but not all necessary. (3) has the least weight, so
  that non-linguistic animals can have A-conscious states.
- **Three differences (§4).** P content is phenomenal, A content
  representational (roughly; many phenomenal contents are also
  representational). A is functional and system-relative, P is not. There
  are P-conscious *types* (every pain feels like pain), whereas a thought
  accessible now may not be later.
- **A without P (§4.1).** The superblindsighter, trained to prompt himself,
  knows there is an X in the blind field but says he does not see it. His
  perceptual state is A-conscious and not P-conscious. Block knows of no
  actual case. The monkey Helen and patient GY are both doubtful.
- **P without A (§4.2).** The drill noticed at noon, heard all along:
  P-conscious throughout, A-conscious only at noon. Animals with intact P
  systems and damaged reasoning systems are conceptually possible, and the
  dorsal/ventral division (Goodale & Milner) suggests the two could be
  damaged separately.
- **The fallacy (§6).** Searle and van Gulick give no reason to think P is
  absent in petit mal automatism. Penfield's own descriptions are of
  deficits in thinking, planning and deciding, "No mention is made of any
  sensory or phenomenal deficit". For blindsight, the missing access
  explains why the thirsty patient does not reach. Searle contradicts
  himself, saying a "totally unconscious" epileptic can drive home and that
  a totally unconscious driver would crash: the second is A.
- **Conflations named (§5).** Baars, Shallice, Mandler, Edelman, Kosslyn and
  Koenig, Johnson-Laird, Kihlstrom et al., Andrade, Jacoby et al. and
  Schacter each say they are explaining experience and give an account of
  A, monitoring or self-consciousness. Baars's global workspace is "a
  'global workspace' model of A-consciousness".
- **Three models (§§6–7).** Schacter's (P is the gateway to the Executive
  System), the collapse hypothesis (P and the Executive System are one)
  and epiphenomenalism (the two transposed). Solutions that "pop into" P
  count against the collapse model, since the reasoning that produced them
  was neither P- nor A-conscious.

## Concepts

- **P-consciousness**: experience; "what it is like" to be in a state.
- **A-consciousness**: a content poised for use as a premise in reasoning
  and for rational control of action and speech.
- **monitoring consciousness**: inner perception, internal scanning, or
  higher-order thought about one's own states (§4.2.2).
- **self-consciousness**: having and using the concept of oneself (§4.2.1).
- **mongrel concept**: a single word or mental representation that
  functions as one concept while covering several, inviting conflation
  (§4.2.2). Block contrasts it with a cluster concept.
- **superblindsight**: imagined blindsight in which the patient prompts
  himself and so has A without P (§4.1).
- **Cartesian modularism**: a model in which P is one module (§2), named
  after Dennett and Kinsbourne's Cartesian materialism.

## Limitations

- **The P-without-A cases are not evidence**, as the author says. Sperling
  rests on Block's own introspection and that of subjects reported by
  Baars. The later papers ([LIT-646](../literature.d/LIT-646.md)) drop the claim that Sperling
  directly shows P without A.
- **A is defined to suit the argument** (§4: "my choice here is mainly
  determined by the needs of the argument").
- **The commentaries were not read.** Thirty-odd replies, including
  Dennett's "The path not taken", which Block (2005) cites as denying the
  conceptual distinction, are where the distinction was contested.

## Open questions

- Is there any actual case of A without P? Block reports that each lead
  from training blindsight patients failed.
- Does P cause A, follow from it, or coincide with it? The paper leaves the
  three models open and offers introspective considerations only.
