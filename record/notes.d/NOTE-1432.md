# NOTE-1432: boyd — Taken Out of Context: networked publics and context collapse

## Contribution

An ethnography of American teenagers on MySpace and Facebook in 2004–2008,
organised around three relations: identity (Chapter 4), peer sociality
(Chapter 5) and power over teens (Chapter 6). Its general apparatus,
introduced in Chapter 1 and used throughout, is a set of four properties of
networked publics (persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability)
and three dynamics they produce (invisible audiences, collapsed contexts,
the blurring of public and private). It extends Meyrowitz's account of
broadcast media (1985) from public figures to everyday people.

## Key insight

People locate a performance by knowing its audience. Networked media hide
the audience and keep, copy and index the performance, so the context a
person writes for and the context in which it is read come apart. What
changes is not how teens present themselves but the boundaries that used to
keep each presentation with its audience.

## Assumptions

- **A constructivist view of technology** (§1.3): properties and dynamics
  "do not determine teen practices" (p. 3) but reshape the environment.
- **Goffman's impression management** as the core frame for identity
  (p. 125), with profiles as "digital bodies".
- **A public** is "both a space where people may gather, interact, and be
  viewed and also an imagined community" (p. 21).
- **The fieldwork**: 2.5 years of interviews and observation of teens
  across the United States (p. 2). Its sample and protocol are in Chapter
  2, not read here.

## Key results

- **Networked publics (p. 15).** Publics "restructured by networked
  technologies", at once "the space constructed through networked
  technologies" and "the imagined community that emerges".
- **Four properties (p. 27).** Persistence, replicability, scalability
  and searchability. None is new alone; in networked publics they are "a
  part of the environment by default", "persistent-by-default,
  ephemeral-when-necessary" (p. 30). Scalability is "the possibility of
  tremendous visibility, not the guarantee of it" (p. 31).
- **Three dynamics (p. 34).** "Invisible audiences: not all audiences are
  visible when a person is contributing online, nor are they necessarily
  co-present. Collapsed contexts: the lack of spatial, social, and
  temporal boundaries makes it difficult to maintain distinct social
  contexts. The blurring of public and private: without control over
  context, public and private become meaningless binaries". They are "an
  amplification of those Meyrowitz astutely recognized resulting from
  broadcast media" (p. 34).
- **The paradigm case (pp. 36–37).** Carmichael, after Meyrowitz (1985:
  43): "very different rhetorical styles when speaking to blacks and
  whites"; "There was no neutral speaking style"; he "was able to maintain
  distinct styles as long as he was able to segment social groups, he ran
  into trouble when electronic media collapsed those social groups".
- **Segmenting fails online (p. 37).** People "try to segment contexts"
  by discouraging audiences, limiting searchable information and privacy
  settings, "Yet a motivated individual can often circumvent any of these
  approaches."
- **Context is not deception (p. 37).** "What is lost in this approach is
  the ways in which context helps people properly locate their
  performances. Bilingual speakers choose different languages depending on
  context".
- **Collisions before and after (p. 38).** "Events like weddings, in which
  context collisions are common, are frequently scripted to make everyone
  comfortable. Unexpected collisions, like running into one's boss while
  out with friends, can create awkwardness, but since both parties are
  typically aware of the collision, it can often be easy to make quick
  adjustments". "In networked publics, contexts often collide such that
  the performer is unaware of audiences from different contexts,
  magnifying the awkwardness and making adjustments impossible."
- **Facets, not fragments (p. 125).** Against Turkle, teens are not
  "fragmenting their identities through online engagement"; "they present
  a facet of their identity based on the social context involved", which
  does not create "identity crises".
- **Imagined audiences (pp. 143–146).** Teens write for friends; "their
  potential audience might be global, their imagined audience is very
  local" (pp. 144–145). Strangers are assumed to "walk on by" (p. 146).
  "Negotiating multiple audiences creates context collisions and teens
  feel more pressure when they are forced to contend simultaneously with
  different audiences, such as peers and parents" (p. 146).
- **Two tactics (p. 147).** Structural ("security through obscurity":
  false information, privacy settings) and social (Friends lists and
  "Keep Out" demands). "But it's my space" against "But it's public":
  teens' "sense of privacy has to do with context and control, not
  potential access".
- **Search collapses contexts (p. 159).** "Online, boundaries are
  porous—search collapses contexts, replicability allows traces of social
  acts to be copied to other spaces".
- **Public by default, private when necessary (p. 163).** Early MySpace
  users kept public profiles. "Being 'public' made sense when the
  relatively homogeneous potential audience did not create conflicts in how
  people presented themselves, but when teens started facing unwanted
  audiences, many chose to control access instead of limit
  self-presentation."
- **Documented collisions (pp. 165–166).** Kira's grandfather read a quiz
  result on her public profile (song lyrics about smoking joints) as drug
  use, and her family "flip[ped] out"; after she went private, her
  grandparents used her stepmother's account and read a photo caption as
  evidence that she was "sleeping around"; she then de-Friended all adults.
  Clyde keeps his profile private lest friends' joking insults make him
  look like "a jerk". James went private after a friend was grounded over
  party comments others left on the friend's page.
- **Privacy settings do not restore a context (p. 167).** "Their ability
  to constrain the audience does not necessarily resolve their desire to
  have a coherent context in which to act", since Friending pressures pull
  people from different contexts into one list.
- **No feedback loop (p. 169).** "There are no feedback loops that allow
  teens to adjust based on real audience feedback and, often, there is no
  way of truly knowing who the actual audience is."
- **Conclusion (pp. 294, 300).** "At the core, for most teens, not much
  has changed"; the properties and dynamics "are not wholly new" but an
  extension of Meyrowitz's, newly interrelated and widespread.

## Concepts

- **Networked publics** — publics restructured by networked technologies,
  as space and as imagined community (p. 15).
- **Collapsed contexts** — "the lack of spatial, social, and temporal
  boundaries makes it difficult to maintain distinct social contexts"
  (p. 34).
- **Context collision** — the meeting of audiences from different
  contexts; scripted (a wedding), noticed (meeting the boss), or unseen by
  the performer online (p. 38).
- **Invisible audiences** — audiences not visible or not co-present when
  one contributes (p. 34).
- **Imagined audience** — the audience a teen writes for, local and
  peer-based, as against the potential audience (pp. 143–145).

## Connections

Meyrowitz's *No Sense of Place* (1985) is the source of the collapsed
contexts dynamic and of the Carmichael case; Goffman (1959, 1963, 1966,
1967) is the frame for self-presentation; Livingstone (2008) for privacy as
control over a situation; Nissenbaum (2004) on contextual integrity is
mentioned for privacy (p. 40). None of these is held in the record.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-406](../theory.d/THEORY-406.md): not at all, for its promote_when.** The promote_when asks
  for a study that observes the same people presenting differently to two
  audiences, records when the audiences are kept apart and when a channel
  brings them together, and measures conflict in both conditions, with the
  comparison fixed before the data are collected. boyd supplies no such
  study and makes no such comparison. The closest she comes is the remark
  that public profiles "made sense when the relatively homogeneous
  potential audience did not create conflicts" and that teens went private
  when adults arrived (p. 163), a recollected before-and-after with no
  measure of conflict. Her evidence is the kind the promote_when says
  cannot settle the question: vivid cases and interviews in which
  collisions are reported as awkward.
- **[THEORY-406](../theory.d/THEORY-406.md), as a reading of boyd.** The full chapters confirm what
  [THEORY-406](../theory.d/THEORY-406.md) quotes (pp. 34, 36–38, 146) and the "unseen" clause (p. 38,
  p. 169). They add two refinements a status review may want:
  - **What collides.** In the cases boyd documents (pp. 165–166), the
    conflict usually comes from a single presentation, often content others
    co-wrote, read by an unintended audience that lacks the context to
    interpret it: a quiz result taken for drug use, joking insults taken
    for meanness. [THEORY-406](../theory.d/THEORY-406.md)'s title speaks of presentations that differ by
    audience. Carmichael is that case; the teens' cases are mostly
    misreading out of context.
  - **The remedy.** Teens restore separation by controlling the audience
    rather than by changing what they present (p. 163), and boyd says this
    does not fully restore a coherent context (p. 167). This is consistent
    with [THEORY-406](../theory.d/THEORY-406.md)'s conditional (trouble when boundaries go), not a test
    of it.
- **[CLAIM-317](../claims.d/CLAIM-317.md) and [CLAIM-196](../claims.d/CLAIM-196.md).** The reading gives them no measured
  evidence beyond [NOTE-710](NOTE-710.md)'s. [CLAIM-196](../claims.d/CLAIM-196.md)'s thesis that role conflict needs
  an overlap of contexts gets illustration from pp. 146 and 163–166, not a
  comparison.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Ethnographic and unmeasured.** No count of conflicts, no comparison of
  segmented with collapsed audiences.
- **US teenagers, 2004–2008**, on two sites, with Carmichael as the only
  case outside them, and that one at second hand through Meyrowitz.
- **Not read in full.** The methods chapter and the chapters on peers and
  adults were not read beyond the passages named, so the sample and its
  limits are not stated here.

## Open questions

- Does conflict concentrate where audiences meet, when it is counted
  against cases where they stay apart? boyd's framework predicts it; no
  study in the record measures it.
- Is the trouble in collapse the meeting of differing presentations, or
  the reading of one presentation without its context? The two predict
  different remedies: consistent presentation for the first, context
  markers or audience control for the second.
