# NOTE-568: The Corporation as a Moral Person

## Contribution

It moved the question of corporate responsibility off legal personhood and
onto agency. It proposed a specific organizational feature, the CID
Structure, as what makes some collectivities intentional agents and others,
such as mobs, mere aggregates.

## Key insight

The same event can be described as several individuals voting and as a
corporation deciding. Intentional description is opaque to substitution, so
the two descriptions can differ in truth-value. A corporation's decision
structure and policy are what license the corporate description, and when
they do, the act is done for the corporation's reasons, whatever the
members' own reasons were.

## Assumptions

- Davidson's account of intentional action: to do y intentionally is for a
  reason one had to be the cause of one's doing it.
- An interdependence view of metaphysical and moral persons, which French
  shares with Locke while rejecting Locke's rooting of both in the juristic
  person.
- That Davidsonian agency is necessary and sufficient for moral personhood.
  French says he has argued this "elsewhere" and "cannot further argue"
  it here (p. 215).
- A corporation with a functioning CID Structure and established policy.

## Key results

- **Against legal theories** (pp. 208–210). Fiction, Aggregate and Reality
  theories of corporate legal personhood do not decide moral personhood.
  The Aggregate Theory cannot tell a corporation from a mob.
- **Two kinds of responsibility ascription** (pp. 210–211). The first says
  who or what did it. The second, parasitic on the first, says the subject
  is accountable, and asserts both causation and intention.
- **The CID Structure** (pp. 212–213) has two elements: an organizational
  or responsibility flow chart of stations and levels, and corporate
  decision recognition rules, usually embedded in "corporation policy".
  When operative it "accomplishes a subordination and synthesis of the
  intentions and acts of various biological persons into a corporate
  decision".
- **Procedure versus policy** (p. 213). Procedural recognition exposes an
  event as procedurally corporate only. An act consistent with, or an
  implementation of, established corporate policy is done for corporate
  reasons and is corporate intentional.
- **Two levels of responsibility** (p. 214). The bribed director is
  responsible for his act; whether the corporation is responsible for
  joining the cartel is a separate question.

## Concepts

- **CID Structure** — Corporation's Internal Decision Structure: chart plus
  recognition rules.
- **recognition rules** — procedural recognitors (who decides at which
  level and who ratifies) and policy, which gives "conclusive affirmative
  indication" (after Hart) that an act was done for corporate reasons.
- **aggregate collectivity** — one with no identity beyond the sum of its
  members' identities, such as a mob.
- **second-type responsibility ascription** — an ascription of
  accountability: X caused the event and intended the act.

## Connections

It answers the Aggregate Theory, and names Jensen and Meckling's "nexus
for contracting relationships" as a flagrant example of the view it opposes
(p. 207, n. 3). It charges Rawls with an "anthropocentric bias". Pettit's
chapter ([LIT-718](../literature.d/LIT-718.md)) answers the same nexus-of-contracts objection with a
different mechanism: authorised avowal and correction of inconsistent
aggregates.

## Bearing on the record

Source of [THEORY-131](../theory.d/THEORY-131.md). It sits beside [THEORY-139](../theory.d/THEORY-139.md) and [THEORY-127](../theory.d/THEORY-127.md).
In the record's reading, French and Pettit both put corporate agency in
organizational procedure, but French grounds it in policy that makes a
reason corporate, and Pettit and List in the need to correct members'
aggregated judgments. French claims more, moral personhood, and argues less
for the step to it.

For the owner's essay, §7.1 (the unwilling corporation): French's corporate
reasons are fixed by established policy, "regardless of the transient
self-interest of directors, managers, etc." (p. 214). That supplies one
reading of what a corporation's own will is, and also a difficulty. A
corporation whose policy is itself what it would disown has no further
standpoint in French's account from which to disown it, and "Radical policy
alteration constitutes a new corporation" (p. 214). French does not
discuss this. For §5.3, the paper does not offer a causal test of the
corporate level; that is what List and Menzies ([THEORY-133](../theory.d/THEORY-133.md)) supply.

## Limitations

The sufficiency of agency for moral personhood is asserted. The one worked
example is stylized: a unanimous vote of three officers, ceteris paribus.
Informal organizations and corporations with unclear or contested policy
are not treated. Corporate intentionality is established by licensed
redescription, with no empirical or causal criterion, and the promised
account of causal inseparability is not given.

## Open questions

What the CID account says when policy and practice diverge, or when policy
is contested among members; and whether the redescription it licenses can
be given a causal test, for example whether the corporate outcome survives
a change of the people in the stations.
