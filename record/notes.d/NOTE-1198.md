# NOTE-1198: Cammarata et al. — Curve detectors

## Contribution

It takes one neuron family of one trained vision model and argues, with
several kinds of evidence that could have disagreed, that it is what it
looks like: a family of curve detectors. The article is explicit that it
is meant to settle a dispute, whether "neural network representations
[are] composed of meaningful features", and it separates what it claims
into four parts (causality, generality, purity, family), each with the
experiment it thinks best supports it. It is the thread's first article
devoted to a single family, and the vision case
the transformer circuits work later points back to.

## Key insight

"What exactly are we claiming when we say these neurons detect curves?"
That a unit fires on curves when it fires strongly is easy to show. The
contentious claims are that curves cause the firing, that it generalizes
across curve stimuli, that the unit does nothing else, and that the family
covers the space. Each needs its own test, and the strongest single tests
are the cheap ones artificial networks allow: optimize an input, render a
synthetic stimulus, rotate a real image.

## Assumptions

- **Model.** InceptionV1 trained on ImageNet. The curve family studied is
  the ten units of mixed3b ("3b"), which the article calls the third of
  four contiguous layers with curve units (conv2d2, 11 units; 3a, 11; 3b,
  10; 4a, 12, by the opening figure's labels).
- **The unit studied in depth.** The dataset analysis, the attribution
  quilts and the hand-labelling are of 3b:379 only, "because some
  experiments required non-trivial manual labor"; the authors say the core
  ideas "apply to all curve detectors in 3b".
- **Attribution** is the linear one: 3b's pre-activation is a linear
  function of 3a, so a 3a unit's contribution is its activation times its
  weight, and the article renders FeatureVisualization(abs(W ⊙ h_prev)).
- **Activation statistics** are of pre-ReLU values over receptive-field
  sized crops of ImageNet images.
- **Human labels** are one author's (Nick Cammarata), made on pixels alone
  with a four-class rubric: curve, imperfect curve, unrelated, opposing
  curve (orientation more than 45° from the unit's).

## Key results

- **Feature visualization.** Every optimization for maximal firing
  produces a curve, "even when we explicitly incentivize the creation of
  different kinds of images using a diversity term", which the authors take
  as "strong evidence that curve detectors aren't polysemantic in the sense
  we usually use it". The visualizations also show opposite hues across the
  boundary and small perpendicular lines ("combing").
- **Dataset statistics of 3b:379.** Pre-activation mean "about -200"; it
  fires on "just 11% of cases across the dataset", and both tails of the
  pre-activation distribution are exponential. Randomly sampled images by
  activation level: strongest positive activations are curves like the
  visualization, weak positive ones imperfect curves, near-zero ones lines
  or curves about 45° off, and the most negative ones curves more than 45°
  away.
- **Attribution** of activating images back through 3a shows the curves
  that caused the firing, which rules out, for instance, a clock detector
  (many top images are clocks).
- **Blind hand-labelling.** Over 800 images, sampled at random in
  activation bins of 100 ("We did not cherry-pick from these bins"),
  labelled without activations. The labels "separate cleanly into different
  activations". Many images labelled unrelated contain subtle curves that
  attribution reveals, and the authors call the unit "a superhuman curve
  detector".
- **The weak tail.** By probability density the strongly firing cases are
  invisible, and "the majority of stimuli classified as curves also fall in
  these cases" of weak or no firing. By contribution to expected value,
  x·p(x), "curves and imperfect curves form 55%". No precision–recall
  curve is given, because recall "would be dominated by potential labeling
  error".
- **Joint tuning.** Rotating each unit's top dataset examples in 1°
  steps gives each unit a "gaussian-like bump" around its preferred
  orientation, and the ten together "jointly span all orientations".
- **Synthetic curves.** Rendered curves varied in orientation and
  curvature drive 3b:379 "up to 24 standard deviations above the average
  dataset activation"; the response region is a triangle, wider in
  orientation at higher curvature, with "wisps" where tiny changes swing
  activations several standard deviations, which the authors call fragile
  and non-robust and see as early as conv2d1's Gabor family. Responses are
  invariant to fill and colour. Synthetic angles drive the unit where one
  arm is tangent to its preferred curve, most where both are; rounder
  stimuli drive it more.
- **Neighbouring families.** conv2d2 has concentric-curve and
  combed-edge detectors, with an "echo" 180° away; 3a curve units also have
  echoes; 3b's do not, and gaps in 3b's coverage are partly filled by
  double-curve detectors; 4a's curve units "are better thought of as
  corresponding to specific worldly objects", for example 4a:406, a tilted
  3D circle (tops of cups and pans). The authors call 4a the point where
  "interpretability can be subjective".
- **Uses.** Attribution to curve units as curve extraction on an
  angiogram, compared qualitatively with Canny edge detection; spline
  tracing by backpropagating into a differentiable spline; butterfly
  tracing by composing with NMF factors ("We traced 23 butterfly images
  and chose our 15 favorites"). None is benchmarked, and the authors say
  so.
- **Combing.** Excitation by short perpendicular lines is seen in curve
  units of AlexNet, InceptionV1 and InceptionV3, in a Places365 model, and
  in one V4 neuron's optimized stimulus in a macaque. Four explanations are
  offered, none preferred; the last is that it is a shortcut in how
  inhibition is implemented.

## Concepts

- **Neuron family**: a set of units implementing variants of one feature,
  here curves at different orientations.
- **Joint tuning curve**: every family member's response to one unit's top
  dataset examples rotated through 360°, aligned and averaged.
- **Radial tuning curve**: a polar plot of each family member's response
  to synthetic curves by orientation.
- **Contribution to expected value**: x·p(x) over the activation
  distribution, the article's measure of how much each activation level
  matters to the unit's output.
- **Echo**: a weaker response 180° from a unit's preferred orientation.
- **Combing**: excitation by small lines perpendicular to the curve.

## Connections

It is the third article of the Circuits thread (DOI suffix .003), after
Zoom In ([LIT-1543](../literature.d/LIT-1543.md)) and An Overview of Early Vision in InceptionV1, both by

neuroscience of curvature-tuned V4 neurons (Pasupathy and Connor 1999;
Jiang, Li and Tang 2019) as the model for its synthetic-stimulus

names "the curve circuits work" as the circuits work that "comes closest

mechanistic evidence.

## Limitations

- **One unit for the dataset analysis**, and one labeller, whose labelling
  the authors describe as hard ("many images seemed to fall within the
  boundaries of the rubric") and affected by afterimages.
- **The weak tail is not explained.** Most firings are weak, and most
  images labelled curves fall where the unit fires weakly or not at all.
  The authors attribute this "at least partly" to labelling error and the
  rarity of curves, and give no precision–recall analysis.
- **Purity is not shown here.** The article's own words are that any
  secondary function "would need to be rare", and the mechanistic evidence
  is deferred.
- **Feature visualization's causal reading** is overstated as Zoom In's
  was ([NOTE-1182](NOTE-1182.md)). An optimized input shows that curves can drive the unit,
  not that curves are what drive it on natural images. The attribution
  and labelling arguments carry that.
- **The figures were not rendered** in this reading, so no number was
  taken from a chart.
- **Internal inconsistencies.**
  - The opening section says curve detectors fire on "only 10% of spatial
    positions"; Dataset Analysis and its caption say 11%.
  - Curve detectors are "only a couple convolutional layers deep", and in
    the same section "implemented using five convolutional layers". mixed3b
    is the fifth convolutional layer (conv2d0, conv2d1, conv2d2, 3a, 3b),
    so the second is right.
  - Typos with no effect on content: "syntheti stimuli", "our cure
    neuron", "Perpindicular".
- **The applications are prototypes** with no quantitative comparison, as
  the authors say.

## Open questions

- What fraction of mixed3b's 480 units, or of its activation variance, is
  accounted for by families as well understood as this one?
- What do the weak firings encode? Are they curve evidence the labeller
  missed, or a secondary function too diffuse to see in a quilt?
- Does the curve family survive a change of basis, for example a
  sparse dictionary over mixed3b, or does it split or merge?
- Is combing a feature of the data or a shortcut of the circuit? The
  authors' own suggestion is to test it in adversarially robust models.
