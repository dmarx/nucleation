# NOTE-tmpwwa8k: Astronomical Waste: The Opportunity Cost of Delayed Technological Development

## Contribution

A short paper that turns the size of the reachable universe into a
practical conclusion. It counts the potential lives lost to delay. It
then shows that the same numbers make the probability of eventual
settlement matter far more than its timing. It is the source of the
expected-value argument for prioritizing existential risk, using the
concept from Bostrom 2002 (§III, n. 8).

## Key insight

Delay is measured in years and the future in billions of years. So
anything that changes *whether* settlement happens outweighs anything
that changes *when*. "If our actions have even the slightest effect on
the probability of eventual colonization, this will outweigh their effect
on when colonization takes place" (§III ¶3).

## Assumptions

- **Lives that could be created would be worth living** (§II ¶1), called
  a weak assumption.
- **Aggregation without time-discounting.** The conclusion holds for any
  evaluation that "does not count one person's welfare for less just
  because there are many other persons" and "is not relativized to a
  particular point in time (no time-discounting)" (§II ¶3). Partial
  aggregation and "some degree of time-discounting" are said to leave it
  standing (§II ¶4).
- **Minds can be run on computers**, for the larger estimate (§I ¶4,
  n. 5). A biological estimate is also given.
- **Infinite consequences are set aside** (n. 7).

## Key results

- **The rate of loss** (§I).
  - The Virgo Supercluster has about 10^13 stars. A star with
    nanotechnological computing gives 10^42 operations per second; a
    human brain about 10^17.
  - So about 10^38 potential lives are lost per century of delay, or
    about 10^29 per second (§I ¶4).
  - With 10^10 biological humans per star, 10^23 in all, the loss is
    over 10^13 per second: "over ten trillion potential human beings"
    (§I ¶5–6).
- **Total utilitarian conclusion** (§§II–III).
  - Advancing settlement by one second brings about more than 10^29 (or
    10^13) lives (§II ¶2).
  - But a single percentage point of existential risk reduction "would be
    worth ... a delay of over 10 million years" (§III ¶2).
  - "For standard utilitarians, priority number one, two, three and four
    should consequently be to reduce existential risk" (§III ¶3).
- **Person-affecting conclusion** (§IV).
  - On that view, obligations run "primarily towards currently existing
    persons and to those persons that will come to exist". Extinction is
    bad because it makes lives worse, not because it loses potential
    lives (§IV ¶1).
  - If no one now alive will reach the cosmic resources, extinction costs
    about 40 years for each of six billion people: a very important
    concern, "in the same big ballpark" as poverty, hunger and disease,
    and not dominant (§IV ¶2).
  - But given a chance (Bostrom suggests not less than 1%) of a
    singularity or life extension within current lifetimes, the expected
    benefit to people now alive of "living for perhaps billions of
    subjective years" may be astronomical (§IV ¶3–5).
  - So the person-affecting utilitarian must balance safety against
    speed: "it is not enough that humankind survives to colonize; it is
    crucial that extant people be saved" (§IV ¶6). The total utilitarian
    always picks safety.
- **Non-identity** (n. 14). On the person-affecting view, actions with
  large effects on the future change which people come to exist, so
  "there would typically be no future persons whose level of well-being
  is affected by our current actions".

## Concepts

- **astronomical waste**: the potential value lost as resources that
  could sustain worthwhile lives go unused.
- **existential risk**: an outcome that "would either annihilate
  Earth-originating intelligent life or permanently and drastically
  curtail its potential" (§III ¶2).
- **person-affecting utilitarianism**: obligations primarily to existing
  persons and those who will come to exist (§IV ¶1). Bostrom calls the
  formulation simple, "not necessarily the best possible one" (n. 9).

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** These are inferences; Bostrom
  does not discuss motivation or the self.
  - **Two grounds, side by side.** On the total view the obligation is to
    an impersonal sum of value, owed to no one. On the person-affecting
    view it is grounded in the good of people now alive. Bostrom shows
    that the two give different advice (safety alone, or safety balanced
    against speed).
  - **The person-affecting case is ordinary self-interest.** The reason it
    gives a living person to care about the far future is that they, or
    people now alive, might live billions of subjective years in it.
    That is concern for the future as one's own future, with no larger
    self needed. It is a third reading beside the thesis's extended self
    and the impersonal good, closest to Pitcher's on QUESTION-tmpruhu4.
  - **Nothing on motivation.** The paper says what "standard utilitarians
    ought to" do (abstract) and gives no thought to why anyone is moved.
- **Discounting.** The conclusion needs no time-discounting but survives
  "some degree" of it (§II ¶4), a weaker dependence than Greaves and
  MacAskill concede.
- **Proposed dated note on CLAIM-tmpjylq8**: "Bostrom's Astronomical Waste
  (NOTE of 2026-10-29) gives two grounds for concern with the far future
  and shows they diverge. On the total view the far future is owed to an
  impersonal sum, and its advice is safety alone. On a person-affecting
  view it matters because people now alive might live billions of years
  in it, which is ordinary self-interest, and its advice is to balance
  safety against speed. A thesis-friendly reading of longtermist concern
  needs no extended self where that second ground operates."

## Limitations

- **Author's HTML, not the journal text.** Not compared.
- **The figures are speculative**, as Bostrom says (§I ¶6). The
  computational estimate assumes uploads and star-scale computers.
- **The person-affecting section rests on guesses**: a probability of
  radical life extension "of less than, say, 1%" called unwarranted
  (§IV ¶4), and non-declining marginal utility of lifespan (§IV ¶5).
- **Mixed views are left open** (§IV ¶6: they "might or might not" yield
  either bottom line).
- **Typos**: "One exception are fetuses" (n. 14).

## Open questions

- Do people who care about existential risk do so for the person-affecting
  reason (their own or their contemporaries' long future) or for the total
  one? Schubert et al. (LIT-tmpk72ci), when read, may bear on it.
