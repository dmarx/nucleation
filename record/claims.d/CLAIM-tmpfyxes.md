# CLAIM-tmpfyxes: That some ways of living are more worthwhile than others, and that this can be judged fallibly, licenses institutions to cultivate the conditions in which people can engage with what is worthwhile, not to impose a conception of it: a reasoned judgment of worth is not by itself a warrant for coercion

## The claim

A9 §VII, "fallibilist value pluralism", in three parts: not all activities are equally worthwhile because someone prefers them; judgments of worth are "imperfect, historically situated, and open to rational disagreement"; and "Third, believing that some values are genuine does not entitle any particular institution to impose its conception of the good life on everyone else." Its slogan: "**The existence of objective value does not imply the existence of an authority with infallible access to it.**" A9 §VIII draws the boundary:

> **Institutions can cultivate the conditions for meaningful engagement,
> but they cannot legitimately guarantee meaning by imposing particular
> commitments.**

A10 restates it with Sen: "**The political obligation is primarily to expand the capability for meaningful engagement, not to compel its realization.**" (§III), and "Our synthesis needs a distinction between **philosophical evaluation and political legitimacy**." (§IX).

## What grounds it, and what is A's

Wolf supplies the first two parts. Asked "Who's to say?" what is worthwhile, her answer is "no one in particular", with fallibilism and pooled judgment, and she calls an account of value's objectivity "an unsolved problem in philosophy" ([THEORY-tmp4ajaz](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp4ajaz.md), [NOTE-tmpzth5f](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpzth5f.md) results 5–6). The third part, the political boundary, is A's. Wolf disclaims practical use, so the inference from her fallibilism to a limit on institutions is A's, argued in [ARG-tmpl8zwp](../arguments.d/ARG-tmpl8zwp.md). Sen's public reasoning about weights ([THEORY-411](../theory.d/THEORY-411.md)) is a neighbour: it leaves the weighting of functionings to public reasoning, which is a procedure for disagreement, not a ban on imposition. Marcuse's "no tribunal can justly arrogate to itself the right to decide which needs should be developed and satisfied" ([THEORY-tmpoxssh](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpoxssh.md)) agrees in words and differs in reason: he defers to individuals only "if and when they are free to give their own answer".

## What it does not say

- Not neutrality. Institutions may judge some ways of living better and act on it by cultivating conditions: education that introduces people to fields of worth, support for valuable activities markets neglect, limits on exploitative practices (A9 §VIII, A10 §IX).
- Not that coercion is never justified. The claim denies only that the worth of a commitment, by itself, justifies imposing it.
- Not how to tell cultivation from imposition in hard cases. Nudges and defaults sit between them; [CLAIM-tmpplibk](CLAIM-tmpplibk.md) says what goes wrong when an institution measures meaning.
