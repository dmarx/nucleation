**The introduction** (read). The social sciences are failing despite
abundant data. The usual diagnoses (bad models of individuals, of
aggregation, of style, of level, or of grand theory) would apply equally
to meteorology or cell biology. The deeper flaw is an "anthropocentric"
picture on which social objects are built out of people "much as an ant
colony is built out of ants". The worked example is the US Supreme
Court: its powers, its membership and its very existence are not
determined by the nine justices.

**The framework**, as the précis and the SEP entry state it. A social
fact is grounded by other facts. A frame principle says how facts of
that kind are grounded across a frame, a set of possible worlds. What
puts the frame principle in place is its anchor: agreement, acceptance,
practice, or something else. Searle's constitutive rules are frame
principles of one narrow form, and his collective acceptance is one
theory of anchoring; the précis calls those limitations "radically
inadequate". Chapter 9 argues against "conjunctivism", the view that the
anchors are just more grounds. The précis says both ontological
individualism and anchor individualism are false, and that the book
argues only the first.

**Part Two**, as the précis states it. Groups are entities "constituted
by and only by people", yet distinct groups can share their members and
a group can persist with none. Facts about a group (the Supreme Court's
existence, membership, powers) are grounded by heterogeneous facts, many
not about members. Group action and group intention can depend on
non-members, on hierarchy, on constraints of jurisdiction and session,
and on membership rules. "Social integrate" models (Gilbert, Bratman,
Pettit) and "status" models (Searle, Tuomela, Hindriks) share the
mistaken assumption that group agency must be fully implemented by
members. For a group to be an agent it must realise "a system of
practical activity", and status assignments "do not do anything to make
a group into an agent".

**Bearing on [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)**, from those statements; the argument itself is
unread. [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)'s two routes to refusing a group mind both treat the
whole as fixed by its members: Kammerer's anti-nesting principle asks
what members consciously represent, and List's low-Φ verdict
([LIT-401](LIT-401.md)) looks at how members are coupled. If Part Two is right, a
group's functional organisation is grounded partly in non-members and
infrastructure, so a criterion computed over members alone is computed
over the wrong system. And because coinciding groups are possible, "the
group" is not one candidate whole. The SEP reading ([NOTE-tmpodceb](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpodceb.md)) works
this out. Neither text the record holds says anything about group
phenomenal consciousness. The book's stated position on agency (agency
is realised, not conferred) agrees with [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)'s finding that agency
survives both directions.

**Neighbours.** List & Pettit ([LIT-391](LIT-391.md)) and List ([LIT-401](LIT-401.md)) are among the
member-centred accounts Part Two argues against. Huebner ([LIT-426](LIT-426.md)) is
the record's other account of minds in large structured groups.
Griesemer & Shavit ([LIT-188](LIT-188.md)) classify collectives by how members
interact; chapter 13, *Kinds of Groups*, and the SEP entry's report of
Epstein's case against simple typologies of groups are the metaphysical
counterpart. Hindriks & Guala ([LIT-tmpjftou](LIT-tmpjftou.md)) also find Searle's single
form too narrow, but replace it with equilibrium rather than anchoring.
Dennett's "Real Patterns" ([LIT-220](LIT-220.md)) defends realism about patterns from
their predictive power. Epstein's case against individualism is
ontological rather than predictive, so the two meet only at the shared
target, a reduction of the higher level to its parts.

**Topic.** `social-ontology` first. `metaphysics` holds grounding,
anchoring and frames. `philosophy-of-science` holds the book's aim of
rebuilding the foundations of the social sciences. `agency` holds
chapters 14–18. `mereology` and `individuation` hold chapters 10–13
(constitution, coinciding groups, criteria of identity, persistence
without members). `social-science` holds its target audience and its
case studies, from macroeconomics to legislatures.

**What a reading should check.** Chapter 6's account of how anchors
differ from grounds, and chapter 9's arguments against conjunctivism,
which Schaffer and Hawley contest (per the SEP entry). Chapter 16's case
that a group's intentions can be grounded in non-members, which is the
claim that bears most on [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md). Whether the book anywhere discusses
group experience.
