# THEORY-838: The emphasis the news media give to issues raises the importance their audiences assign those issues, independently of any change in the audiences' attitudes toward the issues: the media influence what people think about more than what they think

## Source

McCombs and Shaw (1972), [LIT-1335](../literature.d/LIT-1335.md), read in [NOTE-1046](../notes.d/NOTE-1046.md). The
hypothesis is older: Cohen (1963), "it is stunningly successful in telling
its readers what to think about", and Lang and Lang, "The mass media force
attention to certain issues." The paper states it: "While the mass media
may have little influence on the direction or intensity of attitudes, it is
hypothesized that the mass media set the agenda for each political
campaign, influencing the salience of attitudes toward the political
issues."

## What was actually shown

The aggregate correlations of [THEORY-842](THEORY-842.md) and its comparison against
selective perception. Those are what agenda-setting predicts. The
direction from media to public is defended by argument only: few voters
observe a campaign directly, journalists' news values usually diverge from
their audiences', and "It is easier to argue that Chapel Hill voters fit
their judgments of issue salience to the mass media than the reverse"
(fn. 13).

## What this does not say

- **Not that the media decide what people conclude.** The hypothesis is
  about the ranking of issues, and is stated against an effect on
  attitudes.
- **Not that the media originate the agenda.** In 1968 the media's
  emphasis reflected the candidates' to a large degree; the hypothesis
  concerns the transfer from news to audience, whoever set the news.
- **Not established by this source.** The study read here could not tell
  the hypothesis from a common response to events or from media catering
  to their audience, except by argument.
