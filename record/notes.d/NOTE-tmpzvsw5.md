# NOTE-tmpzvsw5: Ashby — Design for a Brain

## Contribution

The book gives a mechanistic account of how a determinate machine can
change from unadapted to adapted behaviour. It defines system, state,
field, stability, parameter, step-function and independence by observable
operations (Chs. 2–6, 12). It deduces that adaptation by trial and error
needs a second, slower feedback through the essential variables to the
parameters of the first (Ch. 7), builds the Homeostat to show it (Ch. 8),
and then spends half the book on the difficulty that such a system, if
large and richly joined, would take impossibly long (Chs. 11–17). The
answer is that adaptation succeeds in practicable time only where
partial adaptations can be retained, which needs temporary independence
between parts.

## Key insight

Stability belongs to the whole, and a whole made of many richly joined
parts is almost never stable by chance. A system finds stability by
changing its own parameters blindly until a configuration holds, and it
can do this in useful time only when what it has already got right is
kept apart from what it is still trying, so that success can accumulate
part by part, as a crystal grows molecule by molecule rather than forming
all at once.

## Assumptions

- **Determinacy of the significant units.** Each part, "if in a particular
  state internally and affected by particular conditions externally, will
  behave in one way only" (S. 1/14).
- **State-determined systems.** The organism and its environment together
  form a state-determined system (S. 3/9); variables are dial readings,
  and the observer controls all states (S. 2/7).
- **Survival as the criterion.** Essential variables, with limits fixed by
  the species, define adaptation (Ss. 3/14, 5/3). Reproduction and the
  "racial" behaviour of Holmes's table are set aside (S. 5/9).
- **Essential variables affected by the environment only.** The case taken
  is the hardest, in which the reacting part cannot act on the essential
  variables except through the environment (S. 7/3), and does not know how
  the environment is coupled to them (S. 7/4).
- **No consciousness, no teleology.** Neither is used (Ss. 1/13, 1/16).
- **Idealized cases.** The book follows "the strategy for the complex
  system" (S. 2/17): exact treatment of type-cases, with real systems
  assumed to behave like the nearest one.

## Key results

- **Adaptation is stability (Ss. 4/8, 5/8).** "A region is stable if the
  lines of behaviour from all points in the region stay within the
  region." "'Adaptive' behaviour is equivalent to the behaviour of a stable
  system, the region of the stability being the region of the phase-space
  in which all the essential variables lie within their normal limits."
  Stability "is a property of the whole system and can be assigned to no
  part of it" (S. 4/18): two stable systems can join to an unstable whole,
  two unstable ones to a stable whole (S. 21/12 gives matrices).
- **Parameters change fields (S. 6/3).** "A change in the value of an
  effective parameter changes the field", so "a system can, in general,
  show as many fields as its parameters can show combinations of values".
- **The veto (Ss. 6/8–6/9).** "That a whole dynamic system should be in
  equilibrium at a particular state it is necessary and sufficient that
  each part should be in equilibrium at that state, in the conditions
  given to it by the other parts." Every part "has a power of veto over
  the states of equilibrium of the whole".
- **The second feedback is necessary (Ss. 7/4–7/8).** Against an
  environment that is a Black Box, only trial and error can elicit the
  needed information (S. 7/4). The basic rule is "If the trial is
  unsuccessful, change the way of behaving; when and only when it is
  successful, retain the way of behaving" (S. 7/7). So: when the essential
  variables are outside their limits, no state of the parameters S is
  equilibrial; when inside, every state is. Any adaptive system "must have
  a second feedback formally identical (isomorphic) with that described
  here" (S. 7/8); otherwise it would be "a candidate who gives the correct
  answers without being given the questions".
- **And sufficient (S. 7/11).** By the veto, "all the possible equilibria
  of the whole have the essential variables within the given limits". "The
  mechanism has been shown to be necessary by S. 7/8, and sufficient by the
  present section."
- **Step-functions (Ss. 7/13–7/23).** A variable with "finite intervals of
  constancy separated by instantaneous jumps", changing at "critical
  states". With n two-valued step-mechanisms the main variables show 2ⁿ
  fields, and the whole stays determinate while its main variables appear
  to change "spontaneously" (S. 7/21).
- **The ultrastable system defined (S. 7/26).** "Two systems of continuous
  variables (that we called 'environment' and 'reacting part') interact,
  so that a primary feedback … exists between them. Another feedback,
  working intermittently and at a much slower order of speed, goes from the
  environment to certain continuous variables which in their turn affect
  some step-mechanisms, the effect being that the step-mechanisms change
  value when and only when these variables pass outside given limits. The
  step-mechanisms affect the reacting part; by acting as parameters to it
  they determine how it shall react." Ashby adds that this form "is only
  one of a large class of related forms", kept as a type.
- **Random construction is no obstacle (S. 7/25).** The second feedback
  "is in no way put out of order if R is initially constructed at random
  or if the successive values at S occur at random".
- **The Homeostat (Ch. 8).** Four units, each a pivoted magnet driving a
  current to the others through commutators and potentiometers set by a
  25-position uniselector wired from Fisher and Yates' random numbers
  (390,625 combinations); a relay F closes when a needle passes about 45°
  and advances the uniselector, tested every 1–10 seconds (S. 8/2).
  Reversing a connection by hand produces a runaway, then uniselector
  changes until a compensating reversal is found (Figures 8/4/1, 8/5/1),
  as in Marina's and Sperry's crossed-muscle and crossed-nerve monkeys.
  It adapts to a glass fibre joining two magnets and becomes unstable
  when the fibre is removed (S. 8/11); with an alternating commutator it
  keeps the first setting stable for both (S. 8/10). "Faults" are shared
  with organisms: defenceless against a tasteless poison or congenital
  insensitivity to pain (S. 8/13), against sudden discontinuities such as
  traps (S. 8/14), and dependent on a trial duration neither too short nor
  too long (S. 8/15).
- **Bimodal disturbance (Ss. 9/14, 9/19).** The ultrastable form is
  "the optimal mechanism" when disturbances are of two classes: "frequent
  … small impulsive disturbances to the main variables" and "occasional
  changes, of step-function form, to its parameters".
- **Goals can be moved (S. 9/16).** Whatever moves the critical states, or
  transforms the input, moves the goal.
- **No accumulation in the Homeostat (Ss. 10/5–10/9).** Given A, then B,
  then A again, "it treats A as if it had never encountered A before";
  retroactive inhibition is "complete". For adaptations to accumulate, "the
  step-mechanisms must be divisible into non-overlapping sets", with a
  "gating mechanism" Γ, controlled by the situation P, connecting one set
  at a time to the second feedback.
- **Adaptation time (Ss. 11/2–11/6).** With 100 essential variables each
  brought within limits with probability ½ per trial, the expected number
  of trials is 2¹⁰⁰, about 10²² years at one per second. For N wheels each
  showing A with probability p: all at once takes (1/p)ᴺ spins; one at a
  time, about N/p; spinning only the failures, a little more than the
  single-wheel time. With p = ½ and N = 1,000 at one spin a second:
  about 10²⁹³ years, about 8 minutes, a few seconds. "What had made the
  processes … so excessively time-consuming is that partial successes go
  for nothing."
- **Crystallisation (S. 11/8).** "If a visible crystal could form only when
  all its molecules happened simultaneously to be properly related … then
  crystallisation could never occur … But in fact crystallisation can
  occur by succession." "A compound event that is impossible if the
  components have to occur simultaneously may be readily achievable if
  they can occur in sequence or independently."
- **Accumulation needs disconnection (S. 11/10).** If adapting essential
  variable 3 leaves 1 and 2 adapted, there can be no channel from the
  step-mechanisms changed for 3 to the parts that serve 1 and 2. "For the
  accumulation of adaptations to be possible the system must not be fully
  joined." The Homeostat "is too well integrated, cannot accumulate
  adaptations" (S. 11/12).
- **Walls of constancy (Ss. 12/10, 12/17, 22/14).** "That A and C should
  be independent and state-determined it is necessary and sufficient that
  the variables B should be null-functions": "a wall of constancies". "If
  a sufficient proportion of its variables go constant", a connected
  system "may … be temporarily equivalent to a set of unconnected
  subsystems. Constancies, in other words, can cut a system to pieces."
  Thresholds readily give such constancies (S. 12/15).
- **Polystable systems (Ch. 13).** In a richly joined system of parts
  rich in equilibria, a near-total equilibrium (999 of 1,000 parts) falls
  back to the average: it "is thus typically unable to retain partial or
  local successes" (S. 13/9). In a poorly joined one the count of parts at
  equilibrium "cannot fall with time" and the whole progresses by
  "accumulation of local equilibria" (Ss. 13/10, 13/13). A
  state-determined subsystem at equilibrium among constant neighbours is
  "trapped in equilibrium": "change can come only from change" (S. 13/4).
- **Habituation (Ch. 14).** Under a repeated stimulus a polystable system
  sticks "at a state from whose confluent the stimulus cannot shift it",
  so responses shrink; under small random disturbances it goes to
  equilibria "immune to the action of small irregular disturbances"
  (S. 14/8), and "roughing it" removes fields with equilibria near the
  edge of the region (S. 14/10).
- **Serial and iterated adaptation (Ch. 15).** The terrestrial environment
  is mostly part-functions, constant much of the time, each affecting few
  others (S. 15/2). Iterated subsystems adapt in about the time of one;
  a chain adapts in its natural order, "A first, then B, then C", and only
  if the channels down the chain have low capacity (S. 15/11).
- **The multistable system (Ch. 16).** Subsystems, each ultrastable,
  weakly joined: "subsystem adapts to subsystem in exactly the same way as
  animal adapts to environment" (S. 16/7). Its step-mechanisms stop when,
  and only when, every combination that occurs is stable (S. 16/8). With
  few active variables per stimulus, two stimuli activate overlapping sets
  in proportion to the product of the fractions, so different stimuli use
  different step-mechanisms, a "functional equivalent of the gating
  mechanism" (Ss. 16/12–16/13). As stimuli grow alike the overlap grows,
  and "the more the new learning resembled the old the more marked was
  the interference" (S. 16/14; Robinson, Skaggs). Retroactive inhibition
  and accumulation are "opposed" demands; an intermediate multistable
  system shows both partially (S. 16/15).
- **Communication is not always good (Ss. 17/3–17/7).** Too fast a change
  of step-mechanisms, any channel from the essential variables to the
  stores of other adaptations, and every added connection between
  subsystems all harm adaptation. Coordination between parts can pass
  through the environment, as the server's thrown ball guides the striking
  arm (S. 17/4). Richness of connection has costs and benefits and must
  lie between limits.
- **Ancillary regulations (Ss. 17/8–17/13).** Trial duration, the
  hunt-at-bad, stick-at-good relation, the gating mechanism, the richness
  of equilibria, the degree of connection, and the distribution of the
  second-order feedbacks to the parts that caused the trouble must all be
  set. A second essential variable F, the time E takes to adapt, when
  exceeded should change "those conditions that have continued unchanged
  throughout E's hundred trials", so that trials are drawn "from a new
  statistical population". All such selection obeys the law of requisite
  variety and comes ultimately from natural selection.
- **Selection is the rule (Ss. 18/1–18/7).** Every isolated
  state-determined system is selective, for "two lines of behaviour may
  become one, but one line cannot become two". "Every single-valued
  operation tends to select forms that are peculiarly able to resist its
  change-inducing action." Learning is indirect regulation, which can
  "amplify" regulation beyond what the gene-pattern transmits, as teaching
  a child to use a dictionary beats ten minutes of definitions.
- **Probability of stability (S. 20/10).** For random linear systems with
  entries uniform on −9 to +9, tested by Hurwitz's rule: 2×2, 77 of 320
  stable (24%); 3×3, 12 of 100; 4×4, 1 of 100, "compatible … with the
  hypothesis that the probability for a matrix of order n × n is 1/2ⁿ".
  With intrinsically stable diagonals, 2×2 72% and 3×3 55%. "Large linear
  systems should be assumed to be unstable unless evidence to the contrary
  is produced."
- **Trials to a terminal field (S. 22/7).** If each new field is stable
  with independent probability p, the expected number of fields is 1/p.

## Method

The Homeostat (S. 8/2; equations S. 19/11): four units, magnet deviations
xᵢ obeying, in the high-viscosity limit, dxᵢ/dt = Σⱼ aᵢⱼxⱼ, with the aᵢⱼ
step-functions drawn from a rectangular distribution on −1 to +1 when the
uniselector of unit j advances, which it does when |xⱼ| crosses its
critical surface (S. 22/6). Relays F stand for essential variables;
uniselectors for the step-mechanisms S; the four magnets for environment
and reacting part together, divided arbitrarily.

## Concepts

- **State-determined system.** One in which a given state, under given
  conditions, is always followed by the same transition (S. 2/15);
  equivalently dxᵢ/dt = fᵢ(x₁, …, xₙ) (S. 19/9).
- **Field.** All lines of behaviour from all initial states under one set
  of conditions (S. 2/14).
- **Parameter.** A variable not in the system that affects it; its value
  selects the field (Ss. 6/2–6/3).
- **Essential variables.** Variables closely linked to survival, with
  limits fixed by the species (S. 3/14).
- **Step-function, step-mechanism, critical state.** A variable constant
  between instantaneous jumps; the mechanism showing it; the states at
  which it jumps (Ss. 7/13, 7/18).
- **Ultrastable system.** S. 7/26, quoted above.
- **Part-function, null-function.** A variable with intervals of change
  and of constancy; one that never changes (S. 7/13).
- **Independence, dominance.** Y is independent of X if two transitions
  from states differing only in X give the same Y (S. 12/3); X dominates Y
  if Y depends on X and not conversely (S. 12/6).
- **Polystable system.** Parts rich in equilibria, taken and joined at
  random (S. 13/2).
- **Multistable system.** Weakly joined ultrastable subsystems (S. 16/6).
- **Dispersion.** Different inputs activating different sets of variables
  (S. 13/14).
- **Confluent.** The basin of one equilibrium or cycle (S. 14/2).
- **Ancillary regulation.** A regulation of the parameters on which the
  efficiency of the basic adaptation depends (Ch. 17).

## Connections

The book builds on Cannon's homeostasis, Sherrington's reflexes, Pavlov
and the learning literature, and the wartime servomechanism work; it
acknowledges Sommerhoff's *Analytical Biology* as parallel (fn. to
S. 5/12), and refers its foundations to *An Introduction to Cybernetics*
(1956), which states the law of requisite variety. Its stability results
for random linear systems anticipate May (1972; [LIT-930](../literature.d/LIT-930.md), held unread) and
its gating of separate stores anticipates later accounts of
catastrophic interference.

## Bearing on the record

- **[LIT-787](../literature.d/LIT-787.md)'s entry is right on ultrastability and misses the book's
  second half.** The summary's two feedback loops and step-functions are
  as Ashby defines them (S. 7/26). Three additions matter:
  - The second feedback is offered as *necessary* for any system that
    adapts by trial and error (S. 7/8), not as one mechanism among others;
    in the second edition the particular form is "only one of a large
    class of related forms" (S. 7/26).
  - The parameter changes are blind. The step-mechanisms take new values
    "at random", and the mechanism works only because the essential
    variables veto bad ones (Ss. 7/25, 8/3). The owner's essay reads
    ultrastability as regulation against "alteration of the parameters of
    regulation"; in Ashby that alteration is undirected search, not a
    choice of new parameters.
  - Chapters 10–17, more than half the main text, are about why a large
    ultrastable system would take impossibly long and what lets it not:
    retained partial adaptations, temporary independence, and separate
    stores for separate situations.
- **[CLAIM-185](../claims.d/CLAIM-185.md) has a precise ancestor here.** Its thesis, that structure
  evolves only on persistent anchors, is Ashby's result that adaptation
  finishes in practicable time only if "partial successes … can be
  conserved and accumulated" (S. 11/6), with crystallisation by
  succession as his own example (S. 11/8). Ashby adds the condition the
  claim does not state: retention requires that the retained part be cut
  off, by constancies, from what is still changing (Ss. 11/10, 12/17). A
  persistent structure that later changes can reach is not retained.
- **[QUESTION-038](../questions.d/QUESTION-038.md), what turns persistence into an anchor.** Ashby's answer
  is functional isolation plus access: the adapted step-mechanisms must be
  closed to the second feedback while other problems are worked on, and
  re-connected when their situation recurs (Ss. 10/8–10/9). In record
  terms, an Active entry that new filing cannot overwrite, and a way to
  reach it by its situation. Retirement by status, never deletion, is the
  first; codes and indexes are the second. This is analogy, and the record
  should treat it as such.
- **[CLAIM-176](../claims.d/CLAIM-176.md), recurrence.** Ch. 10 is about the recurrent situation: the
  benefit of a recurrence is that the second response can be immediate,
  and that requires the store for that situation to be selected by the
  situation itself. Ch. 14 adds that a repeated disturbance selects states
  immune to it. Neither says that recurrence should trigger capture; both
  say what a system must have for recurrence to be exploited.
- **[CLAIM-180](../claims.d/CLAIM-180.md), drift and interference.** Ashby's interference grows with
  the similarity of the situations, because similar inputs use the same
  parts (S. 16/14). Read across, a close restatement of an idea is the one
  most likely to overwrite its earlier form, the converse of [CLAIM-180](../claims.d/CLAIM-180.md)'s
  concern that a distant one is taken for new. Both follow from content
  addressing; a code avoids both.
- **[CLAIM-179](../claims.d/CLAIM-179.md), the May note.** Ashby's sampling (S. 20/10) is an earlier,
  empirical form of the same conclusion: stability of a randomly joined
  system falls steeply with size, roughly as 1/2ⁿ in his samples, and
  "large linear systems should be assumed to be unstable". It also gives
  the escape the note asks about: weak joining and many constancies
  (Chs. 13, 16).
- **[THEORY-217](../theory.d/THEORY-217.md) and [THEORY-153](../theory.d/THEORY-153.md).** Barandiaran's essential variables and
  Beer's requisite variety are Ashby's (Ss. 3/14, 17/13); this reading is
  their common source.
- **Filed from this reading:** [THEORY-tmpxwn52](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpxwn52.md), the ultrastability
  account, and [THEORY-tmp9yigf](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp9yigf.md), adaptation time and the retention of
  partial adaptations. No existing THEORY held either.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- The account of the brain is a theory of possible mechanism. Ashby says
  of the step-mechanisms "we have at present practically no idea of where
  to look, nor what to look for" (S. 9/4).
- The fit to real brains (Chs. 13–16) is qualitative. Adaptation times are
  calculated for type-cases with independent probabilities, not measured.
- The Homeostat is linear, with one equilibrium and four units.
- The stability statistics are 520 small matrices.
- Reward is set aside: training is treated as punishment (S. 8/8).

## Open questions

- Whether the time of adaptation in real organisms scales with the degree
  of connection as Chapters 11–16 predict; Ashby says the study "should
  not be beyond the powers of the present-day experimenter" (S. 16/11).
- How the ancillary regulations are set in an individual, beyond "by the
  gene-pattern or … under an ultrastability of their own" (S. 17/13).
- The probability of stability for large random matrices, which Ashby
  calls unsolved (S. 20/10) and May later bounded.
