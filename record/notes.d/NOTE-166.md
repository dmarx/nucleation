# NOTE-166: Personal Identity and Ethics (SEP)

## Contribution

The entry surveys how our being temporally extended and distinct individuals bears on ethics. It sets out the psychological and biological accounts of personal identity and Parfit's challenge that identity itself is not what matters, then works through the practical consequences: special prudential concern for one's future, responsibility for past acts, the beginning and end of life (abortion, death, dementia and advance directives), and the separateness of persons in normative ethics and distributive justice. It ends by noting that it is unclear whether a theory of what matters should be fixed first or developed in reflective equilibrium with ethics.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1 distinguishes numerical from qualitative identity; the entry's "identity" is persistence through time.
- §2 and §4 set the psychological criterion (Locke; Shoemaker, Parfit — unique psychological continuity) against the biological view (Olson 1997, DeGrazia 2005 — sameness of biological life).
- §3 uses a fission/branching case to argue that psychological continuity, not numerical identity, carries prudential and perhaps moral concern.
- §§5–8 apply this to egoistic concern for the distant future, Locke's link between identity and responsibility, abortion arguments, and death and advance directives under dementia.
- §§9–10 treat Rawls's and Parfit's "separateness of persons" and whether it is a metaphysical fact or a normative ideal (Rawls 1974 takes the latter).
- §11 concludes that the correct theory of identity, whether identity matters, and the metaphysics–ethics priority all remain open.

## Open questions
