# NOTE-tmp53juk: Hayek — The use of knowledge in society

## Contribution

It recasts the socialist-calculation debate as a question about
knowledge, not computation. The "data" of the economic calculus "are never
for the whole society 'given' to a single mind ... and can never be so
given." Which arrangement is more efficient depends on which makes fuller
use of knowledge that is initially dispersed. It names a kind of knowledge,
of particular circumstances of time and place, that a central authority
cannot hold in principle, and it reads the price system as a mechanism for
communicating the part of that knowledge each decision needs.

## Key insight

A decision maker needs to know how much scarcer something has become, not
why. In the tin example: "It does not matter for our purpose—and it is
very significant that it does not matter—which of these two causes has made
tin more scarce." A price condenses the significance of a change "in view
of the whole means-end structure" into one number. Through chains of
people whose "limited individual fields of vision sufficiently overlap",
the whole economy adjusts while almost nobody knows the cause. Decisions
that depend on local knowledge can therefore be decentralized and still fit
together.

## Assumptions

- The economic problem is "mainly one of rapid adaptation to changes in the
  particular circumstances of time and place". "economic problems arise
  always and only in consequence of change." If plans could be laid down in
  advance and kept, the case for central planning would be "much less
  formidable".
- Two kinds of knowledge: scientific knowledge of general rules, which a
  body of experts might command, and knowledge of particular circumstances,
  which "by its nature cannot enter into statistics and therefore cannot be
  conveyed to any central authority in statistical form".
- Statistics are made "by lumping together, as resources of one kind, items
  which differ as regards location, quality, and other particulars".
- The stability of aggregates is not the law of large numbers: "The number
  of elements with which we have to deal is not large enough for such
  accidental forces to produce stability." It is kept by "constant
  deliberate adjustments".
- No model is given. The argument is conceptual, with examples (the
  shipper of tramp-steamers, the estate agent, the arbitrageur, the tin
  shortage).

## Key results

- **§I.** The optimum problem, given all information, preferences and
  means, is "purely one of logic", solved by equal marginal rates of
  substitution. "This, however, is emphatically not the economic problem
  which society faces."
- **§II.** All economic activity is planning. The dispute is over who
  plans: "centrally, by one authority for the whole economic system, or ...
  divided among many individuals". "The halfway house between the two ...
  is the delegation of planning to organized industries, or, in other
  words, monopoly." Efficiency turns on whether it is easier to bring
  dispersed knowledge to a central authority or to convey to individuals
  "such additional knowledge as they need in order to enable them to fit
  their plans with those of others."
- **§§III–IV.** Knowledge of particular circumstances gives "practically
  every individual ... some advantage over all others", usable "only if the
  decisions depending on it are left to him or are made with his active
  coöperation". Central planning on statistical information "by its nature
  cannot take direct account of these circumstances".
- **§V.** "We must solve it by some form of decentralization." But the man
  on the spot still needs information from beyond his horizon, and needs
  only the relative importance of the things he deals with. Prices supply
  it, as "rates of equivalence" do for a single mind solving its own
  allocation.
- **§VI.** The price system is "a mechanism for communicating
  information", whose most significant feature is "the economy of knowledge
  with which it operates". It "fulfils [this] less perfectly as prices grow
  more rigid". It is "not the product of human design", and the problem is
  "how to extend the span of our utilization of resources beyond the span
  of the control of any one mind". The same problem arises "with language
  and with most of our cultural inheritance".
- **§VII.** Against Schumpeter's claim that consumers' valuation of goods
  "ipso facto" values the factors of production: implication "can be
  meaningfully asserted only of propositions simultaneously present to one
  and the same mind." The task is to show "how a solution is produced by
  the interactions of people each of whom possesses only partial
  knowledge."

## Concepts

- **Planning**: "the complex of interrelated decisions about the allocation
  of our available resources". Everyone plans; the dispute is who.
- **Knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place**:
  "unorganized knowledge which cannot possibly be called scientific in the
  sense of knowledge of general rules", fleeting and local.
- **The man on the spot**: the person familiar with the circumstances, who
  "know[s] directly of the relevant changes and of the resources
  immediately available to meet them".
- **Economy of knowledge**: "how little the individual participants need to
  know in order to be able to take the right action".
- **Datum**: Hayek's charge is that the word's ambiguity, given to whom,
  sets "a trap" for economists who treat facts known to the observing
  economist as if they were known to the agents.

## Connections

It continues the socialist-calculation debate begun by von Mises "twenty-five
years ago", answering Lange and Lerner, and it rejects the reading of Pareto
and Barone as having solved the problem: they "merely ... state[d] the
conditions which a rational allocation of resources would have to satisfy."
Its foil is Schumpeter's *Capitalism, Socialism, and Democracy*. Its
mechanism, prices as low-dimensional messages, was later formalized as the
informational efficiency of the competitive mechanism (Hurwicz; Mount and
Reiter). The record holds none of those works.

- **CLAIM-tmpyxupb.** A18 cited Hayek for "the informational

  "halfway house", planning delegated to organized industries, is a case

## Limitations

- **No model and no evidence.** Every result is argued from examples and
  from the logic of choice. The claim that prices convey "the relevant
  information" is never stated precisely enough to test: what counts as
  relevant, and how close to right the resulting adjustment is, are left
  open. Hayek grants that the adjustments are "probably never 'perfect'".
- **Prices only.** The essay says nothing about what prices fail to carry:
  externalities, information held strategically, or knowledge that matters
  for decisions with no market. Its claim that the coordination problem is
  shared by language and culture is asserted, not shown.
- **The comparison is two-sided.** It argues that dispersed knowledge
  cannot be centralized. It does not show that decentralized decision makers
  get the knowledge they need in every case, only in the tin example.
- **Reprint, not page images.** The Econlib text was not compared with the
  original pages.

## Open questions

- For which decisions is local knowledge decisive and for which is it not?
  Hayek grants that experts may command scientific knowledge. The essay
  gives no criterion for when a decision depends mainly on one kind.
- What does the argument imply for allocations made with no price, inside
  an organization or a state? The halfway house of §II is named and not
  analysed.
