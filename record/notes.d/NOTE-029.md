# NOTE-029: Krauska & Lau 2023, moving away from lexicalism

## Contribution

Mainstream psycho- and neurolinguistic models retrieve "lemmas" or "lexical items" and combine them. This assumes lexical items store meaning, syntax and form together, that lexical and syntactic processes are separate, and that syntax does not go below the word. Decades of cross-linguistic work contradict this: syntax operates both above and below the word, and meaning and form depend partly on syntactic context. The authors review the arguments against lexicalism, show how lexicalist assumptions shape production models, and list phenomena those models struggle with. They sketch a non-lexicalist production model without lemmas, using separate meaning–syntax and syntax–form mappings.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1 Introduction: lexicalism entered processing models as "an accident of history", since early researchers mostly spoke English or Dutch and the dominant syntax theories treated words as the units of combination.
- §2.1 Lemmas (Fig. 1, Levelt et al. 1999, "escorting"): lemmas are terminal syntactic nodes with diacritic parameters pointing to morpheme forms. Morphologically complex words like "nationalize" get simplex lemma representations. §§2.1.3–2.1.5 cover incrementality, treelet approaches (described as a step in the right direction) and language-specific optimization.
- §3.1 Non-lexicalist model: eight stages. Message generation, message-to-syntax mapping, pre-syntactic prosody, syntax-to-phonology segments, cognitive control for linearization, local phonology and buffer, post-syntactic prosody, and articulation.
- §3.2 Aphasia: because the mappings are distinct, damage to syntax building could still yield fluent but syntactically erroneous speech (paragrammatism), and deficits can be masked by later well-formedness rules.
- §4 Conclusion: intuitive wordhood is not a unit of meaning, syntax or phonology (examples include Inuktitut, Vietnamese, idioms, "dyawanna"). The authors suggest it may come from orthographic white space, citing Haspelmath (2017).

## Open questions

- It carries the typological argument (c61, c63) into cognitive and neural models. The claim that wordhood intuitions come from orthography matters for how word-level units in language models are interpreted.
- The proposed model is a sketch without computational implementation or new data. Check which predictions are concrete enough to test.
- It depends on Bruening (c63) and Haspelmath (c61) for the linguistic case, so filing these together makes sense.
