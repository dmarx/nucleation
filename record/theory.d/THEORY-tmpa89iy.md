# THEORY-tmpa89iy: Automated decision systems in public administration combine individual adjudication with rulemaking while observing the procedural safeguards of neither, because programmers change policy when they encode it and the systems decide cases opaquely, so that a technological due process is needed to restore those safeguards' aims

Citron (2008), LIT-tmp7wp1i.

## The argument

"Code, not rules, determines the outcomes of adjudications" (abstract).
Programmers "in fact" make rules when they encode policy (p. 1254), "a
troubling delegation of legislative power" (p. 1288). Individual
adjudication fails in turn: notice without an audit trail cannot say
which facts or rules decided a case, hearing officers defer to the system
("Automation bias effectively turns a computer program's suggested answer
into a trusted final decision", p. 1272), and a Mathews analysis of one
case captures "only a tiny fraction" of the benefits of better process
(p. 1285). The evidence is case narratives from mid-2000s benefits and
watch-list systems.

## What it does not show

- **That the remedies work.** Audit trails, open code, testing suites and
  IT review boards are proposals.
- **That the argument carries to learned models.** The systems in view
  are rules engines and data-matching systems.
- **That a human in the loop never helps.** The claim about mixed systems
  rests on the automation-bias literature as it stood in 2008.
