# THEORY-514: Self-maintenance through closure of constraints does not single out organisms, since colonies, holobionts, symbioses and ecosystems meet it too, so a persister criterion applied at whatever level meets it turns organisms into parts; what is proposed to mark organisms is that they can also induce, control and come through phases of destabilised organisation and reach a new closure regime, though that only organisms can do so is asserted

## Source

Jan Baedke (2025), *The Organism*, Cambridge Elements in the Philosophy of
Biology, [LIT-167](../literature.d/LIT-167.md), read in full in [NOTE-148](../notes.d/NOTE-148.md).

## What was actually shown

**The problem (p. 24).** An Inward Challenge, what internal organisation
makes something an organism rather than another unit, and an Outward
Challenge, how to separate an organism from an environment it
co-constructs.

**Persistence is not enough (pp. 28–34).** Godfrey-Smith's "Organisms are
essentially persisters", Smith's version, and the metabolic, thermodynamic
and closure views all admit too much. Closure has been applied to
symbioses, ecosystems and eusocial colonies; Smith applies persistence to
siphonophores, beehives and holobionts; Mossio concedes that organised
systems are not necessarily organisms (fn. 22). Applied at whatever level
meets it, the criterion makes every contributing unit "a mere part"
(p. 31): either humans are parts of holobionts, or "organisms can be made
up of organisms" (fn. 23).

**Overcomers (pp. 38–45).** Against the view that a system goes from one
closed regime to another or dies, Baedke claims that organisms tolerate
and actively induce deorganisation and reorganisation, as a
non-pathological part of life cycles, and that change need not be a
continuous series of states of closure. Cases: anglerfish sexual
parasitism (loss of adaptive-immunity genes, fusion into "a new metabolic
and morphological whole"); *Elysia marginata* regenerating a whole body
from its head within about 20 days; *Harpegnathos saltator* gamergates
(brain volume −20%, ovaries 5×, lifespan from about six months to three
years or more, reversibly).

**The verdicts (pp. 43, 48–49).** The *Harpegnathos* colony is a
"functionally immortal persister" that "is not an organism"; the
individual ant is the overcomer. Ruminant microbes are distinct organisms
with the rumen as their environment, not parts of a holobiont individual.

**What could have come out otherwise.** The negative half could have
failed if closure criteria, applied carefully, ranked colonies below their
members. The literature Baedke surveys applies them to colonies without
such a ranking.

## What this does not say

- **That only organisms overcome.** "Only organisms can" (pp. 39–40) is
  asserted. No non-organismal persister is examined; a colony that loses
  its queen and reorganises through a tournament is a plausible candidate,
  and is described only as persisting.
- **That the cases show organisms acting.** The anglerfish's immune-gene
  loss happened over evolutionary time in lineages; the gamergate
  transition is triggered by the colony context. "Deliberately", in the
  earlier skim, is not Baedke's word.
- **A definition.** Persister, overcomer and bounded locus of causation are
  listed as features (Box 3), with no sufficient condition.
- **That collectives are not individuals of any kind.** Organismality and
  individuality are separate questions here (fn. 13): a colony can be an
  evolutionary individual and not an organism.
- **How to individuate against the environment.** That is done by a
  separate, relational model, the unrolled organism–environment graph of
  [LIT-110](../literature.d/LIT-110.md), which picks out boundaries relative to an explanation;
  "unambiguously" (pp. 3, 46) claims more than it gives.
- **Which piece of an autotomised slug, or which of a fused anglerfish
  pair, is the organism.** The part–whole puzzles the cases raise are left
  open.
