# LIT-tmp5ozbs: Personal Identity

Derek Parfit (1971), "Personal Identity", *The Philosophical Review* 80(1),
January 1971, pp. 3–27 — DOI-10.2307/2184309

**On the citation.** The brief's details are all right: author, title,
journal, year, volume 80, issue 1, pages 3–27. Crossref gives only the first
page and the month; the JSTOR cover page gives the range. `published:` is
the issue month, since no source gives a day.

## Key takeaways

- **Two targets.** The belief that in every describable case "the question
  about identity must have an answer", and the belief that "unless the
  question about identity has an answer, we cannot answer certain
  important questions" (survival, memory, responsibility) (pp. 3–4).
- **Division breaks the tie between identity and what matters.** If each
  half of my brain is housed in a new body, "I do not survive" is absurd
  ("How could a double success be a failure?"), and "I survive as one" is
  arbitrary. "The relation of the original person to each of the resulting
  people contains all that interests us—all that matters—in any ordinary
  case of survival" (p. 10).
- **Identity is one-one and all-or-nothing; what matters need not be
  either.** Division shows it need not be one-one; fusion and gradual
  change show "most of the relations which matter in survival are, in
  fact, relations of degree" (p. 11).
- **A criterion survives the objection.** "X and Y are the same person if
  they are psychologically continuous and there is no person who is
  contemporary with either and psychologically continuous with the other"
  (p. 13), offered as sufficient, not necessary.
- **The relations can be described without identity.** Q-memory and
  q-intention are memory and intention without the condition that the
  experience or action was one's own (pp. 14–17).
- **Connectedness versus continuity.** Connectedness (direct relations) is
  not transitive and comes in degrees; continuity (overlapping chains) is
  transitive (p. 20). Speaking of "past selves" and "future selves" by
  degree of connectedness, "there is no underlying person who we both are"
  (p. 25).
- **The principle of self-interest has no force of its own** (p. 26). Its
  apparent force comes from caring what happens to one, or from
  impartiality; "all the parts of his future are equally parts of his
  future" is "a truth too superficial to bear the weight of the argument".

Where it meets the record, set out in the NOTE:

- **The survey** ([LIT-130](LIT-130.md), [NOTE-166](../notes.d/NOTE-166.md)) reports the fission argument as the
  route by which "what matters" is detached from identity (its C1), and
  gives the psychological criterion as unique psychological continuity.
  Both are here, in their first form; the survey cites the later works.
- **Birch's persisting-interlocutor argument** ([LIT-111](LIT-111.md), [NOTE-183](../notes.d/NOTE-183.md)) denies
  that a chatbot "friend" persists because conversation history is not
  psychological continuity. The deciding condition is this paper's clause
  (2) of q-memory: a q-memory must depend on the experience "in the same way
  (whatever that is)" as a memory does. A transcript handed to a successor
  does not. Birch argues from Parfit's later "Relation R"; the condition is
  already here.
- **Goldstein and Lederman** ([LIT-206](LIT-206.md), [NOTE-138](../notes.d/NOTE-138.md)) make the per-conversation
  instance, not the model, the bearer of beliefs and desires. In this
  paper's terms, instances drawn from one model are a tree of descendant
  selves with a shared ancestral self and no connectedness between
  branches. The paper's point that each branch is "as good as survival"
  while none is the original is the pattern their instance view needs, and
  does not argue for. The pairing is mine.
- **Dennett's "Where Am I?"** ([LIT-405](LIT-405.md), [NOTE-338](../notes.d/NOTE-338.md)). Yorick and Hubert in
  step are Parfit's divided mind before reunion; their drift is division
  without reunion, and Dennett's "two persons" is Parfit's verdict that past
  the borderline "it would become intolerable to deny that they were
  different people" (p. 7).
- **The narrative self** ([LIT-442](LIT-442.md), [NOTE-364](../notes.d/NOTE-364.md)). Dennett's two selves in one
  body when the story coheres around two points (its C5) and Parfit's
  successive selves within one life are both selves individuated more finely
  than persons. Parfit's are fixed by degree of connectedness, Dennett's by
  the coherence of an interpretation.
- **Frankfurt and Velleman** ([LIT-293](LIT-293.md), [LIT-292](LIT-292.md), [NOTE-268](../notes.d/NOTE-268.md)). [NOTE-268](../notes.d/NOTE-268.md) records
  Velleman reading Frankfurt's late view as Parfitian in counting the
  persistence of valued traits toward survival. That strand is in this paper
  as "lasting q-characteristic" among the direct relations (p. 20), though
  the weighting by value comes later.
