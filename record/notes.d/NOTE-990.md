# NOTE-990: Lush, Botan, Scott, Seth, Ward & Dienes — Trait phenomenological control and the rubber hand illusion

## Contribution

It put a measured suggestion trait beside the rubber hand illusion in a
sample large enough to see the relation. Before it, the illusion's
suggestion checks were the asynchronous condition and control statements,
and two smaller studies had read non-significant correlations with
suggestibility as evidence against a role for suggestion (p. 6). Here, in
353 people, trait hypnotisability predicted the ownership reports, and
predicted them as much after asynchronous brushing as after synchronous
brushing. It predicted the control item too. The same trait predicted
vicarious pain and mirror touch in two other samples. After this paper,
the usual checks cannot be cited as having controlled for suggestion.

## Key insight

The rubber hand illusion's controls compare a condition people expect to
work with one they expect not to. A trait that makes people experience
what they expect therefore raises the illusion score, the control score
and the control item together, and the synchronous-minus-asynchronous
contrast does not remove it. Whether anything is left of the illusion
once expectation is matched is the question this paper sharpens and does
not answer.

## Assumptions

- **Hypnotisability measures one context-general ability.** Response to
  imaginative suggestion is "the top-down voluntary control of action and
  experience (which is experienced as involuntary) according to a stable
  trait ability", and hypnosis is just one context for it (p. 2). The
  authors call it "phenomenological control". They present this as
  consensus in hypnosis research (p. 2; PRF), which reviewer 2 disputed:
  what was measured is the relation between suggestibility and the
  illusions, which "doesn't demonstrate control in any strong sense".
- **Responses to suggestion are genuine experiences.** "If
  phenomenological control produced effects in embodiment experiments,
  the resulting reports of experiences would be genuine" (p. 6). This is
  taken from the hypnosis literature, not tested here.
- **A shared trait means a shared mechanism.** Correlations of the size
  between single hypnosis items and the whole scale are read as the two
  measures reflecting the same ability, "a parsimonious theory ... which
  cannot currently be ruled out" (pp. 5, 7).
- **SWASH subjective scores measure the trait.** The subjective scale
  (0–5 per item, averaged) was used for its "greater reliability than the
  objective scale" (p. 8). In study 2 it was given by computer with a
  recorded induction (pp. 7–8).
- **Synchronous-condition scores are the field's measure.** The headline
  analyses use synchronous scores, not difference scores, because the
  twenty most-cited studies mostly do (p. 4; SI p. 2).

## Key results

- **Samples (pp. 7–8).** Study 1 matched existing SWASH data to 404 people
  screened for vicarious pain and 154 screened for mirror touch (the
  abstract says 156). Study 2: 385 undergraduates did SWASH screening in a
  class practical, and 353 completed both it and the rubber hand session.
  They were assigned to a synchrony-instruction group (n = 114), an
  asynchrony-instruction group (n = 115) or an uninstructed control group
  (n = 124, including nine whose instructions failed to play). Sixteen
  experimenters, blind to instruction, ran five set-ups at once.
- **Procedure (pp. 7–8, Table 1).** Right hand hidden in a box, its index finger 20 cm
  lateral to the rubber hand's, both middle fingers brushed at about 1 Hz for 60 s,
  synchronously or with an asynchrony, in alternating order. Before
  induction participants rated two expectancy items (E1, E2: how strongly
  they expected to feel the rubber hand as theirs under synchrony and
  under asynchrony, −3 to +3). Drift: pointing to the felt position of the
  index finger on a ruler offset by a variable amount, before and after
  each induction. Afterwards four statements, −3 to +3: S1 touch felt
  where the rubber hand was touched, S2 touch caused by the brush on the
  rubber hand, S3 "I felt as if the rubber hand were my hand", and S4
  (control) "The rubber hand began to resemble my own (real) hand".
- **Vicarious pain (p. 3, Fig. 1).** Per SWASH point, b = 1.25 more pain
  responses out of 16 (t(402) = 5.55, R² 0.071, rs 0.26) and b = 0.46 more
  intensity (R² 0.082, rs 0.27); Bayes factors 5.7 × 10⁵ and 2.6 × 10⁶.
- **Mirror touch (p. 3, Fig. 2).** Touch to a human: b = 0.83 responses out
  of 14 (t(152) = 3.07, p = 0.003, R² 0.058, rs 0.19, B = 11.98). Touch
  to objects: b = 0.18 out of 6 (p = 0.012, R² 0.041, rs 0.21, B = 5.23).
- **The expectation manipulation failed (pp. 3–4).** Mean expectancy for
  synchronous induction was 0.9, 0.9 and 0.7 in the synchrony, asynchrony
  and control groups; for asynchronous induction −0.1, 0.2 and −0.6. The
  instruction × induction interaction on expectancy was insensitive
  (F(1,255) = 2.06, p = 0.153, B = 0.72). "We are unable to directly
  establish a causal role for phenomenological control in the RHI" (p. 4).
  The authors speculate that participants knew of the illusion, that the
  task carries "strong expectancies inherent" in it, or that the
  asynchrony rationale (that 1/3 s is "the optimal delay", SI p. 3) was
  not believed. Kirsch had asked whether the rationale was convincing
  (PRF).
- **The illusion appeared by the usual check (p. 4).** In the control
  group, synchronous drift exceeded asynchronous (0.93 against 0.38 cm,
  t(123) = 2.18, p = 0.031, d = 0.20, B = 4.51), and S1–S3 was higher (0.9
  against −0.7, t(123) = 9.89, d = 0.89). The group's expectations ran the
  same way: 0.7 for synchrony against −0.6 for asynchrony.
- **Pre-registered key tests, control group (p. 4).** Hypnotisability
  against the synchronous-minus-asynchronous difference: for drift, b =
  0.64 cm per SWASH point, one-tailed p = 0.037, B = 2.91, "anecdotal";
  for S1–S3, b = −0.002, B = 0.61, "no sensitive evidence for or against".
- **The analyses not pre-registered, all 353 combined by fixed-effects
  meta-analysis (pp. 4–5, Fig. 3; SI Table 1).** Per SWASH point after
  synchronous brushing: S1–S3 b = 0.57 (R² 0.09, rs 0.26, B = 1.5 × 10⁶);
  S4 b = 0.83 (R² 0.11, rs 0.31); drift b = 0.58 cm (p = 0.008, R² 0.02,
  rs 0.11, B = 14.66). After asynchronous brushing: S1–S3 b = 0.70 (R²
  0.09); S4 b = 0.87 (R² 0.12); drift b = 0.02 cm, B = 0.67, insensitive.
  So "for each 1 point increase in SWASH score ... subjective report
  (S1–S3) increased by approximately half a point ... and proprioceptive
  drift increased by approximately 0.5 cm" (p. 6).
- **The drift slope is not uniform across groups (SI Table 1).**
  Synchronous drift on SWASH was b = 1.06 cm [0.42, 1.70] in the control
  group, 0.49 [−0.31, 1.29] in the synchrony-instruction group and 0.009
  [−0.72, 0.74] in the asynchrony-instruction group. The questionnaire
  slopes were similar across groups (0.50 to 0.65).
- **Expectancy predicted report, not drift (p. 5, Fig. 3).** Synchronous
  expectancy predicted synchronous S1–S3 (b = 0.33 per expectancy point,
  R² 0.06, rs 0.23, B = 1.66 × 10⁴). Its relation to synchronous drift was
  insensitive (b = 0.09, p = 0.551, B = 0.38).
- **Combined with Walsh et al. (p. 6).** Walsh et al. reported r = 0.55
  between a drift difference score and hypnotisability; combining that
  with these data gives r = 0.22 [0.05, 0.38], B = 13.86, "even when
  measured in this way, proprioceptive drift is related to
  hypnotisability".
- **The field's practice (SI p. 2).** Of the twenty most-cited studies
  reporting subjective measures, none used the synchronous–asynchronous
  difference as the measure; most tested the difference and then reported
  synchronous scores. Of twenty reporting drift, seven used the difference
  and thirteen the synchronous magnitude. The lists include Botvinick &
  Cohen 1998, Rohde et al. 2011, Kalckert & Ehrsson 2012, Longo et al.
  2008, Suzuki et al. 2013 and Tsakiris et al. 2011.
- **What the authors conclude (pp. 5–7).** "Existing approaches to control
  for suggestion effects in these measures are not effective" (p. 5). "Future
  RHI studies must ensure that expectancies and difficulty are closely
  matched across all control and illusion conditions" (p. 6). "To the
  degree to which measures of embodiment effects are driven by
  phenomenological control, they cannot provide a solid foundation for
  theories about embodiment" (p. 6). Physiological measures (skin
  conductance, histamine reactivity) and brain imaging are "also not
  evidence against suggestion effects", since suggestion can produce them
  (p. 6). They predict the same relation for the full-body illusion and
  for reports of agency (p. 7), untested.
- **What the peer review changed (PRF).** The first version's claims that
  the effect would "transform human behavioural science" and was "likely
  to confound measures across behavioural science" were removed at
  reviewer 2's objection. S4 went from "is an example of demand
  characteristic driven visual hallucination" to "may be an example of
  demand characteristic driven subjective report compatible with a visual
  hallucination" after the reviewer wrote that "It is just ratings of
  agreement with a statement".

## Concepts

- **Phenomenological control** — "context-general top-down effects on
  perception in response to imaginative suggestion" (p. 2), measured in a
  hypnotic context by hypnotisability scales; voluntary, though
  "experienced as involuntary".
- **Imaginative suggestion** — a suggestion to experience something
  imagined as real; hypnotic induction adds "only a small boost in
  response (around 10%)" (p. 2).
- **Demand characteristics** — after Orne, cues to the experimenter's aims
  that can change behaviour; the authors stress that Orne allowed them to
  change "genuine" phenomenology too (p. 7).
- **SWASH** — the Sussex-Waterloo Scale of Hypnotisability, ten
  suggestions, one tactile (a mosquito on the hand), scored objectively
  and subjectively (pp. 6–7).
- **Proprioceptive drift** — the change in the pointed position of the
  hidden hand from before to after induction (p. 2).
- **Mirror touch and vicarious pain** — reports of touch or pain on one's
  own body while watching touch or pain to another (p. 2).

## Connections

It builds on the hypnosis literature on imaginative suggestion (Kirsch,
Lynn, Terhune) and on Dienes's cold-control theory, and on Lush 2020 in
Collabra, which found that naive participants told about the procedure
expected the classic pattern, the illusion under synchrony and not under
asynchrony (p. 6, ref. 36). It reinterprets illusions reported without
synchronous touch, from mere expectation of touch (Ferri et al.), for
drawings of hands and for empty space, as suggestion effects (p. 6). It
cites Rohde et al. 2011 among unimodal-stimulation results (ref. 55). It
lists Botvinick & Cohen 1998, Kalckert & Ehrsson 2012 and Longo et al.
2008 among the most-cited studies whose controls it calls ineffective (SI
p. 2). It disagrees with Walsh et al. 2015 and Marotta et al. 2016, which
argued from non-significant correlations that drift is not suggestion
(p. 6). The Nature Communications exchange that followed, Ehrsson et al.
2022 and Lush & Seth 2022, and Roseboom & Lush 2022 are being filed
alongside this reading. Seth is a co-author, and his 2013 article on the
illusion ([LIT-1263](../literature.d/LIT-1263.md)) builds on results of the kind this paper says are
not controlled for suggestion.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-765](../theory.d/THEORY-765.md) (the synchrony effect).** This is the design
  that THEORY's `promote_when` asks for, in form. It gives sample sizes,
  named tests and effect sizes for both measures, measures trait
  suggestibility and measures expectations. In substance it neither
  meets the promotion test nor refutes the THEORY.
  - *Promotion is not met.* Expectations were not matched: the
    manipulation failed, and in the control group they ran in the
    direction of the effect, 0.7 for synchrony against −0.6 for
    asynchrony. Trait suggestibility was measured, but the test of whether
    the synchrony effect survives it, the regression of the difference
    on hypnotisability, was insensitive for report (B = 0.61) and
    anecdotal for drift. Whether the synchrony difference remains at low
    hypnotisability was not reported. The data are on OSF.
  - *The refutation conditions are not met either.* With expectations
    unmatched, the first cannot be tested here. On the second, drift was
    larger under synchrony (0.93 against 0.38 cm, d = 0.20), so it did not
    appear as strongly under asynchrony.
  - *It bears against the THEORY's evidence, not its claim.* The
    ownership reports Botvinick & Cohen relied on are shown to share
    variance with a suggestion trait, at both levels and in the control
    item. The "control items guard against blanket agreement, not
    against suggestion" caveat in the THEORY is borne out with data.
  - *Proposal only:* the `promote_when` could add that a measured trait
    must be shown not to carry the synchrony difference, for instance by
    the difference at low hypnotisability, since "survives" is ambiguous
    between those. The status should stay Proposed.
- **[THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md) (graded common-cause belief).** No parametric
  manipulation of reliability or correlation, so its `promote_when` is
  untouched. Two indirect bearings.
  - Ownership reports are graded, but here the gradation tracks a trait
    unrelated to the sensory input, so graded questionnaire ownership by
    itself no longer favours the common-cause account. A model-based test
    should include such a covariate or a matched-expectation design.
  - The paper argues that skin conductance and histamine changes can be
    produced by suggestion. That adds a second reason, beside the one the
    THEORY gives, for its exclusion of those measures from the "real hand
    loses ownership" prong.
  - The synchrony instruction given here was itself the common-cause
    rationale, and it did not raise expectations beyond the uninstructed
    group's.
- **[THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md) (interoception in ownership).** The paper does not
  mention interoception. Its supplement lists Suzuki et al. 2013 and
  Tsakiris et al. 2011, the THEORY's two named sources, among the
  most-cited studies using the controls it calls ineffective. Two
  consequences, which are the record's inference:
  - the cardio-visual synchronous-against-asynchronous contrast is the
    kind of control shown here not to exclude expectation, so the first
    prong should also ask for matched expectations or a suggestion
    covariate, beside the non-bodily periodic signal it already asks for;
  - the sensitivity–susceptibility relation could be carried by a trait
    other than interoception, so the second prong should ask that it
    survive hypnotisability.
  This is a proposal to tighten the `promote_when`, not evidence against
  the THEORY. Seth co-authored both papers.
- **[THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) (minimal selfhood).** Neither prong is touched:
  self-location and whole-body self-identification were not measured
  during the rubber hand illusion, and nobody without an agency history
  was tested. The authors predict the same relation with hypnotisability
  for the full-body illusion (p. 7). If it holds, the questionnaire
  evidence from Lenggenhager et al. 2007 that the THEORY leans on, Q3
  after synchronous stroking, carries the same confound.
- **[THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md) (ownership and agency).** The paper predicts, without
  testing, that reports of changes in the sense of agency will track
  hypnotisability (p. 7). Its framing of suggestion responses as
  voluntary control "experienced as involuntary" is itself a claimed
  dissociation between being the source of an experience and feeling
  oneself its source, which bears on the agency half of that THEORY. It
  is asserted here, not shown.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md) (what kinds of evidence show).** A worked case.
  Report measures shared variance with a suggestion trait and with
  expectation. The behavioural measure, drift, shared much less (rs
  0.11) and was not shown to follow expectancy. The authors grant that
  physiological and imaging measures cannot settle it either, since
  suggestion produces them. The paper is a direct argument that a report,
  however genuine the experience behind it, does not say what produced
  the experience.
- **[NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md), claim C6.** "Body ownership can be manipulated by
  multisensory conflict" assumed that the manipulation acts through the
  multisensory conflict. This paper shows that the reports it rests on
  are also predicted by a trait unrelated to the conflict. That weakens
  the attribution, not the manipulability.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **Correlational.** The expectation manipulation failed, and the authors
  say they "do not directly provide evidence for anything beyond these
  relationships" (p. 7).
- **The headline analyses were not pre-registered.** The authors justify
  the switch to synchronous-only scores by the field's practice, and say
  that the pre-registered measures "were not well motivated by the
  literature" (p. 4). The pre-registered tests were inconclusive.
- **The effects are modest.** R² is 0.06 to 0.12 for reports and 0.02
  for drift; most of the variance in the illusion is not this trait.
- **Brief induction, small drift.** 60 s of brushing gave mean drift under
  1 cm, much smaller than the 30-minute exposures of the original report.
- **The asynchrony is loosely specified.** It is given as "an asynchrony
  of approximately 0.5 Hz" (p. 8), a frequency, not a delay.
- **Slips in the text.** The abstract gives 156 for the mirror-touch
  sample where the results and methods give 154. Fig. 3's caption puts
  drift in panel b and S4 in c, the text the reverse. The methods define
  difference scores as asynchronous minus synchronous, the results as
  synchronous minus asynchronous. Two printed t values do not follow from
  their slopes and standard errors: asynchronous S1–S3 repeats the
  synchronous t(347) = 5.86, and asynchronous drift gives t(347) = 1.03
  for b = 0.02, SE 0.25. Two confidence intervals in Supplementary
  Table 1 repeat the row above. None changes an inference the authors
  draw.
- **Sample.** Undergraduates, mostly women, in a class practical; SWASH by
  computer.
- **"Control".** Reviewer 2's objection stands: the study measures an
  association between suggestibility and the illusions, and that the
  ability is "control" is imported from the hypnosis literature.

## Open questions

- With expectations matched, by a credible manipulation or by a
  condition people do not know to expect, does synchronous brushing still
  produce ownership reports beyond asynchronous brushing? This is the
  test [THEORY-765](../theory.d/THEORY-765.md) needs and this study could not run.
- In the OSF data, does the synchrony difference remain among low
  hypnotisables? The regression intercept would say, and it is not
  reported.
- Is drift, which shared little variance with the trait, the measure least
  driven by expectation, or only the noisiest?
- Does the relation hold for full-body illusions and for agency reports,
  as the authors predict?
- Ehrsson et al. 2022 contest the inference; whether their reanalysis or
  new data change any number above is for the readings of that exchange.
