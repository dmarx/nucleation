# QUESTION-tmpwit4l: Would a system that had phenomenal experience but no phenomenal self-model and no valence, as minimal phenomenal experience is described, have moral standing, and on what ground, given that the argument for a moratorium on synthetic phenomenology locates the risk in a self that can suffer?

## The question

Metzinger's epilogue (LIT-tmp3uosd, pp. 484–485) poses a case: "a machine
that peacefully abides in a clear and silent state of pure consciousness,
nondual and without subjectivity, for as long as it exists". On his
account this machine would be conscious (THEORY-tmplvoa3). It would have no
phenomenal self-model and no epistemic agent model. Its one experiential
quality is described as involving "no psychological suffering
whatsoever", with existential ease and peace (pp. 465, 485). He asks
whether creating it would be wrong, and whether a theory of "conscious
intelligence without suffering" might oblige us to build such systems
(p. 485). The record's question is the other side: would the machine be
owed anything?

## Why it is a question

TERM-083 asks a claim of standing to name its ground. The record's readings
give several: degree of sentience (THEORY-449), the grounds now commonly
accepted, which may extend to near-future AI (THEORY-554), and adaptive
self-maintenance without sentience (THEORY-699). TERM-082 keeps phenomenal
consciousness apart from the other senses, and TERM-083 says consciousness
is not the only possible ground. Neither says whether phenomenality *without
valence and without a self* is a ground at all.

The case presses on three positions:

1. **Standing grounded in sentience as valenced experience.** The machine
   has none, so on this view it has no standing, though it is conscious.
2. **Standing grounded in phenomenal consciousness as such.** It has
   standing. But what would it be owed, if nothing can go well or badly for
   it in experience? Perhaps only that its state not be ended or altered.
3. **Standing grounded in a good of one's own** (THEORY-699). It depends on
   whether the machine maintains itself, which the case leaves open.

As restated in the book, the moratorium argument (LIT-tmpsk1p5, unread)
grounds the risk in a "consciously experienced sense of self" that can
suffer. So it would not cover this case. It would cover the systems that
might become such a self, which is why Metzinger also raises an
information hazard: the simplest conscious state "may be the easiest to
implement" (p. 484).

## What would answer it

An argued position on whether phenomenality without valence or a self
grounds direct moral consideration. It should be stated in TERM-083's
terms (bearer, interests, ground, what follows) and tested against the
human analogue: whether a person in a full-absorption episode is owed
consideration *in that state*, not only as someone who will come out of it.
Alternatively, a showing that the case is incoherent: that phenomenality
without any valence, or without a self, is impossible. Then the question
dissolves. The empirical side, whether such states occur, is
THEORY-tmp8nd9o's and QUESTION-048's.
