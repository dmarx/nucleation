# NOTE-745: Susser, Roessler and Nissenbaum — Technology, autonomy, and manipulation

## Contribution

A short policy-facing essay that gives "online manipulation" a definition
and a harm. The definition makes covertness, not the technique, the mark
of manipulation, and so separates manipulative nudges from informational
ones and deception from manipulation in general. The harm is placed in
autonomy, read in its competency and authenticity conditions, with the
economic harm derivative of it. Four policy directions follow.

## Key insight

Manipulation is influence the target does not and could not easily see.
Persuasion and coercion address their targets openly; manipulation works
only while it is hidden, and once an influence is known it becomes one
more input to the target's own deliberation. Digital technology suits it
because it exposes vulnerabilities, adapts to them in real time, and
itself recedes from attention through habit.

## Assumptions

- **Influence on individual decisions only (p. 3).** Manipulation as
  steering instruments, or as "attempts at steering or controlling
  institutions and systems" (Russian trolls and the US media), is set
  aside. "It is manipulation in this sense—the attempt to influence
  individual decision-making and behaviour—that we focus on in what follows."
- **A broad, relational conception of autonomy (pp. 8–9).** Deliberation
  includes "emotions, convictions, and experiences", and "critical
  reflection can be counterfactual (we must in principle be able to
  critically reflect on and endorse our motivations for acting, but we
  need not actually reflect on each and every move we make)". Persons are
  "socially, culturally, historically, and politically situated".
- **Liberal policy frame (p. 12).** The proposals are deliberately
  "situated firmly in the terrain of contemporary liberal political
  discourse".

## Key results

- **The definition (p. 4).** "In our view, manipulation is hidden
  influence. Or more fully, manipulating someone means intentionally and
  covertly influencing their decision-making, by targeting and exploiting
  their decision-making vulnerabilities. Covertly influencing
  someone—imposing a hidden influence—means influencing them in a way
  they aren't consciously aware of, and in a way they couldn't easily
  become aware of were they to try and understand what was impacting
  their decision-making process." It "hews especially close to the
  trickery view, but operationalises the notion of trickery more
  concretely".
- **Against persuasion and coercion (p. 4).** Both "are both forthright
  forms of influence". "Manipulation, by contrast, is hidden—we only
  learn that someone was trying to steer our decision-making after the
  fact, if we ever find out at all." What marks it is that "when we
  learn we have been manipulated we feel played"; "at the time of
  decision we didn't understand our own motivations."
- **Deception is a special case (p. 4).** One way to covertly influence
  is to plant false beliefs; others play on desires and emotions or on
  cognitive biases (p. 5) "without the need for outright deception".
- **Overt temptation and guilting (p. 5).** These are failed attempts at
  covert influence: "it is the attempted covertness that is central to
  manipulation, rather than the particular strategy". Someone who
  tempts overtly is manipulative in the sense that they "frequently
  attempt to manipulate", but "strictly speaking we would only say that
  they have succeeded in manipulating when their target is unaware of
  their machinations."
- **Nudges (pp. 5–6).** Some are manipulative and some are not, "the
  distinction hinging on whether or not the nudge is hidden, and whether
  it exploits vulnerabilities or attempts to rectify them". Nutrition
  labels are not; the cafeteria placement nudge "seems plausibly
  manipulative". "just because it's manipulative does not mean it isn't
  justified".
- **Online manipulation (p. 6).** "the use of information technology to
  covertly influence another person's decision-making, by targeting and
  exploiting decision-making vulnerabilities." Three reasons technology
  suits it (pp. 6–7):
  - surveillance puts vulnerabilities "on permanent display", including
    "person-specific" ones (Cambridge Analytica's psychometric targeting;
    a reported Facebook ability to detect insecure teenagers);
  - platforms are "dynamic, interactive, intrusive, and adaptive choice
    architectures" (Yeung's "highly personalised choice
    environment[s]");
  - habituated technology becomes "invisible", so "the influences they
    facilitate are often hidden, and thus potentially manipulative".
- **Not every case manipulates (pp. 7–8).** Dark patterns may "overtly
  inconvenienc[e] users, rather than hiding their intentions"; of Uber's
  and Lyft's prompts, "not all such practices necessarily
  manipulate—people are savvy". Manipulation "is not a binary
  phenomenon" perhaps, and influences may be "more or less manipulative"
  (p. 10).
- **The autonomy harm (pp. 9–10).** To manipulate is "to intentionally
  alter their decision-making process without their conscious
  awareness". It undermines autonomy in two ways: it "can lead them to
  act toward ends they haven't chosen" and "for reasons not authentically
  their own". It challenges both the competency condition and the
  authenticity condition.
- **Welfare harm is derivative (p. 10).** The economic harm "is
  derivative of the deeper harm to autonomy".
- **Beneficent manipulation still harms (pp. 10–11).** "the fundamental
  harm of manipulation is to the process of decision-making, not its
  outcome". The paternalist harms his target "by rendering him opaque to
  himself"; someone whose lifelong bad habit suddenly vanished "might
  also be deeply confused and suspicious about the source of the
  change".
- **Harm, not wrong (p. 11).** "we have not demonstrated that
  manipulation is necessarily wrong in every case—only that it always
  carries a harm". Cases where the harm is outweighed "are likely few and
  far between".
- **Collective harm (p. 11).** "autonomy is writ small what democracy is
  writ large".
- **Policy (pp. 11–13).** Curtail digital surveillance; problematise
  personalisation; promote awareness and understanding, noting that
  notice alone fails because neutralising manipulation needs knowing "who
  the manipulator is, what strategies they are deploying, and why"; and
  attend to context, since tolerance of influence differs between
  commerce and politics.

## Concepts

- **Manipulation.** Intentionally and covertly influencing someone's
  decision-making by targeting and exploiting their decision-making
  vulnerabilities.
- **Covert (hidden) influence.** Influence the target is not consciously
  aware of and could not easily become aware of on trying.
- **Online manipulation.** Manipulation by means of information
  technology, "online" read expansively; it "could just as easily be
  termed 'digital manipulation' or 'automated manipulation'".
- **Decision-making vulnerability.** Cognitive biases common to most
  people and "person-specific" fears, desires and circumstances.
- **Invisibility.** The phenomenologists' "technological transparency":
  a habituated device recedes from attention.
- **Autonomy.** After Raz, "people should make their own lives", with
  competency and authenticity conditions after Christman.

## Connections

The three-way division of existing accounts is taken from the 2018
version of Noggle's SEP entry ([LIT-1007](../literature.d/LIT-1007.md)), whose 2026 revision quotes this
paper's definition as "an especially influential version" of the
covert-influence view. The definition follows Ware and Strudler in that
family and claims kinship with the trickery view. The autonomy account
draws on Raz, Christman, Mackenzie and Stoljar's relational autonomy, and
Scanlon. The technology argument draws on Calo's "digital market
manipulation", Yeung's "hypernudge" and Susser's own "Invisible Influence"
(2019). Coons and Weber's volume ([LIT-1003](../literature.d/LIT-1003.md)) supplies its Wood citation.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md) quotes the definition correctly**: the paper does
  require that the influence be intentional, and says so three times
  (pp. 4, 5, 9). But the paper never discusses the requirement. It treats
  firms (advertisers, Facebook, Uber, Lyft, "data collectors and their
  clients") as the intending manipulators and never asks whether an
  adaptive system's influence can be manipulation when no one chose it.
  Its own mechanisms, though, are of exactly that kind: interfaces
  "configured in real time" that "continue to learn about us". So the
  paper supplies the definition [QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md) tests, and the cases that
  strain it, without noticing the strain. On its definition the [LIT-179](../literature.d/LIT-179.md)
  agent's influence would be manipulation only if the intent of those
  who deployed it carries over to the exploit it found.
- **The paper excludes manipulation of institutions and systems (p. 3)**,
  and it never considers manipulation by them. It is no evidence either
  way on whether an organized whole can manipulate.
- **For [QUESTION-071](../questions.d/QUESTION-071.md), the paper places manipulation's harm on the
  authenticity line the record has set aside.** It says manipulation leads
  people to act "for reasons not authentically their own" and challenges
  "our capacity to reflect on and endorse our reasons for acting as
  authentically on our own". That is the authenticity condition, the
  family of [CLAIM-016](../claims.d/CLAIM-016.md). The paper does not say how a manipulated agent's
  present structure differs from an unmanipulated one, and its
  counterfactual-reflection condition is itself a present-structure
  condition of the kind [THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md) says a manipulator can install.
- **But its criterion of manipulation is historical and epistemic, which
  bears on [QUESTION-071](../questions.d/QUESTION-071.md)'s second part.** Whether an influence manipulated
  depends on whether the agent was, and could easily have become, aware
  of it. The harm is self-opacity: "at the time of decision we didn't
  understand our own motivations"; the person whose habit vanished is
  "confused and suspicious about the source of the change". That makes
  the mark of manipulation a fact about the agent's access to the causal
  history of her state, not about the state. It is one answer to "what
  in its history would have to be recorded": whether the influence was
  available to the agent's reflection when it acted. The paper does not
  argue this for global manipulation. Its scope is single decisions.
- **[QUESTION-071](../questions.d/QUESTION-071.md) says the analyses filed with it say what makes an
  influence manipulation as against persuasion or coercion.** For this
  paper that is true: openness is the line (p. 4). It is not an account of
  the four-case argument's manipulation, which installs a whole
  character, and the paper says nothing on that.
- **[CLAIM-022](../claims.d/CLAIM-022.md) and [THEORY-047](../theory.d/THEORY-047.md).** The vanished-habit case agrees with
  [CLAIM-022](../claims.d/CLAIM-022.md) in kind: the agent is better off and her will works, and the
  paper still counts a harm in how the change came about. [THEORY-047](../theory.d/THEORY-047.md)'s
  controlling conditions of acquisition are not discussed.
- **[THEORY-139](../theory.d/THEORY-139.md).** No bearing; nothing on group agency.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice. Its subject, the ethics
  and regulation of influence by technology, is not one the anthology's
  topics hold.

## Limitations

- An essay of fourteen pages, by its own account a shortened version of
  a longer paper. The definition is stipulated and motivated by contrast,
  not defended against counterexamples. The objection that nagging, peer
  pressure and guilt trips are manipulative though open is answered only
  by calling them attempted manipulation (p. 5).
- No empirical work. The technology examples are reported, and the paper
  concedes that some of them may not manipulate.
- The requirement of intent is stated, not examined. Non-human or
  distributed manipulators are not considered.
- Autonomy is assumed to be the right frame; the arguments of S. Buss and
  others that manipulation's wrong has little to do with autonomy are not
  engaged.

## Open questions

- Whether "intentionally" can be dropped or relocated while keeping the
  line between manipulation and harmless influence, given the adaptive
  systems the paper itself describes.
- How "couldn't easily become aware of" is to be measured, and for whom.
  The paper's own policy section says awareness that one is targeted is
  not enough.
- Whether the harm to autonomy is a harm to the agent's present capacity
  or to her relation to the history of her choice. The vanished-habit
  case suggests the second.
