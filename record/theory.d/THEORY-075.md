# THEORY-075: Obligation is the reflective rejection of what would violate the agent's practical identity, and moral obligation follows only because valuing any practical identity commits the agent to valuing her humanity

## Which unity, and which word

This is an account in ethics, of where obligation comes from, and it is
about **self-governance**: being "a law to yourself". "Practical identity"
is not personal identity in the sense of what makes someone the same person
over time. It is "not a theoretical one, a view about what as a matter of
inescapable scientific fact you are" ([LIT-564](../literature.d/LIT-564.md), p. 83). It does bear on
**personhood**, since Korsgaard ties living up to one's standards to
integrity, "what makes him a person at all" (p. 84). It is not a claim about
the self as subject, and not about narrative unity: a practical identity is
a role or standing description ("a woman or a man, an adherent of a certain
religion ... someone's friend"), not a story.

## What was actually shown

**From reflection to identity.** "The reflective mind cannot settle for
perception and desire"; it "needs a reason" (p. 79). Choosing requires identifying
with a principle, and so a conception of one's practical identity, "a
description under which you value yourself, a description under which you
find your life to be worth living and your actions to be worth undertaking"
(p. 83). "For the average person there will be a jumble of such
conceptions" (p. 83). "Your reasons express your identity, your nature;
your obligations spring from what that identity forbids" (p. 84).

**Obligation as reflective rejection.** The identities most important to an
agent give unconditional obligations, because to violate them "is to lose
your integrity and so your identity", "to be for all practical purposes dead
or worse than dead" (p. 84). "If reasons arise from reflective endorsement,
then obligation arises from reflective rejection" (p. 85). Lecture III ends
with the formula: "Obligation is the reflective rejection of a threat to
your identity. Pain is the unreflective rejection of a threat to your
identity" (p. 106).

**To morality, and only by one route.** The argument so far "is formal, and
in one sense empty": it does not say which law an agent should be to
herself, "so moral obligation is not yet on the table" (p. 85). The step to
morality is Kant's argument for the Formula of Humanity, recast. Since a
human being "needs a practical conception of her own identity" in order to
act at all, and you are such an animal, "you must endorse your own humanity
if you are to act at all", and "Enlightenment morality is true" (pp. 92–93).
Conflicts between moral and less fundamental identities are left open:
the more fundamental laws need not "always trump" the others (p. 92, n. 16).

So the lectures argue both halves of the title. They flag the first step,
from "must have a law" to "must have an identity", by appeal to how
deliberation seems from inside, and they summarise the second from her
paper "Kant's Formula of Humanity" ([NOTE-465](../notes.d/NOTE-465.md), Limitations). "What I say
will necessarily be sketchy, and sketchily argued" (p. 78).

## What this does not say

- **It does not say every obligation is moral.** Most obligations, on the
  account, come from contingent identities (a psychiatrist's confidentiality,
  p. 84) and are as relative as those identities. Only the humanity step
  gives obligations every agent has.
- **It does not say how conflicts between identities are resolved.** Note 16
  leaves that open, and the book's reply is the next place to look.
- **It does not say identities are chosen freely or alike.** Human identity
  "has been differently constituted in different social worlds" (p. 89).
  How an identity was acquired is not discussed.
- **It does not rest on the argument about animals.** The claim that pain
  is the unreflective rejection of a threat to identity, so that animals can
  obligate us (pp. 101–106), is sketched, by its author's account, and
  this theory does not rest on it.

## Connections

- **Frankfurt's caring ([LIT-293](../literature.d/LIT-293.md), [NOTE-264](../notes.d/NOTE-264.md)).** Frankfurt's Tanner Lectures
  answer the same normative question from the will rather than from
  reflection. Love is "strictly volitional", not based on reasons, and is
  "the only legitimate authority" for our normative convictions; morality's
  authority is not the authority of reason, and moral necessity is the
  universal volitional necessities of human nature, projected ([NOTE-264](../notes.d/NOTE-264.md), C9,
  C10, C14–C15). The two accounts cannot both be right about the source:
  for Korsgaard, valuing humanity is required by the structure of reflective
  choice; for Frankfurt, nothing is important except as cared about, and
  what everyone cares about is a fact about human volition. No THEORY in the
  record states Frankfurt's account, so no `rivals` is declared. If one is
  filed, it should be. Korsgaard borrows Frankfurt's "wanton" and argues
  that a reflective person "cannot be like that": acting on the desire of
  the moment treats it as a reason, and that commitment "counts as a
  second-order volition" (p. 82, n. 4).
- **[THEORY-029](THEORY-029.md)** (the regress). The wanton argument makes every reflective
  agent's action express some law, a stopping point specified by the
  structure of reflection rather than by an attitude's order. But which
  identity counts as one's own is still how she conceives herself, and
  practical identity is chosen or constituted, so it looks like the
  dilemma's second horn. [THEORY-029](THEORY-029.md)'s Connections take this up.
- **[THEORY-065](THEORY-065.md)** (unity of agency by normative constitution) is the
  2014 form of the same view. There, practical identity is what the content
  of chosen maxims makes one; here, it is the source of reasons and
  obligations.
- **The SDT accounts ([THEORY-054](THEORY-054.md), [THEORY-047](THEORY-047.md)).** SDT's integrated regulation
  is a value "brought into congruence with one's other values and needs",
  and some values are said to be "not integrateable" ([THEORY-054](THEORY-054.md)). That is
  a descriptive counterpart of an obligation springing from what one's
  identity forbids, with no claim about normativity. SDT's claim that the
  conditions of acquisition fix how a value is held ([THEORY-047](THEORY-047.md)) addresses
  the question these lectures leave open.
