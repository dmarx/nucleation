# NOTE-tmp58s6l: Tessman (2016) — Moral Failure: Response to Critics

## Contribution

The author's summary of Moral Failure ([LIT-tmp5l3yx](../literature.d/LIT-tmp5l3yx.md)) in a symposium reply to Rivera, Schwartzman and Kittay, and an extension to "the concept of unfair duties" (p. 16), taken from Karnein (2014).

## Key results

1. p. 1: "there can be situations of unavoidable moral failure, or, put differently, that there can be impossible moral requirements"; "Nonnegotiable moral requirements remain binding even when they become impossible to fulfill."
2. p. 9: nonideal theory "inadvertently idealizes the moral agent" by counting the best available action as no failure. Against this, p. 12: "we can still judge that performing this action constitutes a failure".
3. p. 11: "Unavoidable moral failure is not the same as avoidable moral failure", and blame does not fit it in the same way.
4. p. 14, a three-party case. A coerces B into having a child, isolates and impoverishes her, and leaves her to care for C alone. "Clearly, A is wronging B in some very serious ways that go beyond simply being unfair". Yet "A’s noncompliance functions like luck for the purpose of the relationship between B and C", and B must take up A's slack. "A owes all kinds of reparations to B."
5. p. 15: B may rightly put her own needs first ("I am not saying that the correct action-guiding decision for B is necessarily to meet C’s needs"), but then she fails C unavoidably.
6. p. 15: "it is crucial to the case (as for Karnein’s cases) that the wrongdoer, A, is not the same person as the dependent, C." Where the dependent is the wrongdoer: "Part of the wrongdoer’s being accountable for the wrongdoing may mean forfeiting the care of the person whom he has wronged."
7. p. 16: "There are moral requirements that it is unfair for us to be bound by, but that nevertheless are binding on us."

## Assumptions

Constructivist anti-realism with a plural, unsystematic construction of morality; dual-process moral cognition and sacred values as evidence that some requirements are grasped intuitively; Kittay's vulnerability model of moral requirements.

## Concepts

Impossible moral requirements; unavoidable moral failure; negotiable and non-negotiable requirements; the unthinkable; action-guidance and action-assessment; unfair duties; vulnerability-responsive requirements; moral remainder.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-tmpd6uxt](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpd6uxt.md)**: corroborates it from a line independent of Keller. A third party's wrongdoing can leave an agent genuinely bound to an innocent dependent; she is wronged, and the wrongdoer owes her reparation (results 4, 7). It also qualifies the claim: when the person owed is himself the wrongdoer, the obligation may be forfeited (result 6). Keller's patient who misses his bus on purpose is such a case, and his dog and students are not.
- **[CLAIM-tmpnw7ez](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpnw7ez.md)**: corroborates it. A answers to B for the predicament he made, while B still answers for meeting or failing C's need; A's wrong is "luck" in one relationship and agency in the other.
- **[QUESTION-tmpgyn11](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpgyn11.md)**: bears on it. In result 4, A controls B's money, support and exit and so leaves her bound; the obligation persists, which is the question's moral cost of withdrawal.
- **[THEORY-118](../theory.d/THEORY-118.md)**: consistent with it. Blame does not fit unavoidable failure, though "ought implies can" is denied for requirement.
- **[CLAIM-671](../claims.d/CLAIM-671.md)**: bears on its principle. Tessman contests Kittay's view that coercion into a caring position prevents the requirement from arising, where the dependent is a third party.
- **The exchange** (A7 closing): its description of Moral Failure is accurate in substance. The A/B/C case is nearer the exchange's subject than anything A cited.

## Limitations

A short reply, not the book. The unfair-duties argument is Karnein's, which was not read, and Karnein confines it to duties that can be met.
