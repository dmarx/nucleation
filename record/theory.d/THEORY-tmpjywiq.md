# THEORY-tmpjywiq: If moral evaluation has thresholds below which effects are negligible, a system of agents can do good or evil that none of its members' interactions does, and the same thresholds make an environment tolerant of small evils and inert to small goods; on the examples offered the non-additivity lies in the evaluation, as a step function of summed impact, not in the actions

## Source

Floridi (2013), "Distributed Morality in an Information Society", [LIT-901](../literature.d/LIT-901.md),
read in full in [NOTE-698](../notes.d/NOTE-698.md).

## What was actually shown

A model, drawn in Figures 1–3, with four examples and a policy programme.

- **The phenomenon (p. 729).** Distributed morality covers "moral actions that
  are the result of otherwise morally-neutral or at least morally-negligible
  … interactions among agents constituting a multiagent system, which might
  be human, artificial, or hybrid". "Can 'big' morally-loaded actions … be
  the result of many, 'small' morally-neutral or morally-negligible
  interactions? I hold the answer to be yes."
- **Thresholds (pp. 730–731).** Left unchecked, consequentialist promotion of
  every action to good or evil leaves no neutral actions, which Floridi
  calls "too implausible to be acceptable". The fix is the morally
  negligible, modelled as two thresholds "the value of which can be left
  unspecified". The result is moral inertia: "most actions are morally
  neutral and tend to stay that way".
- **Resilience and inertia (pp. 732–733).** Possible evils that stay below
  threshold make environments "morally resilient", "fault-tolerant".
  Possible goods that stay below make them "morally inert … potential
  goodness can be too weak to become actual goodness". "It is only by
  aggregating and merging individual courses of action that a moral
  difference is made" (p. 733).
- **The examples (pp. 734–736).** The tragedy of the commons is named as
  negative distributed morality and not discussed. The positive cases are
  cause-marketing, the Co-operative Bank's charity cards (£3 million to
  Oxfam, 1994–2007), JustGiving and peer-to-peer lending.
- **The programme (p. 736).** Aggregate possible goods, fragment possible
  evils, and build "moral enablers". Their ensemble is *infraethics*, a
  first-order framework of expectations, attitudes and practices (trust,
  respect, privacy, transparency) that facilitates moral action without
  being morally good in itself (pp. 738–740).

The first claim follows from the model once thresholds are granted ([NOTE-698](../notes.d/NOTE-698.md),
C1, moderate). Nothing else could have come out otherwise, since nothing is
tested.

## What this does not say

- **That distributed morality is the moral counterpart of distributed
  knowledge.** In the epistemic case different contents (P∨Q and ¬P) combine
  logically into one neither member holds to any degree. Every moral example
  sums contributions of one kind (pounds, purchases, loans, bike rides), each
  already carrying "some moral value" (p. 733). What makes them distributed
  morality is only that evaluation is a step function of the sum. No
  aggregation operator for actions is given (fn. 2) ([NOTE-698](../notes.d/NOTE-698.md), limitations).
- **Where the line falls.** A thousand £100 donations, each above threshold,
  are not distributed morality; a million 25p contributions, each below it,
  are. Nothing but the unspecified threshold separates them.
- **That it happens without design.** The definition's "invisible hand"
  (p. 728) fits the commons. All four positive examples are aggregators
  somebody designed, and the paper says that "unless A and B interact
  properly" the outcome does not emerge (p. 733).
- **That fragmentation works.** It is named and never illustrated.
- **That enablers are morally neutral.** The abstract says so; the body says
  only that infraethics "is not necessarily morally good in itself", concedes
  that the separation "never occurs in reality" (p. 739), and lists as
  enablers things usually called values.
- **Who is responsible.** Responsibility for distributed moral outcomes,
  promised by Floridi & Sanders 2004 ([LIT-898](../literature.d/LIT-898.md)), is not given here. It is the
  subject of the 2016 paper ([LIT-899](../literature.d/LIT-899.md), [THEORY-207](THEORY-207.md)).
- **That the "supra-agent" is an agent.** No criterion of agency is applied
  to it; it is an agent by fiat at a level of abstraction that hides the
  members (fn. 1). It bears on [QUESTION-009](../questions.d/QUESTION-009.md) only by showing how freely the
  word is used.

## Connections

- [THEORY-207](THEORY-207.md) (Floridi 2016) proposes allocating responsibility for such
  outcomes in full to every causally accountable node, and takes the
  phenomenon stated here as its premise.
- Ostrom's commons institutions ([LIT-719](../literature.d/LIT-719.md), [NOTE-563](../notes.d/NOTE-563.md)) argue against Hardin's
  tragedy, which Floridi takes as his paradigm of negative distributed
  morality. In his terms her monitoring, sanctions and communication are
  infraethics and fragmentation; [THEORY-149](THEORY-149.md) states what she found.
- The distributed-knowledge example is the epistemic face of List and
  Pettit's supervenience result ([THEORY-127](THEORY-127.md)), on which the aggregation used
  can make a group depart from what every member holds. Floridi does not
  take that step.
