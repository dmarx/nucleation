# THEORY-092: Behavioural capacity does not fix intelligence: a system with the capacity to respond sensibly to every possible input can lack it, so whether behaviour is intelligent, or the mark of any mental property, depends partly on how it is produced

## Source

- Block (1981), *Psychologism and Behaviorism*, [LIT-642](../literature.d/LIT-642.md), read in
  full in [NOTE-506](../notes.d/NOTE-506.md): pp. 5–6 (the doctrine), 14–21 (dispositions,
  capacities and the machine), 25–43 (the objections), nn. 13, 17 and 21.
  Page numbers are the journal's.
- Block (1995), *The Mind as the Software of the Brain*, [LIT-630](../literature.d/LIT-630.md),
  read in full in [NOTE-490](../notes.d/NOTE-490.md): §11.1.1 (pp. 381–384), the Aunt Bubbles
  machine and its n. 1.

## What was actually shown

**The doctrine.** Block (1981), [LIT-642](../literature.d/LIT-642.md), defines psychologism as "the
doctrine that whether behavior is intelligent behavior depends on the
character of the internal information processing that produces it". Two
systems "could be exactly alike in their actual and potential behavior,
and in their behavioral dispositions and capacities and counterfactual
behavioral properties", and still differ so that "one is not at all
intelligent while the other is fully intelligent" (p. 5).

**The target is made as strong as it can be.** Block grants the
behaviourist a non-question-begging definition of "sensible", "an
enormous concession" (p. 11). He then moves from a disposition to a
capacity, the *neo-Turing Test conception*: intelligence is "the capacity
to produce a sensible sequence of verbal responses to a sequence of verbal
stimuli, whatever they may be" (p. 18). Stated with internal states in the
antecedents of its conditionals, it escapes the Chisholm–Geach, perfect
actor and paralytic objections. Those objections, he argues, only ever
told against behaviourist *necessary* conditions, so the sufficient
condition had survived them (pp. 14–19).

**The counterexample.** Programmers list every typable string of an hour's
conversation in which one party is sensible. The machine finds the strings
that begin with the conversation so far and types the next sentence; a
tree-searching variant stores one reply per branch. It has the capacity
the conception requires, "But actually, the machine has the intelligence
of a toaster. All the intelligence it exhibits is that of its
programmers" (p. 21). The length of the test is not essential: any finite
length can be met the same way. Block concludes that "the capacity to emit
sensible responses is not sufficient for intelligence", that the
neo-Turing conception is refuted "along with the older and cruder Turing
Test conceptions", and that "whether behavior is intelligent behavior is
in part a matter of how it is produced" (p. 21). Note 21 says what
disqualifies it: not that its replies are echoes, but that its processing
"is of the most elementary sort", which would hold as well for a machine
produced by cosmic accident. The argument needs only logical possibility,
because behaviourist analyses are conceptual (Objection 6, pp. 30–33).

**Not chauvinism.** Psychologism "requires only that intelligent behavior
not be the product of a (at least one) certain kind of internal
processing" (pp. 6, 21). It does not require our kind: a Martian with
rich but alien processing still counts.

**Wider than intelligence.** Block (1995), [LIT-630](../literature.d/LIT-630.md), restates the
machine as the "Aunt Bubbles machine", which "has the intelligence of a
jukebox" (p. 383). Its n. 1 draws the wider conclusion: the machine
"refutes something stronger than behaviorism, namely the claim that the
mental 'supervenes' on the behavioral; that is, that there can be no
mental difference without a behavioral difference", a doctrine Block notes
is held even by those who reject behaviourism. The chapter also names the
point the 1981 paper's capacity move turned on: the Turing test "focuses
on performance rather than on competence", and competence, idealised
performance, needs a distinction among components of the mind that no
behaviourist can draw (p. 384). The list-searcher was first sketched in
"Troubles with Functionalism" ([LIT-407](../literature.d/LIT-407.md), pp. 294–295), where it "clearly
has no mental states at all".

What could have come out otherwise: the machine could have been
incoherent as specified, or the standard objections could have reached
the sufficient condition. Block argues neither holds; the first is
answered by tree-search and logical possibility, the second case by case.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say behaviour is no evidence.** "Of course, performance is
  evidence for competence" ([LIT-630](../literature.d/LIT-630.md), p. 384). The claim is that
  behaviour does not *constitute* intelligence, so its evidential force
  rests on an assumption about how it was produced.
- **It does not say which processing intelligence requires.** "Richness"
  is named, not defined ([NOTE-506](../notes.d/NOTE-506.md), Limitations), and Block's closing
  case against simulators of physics, neurons or psychology is, in his
  word, "incomplete" (pp. 40–43). The account excludes one kind of
  processing and requires none.
- **It does not say any real system is a Blockhead.** The machine is a
  lookup over complete conversation histories, about 10^1500 strings by
  Miller's estimate (n. 24). Whether the lesson transfers to systems that
  compress or generalise from stored text is not addressed, and Block's
  reply to Objection 1 counts learning machines' intelligence as their
  own.
- **"Any mental property" rests on one note.** The 1981 argument is about
  intelligence in the sense of thought or reason (p. 8). The extension to
  the mental generally is Block's in [LIT-630](../literature.d/LIT-630.md), n. 1, and in [LIT-407](../literature.d/LIT-407.md);
  for experience in particular Block's grounds are different, and the
  chapter's doubt that the Chinese system is conscious rests on
  implementation, not on behaviour.

## Turing, and why no `corrects`

Turing ([LIT-394](../literature.d/LIT-394.md)) replaced "Can machines think?" with the imitation game,
and Block (n. 13) quotes him settling for passing it as a *sufficient*
condition. This account denies exactly that condition, in its strongest
capacity form, which is why [LIT-642](../literature.d/LIT-642.md) declares `corrects` on [LIT-394](../literature.d/LIT-394.md).
But [LIT-394](../literature.d/LIT-394.md) is a work, and no theory in the record holds behaviourism
about intelligence or the Turing-test criterion. So there is nothing for
this account to correct, and no relation is declared. If the record files
a behavioural criterion as a theory, this account corrects it.

## How it bears on the record

**The mimicry clause of [THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md).** [THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md) holds that mimicry
undercuts behavioural evidence that an AI system is conscious. That
clause presupposes something this account states: if behaviour, actual
and counterfactual, fixed the mental property, a mimic's behaviour could
not be undercut, since there would be nothing further for it to lack.
Psychologism is the conceptual premise that lets "how was this behaviour
produced?" matter at all. It also fits the clause's strength.
Schwitzgebel's mimicry argument *undercuts* rather than *rebuts*, and
Block's account says the same of behaviour: it remains evidence, defeated
when its etiology is of the wrong kind. Three limits keep the bearing
indirect. Block's argument is about intelligence; consciousness enters
only through the supervenience note. His machine is built to reproduce
stored responses, where [THEORY-023](THEORY-023.md) is about systems trained on human
output, a different etiology. And no relation is declared, since this
account neither builds on nor competes with that one.

**[NOTE-052](../notes.d/NOTE-052.md).** Butlin et al.'s reading records their reason for
discounting behavioural tests: systems can be trained to mimic human
reports while working differently (C2 there, an informal argument with
LLMs as the existence case). Block's machine is the classic argument for
that premise in its strongest form, for intelligence and with a lookup
table, and their turn to architecture, judging a system by what processes
it implements, is psychologism applied to consciousness.

**The flag.** `anthology-candidate` is carried from [LIT-642](../literature.d/LIT-642.md): the claim
that no behavioural test of bounded length certifies intelligence without
a claim about how the responses are produced is a claim about what a
measurement cannot tell you. The reading here is about the nature of
intelligence.
