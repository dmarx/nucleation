# NOTE-tmp7fqen: The stance triangle

## Contribution

A single model for what other traditions had split into separate kinds of
stance: evaluation (assessment, appraisal), affective and epistemic
positioning, and agreement or alignment. Du Bois argues that these are not
types of stance but three aspects of every stance act, and gives the act a
fixed structure of two subjects, one shared object and three directed
relations among them. The structure is offered as both an analytic tool
and a resource participants themselves use to infer what is left unsaid.
He adds two methods for showing it in data: the diagraph from his dialogic
syntax, and a "stance diagraph" that labels subjects, object and actions.

## Key insight

No stance stands alone. "I'm glad", "I know" and "I agree" are complete
sentences and incomplete stances: each needs an object (glad about what,
know what) and a prior stance it answers, and both are usually supplied by
someone else's earlier utterance. Because two people can orient to the
same object, evaluating it also places each relative to the other, so
alignment is not an extra act but a consequence of evaluating something
someone else has evaluated. Hence the gloss: "I evaluate something, and
thereby position myself, and thereby align with you."

## Assumptions

- **Data**: short excerpts of American English conversation, mostly from
  the Santa Barbara Corpus of Spoken American English and a few from the
  Longman Spoken American Corpus, both built under the author's direction;
  paraphrases of the emergent stance are his.
- **Stance subject ≈ speaking subject** in nearly all examples; note 13
  sets aside multivocality (Bakhtin; Goffman 1981, the book filed as
  [LIT-tmpsg9d2](../literature.d/LIT-tmpsg9d2.md)) for separate treatment.
- **A shared stance object** is assumed for the full triangle. Cases
  without one are acknowledged as a challenge; the reply (Section 6, note
  29) is that, following Tomasello, all meaningful language use
  presupposes shared orientation, a claim deferred to future work.
- **Alignment is scalar**, convergent or divergent by degrees, not a binary
  aligned/disaligned.
- **The too/either result** depends on conditions only partly stated: a
  stance follow juxtaposed with a resonant stance lead, with subjective
  intentional predicates such as like, love, know, think and want (note
  26), and it rests on a separate study (Du Bois 2004) not reported here.

## Key results

- **Three questions for interpreting any stance** (Section 3): who is the
  stancetaker, what is the object of stance, and what stance is the
  stancetaker responding to. Attested utterances show each answer is
  routinely found outside the stance utterance: "it was really great"
  (a weekend) and "That stuff is great" (hibiscus cooler) are different
  stances; "I would love to go" is about Nicaragua, 40 lines earlier;
  Melissa's "I totally agree" answers her mother's directive 29 lines
  back.
- **Subjectivity takes an object** (Section 4). "I'm glad" and "I'm so
  glad" differ less by *so* than by what each is glad about (no
  significant problems to talk about; you guys having fun), which the
  prior turns supply. "I know" and "I don't know" likewise take their
  objects from the partner's prior utterance.
- **Intersubjective marking** (Sections 5–6). In "I don't like those" /
  "I don't either" and "I don't know if she'd do it" / "I don't know if
  she would either", the second speaker's *either* cannot be dropped
  without pragmatic anomaly, and the first speaker cannot use it. Stance
  lead and stance follow are named after Tomasello's gaze following.
  Generalised: under the stated conditions a resonant stance follow must
  be marked, *too* for positive and *either* for negative utterances.
- **The stance differential** (Section 6). In Ken "I would love to go" →
  Lenore "Yeah." → Joanne "Yeah?" → "I want to go too", Joanne's rising
  *Yeah?* and her *want* for *love* register a weaker alignment inside a
  formally parallel follow.
- **The definition** (example 49): "Stance is a public act by a social
  actor, achieved dialogically through overt communicative means, of
  simultaneously evaluating objects, positioning subjects (self and
  others), and aligning with other subjects, with respect to any salient
  dimension of the sociocultural field."
- **The triangle** (Figure 1). Nodes: Subject 1, Subject 2, Object. Two
  sides are evaluative vectors from each subject to the shared object; the
  third side is alignment between the subjects, in either direction;
  positioning is a reflexive vector shown only by its arrowhead on each
  subject ("blowback" from evaluating, note 22). Six arrowheads in all:
  three acts, doubled for two subjects. Any unspecified part can be
  inferred from the rest: agreeing implies sharing the prior evaluation,
  and repeating the evaluation implies convergent alignment.
- **Stance in one line** (Section 8): "an act of evaluation owned by a
  social actor", tying act, responsibility and value; proposed, if it
  binds the minimum structures of social action, as "perhaps, the smallest
  unit of social action".

## Method

Two notations from dialogic syntax (Du Bois 2001). The **diagraph** sets
two or more utterances in rows (intonation units, otherwise clauses) and
aligns resonating elements in columns, with speaker labels and line
numbers back to the transcript. The **stance diagraph** adds column
headings for speaker, stance subject, the positioning/evaluating
predicate, stance object, and alignment marker, with recovered material
in braces ({to Nicaragua}). The analysis maps a stance diagraph onto the
triangle.

## Concepts

- **stance**: the definition at (49) above.
- **evaluation**: the stancetaker orients to an object of stance and
  characterizes it as having some quality or value.
- **positioning**: situating a social actor with respect to responsibility
  for stance and for invoking sociocultural value; affective (glad) or
  epistemic (know).
- **alignment**: calibrating the relationship between two stances, and so
  between two stancetakers; scalar, convergent or divergent.
- **stance object / shared stance object**: what is evaluated; when
  several speakers orient to it across turns, the cornerstone of
  intersubjectivity.
- **stance lead / stance follow**: the first stance and a resonant second
  one built on it.
- **stance differential**: the differences within a formally parallel
  follow (intonation, word choice) that calibrate alignment.
- **other-positioning** (note 16): proposing a candidate stance for the
  addressee, as ordinary questions do.
- **intersubjectivity**: the relation between one actor's subjectivity
  and another's, realized here through orientation to a shared object.

## Connections

The chapter draws on dialogism (Bakhtin, Voloshinov, Linell), conversation
analysis on assessment and agreement (Pomerantz, the Goodwins, Heritage and
Raymond), linguistic anthropology on indexicality and responsibility
(Silverstein, Hanks, Hill and Irvine), work on subjectivity in language
(Benveniste, Langacker, Traugott), and Tomasello's joint attention. It
names Goffman's *Forms of Talk* ([LIT-tmpsg9d2](../literature.d/LIT-tmpsg9d2.md)) once, in note 13, for the
multivocality it sets aside: Goffman's decomposition of the speaker is
where the chapter's simplification "stance subject ≈ speaking subject"
would be tested. It closes on Wittgenstein: "There are no private
stances."

In this record, the shared object that binds two subjects is close in
structure to the joint attention and shared-intention accounts filed among
the social-ontology works (Tomasello, [LIT-504](../literature.d/LIT-504.md); Gilbert's "Walking
Together", [LIT-477](../literature.d/LIT-477.md)), both Deferred and unread; the connection is mine.

## Bearing on the record

- **No THEORY filed.** The triangle is mainly a definition and an analytic
  frame, and its empirical claim (obligatory *too*/*either* on a stance
  follow) rests on a study not reported here. A candidate, not filed: "In
  English conversation a stance that resonates with a prior stance on the
  same object with a subjective intentional predicate must mark the
  relation with *too* or *either*, and the prior stance cannot." Source
  this chapter; promote when Du Bois (2004) or another corpus count is
  read, with its conditions and exceptions.
- **[LIT-tmpsg9d2](../literature.d/LIT-tmpsg9d2.md) (Goffman).** The chapter defers to Goffman on who the
  speaking subject is; the two filings bear on each other through the
  stance subject, and a reading of "Footing" would show whether the
  triangle survives a speaker split into animator, author and principal.
- **The LIT as filed** described the triangle correctly in outline. Two
  refinements: the triangle has two evaluative sides and one alignment
  side, with positioning reflexive at each subject, not one act per side;
  and alignment is scalar, covering divergence, not just alignment versus
  disalignment.
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- **Qualitative and illustrative.** No counts, rates or systematic
  sampling; the excerpts are chosen, and the paraphrases of emergent
  stances are the author's, which note 9 itself calls too explicit.
- **One language and register**: American English conversation.
- **Speaker = stance subject** is assumed; reported speech, animated
  voices and institutional speaking are left out (note 13).
- **The shared-object requirement** is not shown to hold generally; the
  defence is deferred.
- **The too/either generalization** is stated with conditions the chapter
  does not give in full, and *too*/*either* are not always intersubjective
  (note 27).
- **A slip in the text**: discussing example (48) the chapter says "Alice
  responds to Mary's I don't know if she'd do it", although in (47) Alice
  speaks first and Mary second; the analysis that follows treats Alice as
  the lead, so the slip does not change the argument.

## Open questions

- Can evaluation, positioning or alignment occur without the others? A
  case of alignment with no evaluated object, or evaluation that commits
  the speaker to no position, would break the "one act" claim; the
  chapter argues for co-occurrence without searching for such cases.
- How does the triangle treat a speaker who animates someone else's
  stance? Goffman's footing is the obvious test.
- How do stances invoke and reproduce systems of sociocultural value
  (note 28)? Named as the main open topic.
- Do the too/either conditions hold in other languages and in written or
  institutional discourse?
