# LIT-tmpne8q4: Précis of Breakdown of Will

George Ainslie (2005), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 28(5):635–650 (target
article; pp. 650–673 are the commentaries and the author's response) —
DOI-10.1017/S0140525X05000117. A précis of *Breakdown of Will* (Cambridge
University Press, 2001), filed here as [LIT-tmpmhu3j](LIT-tmpmhu3j.md).

## Key takeaways

- **The discount curve is hyperbolic, and that alone produces temporary
  preference.** Value falls roughly in proportion to delay (Mazur's
  formula, with one free "impatience" parameter), in animals and in people,
  with real and hypothetical rewards. A smaller, sooner reward is preferred
  in the period just before it is due and dispreferred from a distance.
- **A person is a population of interests, not a unitary maximizer.** An
  interest is the set of processes selected by one kind of reward over one
  time range: a diet interest and an ice cream interest, not a chocolate and
  a vanilla one. Each survives by forestalling the others, and "she" is "just
  the resultant of their activities". The précis calls this the "bottom up
  model" and sets it against a governing ego.
- **Will is intertemporal bargaining.** Treating a current choice as a test
  case that predicts future choices "bundles" the rewards of the whole
  series, and bundled hyperbolic curves favour the larger, later reward. A
  lapse lowers the expectation that the rule will hold, so "personal rules
  are a recursive mechanism; they continually take their own pulse". The
  précis cites bundling experiments in students (Kirby & Guastello 2001) and
  rats (Ainslie & Monterosso 2003).
- **The will has predictable side effects.** Rules make choices legalistic.
  Lapses get fenced into "lapse districts", which clinicians call symptoms.
  Both long-range and short-range interests are motivated not to notice a
  lapse. Rules favour well-marked criteria, which the précis offers as a
  mechanism for disorders of over-control such as obsessive-compulsive
  personality disorder.
- **Emotions and pain are reward-dependent, not conditioned.** A very fast
  cycle of brief reward followed by longer non-reward would make a process
  that attracts attention and is avoided at a distance. Positive emotions
  are limited by "premature satiation": they stay intense only when their
  occasions are surprising, which the précis uses to explain belief, the
  value of other people, and why some goals must be pursued indirectly.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, for the record's coverage of
behavioural control and self-control. The owner named the précis as the
reading to stand in for the 2001 book. The book is registered beside it as
a Deferred seed, [LIT-tmpmhu3j](LIT-tmpmhu3j.md), because it is the citation others reach for.

What it adds that the record lacked is a mechanism for self-control that is
neither a higher faculty nor a single preference. It bears on four things
already held:

- **Frijda's account of impulse control ([LIT-540](LIT-540.md)) and [THEORY-056](../theory.d/THEORY-056.md).** Frijda
  holds that most emotion regulation is one motive state checking another,
  with no separate regulator. Ainslie reaches a similar architecture from
  the economics of choice. Successive interests forestall each other, and
  "stable choice has to be achieved as it is in the kind of markets that
  don't have governors". He adds what Frijda leaves open, a rule for which
  state wins: the one with the greatest discounted reward at that moment,
  with bundling added by self-prediction. See [NOTE-tmp0kj10](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp0kj10.md).
- **The hierarchical accounts of agency in [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md).** The précis is not
  about whose a motive is, so it does not answer Velleman's dilemma. It
  does state, against "organ" models of will, that a governing strength
  "has to be guided by some evaluation process outside of motivation" and
  asks "What keeps this strength from being co-opted by the bad option?".
  That is the same regress, posed about force rather than ownership.
- **Self-determination theory ([LIT-558](LIT-558.md), [LIT-560](LIT-560.md)).** Ainslie's account of
  rule-bound, legalistic willpower that loses "the here-and-now" resembles
  SDT's controlled and introjected regulation, and his "indirection puzzle"
  (trying to have fun spoils the fun) parallels the undermining of
  intrinsic motivation by control. The mechanisms differ. SDT locates the
  cost in the experience of being controlled. Ainslie locates it in the
  efficiency that bright-line rules force on appetite.
- **Bratman's planning agency.** The précis treats Bratman's "resolute
  choice" as a rival model of will and suggests it "may turn out to be
  another name for intertemporal bargaining". The Bratman text the précis
  uses is *Faces of Intention* (1999, pp. 35–57: the pianist and the
  toxin). That book is not in the record. Bratman's planning theory of
  self-governance is being filed in this batch by another reader, and the
  link between the two should be made once those entries are complete.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing here belongs in the
