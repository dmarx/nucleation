# SECTION-007: Persistence, product and producer

## Manuscript text

Organizational identity across time complicates the picture. A pattern can continue while all of its material constituents change, but the persistence of the pattern is not automatically the persistence of the organization producing it. The r/place case makes this clear: an image can remain recognizable through continuous editing and repair, even where some participants oppose it and others replace one another. The visual product, its temporary coalition of maintainers, and the practices transmitted among contributors are three candidates for continuity, not automatically one individual. <!-- ref::asserts: CLAIM-280, CLAIM-tmpx8nob --> <!-- ref::discusses: CASE-079 -->

Several forms of persistence should be distinguished. State continuity concerns the retention of an observable feature. Dynamical continuity concerns continued generation of characteristic responses. Historical continuity concerns the causal transmission or reconstitution of productive organization. Viability concerns access to future trajectories compatible with specified conditions; reinstatability concerns the capacity to recover an organization after interruption. These can diverge.

A historical lineage can branch. Legal continuity, civic identification, institutional practice, and narrative succession may pass to different successors, without numerical identity itself branching. Conversely, an independently arising organization can resemble an earlier one without being its continuation. Thus neither resemblance nor causal descent alone settles every identity question; the relevant organizational relations must be specified. <!-- ref::discusses: CLAIM-329 -->

A further distinction concerns restoration. The environmental conditions sufficient for an organization's *continuation* need not suffice for its *reconstitution* after it has lost its productive machinery. In systems with bistability or hysteresis, returning a parameter to an earlier favorable value can leave the system trapped in a low-capacity regime. The past makes a difference because it determines which mechanisms remain available for recovery.

These considerations do not yield a universal numerical-identity criterion. They explain why synchronic equivalence cannot be assumed to settle diachronic continuity. A complete account of organizational identity must distinguish the history of a product from the history of its producer and articulate which continuity matters for the kind of entity in question. <!-- ref::asserts: CLAIM-280 -->
