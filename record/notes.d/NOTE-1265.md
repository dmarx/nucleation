# NOTE-xxxx: A critical view on transgenerational epigenetic inheritance in humans

## Contribution

A short sceptical statement by a human geneticist, aimed at human
studies. Its distinctive contributions are two. It names the commonest
false positive in families, the secondary epimutation. And it gives a
three-step roadmap for proving germline epigenetic inheritance, then
concedes that in humans the first and last steps cannot be completed.

## Key insight

Offspring inherit from their parents genes, an environment and a culture,
and the environment and culture were partly built by earlier generations
(Fig. 1a). Shared epigenetic marks between parent and child are therefore
expected without any germline transmission: "it is extremely difficult to
divide out whether these features have been transmitted through the
germline or established anew in each generation by the action of shared
genes and shared environments" (Secondary epimutations). Of the three
rivals, genes can be excluded in humans; environment and culture cannot.

## Assumptions

- **Epigenetics is cellular inheritance** of chromatin and expression
  states, by feedback loops, chromatin marks and long-lived non-coding
  RNA. Transmission through the germline is an extension that must be
  proved, not a part of the definition.
- **The Weismann barrier** separates mammalian germ cells from somatic
  ones, so an acquired somatic mark would have to appear in germ cells as
  well.
- **Proof means a causal factor in germ cells**, not a correlation between
  an ancestor's exposure and a descendant's outcome.

## Key results

- **The barriers** (Defining transgenerational epigenetic inheritance).
  The Weismann barrier, and the reshaping of chromatin in germ-cell
  differentiation and again after fertilization. Some loci escape
  germline reprogramming. Long-lived RNA is "less affected by these
  barriers" and so a likelier carrier, though its mechanisms are "largely
  unsolved".
- **Rodent evidence** (Evidence). Reports in mice and rats over ten years
  involve endocrine disruptors, high-fat diet, obesity, diabetes,
  undernourishment and trauma. Two are described:
  - prediabetes in male mice altered sperm methylation and the islets of
    F1 and F2 offspring (Wei et al.);
  - early-life trauma in male mice altered offspring behaviour and
    metabolism, and injecting their sperm RNA into normal fertilized eggs
    reproduced the changes (Gapp et al.).
  Most such studies are "technically sound" but "still await independent
  confirmation", and the endocrine-disruptor and high-fat-diet methylome
  results have been challenged.
- **Human evidence** (Evidence).
  - Epidemiological studies link grandparental food supply to
    grandchildren's health. The one cited is Kaati, Bygren, Pembrey and
    Sjöström 2007. The text does not name Överkalix; that the paper uses
    the Överkalix records is known from outside this Comment.
  - An indirect study suggests sporadic Prader–Willi imprinting defects
    come from a grandmaternal imprint passed through the male germline.
  - Both rest on unique cohorts and await replication.
- **The Dutch Hunger Winter** (Fetal programming and intergenerational
  inheritance). Undernourishment reached pregnant women, their fetuses and
  the fetuses' germ cells. The F1 adults' cardiovascular and metabolic
  disease "is not due to the transmission of epigenetic information
  through the maternal germline, but a direct consequence of the exposure
  in utero": fetal programming, or, if F2 is affected, "intergenerational
  inheritance".
- **Secondary epimutations** (Secondary epimutations; Fig. 1b). In several
  families an abnormal promoter methylation co-segregates with a rare
  disease over generations. In each, a mutation in the neighbouring gene
  removed a transcription termination signal, and read-through
  transcription methylated and silenced the gene. If the neighbouring
  gene is expressed in the germline, the epimutation is in germ cells
  too. That is genetic inheritance. A true case needs a primary
  epimutation, independent of sequence, in germ cells.
- **The roadmap** (Roadmap).
  1. *Rule out genetic, ecological and cultural inheritance.* In animals:
     inbred strains, controlled environments; F3 and later after a
     pregnant female is exposed, F2 after a male; better still IVF,
     embryo transfer and foster mothers. In humans "it is impossible to
     rule out ecological and cultural inheritance ... but genetic effects
     should and can be excluded": suspect a Mendelian pattern, check the
     haplotype, sequence the whole genome. Even then "you still cannot be
     100% sure that a genetic variant does not exist".
  2. *Identify the factor in germ cells*, purified (swim-up, micromanipulation)
     and tested for somatic contamination. Female germ cells are "scarse
     or unavailable".
  3. *Show the factor causes the effect*: remove it and lose the effect,
     add it to control germ cells and gain it. Done for sperm RNA in mice;
     DNA methylation and histone marks cannot easily be manipulated; and
     "all of these experiments can hardly be done in humans". This "might
     currently be too much to ask for".
- **Evolution** (In the light of evolution). Almost all mouse models and
  human observations concern deleterious traits; the one exception cited
  is hepatic wound healing. Reprogramming may serve to block the
  transmission of a parent's environmental insults. For long-lived humans
  a prediction of the offspring's environment is likely to be wrong.
- **Conclusion.** Even if mechanisms exist, cultural transmission "by
  communication, imitation, teaching and learning surpasses the effects of
  epigenetic inheritance and our ability to detect this phenomenon". "For
  now, I remain skeptical."
- **Not discussed.** Holocaust offspring, FKBP5 and the Dias and Ressler
  odour study are not mentioned. Trauma appears only in the rodent list
  and in Gapp et al.

## Concepts

- **intergenerational inheritance**: here, an effect reaching F2 through
  fetal germ cells exposed in utero; distinct from fetal programming of F1.
- **transgenerational epigenetic inheritance**: transmission of
  epigenetic information through the germline to unexposed generations.
- **primary epimutation**: an abnormal epigenetic state independent of any
  DNA sequence change.
- **secondary epimutation**: an abnormal epigenetic state caused by a
  sequence change, here read-through transcription from a mutated
  neighbouring gene; it is re-created each generation and so passes as
  genetic inheritance.
- **Weismann barrier**: the separation of germ cells from soma.
- **niche construction**: the author's suggestion of how culture, if it
  affects the epigenome at all, would do so indirectly.

## Connections

- **Heard and Martienssen ([LIT-1745](../literature.d/LIT-1745.md))** is cited for the rodent
  literature; the F2/F3 rule is theirs. Bird ([LIT-1708](../literature.d/LIT-1708.md)) cites this
  Comment as "a recent critical review". Horsthemke and Bird later
  co-authored a paper on mutation-induced CpG island methylation (2023,
  in Bird's references), so the sceptics are not independent voices.
- **The Överkalix study** (Pembrey et al., [LIT-1764](../literature.d/LIT-1764.md)) is in the
  record; the Comment cites its companion paper (Kaati et al. 2007),
  which the record does not hold.
- **The womb** (Heijmans et al., [LIT-1751](../literature.d/LIT-1751.md)). The record already reads
  the Dutch Hunger Winter as intergenerational; this Comment agrees and
  says why.
- **Lehrner and Yehuda ([LIT-1793](../literature.d/LIT-1793.md))**, who join group narrative to the
  biology, meet Fig. 1a's statement that "at present there is no evidence
  for a direct effect of culture on the epigenome".
- **Inherited state in bacteria** ([THEORY-179](../theory.d/THEORY-179.md)). A single-celled organism
  has no Weismann barrier; whatever state a mother cell holds, its
  daughters receive. The barrier is what makes the mammalian case hard.

## Bearing on the record

- **The question ([QUESTION-297](../questions.d/QUESTION-297.md)).** The Comment does two things for
  it.
  - It answers the question's "What would count as an answer" on routes,
    in the negative for humans. The question asks for a human study that
    separates germline from uterine, parenting and cultural routes, with
    fathers' children raised apart or adoptees. Horsthemke's roadmap says
    culture and ecology cannot be excluded in humans at all, and his
    causal step cannot be done in humans. Adoption and paternal-only
    designs would weaken the confounds, not remove them. The question's
    "or evidence that no such separation is achievable" is the answer he
    gives, for now.
  - It turns the three human claims into specific failures: Överkalix
    (unique cohort, unreplicated); the Dutch Hunger Winter (fetal
    programming, so not a germline case); familial epimutations (genetic).
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).** Indirect, and an inference. The
  author's last word is that culture, transmitted by communication,
  imitation, teaching and learning, very likely outweighs epigenetic
  inheritance. That places the bulk of what passes between human
  generations on the social side, where the identity route lies. But his
  "culture" is learned behaviour, not identification with ancestors; the
  Comment gives no support to the identity route over the parenting
  route. No note on the claim is proposed.

## Limitations

- **A Comment, not a review.** Two rodent studies and two human ones are
  described; the rest are referenced in bulk.
- **The cultural conclusion is unsupported here.** No evidence is offered
  that cultural transmission is larger; it is the author's opinion,
  labelled as such.
- **The deleterious-bias argument** is admitted to be open to reporting
  bias.
- **Gapp et al.'s sperm-RNA result** is described without criticism, and
  it is the closest the Comment comes to a trauma mechanism; it is not
  weighed against the roadmap.
- **Typos.** "scarse" for "scarce" (Roadmap); a stray space in "germ
  cells ," (Secondary epimutations).

## Open questions

- If culture and ecology cannot be excluded in humans, what result could
  move the human question at all? Perhaps only a mechanism shown in
  animals and then found, molecularly, in human germ cells.
- Are any trauma-related human methylation findings, such as FKBP5 in
  Holocaust offspring ([LIT-1692](../literature.d/LIT-1692.md)), secondary to genotype? Step 1's
  haplotype test is the one step that can be done in humans.
- How much of what descendants carry is the "culture" this Comment
  means, learned habits and teaching, and how much is identification
  with the ancestors? The Comment does not separate the two.
