# NOTE-570: Shaping the Institutional Mind

## Contribution

It moves the question about institutional minds from metaphysics to the
function of the practice. Instead of asking whether institutions have
beliefs, it asks why ascribing them works, and answers with the
mind-shaping literature: ascription regulates.

## Key insight

The explanatory success of treating an institution as an agent need not
show that it was one beforehand. Attribution can be what makes it behave
as one: a court that says a bank "knew" what its tellers jointly knew
gives banks a reason to build the machinery that would make the
attribution true next time.

## Assumptions

- The regulative view of folk psychology (McGeer, Zawidzki, Andrews),
  assumed, not argued: "I do not have space to argue for the Regulatory
  View of Folk Psychology here" (p. 5).
- "Institution" and "organization" are used interchangeably for "highly
  structured groups with decision-making mechanisms" whose identity
  survives changes of membership (n. 1).
- Narrative is "a special form of folk psychology" (p. 8).

## Key results

- Three opening cases of institutional attribution: the Brown and
  Williamson tobacco documents, the 9/11 Commission's "The FBI lacked the
  ability to know what it knew", and California's Proposition 65 warning
  (pp. 1–2).
- A diagnosis: fictionalists, identity theorists (Quinton's summativism)
  and functionalists (List and Pettit) all treat ascription as tracking
  causal antecedents (pp. 2–4).
- The collective knowledge doctrine replacing respondeat superior, and
  its effect on banks (pp. 6–7).
- Narratives and counter-narratives as regulators, with Philip Morris's
  "Working to make a difference" programme (pp. 7–8).
- A restatement of *Groups as Agents*: attitudes are "dispositional states
  of whole systems", ascribed under norms of rationality (p. 8).
- Agnosticism about institutional minds (p. 9).

## Concepts

- **standard view** — ascription as quasi-scientific tracking of
  pre-existing, internal causes of behaviour (McGeer; p. 2).
- **regulative view / mind shaping** — ascription as an interpersonal,
  norm-governed practice that constructs as well as detects the states it
  ascribes (p. 4).
- **intentional spiral** — Andrews's name for the feedback of successful
  and failed predictions on future conduct (p. 5).
- **collective knowledge doctrine** — corporate criminal law's attribution
  to a corporation of what its employees know collectively, including what
  that knowledge entails (pp. 6–7).
- **respondeat superior** — "let the master answer": the employer is
  attributed an employee's acts and knowledge (p. 6).

## Bearing on the record

Source of [THEORY-137](../theory.d/THEORY-137.md).

- **[THEORY-139](../theory.d/THEORY-139.md) (Pettit).** Pettit makes a corporation's attitudes its
  own through authorised avowal and commitment; Tollefsen's narrative is a
  public self-ascription that "sets up expectations" in the same way. The
  difference is the claim made: Pettit's is that the corporation thereby
  has a mind and is fit to be held responsible; Tollefsen's is only that
  the self-ascription regulates, whatever the metaphysics. They are
  compatible, and no relation is declared.
- **[THEORY-127](../theory.d/THEORY-127.md) (List and Pettit).** Tollefsen does not dispute their
  result; she classes their functionalism under the standard view, whose
  aim she says is detection. The bank case is a group judgment, "the bank
  was said to know that p", that no member held, fixed from outside by a
  court rather than by the group's own procedure.

For the owner's essay:

- **§8.4, externalized governance.** The bank case is a court supplying
  the norm, and the institution building internal compliance and training
  to meet it. Tollefsen's account says the external attribution came
  first and the internal capacity followed.
- **§7.1, corporate endorsement.** A corporate narrative, like a first
  person's avowed intention, "has the status of a commitment rather than a
  report" (p. 5, said of the individual case). That gives endorsement a
  regulative reading without a higher-order corporate attitude.
- **§5.2, declared vs effective commitments (the record's link).** Philip
  Morris's narrative of "a responsible corporation" alongside its product
  is a declared commitment whose effective counterpart was contested by
  counter-narratives (pp. 7–8). Tollefsen does not use the essay's terms.

## Limitations

The regulative view is assumed. The claim that the ruling caused banks'
compliance systems is asserted without data or citation. The court
sentence quoted for the Bank of New England case carries reference [25],
*United States v. T.I.M.E.-D.C., Inc.* (W.D. Va. 1974), not [24], the Bank
of New England decision; the record has not checked which court wrote it.
Moral responsibility, the subject of the book's chapter 6, is not treated.

## Open questions

How to tell shaping from detection in a given case: what an institution
would look like if an attribution had described it rather than shaped it.
What "take control of the intentional spiral" (p. 9) would require in
practice.
