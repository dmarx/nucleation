# NOTE-tmpth9wo: Nida — Principles of Correspondence

## Contribution

The chapter that named formal and dynamic equivalence, in the abridgement
most readers meet. It sets out the two orientations, lists the
characteristics of each, and gathers centuries of translators' statements
of "similar response" as the agreed standard. It moves the measure of a
translation from the text toward the receptor's response, without
dropping the source's meaning.

## Key insight

For Nida, equivalence is a relation between relations: the receptor's
relationship to the translated message against the original receptors'
relationship to the original. The two texts can differ in proposition, as
the kiss and the handshake do, and still be equivalent in that sense.

## Assumptions

- **No two languages are identical**, in meanings or arrangements, so
  there is no absolute correspondence and no fully exact translation (p.
  126).
- **Translation involves interpretation**; "a translation remains perhaps
  the most direct form of commentary" (Rossetti, quoted p. 126).
- **The original receptors' response can be estimated**, though Nida
  grants "one is not always sure how the original audience responded or
  were supposed to respond" (p. 139).
- **Cultural distance matters more than linguistic distance**:
  "differences between cultures cause many more severe complications for
  the translator than do differences in language structure" (p. 130).

## Key results

- **Three factors (pp. 127–129).** Translations differ by (1) the nature
  of the message, whose content or form may dominate, (2) the purposes of
  the author and "by proxy" the translator: information, suggested
  behaviour, an imperative purpose; and (3) the audience, which differs in
  decoding ability (children, new literates, the average literate adult,
  specialists) and in interest. The translator's purposes are "the primary
  ones to be considered in studying the types of translation which
  result" (p. 127).
- **Two orientations (pp. 129–130).** "There are fundamentally two
  different types of equivalence: one which may be called formal and
  another which is primarily dynamic." Formal equivalence "focuses
  attention on the message itself, in both form and content", and its
  extreme is the "gloss translation". In one, "a phrase such as 'holy
  kiss' (Romans 16:16) ... would be rendered literally, and would probably
  be supplemented with a footnote explaining that this was a customary
  method of greeting in New Testament times" (p. 129). Dynamic equivalence
  "is based upon 'the principle of equivalent effect' (Rieu and Phillips
  1954). In such a translation one is not so concerned with matching the
  receptor-language message with the source-language message, but with the
  dynamic relationship, that the relationship between receptor and message
  should be substantially the same as that which existed between the
  original receptors and the message" (p. 129). It "tries to relate the
  receptor to modes of behavior relevant within the context of his own
  culture". J. B. Phillips's New Testament, "perhaps more than any other,
  seeks for equivalent effect. In Romans 16:16 he quite naturally
  translates 'greet one another with a holy kiss' as 'give one another a
  hearty handshake all around'" (pp. 129–130). Between the poles lie
  "intervening grades", and the trend of the past fifty years is toward the
  dynamic (p. 130).
- **Four requirements (pp. 131–134).** From a survey of definitions
  (Procházka, Forster, Campbell, Goodspeed, Phillips, Arnold, Jowett,
  Souter, Knox), a translation should (1) make sense, (2) convey the
  spirit and manner of the original, (3) have a natural and easy form of
  expression, and (4) produce a similar response. Where they conflict,
  "correspondence in meaning must have priority over correspondence in
  style", though not "in a purely mechanical fashion" (p. 134).
  "Definitions or descriptions of translating are not served by
  deterministic rules; rather, they depend on probabilistic rules", and
  "there will always be a variety of valid answers to the question, 'Is
  this a good translation?'" (p. 134).
- **Formal equivalence in detail (pp. 134–135).** An F-E translation is
  "basically source-oriented". It reproduces grammatical units, keeps
  consistency in word usage (concordance) and gives meanings in terms of the
  source context, with notes for what cannot be reproduced. Such
  translations are "often perfectly valid translations of certain types of
  messages for certain types of audiences".
- **Dynamic equivalence in detail (pp. 136–140).** In a D-E translation "the
  focus of attention is directed, not so much toward the source message, as
  toward the receptor response". A bilingual can say of it, "That is just
  the way we would say it". But "a D-E translation is not merely another
  message which is more or less similar to that of the source. It is a
  translation, and as such must clearly reflect the meaning and intent of
  the source" (p. 136). It is "the closest natural equivalent to the
  source-language message": *equivalent* points to the source, *natural*
  to the receptor language, *closest* binds them (p. 136). Naturalness
  must fit (1) the receptor language and culture, (2) the context of the
  message, and (3) the receptor audience (p. 136). It requires that
  "sarcasm, irony, or whimsical interest must all be accurately reflected",
  and that "each participant ... be accurately represented", so that
  "social class or geographical dialect will be immediately evident" (p.
  139). Cultural specialties (Pharisees, Lamb of God) cannot be
  naturalized and keep "foreign associations" (p. 137). Some discrepancies,
  like Jivaro hair or West African branches, are handled with footnotes
  (p. 137). Formal adjustments are unavoidable: "one cannot have his
  formal cake and eat it dynamically too" (p. 139).

## Concepts

- **Formal equivalence (F-E)**: source-oriented correspondence of form
  and content: grammatical units, concordance, source-context meanings (pp.
  129, 134).
- **Gloss translation**: the fullest formal equivalence, with notes, which
  lets the reader identify with "a person in the source-language context"
  (p. 129).
- **Dynamic equivalence (D-E)**: receptor-oriented correspondence of the
  receptor–message relationship, "the closest natural equivalent to the
  source-language message" (pp. 129, 136).
- **Principle of equivalent effect / similar response**: the translation
  should affect its receptors as the original affected its first receptors
  (pp. 129, 133).
- **Naturalness**: fit to the receptor language and culture, the message's
  context, and the audience (p. 136).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-056](../cases.d/CASE-056.md): met, for the quotations.** The case quotes Nida from
  Marlowe's transcription of the book (pp. 159–60). The three passages it
  uses appear here word for word, at Reader pp. 129–130: the gloss
  alternative ("would be rendered literally, and would probably be
  supplemented with a footnote explaining that this was a customary method
  of greeting in New Testament times"), the principle ("the relationship
  between receptor and message should be substantially the same as that
  which existed between the original receptors and the message"), and the
  verdict ("quite naturally translates 'greet one another with a holy kiss'
  as 'give one another a hearty handshake all around'"). Nida's quotation
  of Phillips reads "all around"; [CASE-056](../cases.d/CASE-056.md) quotes it so. The reading
  confirms the case's source text first-hand. It adds no reader evidence,
  so the case's condition (c) is unchanged.
- **[CLAIM-233](../claims.d/CLAIM-233.md): met for its ground, with one qualification.** The claim
  holds that a functional-equivalence account as Nida states it "puts
  fidelity in the effect on the receiver, not in the text", and so predicts
  that judged stance and footing beat embedding similarity. The text bears
  this out. D-E's "focus of attention is directed ... toward the receptor
  response" (p. 136). It must reflect "sarcasm, irony, or whimsical
  interest" and each participant's social class and dialect (p. 139), which
  are stance and footing under other names. The qualification: Nida also
  says a D-E translation "must clearly reflect the meaning and intent of
  the source" (p. 136), and that "correspondence in meaning must have
  priority over correspondence in style" (p. 134). So the account predicts
  that meaning matters as well as response, and a propositional-similarity
  score would track part of what it values. It does not predict that text
  similarity does as well as judged pragmatic observables, so the claim's
  conclusion stands.
- **[CLAIM-205](../claims.d/CLAIM-205.md): partly, for Nida's functional account.** The claim says
  functional accounts need not fix one context or a static profile. Nida
  makes the right translation depend on the message, the purposes, and an
  audience that varies in decoding ability and interest (pp. 127–128). He
  calls the rules "probabilistic" and the valid answers several (p. 134).
  Naturalness is fitted to a particular receptor audience (pp. 136, 139). So
  Nida's account is audience-relative, as the claim says. The chapter gives
  no operationalization and does not bear on the claim's proposed baseline.

## Limitations

- **Abridged, cuts unmarked.** The 1964 chapter runs 37 pages and this
  reprint 15. Whatever Nida says in the omitted pages is not read here.
- **Opinion, not evidence.** The case for similar response is a survey of
  translators' statements. Nothing is measured on receptors, and Nida
  concedes the original response is often uncertain (p. 139).
- **The poles are ideal types.** Nida grants intervening grades and gives no
  criterion for placing a translation among them.

## Open questions

- How is "substantially the same" relationship to be judged, and by whom,
  when the original receptors cannot be asked?
- When meaning and response conflict, as in the handshake, which wins? The
  rule that meaning comes before style does not settle it, because the
  handshake changes the meaning in order to keep the response.
