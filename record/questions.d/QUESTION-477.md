# QUESTION-477: Is there a resource theory of moral commitments, in the formal sense: what are the free transformations among parties who hold commitments, which quantities are monotone under them, and is the leverage one party gains from another's commitment a conversion that is free for the one and costly for the other?

## Why it is a question

The owner, at U8 of the exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b), after A13
on normative manipulation:

> elaborate on the resource theoretical perspective on moral commitments

A15 read "resource" economically. Commitments were predictability,
credibility, motivational force and borrowed legitimacy, which others can
draw on and exhaust. Its §9 is headed "A resource-theoretic theory of moral
domination", but what follows is a list of five kinds of power over another
person's morality, with no formal structure. The economic reading is filed
in [CLAIM-703](../claims.d/CLAIM-703.md) (normative manipulation), [CLAIM-713](../claims.d/CLAIM-713.md) (the moral
subsidy) and [CLAIM-707](../claims.d/CLAIM-707.md) (asymmetric moral accountability).

The record also holds resource theory in the formal sense. Coecke, Fritz
and Spekkens ([THEORY-1397](../theory.d/THEORY-1397.md)): a resource theory is a symmetric monoidal
category of resources and free transformations, and its usual questions
depend only on the preordered monoid of convertibility. Marsden and Zwart
([THEORY-1435](../theory.d/THEORY-1435.md)): quantitative resource theories are enriched in a quantale
that composes costs. [CLAIM-563](../claims.d/CLAIM-563.md) says when processes under a budget are
closed under composition. Nothing on the record applies this apparatus to
commitments.

## What an answer would have to fix

This is the filer's reading, offered as the question's terms.

- **Resources.** A party's situation together with its standing
  commitments, as states.
- **Free transformations.** What a party may do at no cost to itself under
  a declared standard. The question is relative to whose costs count,
  because the same act can be free for one party and costly for another.
- **Monotones.** Quantities that free transformations cannot raise. One
  candidate is a party's capacity to refuse: if keeping a commitment
  removes an option, as A15 §7 has it ("B's ethical commitment gives A an
  option that B doesn't possess"), the refusal option behaves like a
  resource that the committed party cannot regain by free moves.
- **The asymmetry.** A15's humanitarian bargaining case would then read as
  a conversion that is free for A, who threatens to withdraw, and costly
  for B, whose commitment keeps it from withdrawing. Whether that reading
  holds is the question's last clause.

## What would answer it

- A construction: a small theory with stated free transformations, in
  which the leverage of A15 §7 appears as a convertibility A has and B
  lacks, with a monotone that witnesses it. Or a proof that no
  monoidal-preorder structure fits, because commitments do not compose in
  parallel (two commitments held together may constrain more, or less,
  than each alone).
- A reason why the formal sense adds nothing to the economic one: every
  question the record asks about commitments is already answered by the
  control-of-terms account ([CLAIM-690](../claims.d/CLAIM-690.md), [QUESTION-472](QUESTION-472.md)).

## Bearing

[QUESTION-472](QUESTION-472.md) asks whether activating another's obligations gives
control over the terms of their dependence. A formal answer here would
make that question exact: control would be the asymmetry of free
conversions.
