# NOTE-tmpcjj10: The Normative Constitution of Agency

## Contribution

The chapter separates two ideas of agency, natural and normatively
constituted, and argues that the second captures two features of ordinary
action-attribution that the first struggles with. It restates the argument
of *Self-Constitution* in short form. It reads Bratman as able to take the
normative view on board. It then argues that the laws of reason in general,
theoretical as well as practical, are "the laws of mental activity". That
is what makes collective agency possible "in the most literal sense".

## Key insight

The unity that makes a movement someone's action, and not something that
happens through them, is not given by having one mind. Self-consciousness
about one's own attitudes undoes the unity an animal has, and leaves "a
heap" of impulses and perceptions that must be put back together. Following
the norms of reason is how that is done. So the unity of an agent is the same
kind of thing whether the agent is one person or a group: something achieved
by conforming to constitutive norms, not something found.

## Assumptions

- **Kant's two imperatives are constitutive of willing.** "When you will an
  end ... you commit yourself to staying on the track of that end in the
  face of possible changes in your circumstances." The categorical
  imperative is the law of autonomy, and the hypothetical imperative is the
  law of efficacy.
- **Success in action is internal to agency.** "An agent does not just
  effect something ... an agent undertakes to effect something, and to
  undertake to effect something is to set yourself up for success or
  failure." A deviant success, such as a ricocheting bullet, is a failure
  as agency.
- **Animal perception has "teleological content".** Non-human animals
  perceive things as "to-be-eaten", "to-be-fled". This is offered as a
  belief, with a short evolutionary argument.
- **A constructivist, not substantive-realist, view of reasons.** It is
  assumed here, and argued elsewhere.

## Key results

- **§1.1–1.2.** Agency, individual or collective, needs unity, because an
  action is "a movement, or the effecting of a change, that is backed by the
  agent as a whole". Ordinary attribution carries the "activity
  implication" and the "identity implication".
- **§1.3.** Self-constitution explains both, separately. Activity comes from
  the form of the principles (self-determined efficacy). Identity comes from
  their content (practical identities: friend, teacher, parent, citizen).
  The account is indifferent to causal history: "It is the content of the
  principles we follow, not the metaphysical story about how we came to
  follow them, that explains the activity implication."
- **§1.4.** A naturalist can explain the identity implication through a
  Lockean reading of intentions, which the chapter attributes to Bratman.
  Doing so means "adopting the self-constitutionalist view that our
  identities are constituted by the choices that we make when those choices
  are governed by the laws of reason". The Lockean view needs only that the
  agent conform to her principles, not that she choose them. So the
  activity implication still needs self-determination.
- **§2.1–2.2.** Self-consciousness of one's own attitudes, unlike mere
  awareness of one's experiences (which mirror-test animals may have), sets
  the task of unification. Theoretical reason unifies a conception of the
  world, which also unifies the mind as its knower. "To be active, and to be
  under the norms of reason, are one and the same thing."
- **§2.3.** Being one natural mind "sets you the task" of normative
  unification, but it is not the only thing that does. Talking and acting
  together set the same task, so "nothing ... prevents people from acting
  together in the most literal sense of the word".

## Concepts

- **Natural vs normatively constituted agency**: agency identified by a
  causal pathway, against agency whose capacity "consists in or depends on
  the existence of certain normative relations" and whose success "depends
  on conformity to the norms in question".
- **Activity implication**: in acting, the agent's own efficacy, "spontaneous
  in the Kantian sense", is at work.
- **Identity implication**: an action evinces the agent's "essential self",
  which is why love, hate, gratitude and resentment are appropriate
  responses to it.
- **Self-constitution**: making oneself into an agent, and into the
  particular agent one is, by choosing under the formal norms of agency.

## Connections

The chapter's main interlocutor is Bratman. It cites *Intention, Plans, and
Practical Reason* ([LIT-tmpr5hsy](../literature.d/LIT-tmpr5hsy.md)), *Faces of Intention* and *Structures of
Agency* ([LIT-tmpphga0](../literature.d/LIT-tmpphga0.md)), and reports a seminar exchange of spring 2009 on
post-hypnotic intentions. It sets Gilbert's normative account of shared
intention (her "What is it for Us to Intend?" and "Shared Intention and
Personal Intention") beside the political model of Rousseau and Plato. The
record holds Gilbert's earlier "Walking Together" ([LIT-477](../literature.d/LIT-477.md)), Deferred and
unread. It draws on Davidson and Velleman for the natural
conception ([LIT-295](../literature.d/LIT-295.md) is Velleman on action), and on Kant and Plato for the
normative one. Its claim that self-consciousness of attitudes sets the
normative problem is the same claim the Tanner Lectures ([LIT-tmp18qr5](../literature.d/LIT-tmp18qr5.md)) make
under the name of "the reflective structure" of the mind.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md)** (manipulation). C3 is a clear instance of a structural
  account choosing the first option for activity. A manipulated agent who
  follows the right principles is active, whatever their origin. For deep
  responsibility, though, Korsgaard concedes that history matters (note
  15). The reported Bratman exchange is second-hand evidence that Bratman
  also bites the bullet. Korsgaard's own reply is that our objection to the
  hypnotic case "is not after all wholly based on its origin" but on its
  expected ephemerality. That suggests a non-historical condition,
  durability or integration over time, that [THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md) does not consider.
  Worth a line in its connections. Whether it escapes manipulation (a
  manipulator can install durable states, as the Demetrius case shows) is
  exactly what Korsgaard leaves "puzzling".
- **[THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)** (the regress). Korsgaard's stopping point is not an
  attitude but conformity to formal norms that constitute agency. That is a
  candidate for [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)'s refutation condition, "a stopping point that is
  specified independently of agency". It is specified by the norms of
  agency itself, so it may count as the dilemma's second horn. The record
  should weigh it when [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md) is next revised, together with the
  Tanner Lectures' wanton argument ([NOTE-tmpsutam](NOTE-tmpsutam.md)).

## Limitations

- The manuscript was read, not the published chapter.
- Its central Kantian premise, that the categorical imperative has
  determinate content, is assumed ("suppose ... as Kant supposes we can").
- The account of reason as unification is said to be "lifted" from another
  paper and is given here in compressed form.
- The collective-agency conclusion is stated in a paragraph. How a group
  observes "the rules of normative unification together" is not worked out.
- It does not say what unifies a natural mind ("I am certainly not going to
  advance a view").

## Open questions

- Does C3's indifference to history survive the Demetrius case, a
  durable installed identity, or must the account add a condition on how
  the content of one's principles was acquired?
- How does a group constitute itself as a single agent by following norms,
  and do Gilbert's joint commitments ([LIT-477](../literature.d/LIT-477.md)) supply the mechanism?
