# THEORY-tmph5l09: When a President can end a funding lapse selectively, paying the parts of government he favours from funds named by no appropriation, a shutdown stops checking him and instead hands him the choice of what to fund; each payment left unchallenged becomes a precedent, and a Speaker who lets it stand gives up his own chamber's power of the purse

## Source

Moynihan (2026), [LIT-061](../literature.d/LIT-061.md), "Mike Johnson's Institutional Betrayal", read in
[NOTE-084](../notes.d/NOTE-084.md). The memo it discusses (27 March 2026) was checked in that
reading.

## What was actually shown

**The memo.** It directs DHS and OMB "to use funds that have a reasonable
and logical nexus to TSA operations to provide TSA employees with the
compensation and benefits that would have accrued ... consistent with
applicable law, including 31 U.S.C. 1301(a)". It names no appropriation
([NOTE-084](../notes.d/NOTE-084.md), C1, confirmed).

**The legal premise.** Congress controls the "duration, amount, and
purpose" of spending. Glassman (quoted): purpose restrictions are "the most
bedrock feature of appropriations". Chafetz (quoted): unilateral spending
is "anti-constitutional". The concession that carries the argument: "It's
not that the President cannot pay federal employees during a shutdown, but
he has to have a clear appropriations source."

**The mechanism.** A shutdown the President can end for the parts he likes
is not a check on him. Paying the military during the previous shutdown
went unchallenged and "set a precedent": "If a President can point to any
unspent funds for an agency, he can reappropriate it." What is left to
Congress is, in Glassman's words, "the right to not appropriate any money".

**The choice.** The Senate passed a bill funding DHS except ICE and CBP;
the Speaker did not bring it to a vote and the House passed a
Trump-backed bill. "Johnson chose B": letting the President dictate
appropriations. Moynihan concedes that redirecting DHS money to non-ICE
work "might be considered a win for Democrats"; his objection is to the
precedent, not the outcome.

**What could have come out otherwise.** A ruling could hold such payments
lawful under §1301(a), which would leave the mechanism but remove its
illegality; or Congress could reassert purpose limits. Neither had
happened when the post was written.

## What this does not say

- **That the TSA payment is illegal.** That rests on scholars' opinions
  (C2), with no GAO or OLC opinion cited, and the post does not answer the
  memo's own invocation of §1301(a).
- **That the President cannot pay staff in a lapse.** The post concedes he
  can, with a named appropriation.
- **That the Speaker had only two options.** The binary is the author's
  framing, and "would pass handily in the House" is a counterfactual
  assertion (C4).
- **That its supporting facts are established.** The "record 12%" sick-call
  rate is unsourced and not in the memo (C5); the ICE and CBP multi-year
  funding (C3) and partisan steering of funds (C6) were not checked; the
  1980 origin of shutdowns (C7) rests on an unread piece.
- **That the text is as first published.** It was edited at least six days
  after its date.
