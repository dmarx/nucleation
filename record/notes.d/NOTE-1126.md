# NOTE-1126: Friedkin, Jia & Bullo — A theory of the evolution of social power

## Contribution

Opinion dynamics had modelled one issue at a time on a fixed influence
network. This paper models a standing group working through a sequence of
issues, with each member's openness to influence set by how much they
influenced the last outcome, and reports the first experiment long enough
(15 issues, triple the longest before) to look for the predicted
evolution. It finds the individual-level mechanism and several group-level
trends consistent with it.

## Key insight

Influence feeds back on closure. "Success in influencing group issue
outcomes elevates the individual’s self-weight (encourages opinion
assertiveness, confidence, and intransigence)", and failure dampens it
(p. 445). Because a self-weight scales every flow of influence through that
person, a small individual adjustment, iterated over issues in a strongly
connected group, concentrates influence on one member: "Inequalities of
influence centrality are reinforced" (p. 470).

## Assumptions

- **Single-issue dynamics are Friedkin–Johnsen (Eqs 1–2, p. 450).**
  x(k+1) = AWx(k) + (I − A)x(0), with a_ii = 1 − w_ii. Members stay
  anchored to their initial opinions, so exact consensus needs some
  self-weights at 0 or 1. This is not the DeGroot model.
- **Influence centrality (Eq. 8, p. 451).** C_i(s) is the mean, over
  members, of the total relative contribution of i's initial opinion to
  their settled opinions, from V = (I − AW)⁻¹(I − A).
- **Reflection (Eq. 9, p. 452).** w_ii(s+1) = C_i(s), or (Eq. 11) the
  time-average T_i(s) of past centralities. Interpersonal weights are
  w_ij(s+1) = a_ii(s+1) r_ij(s+1), with R free to change from issue to
  issue.
- **Veridical self-appraisal.** "Such adjustments assume that each group
  member has a veridical appraisal of their own relative influence
  centrality on each issue of the sequence" (p. 454). This is assumed, not
  measured.
- **Scope.** "The key scope condition of the postulated reflection
  mechanism is a consensus seeking group in which failures to reach
  consensus are viewed as social problems" (p. 470).
- **Measurement (pp. 454–456; S7–S8).** Undergraduates at a West Coast
  university, randomly assigned to 30 groups of four, each discussing 15
  of 18 Stoner–Wallach choice dilemmas in random order, under high or low
  pressure to agree. Initial and final opinions are private 0–100 ratings.
  W is each member's post-discussion allocation of 100 chips between
  the extent to which the discussion did and did not influence their
  final opinion, the first pile split among the
  others. So w_ii, and every centrality derived from W, comes from the
  same self-report.

## Key results

- **Simulation (pp. 446–449, 453; S1–S3).** With a fixed strongly
  connected R, one self-weight goes to 1 and the rest to 0. With R
  redrawn at random on every issue, in configuration and values, all
  14,000 simulated systems (n = 4 to 50, 1,000 each) still converged to a
  single closed member, though disturbances can change who. The attractor
  is a DeGroot system that reaches consensus on the closed member's
  initial opinion (S2).
- **Specification check (Table 1, p. 455).** The F-J prediction of final
  opinions, with no fitting, correlated 0.872 with observed final opinions
  and 0.811 with observed changes.
- **Reflection (Table 2, p. 457).** Prior centrality C_i(s) predicted
  next-issue closure w_ii(s+1); the effect of C_i(s) on w_ii(s+1) "is
  constant along the issue sequence", while the effect of time-averaged
  centrality rose as 0.404 + 0.095s. Prior centrality also predicted next
  centrality (Table 3), the T_i effect rising as 0.176 + 0.049s. Issue
  closure and centrality correlated at 0.769.
- **Concentration (Figs 4–7, pp. 458–463).** Time-averaged centralities
  stabilized at unequal levels; the chance of complete closure on the next
  issue rose with prior time-averaged centrality; complete closure became
  more frequent along the sequence.
- **Structure (Tables 4–6, Figs 8–11, pp. 463–467).** Discussion time fell
  along the sequence (about 0.9 minutes per issue, with a late rise).
  Network density fell from 84% to 67% by issue 11, then rose to 70%: "the
  sustained engagement along an issue sequence appears to have the
  opposite effect on interpersonal influence allocations" to the usual
  expectation that interaction strengthens ties (p. 465). Density of
  about 67% is the threshold below which unilateral topologies become
  common in four-person groups.
- **Consensus (pp. 467–468, Figs 12–13).** Of 450 discussions, 36.9% ended
  in unreconciled disagreement, 31.8% in consensus on a member's initial
  opinion and 31.3% in compromise consensus. "In our data, the probability
  of reaching consensus does not alter along the issue sequence" (p. 467).
  The trace of AW fell toward 0.5, and a lower trace went with consensus on
  an initial opinion.
- **The authors' summary (p. 471).** "Our group-level findings are signals,
  in the midst of structural disturbances", toward an attractor that
  "expeditiously reaches consensus"; without disturbances "the trajectory
  is toward a system with one dominant individual (a directive leader)
  whose initial opinions are accommodated".

## Concepts

- **Influence centrality**: "the total (direct and indirect) relative
  influence of each individual’s initial opinion to the settled opinions"
  of the group on an issue (p. 444).
- **Self-weight, closure-openness**: w_ii, the weight a member keeps on
  their initial opinion; a_ii = 1 − w_ii is openness.
- **Reflection (reflected appraisal) mechanism**: closure on the next issue
  set by realized influence on prior issues, after Cooley.
- **Attractor system**: the DeGroot system with one closed member and the
  rest fully open, which yields consensus on the closed member's view.
- **Reserved authority (S10)**: a nominal leader who voices no opinion and
  enforces "a full display and vetting of group members’ opinions".

## Bearing on the record

- **The exchange's description.** It cited this paper as "the laboratory
  support for DeGroot–Friedkin social-power dynamics". Two corrections.
  - **The experiment's model is Friedkin–Johnsen, not DeGroot.** On each
    issue members stay anchored to their initial opinions, and the
    predicted attractor is one fully closed member, an autocracy. The
    DeGroot–Friedkin model of [LIT-1146](../literature.d/LIT-1146.md) predicts instead an interior
    equilibrium with power ranked by centrality, and autocracy only for a
    star network. [LIT-1146](../literature.d/LIT-1146.md) notes that the F-J simulations show "a more
    pronounced tendency to autocracy".
  - **What the laboratory supports is the individual step.** Closure
    tracked prior centrality, and network structure shifted. The group-level
    endpoint, a directive leader and consensus on that member's opinion,
    was not reached; consensus did not become more frequent.
- **[CLAIM-448](../claims.d/CLAIM-448.md) (coherence is not integration).** The claim cites
  this paper with [LIT-1146](../literature.d/LIT-1146.md) as the baseline for self-reinforcing authority.
  The reading supports that, and bears on the claim in three further ways.
  - **Supports: efficiency is not quality.** The groups got faster and
    their networks thinner as influence concentrated. The authors say
    their account does not address "whether the evolution of a group
    toward greater efficiency is associated with a decrease or increase of
    collective intelligence" (S11), and connect the trajectory to
    groupthink. That is the claim's point, made by the authors about their
    own data: faster settlement is not evidence of better integration.
  - **Supports: what thins is who is listened to.** Density fell as
    members stopped allocating influence to some others. This is a loss
    of uptake rather than of admission: the dissenters spoke and were
    discounted. It is nearer the claim's "access, recognition, credibility,
    uptake" ladder ([CLAIM-403](../claims.d/CLAIM-403.md)) than to a regime that keeps contests off the
    record, and it shows a coherence that comes from concentration without
    any chair deciding what is admitted.
  - **Complicates the protected-channel prediction.** The claim predicts
    that a protected challenge channel can reveal hidden conflict. The
    supplement argues, untested, that a "reserved authority" who enforces
    full display and vetting of every member's opinion still yields
    concentration: "the reticence of the nominal leader of group encourages
    a competition (explicit or implicit) for influence among the other
    group members" (S10). If so, admitting every view does not by itself
    stop the record from becoming coherent by concentration; the reflection
    step acts after admission.
  - Filed as [THEORY-902](../theory.d/THEORY-902.md).
- **[CLAIM-451](../claims.d/CLAIM-451.md) (cycles, tension and dissonance).** Indirectly, through
  the claim's "entrench" route: one perspective learns to dominate and
  choices become consistent. This paper is a mechanism for that route
  among people. It says nothing about cycles or dissonance.
- **[CLAIM-277](../claims.d/CLAIM-277.md) and [CLAIM-364](../claims.d/CLAIM-364.md) (durable authority; low supervisory cost).**
  A social instance of authority made durable by its own success, and of
  rising efficiency that says nothing about domination.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **One self-report carries both sides of the key regression.** Closure
  w_ii(s+1) and prior centrality C_i(s), which depends on w_ii(s), both
  come from the chip allocations. Closure is persistent across issues, and
  Table 2 does not control for w_ii(s). Some of the "reflection" effect
  could be stable individual differences in self-weight. This is the
  record's reading; the paper does not discuss it.
- **Veridical appraisal is assumed**, and self-reported influence is the
  only measure of influence.
- **Small, homogeneous sample.** 120 undergraduates, groups of four, one
  session, hypothetical choice dilemmas with no correct answer.
- **Weak group-level fits.** R² of about 0.04–0.07 for the trends over
  issues; a rise near the end of the sequence that the authors attribute
  to fatigue.
- **A table hard to read as printed.** In Table 2, the s × C_i(s)
  coefficient of 0.171 with standard error 0.012 carries no significance
  mark, while the text says the C_i effect is constant along the sequence.
  The column layout may be at fault; it does not change the authors'
  reading.
- **Consensus did not change**, so the predicted end state is
  extrapolated.

## Open questions

- Does closure follow centrality once each member's own prior closure is
  controlled for, or with influence measured by observers rather than
  self-report?
- Do groups whose members cannot see their own influence show the same
  trend? Manipulating feedback about influence would test reflection
  directly.
- Does a reserved authority who enforces full vetting slow concentration,
  as the claim would hope, or not, as the supplement suggests?
