# LIT-1396: Hyperstition: An Introduction

Carstens, D., and Land, N. (2009). Hyperstition: An Introduction. Delphi
Carstens interviews Nick Land. Orphan Drift Archive,
https://www.orphandriftarchive.com/articles/hyperstition-an-introduction/.

Read in full on 2026-10-29 ([NOTE-1378](../notes.d/NOTE-1378.md)).

## Why this is here

The owner, on 2026-10-23, after the filing of the attributed self
([TERM-075](../terms.d/TERM-075.md)) and the claim that social judgements should get authority
from their grounds, not their visibility ([CLAIM-425](../claims.d/CLAIM-425.md)): "this is
reminding me of 'hyperstition'". The term names the case that claim did
not cover, an idea whose circulation helps make it true, so that its
visibility is evidence of what it will bring about. It is the source of
[CLAIM-426](../claims.d/CLAIM-426.md).

It differs from Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy ([LIT-1398](LIT-1398.md)) in two
ways the record cares about. Merton's prophecy begins as a false
definition of a present situation; a hyperstition is typically a fiction
or a forecast, never false in that sense, only not yet true. And its
mechanism is circulation, the visibility, repetition and attributed
plurality that [CLAIM-425](../claims.d/CLAIM-425.md) discounts. Land's own framing, theory-fiction
written to bring futures about, is a programme, not a finding; the record
cites it for the definition, not the programme.

Note of 2026-10-29, from the reading ([NOTE-1378](../notes.d/NOTE-1378.md)): the interview does
not bear out the first of these differences. Land sets hyperstition
against superstition, not against a false belief, and says it can
"transmute lies into truths"; his Jerusalem example makes an "assertion
into a truth". So a hyperstition may begin false, as Merton's prophecy
does. The difference that stands is the second, circulation: ideas that
"by their very existence as ideas" help bring about what they say. The
term "theory-fiction" is not in the interview.

## Note of 2026-10-29: Merton's side, read

Merton 1948, [LIT-1398](LIT-1398.md), is now read ([NOTE-1409](../notes.d/NOTE-1409.md)). "Why this is here"
says that Merton's prophecy "begins as a false definition of a present
situation". That holds for his definition, "in the beginning, a false
definition of the situation evoking a new behavior which makes the
originally false conception come true", and for his bank, a solvent bank
emptied by a rumour of insolvency. It does not hold for the essay's
examples: of his three opening ones, two are forecasts, the student
"convinced that he is destined to fail" and two nations that think war
"inevitable" and arm until it is. So the first difference drawn above
is weaker on Merton's side too, as the note above found it on Land's.
The difference that stands is still circulation. The status is
unchanged.
