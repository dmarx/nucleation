# NOTE-1109: Berridge and Robinson — Liking, wanting, and the incentive-sensitization theory of addiction

## Contribution

The authors' summary, a quarter-century on, of the theory they proposed in
1993. It retells how the liking–wanting dissociation was found, says what
incentive salience is psychologically, and defends sensitization against
the rival view that addicts have too little dopamine. It then extends the
theory, tentatively, to behavioural addictions.

## Key insight

Addiction is a disorder of 'wanting', not of pleasure or withdrawal.
"Addiction is not so much about satisfaction, pleasure, need or
withdrawal, by this view, as it is about ‘wanting’" (Psychological
Features of Incentive Salience). What sensitization changes is how
strongly cues pull, and that pull is cut loose both from how much the
outcome is enjoyed and from what the person cognitively wants.

## Assumptions

- **Two senses of wanting.** 'Wanting' in quotation marks is "a particular
  form of desire – namely, mesolimbic incentive salience". Wanting without
  quotation marks is "a cognitive desire with a declarative goal"
  (Psychological Features). The theory is about the first.
- **'Liking' is measured by taste reactivity.** Orofacial reactions to
  sweet and bitter, homologous across infants, apes, monkeys and rats, are
  taken as the measure of hedonic impact (introduction).
- **BOLD reactivity indicates dopamine.** Most human evidence is fMRI, and
  "most reports of such hyper-reactivity used fMRI measures, which do not
  directly measure dopamine" (Sensitization in Human Addiction). The
  authors cite optogenetic and PET work to bridge the gap.

## Key results

As reported by the review:

- **The founding dissociation.** Near-total dopamine depletion left rats'
  'liking' reactions to sugar normal while abolishing their motivation to
  eat. Stimulation of the same system raised 'wanting' without raising
  'liking' (introduction).
- **Incentive salience is cue-bound.** It is "less connected to cognitive
  goals and more tightly linked to reward cues, making those cues
  attention-grabbing and attractive". "The intensity of the triggered urge
  depends both on the cue’s reward association and on the current state
  of dopamine-related brain systems in an individual". So stress,
  excitement, appetite or intoxication can amplify it (Psychological
  Features).
- **It dissociates from cognitive desire and from report.** It "can occur
  either in opposition to a cognitive desire or even unconsciously in
  absence of any cognitive desire". Subliminal cues can raise motivation
  "even though the person remains unable to report any change in
  subjective feelings while motivation increases are revealed in their
  behavior" (after Figure 1).
- **The same system makes dread.** "fearful salience is generated by the
  same mesolimbic circuitry as incentive salience" (Motivational Salience
  in Desire Versus Dread).
- **Sensitization.** Repeated drug use, especially at high doses spaced
  apart, makes the system hyper-reactive. "Once induced, sensitization is
  very long lasting, and possibly even permanent." "‘Liking’, by contrast,
  need not increase with sensitization, and may even decrease. Sensitized
  ‘wanting’ can persist for years, even if the person cognitively doesn’t
  want to take drugs, doesn’t expect the drugs to be very pleasant, and
  even long after withdrawal symptoms have subsided" (Addiction Distorts
  ‘Wanting’ Versus ‘Liking’).
- **Reactive, and gated by context.** "A sensitized dopamine system is not
  always hyper-active, but it is hyper-reactive to drug cues and
  contexts". Testing outside drug contexts can hide sensitization
  entirely (Addiction Distorts; Disentangling Reports).
- **Against the dopamine-deficit view.** Blunted responses to drugs
  themselves are compensatory tolerance and fade within weeks. Cue
  hyper-reactivity does not fade: "if anything, sensitization grows for
  some time during abstinence", the incubation of craving (Disentangling
  Reports).
- **Vulnerability.** "only about 30% of people who take cocaine actually
  go on to become long-term addicts". Genes, sex hormones, stress, route
  and dose matter, as does a sign-tracking disposition to attribute
  salience to discrete cues (Individual vulnerability).
- **Behavioural addictions and Parkinson's.** "neural sensitization of
  ‘wanting’ mechanisms may in some cases occur without drugs", on
  "emerging" cue-reactivity evidence. Dopamine agonists give about 15% of
  Parkinson's patients compulsive gambling, shopping, sex or internet use,
  "intense and idiosyncratically diverse ‘wants’", which fade when the
  medication stops, and "such patients with intense motivations have
  virtually never been reported to experience intense pleasure from their
  medications or compulsions" (Other Behavioral Addictions?).
- **Disease, qualified.** A sensitized person "who faces a series of
  hundreds of intense temptations may eventually be expected to fail", so
  the "brain disease" label is "a reasonably fair description" (Is
  incentive-sensitization a brain disease?).

## Concepts

- **incentive salience ('wanting')**: motivational magnetism attributed to
  cues and representations of reward, mesolimbic and dopamine-related.
- **cognitive wanting**: desire with a declarative goal, which usually
  goes with 'wanting' and can come apart from it.
- **incentive sensitization**: lasting hyper-reactivity of the 'wanting'
  system to drug cues and contexts.
- **incubation of craving**: relapse vulnerability that rises over the
  first month or so of abstinence.
- **fearful salience**: the negatively valenced mode of the same
  mesolimbic system.
- **sign-tracker / goal-tracker**: an animal that approaches the cue that
  predicts reward, against one that goes to where the reward will arrive.
- **dopamine dysregulation syndrome**: compulsions produced by
  dopamine-agonist treatment in Parkinson's disease.

## Connections

The same programme as Berridge and Kringelbach's review ([LIT-1459](../literature.d/LIT-1459.md),
[NOTE-1110](NOTE-1110.md), [THEORY-893](../theory.d/THEORY-893.md)), which states the dissociation and the
'liking' hotspots in more detail. This paper says more about
sensitization and addiction. Dayan and Berridge ([LIT-584](../literature.d/LIT-584.md), [NOTE-463](NOTE-463.md)) give
'wanting' a model-based Pavlovian side. Ainslie's précis ([LIT-586](../literature.d/LIT-586.md), [NOTE-443](NOTE-443.md))
takes Berridge and Robinson's "wanted" but not "liked" behaviours as
instances of its itch-like cycle. Daw, Niv and Dayan ([LIT-580](../literature.d/LIT-580.md)) supply the
arbitration view of controllers that [QUESTION-266](../questions.d/QUESTION-266.md) borrows for
measuring potential. Flayelle et al. ([LIT-1465](../literature.d/LIT-1465.md)) cite incentive
sensitization as one of two model-free routes by which technology design
promotes uncontrolled use, and propose that unpredictable non-drug
rewards can cause it.

## Bearing on the record

**[QUESTION-267](../questions.d/QUESTION-267.md): a mechanism for proxy-keyed potential in persons,
which meets the term's kind clause no better than 'liking' does.**
- **The marks are all there, with a mechanism for each.** On [TERM-076](../terms.d/TERM-076.md),
  potential is priority, persistence and resumption ([THEORY-055](../theory.d/THEORY-055.md)).
  - *Priority*: sensitized 'wanting' wins against "a genuine cognitive
    desire to abstain".
  - *Persistence*: it "can persist for years".
  - *Resumption*: cues reinstate it, and in incubation it grows while
    interrupted.

  The mechanism is a re-keying of strength. How hard a ranking pulls is set
  by "the cue’s reward association and ... the current state of
  dopamine-related brain systems", not by what the outcome does for the
  person, and repeated stimulation inflates it while 'liking' and
  expectation fall. That is a ranking that once tracked the organism's
  good and has come apart from it, the question's personal case, with a
  named mechanism.
- **But the ordering is over cues.** Incentive salience is "tightly linked
  to reward cues" and "less connected to cognitive goals". [TERM-076](../terms.d/TERM-076.md)'s
  kind clause says that a ranking of world states, however potential, is
  not valence "until it ranks the system's own states by their bearing on
  its maintenance". [CLAIM-433](../claims.d/CLAIM-433.md) used exactly that clause to set
  'liking' aside, as "an ordering over stimuli". On this paper's account
  'wanting' is one too. There are two ways out, and they cost the same.
  The kind clause can exclude sensitized 'wanting' too, and then the
  question's answer for addiction is "not valence at all". Or the clause
  can be read so that a ranking of cues counts as a ranking of the
  system's prospective states (obtaining, consuming), and then 'liking'
  can be read the same way, as a ranking of consummatory states. That was
  the rescue [CLAIM-438](../claims.d/CLAIM-438.md) left open. Either way, the asymmetry
  [CLAIM-433](../claims.d/CLAIM-433.md) draws between the two on the kind clause does not
  survive this paper.

**[CLAIM-433](../claims.d/CLAIM-433.md): the pairing of strength with 'wanting' and sign with goal
conduciveness.** The paper supplies the case the claim's third defeater
asks for. Its recovering addict holds a cognitive goal, abstinence, and
relapses on cue. Here incentive salience sets the strength, and the
person's declarative goal and maintenance make the outcome goal-obstructive.
Sensitized 'wanting' persists even when the person "doesn’t expect the
drugs to be very pleasant". [CLAIM-433](../claims.d/CLAIM-433.md) resolves the addict as "goal
conduciveness positive (the goal is attained)". That holds only if the goal
is the drug: the sign read along the ordering that has prevailed, not along
the person's goals or maintenance. Read so, the claim stays consistent,
but its "goal conduciveness" has become conduciveness to whatever prevails.
That presupposes an answer to [QUESTION-267](../questions.d/QUESTION-267.md), namely that proxy-keyed
potential is valence with its sign read on the proxy. Read the component process model's way
instead, by the person's own goals, the relapsing addict has strong
potential and negative goal conduciveness, and taking the drug is
obstructive. The pairing then predicts negative valence at the moment of
taking. The paper does not report on that. It is an empirical question, and
it is where the defeater should be tested.

**Two things that support the account.**
- **Strength and sign are separable.** "fearful salience is generated by
  the same mesolimbic circuitry as incentive salience". The
  salience system supplies strength for both signs, which fits
  [CLAIM-433](../claims.d/CLAIM-433.md)'s separation of strength from sign, and [CLAIM-440](../claims.d/CLAIM-440.md)'s
  view that intensity is not itself signed.
- **Potential without report.** Subliminal cues raise motivation
  "even though the person remains unable to report any change in
  subjective feelings". This is [CLAIM-439](../claims.d/CLAIM-439.md)'s category of unfelt
  valence, potential without the system's model of it, found by the
  layer-1 against layer-3 dissociation that [QUESTION-266](../questions.d/QUESTION-266.md) proposes.
  It is cited, not read here (Winkielman et al. 2005; Childress et al.
  2008).

**[QUESTION-268](../questions.d/QUESTION-268.md): measuring potential.** "not always hyper-active, but
... hyper-reactive", and testing outside drug contexts can hide
sensitization. Potential of this kind is dispositional and gated by
context. A conflict record taken where the cues are absent will read it
as weak. The paper's complaint about scanner studies is the measurement
problem the question faces, and [CLAIM-440](../claims.d/CLAIM-440.md) already flags the same
dispositional character under the counterfactual eraser.

**The organizational side, as analogy only.** Dopamine dysregulation
syndrome shows that the potential's strength can be set from outside, with its
targets "idiosyncratically diverse", and that it fades when the drive
stops. An incentive system that amplifies whatever metric is cued, as
Wells Fargo's did for cross-sell ([CASE-151](../cases.d/CASE-151.md), [CLAIM-432](../claims.d/CLAIM-432.md)), is the analogue
[QUESTION-267](../questions.d/QUESTION-267.md) has in mind. Nothing in this paper is about
organizations, and the record would need an argument that a firm's
incentive system plays the role dopamine does before this grounds
anything there.

**Not an instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- A review by the theory's authors, of mostly their own programme. Rival
  views (dopamine deficit, habit accounts) are presented in order to be
  answered.
- The extension to behavioural addictions rests on "emerging" evidence
  and an unexplained mechanism ("through mechanisms not yet understood").
- Human evidence is largely fMRI cue reactivity, an indirect index of
  dopamine.
- 'Liking' outside taste is not measured. The human 'liking' evidence is
  ratings.

## Open questions

- At the moment of relapse, is the person's goal-conduciveness appraisal
  positive or negative, if measured apart from choice? The answer decides
  [CLAIM-433](../claims.d/CLAIM-433.md)'s third defeater.
- Does sensitization by unpredictable non-drug rewards (Flayelle et al.'s
  proposal) occur in people, by a measure other than self-reported
  craving?
