# NOTE-tmp97rv4: Prest (2018) — Pale imitations: White performances of slave dance in the public theatres of pre-revolutionary Saint-Domingue

## Contribution

Extends the literature on colonial mimesis, which mostly treats the colonized imitating the colonizer, to the colonial imitator, after Ricardo Roque, with a documented corpus of Saint-Domingue theatre announcements (1773, 1777, 1780–81, 1784).

## Key results

1. §1, the thesis: white colonials "sought to control – and to diffuse – the power that they reluctantly detected in slave dance, and especially in vodou dance, by re-performing it in a non-threatening form."
2. Abstract: what was staged was "from the colonial perspective, a less threatening, more Europeanized form of slave dance."
3. Abstract: "white dancers in the theatre appear never to have imitated vodou dances, which were bound up with spirit possession".
4. §5: "While the colonials could not always control dancing black bodies on the plantation and elsewhere, they could control the representation of dancing black bodies in the playhouse."
5. §5: the theatre was to bring French culture, "thereby, it was alleged, rescuing French citizens (the white population) and would-be French citizens (the small number of free people of colour who were admitted to the public theatre and sat in special seats) from the dangers of degeneration."
6. §5: the protagonists of Jeannot et Thérèse "suffer only in love and not in work."
7. §5: "This was a deliberately whitened version of slave dance that sought to be increasingly recognizable at the same time that it kept real slave dance at a safe distance."
8. §5: with one exception, Lise, "A young female actor of mixed racial ancestry" (note lviii), black roles were performed by white performers, "sometimes in blackface."
9. §6: Jeannot et Thérèse "offered a temporary theatrical inoculation against the very local threat posed by the supposed violence and lasciviousness of slave dance on the island."

## Assumptions

Newspaper announcements (the Affiches américaines and supplements) are a fair record of what was staged; how the dances looked is inferred. Moreau de Saint-Méry is evidence of colonial belief, not of what enslaved dancers meant. Some identifications are probable only (that the Léogane children were white; that the 1781 "amateur" was white, in blackface).

## Concepts

Colonial imitator; mimesis and mimicry (after Roque); calenda, chica, vodou; blackface; the theatre as "inoculation"; Créole drama.

## Bearing on the record

- **Corrections to A3** (and to [LIT-tmpefkn6](../literature.d/LIT-tmpefkn6.md)'s first summary): the article documents no staged chica (it describes the chica only from Moreau de Saint-Méry); vodou dance was *not* staged, the only vodou-associated element being a brief pseudo-ritual without dance or possession; and the framework is Roque's colonial imitator, Bhabha being named once among theorists of the reverse direction.
- **[CASE-tmpwmg3h](../cases.d/CASE-tmpwmg3h.md)**: supports the free people of colour admitted to "special seats" (key result 5) and black roles played by whites, often in blackface (key result 8); corrects the case on Lise, a free performer of mixed ancestry; and reads the Créole theatre's slave dances as colonial control, not as resistance to metropolitan authority. Added to the case's `reported_in`; dated note and new version there.
- **[CLAIM-614](../claims.d/CLAIM-614.md)**: illustrates its representational half only. Characters who "suffer only in love and not in work" and dance kept "at a safe distance" keep slavery's harm out of view on stage. The article does not show that the theatre reproduced these to maintain itself, or whose counter-normativity it would be ([QUESTION-449](../questions.d/QUESTION-449.md)). Not written as a relation.
- **[TERM-074](../terms.d/TERM-074.md)**: the theatre is a collective institution, sense 3.
