# NOTE-131: Schwitzgebel — If materialism is true, the US is conscious

## Contribution

Materialists should think rabbits are conscious, since rabbits are biologically and neurophysiologically much like us. They should also accept consciousness in naturally evolved aliens that behave like us but are built very differently, on pain of "Earthly chauvinism". Schwitzgebel argues that someone who accepts weirdly built aliens should also accept spatially distributed group entities, and someone who accepts rabbits should accept even fairly unintelligent group entities. The United States seems to be such an entity. Once our prejudice against spatially scattered beings is set aside, it has all the kinds of properties materialists usually treat as marks of consciousness.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- Introduction (pp. 3–4): the aim is dialectical. Materialism, which philosophers like, should push them toward group consciousness, which they dislike, unless they accept something worse. Bizarreness is a weak objection, since cosmology and microphysics are bizarre too.
- §1 "Sirian Supersquids, Antarean Antheads, and Your Own Horrible Contiguism" (p. 5ff.): thought-experiment aliens build up the case that spatial contiguity is a prejudice, not a criterion.
- §2 "Anti-Nesting Principles" (pp. 11–13): rejects Putnam's stipulation that no pain-feeler decomposes into pain-feelers as unjustified, and questions Tononi's IIT exclusion postulate, which rests on Occam and on the intuition against group minds.
- §6 "What Could Be Missing" (pp. 23ff.): tests the conclusion against four top-to-bottom theories (Dretske, Dennett, Humphrey, Tononi), including Dretske's and Dennett's objections from correspondence.
- §7 "Three Ways Out" (pp. 31–35): eliminativism, extreme sparseness (for example, language is required, though the US arguably "speaks" collectively) and neurochauvinism (Block's China brain, Searle's beer cans). All are costly, and neurochauvinism collapses back into the question "what is so special about brains?"
- §8 Conclusion (pp. 35–36): the argument may be read three ways: as grounds for US consciousness, as a challenge to materialists to state criteria that exclude it, or as a reason to distrust any universal metaphysics of mind, since every developed view carries some bizarre implication. The moral questions (is Exxon-Mobil conscious? would it have rights?) are left open.

## Open questions

- It is the canonical argument that liberal materialist criteria over-generate consciousness. It bears directly on AI-consciousness notes ([LIT-111](../literature.d/LIT-111.md) Birch, [LIT-135](../literature.d/LIT-135.md) Seth, [LIT-185](../literature.d/LIT-185.md) IIT): any indicator-property approach inherits this problem.
- The §2 critique of IIT's exclusion postulate connects to [LIT-185](../literature.d/LIT-185.md)'s defence of IIT and should be read against it.
- Check the published version against the manuscript. Page numbers and possibly wording differ, and the §6 treatment of Dennett and Dretske may have changed.
