# CASE-tmpv0eej: Frankfurt's unwilling addict: a man who has the desire to take the drug and the desire to refrain, wants the desire to refrain to be his will, and is moved by the craving anyway

## What happens

Frankfurt (1971, p. 12), as read in [NOTE-545](../notes.d/NOTE-545.md). The unwilling addict has two
first-order desires, to take the drug and to refrain. He has a
second-order volition that the desire to refrain be his will, meaning
the desire that moves him. The craving moves him anyway, and he is
"helplessly violated by his own desires". The wanton addict, beside him,
has the same first-order conflict and no preference about which desire
wins.

Held fixed: both desires are his, and so is the higher-order preference.
What varies is which desire is effective.

## What it cannot show

Its standing is fictional, and it is a philosopher's example. It shows
that having a desire, or preferring it at a higher order, is not the same
as its being effective. That is a conceptual possibility, and it is what
[CLAIM-047](../claims.d/CLAIM-047.md) uses. Whether identifying with the desire to refrain makes it
"more truly his own" (p. 13) is Frankfurt's account of ownership,
[THEORY-120](../theory.d/THEORY-120.md), and the case does not settle it.
