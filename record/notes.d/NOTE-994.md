# NOTE-994: Borg, Harrison, Stazicker & Salomons — Is the folk concept of pain polyeidic?

## Contribution

The paper is the systematic reply to the experimental-philosophy "bodily
view" of pain. It separates objections to how the bodily view's
experiments were run from objections to what they could show even if
run well. It names an alternative: the folk concept of pain is neither
univocally bodily nor univocally mental, and has several dimensions on
which judgements move with framing. It adds pilot vignettes, reported in
outline, in which participants answered in a mind-centred way.

## Key insight

From "the folk sometimes answer as if pain were bodily" it does not
follow that "the folk concept of pain is bodily", only that it is "(at
least in part) Bodily" (p. 18). The same data that deny pains the
Cartesian marks are open to a non-Cartesian mental reading. And a
question about whether a pain is "in the mind" is heard as asking
whether it is real. So the right model may be a concept whose
dimensions different framings bring forward, not a single view to be
discovered.

## Assumptions

- **The target is the folk concept.** Footnote 8 grants that
  philosophers other than Dennett may not have claimed the mental view
  for the folk. The paper's interest is "the folk view of pain" itself
  (p. 5).
- **The bodily view needs some bodily disturbance.** Footnote 19
  assumes the bodily view requires "some form of bodily injury or
  disturbance" for a pain to exist. Otherwise "the clear blue water
  between the Bodily and the Mental views risks being lost". The pilot
  vignettes are built on this assumption.
- **Bodily and mental conceptions are incompatible.** Footnote 18 agrees
  with the bodily view on this and leaves open whether the folk notion is
  coherent at all.
- **Framing moves judgements.** The polyeidic view's clause (d) is
  assumed, not shown. The pilot vignettes are offered as framings that
  bring out the mental strand.

## Key results

The paper is argument. Its only data of its own are the pilot results
in §3.vi and footnote 20.

- **The bodily view, as characterised (pp. 5–6).** Two claims: "(i) pains
  have non-brain based bodily locations (ii) pains are not in the mind,
  they are not experiential states". On referred pain, Kim et al. (2016)
  leave open whether the pain is where the damage is or where it is felt.
  So pains are not identical to damage on the view, but "properties of
  bodily disturbances", and awareness of pain is "a perception of
  something external to the mind" (p. 6). Footnote 9: a phantom limb
  forces the bodily view either to allow pains at non-bodily locations or
  to say that awareness mislocates them.
- **The evidence reviewed (pp. 7–10).** Sytsma 2010 Study 5: two people
  joined at the leg in a three-legged race were judged to feel different
  pains (mean 5.4 on a 1–7 scale, 7 "clearly different pains"), and
  conjoined twins sharing a leg the same pain (3.29). Study 6, one
  replacement hand wired to two people: 3.42. Unfelt pain during
  distraction: mean 3.02, "significantly below the neutral point of 4".
  Reuter, Phillips & Sytsma 2014: 64.5% allowed that a drug-trial
  participant "merely hallucinated the pain".
- **3.i Which folk (pp. 10–12).** The privacy studies had 35 and 57
  participants (fn 12), some recruited through a philosophy
  personality-test website. The bodily view puts the folk "in diametric
  opposition" to the IASP definition, quoted in full, on which pain is
  "always subjective" and "always a psychological state".
- **3.ii Acute against chronic pain (pp. 12–13).** "All the thought
  experiments run by these theorists concern acute pain". Fibromyalgia
  pain moves around the body or is diffuse. Footnote 16: a pain that
  moves cannot be individuated by its location.
- **3.iii The probe (pp. 14–15).** "Is Jack's pain in his mind?" is
  "extremely likely to be heard by ordinary folk as in fact asking
  whether Jack's pain has a somatic (bodily) cause, or indeed whether he
  is really in pain". Folk may resist saying pain "is in the mind"
  because of "the pejorative implicatures" even if they think of it as
  partly experiential.
- **3.iv Counting pains (pp. 15–16).** The means are near neutral. Even
  the three-legged race, two legs and two people, reaches only 5.4, which
  the authors read as showing that "folk don't really have very firm
  views about how pains get individuated".
- **3.v Non-Cartesian mental states (pp. 16–17).** The folk accept
  unconscious Freudian thoughts as mental. So denying privacy,
  subjectivity and incorrigibility to pains may show "that folk think
  that pain is a non-Cartesian mental state". Footnote 17 quotes Sytsma
  & Reuter (2018) conceding that their results "are consistent with
  both".
- **3.vi The inference (pp. 17–20).** What follows is "(3*) The folk
  conception of pain is (at least in part) Bodily". Pilot vignettes
  (from "Harrison et al, manuscript"): conjoined twins Adam and Zed share
  a scraped leg, and Adam says it hurts while Zed says it does not; Maya
  injures her wrist and says it "never actually hurt". The scale runs
  from 1 "clearly agree" to 7 "clearly disagree". "Zed has pain" averaged
  5.70 and "Maya had pain from this injury" 5.79 (fn 20), and "all the
  results obtained in the pilot study" differed from the midpoint and
  supported a mental-state view. No sample size, test statistic or
  number of vignettes answered is given. The text says "eight scenarios".
- **The polyeidic view (pp. 21–27).** "(a) the concept of pain is an
  amalgam of several distinct dimensions along which beliefs about pains
  can be ranked. b) these ideas or dimensions can conflict with one
  another. c) subjects hold latent positions on these rankings. d)
  rankings are subject to framing effects. e) positions on these
  dimensions have direct behavioural effects." The candidate dimensions
  are mental/bodily, public/private, conscious/unconscious,
  aversive/non-aversive, purposeful/non-purposeful,
  motivational/non-motivational, and sensory or affective ones such as
  the McGill descriptors. There are two models: separate dimensions,
  which keep Hill's paradox, or single scales between poles, which let a
  subject shift over time without contradiction. The authors "prima
  facie" prefer the second (p. 27). A weaker, pragmatic version would put
  the dimensions in the context of judgement, not in the concept.
- **Clinical hypothesis (pp. 28–29).** Clinicians may weight bodily
  aspects and patients experiential and affective ones. How a subject
  conceptualises pain "must, it seems play an intervening role" in
  expectation-based treatments.

## Concepts

- **Bodily view of pain** — the experimental philosophers' claim that
  the folk locate pains in non-brain body parts and do not treat them as
  experiential states.
- **Paradox of pain** — Hill's (2005) claim that the folk concept treats
  pain as both mental and bodily.
- **Cartesian marks** — privacy, subjectivity (existence depends on
  awareness) and incorrigibility.
- **Polyeidic concept** — a concept made of several dimensions, possibly
  conflicting, on which people hold latent and framing-sensitive
  positions.
- **Multidimensionality of pain** — psychology's biopsychosocial
  view, which the polyeidic view refines and grounds in the concept.

## Connections

The paper answers Sytsma (2010), Reuter, Phillips & Sytsma (2014), Kim et
al. (2016), Reuter (2017) and Sytsma & Reuter (2018). It draws on Hill's
paradox of pain, on Aydede's work on the IASP definition, and on Corns
(2014) against unitary accounts of pain. It sets Klein's imperative and
Bain's evaluative theories of pain as each tracking one dimension. It
cites Reuter, Sienhold & Sytsma (2018) only in its bibliography and in a
list of the bodily view's advocates. None was read here.

## Bearing on the record

- **The rubber hand THEORYs: no bearing.** [THEORY-766](../theory.d/THEORY-766.md),
  [THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md), [THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md) and [THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) are about ownership and
  the reports synchronous stimulation produces. This paper is about the
  concept of pain. It meets no promotion test, partly or wholly, and
  touches no refutation condition. Nothing in it concerns agency, so
  nothing bears on [THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md).
- **Felt location and referred touch.** Footnotes 9 and 19 discuss
  referred pain (felt in the arm, damage in the heart) and phantom pain
  (felt where no limb is). In each, a sensation's felt location comes
  apart from its bodily source, which is the structure of referred touch
  in [THEORY-766](../theory.d/THEORY-766.md). The point the paper makes is about what the bodily
  view must say pain *is* in such cases. It reports no judgement or
  experience of felt location, and nothing about multisensory conflict.
  So the likeness is one of structure, and it is mine. It is not
  evidence for or against the referred-touch clause.
- **The framing point and the suggestion debate.** Clause (d), that
  judgements are "subject to framing effects", is at the level of a
  concept what [THEORY-766](../theory.d/THEORY-766.md) worries about at the level of report:
  answers moved by how the question is put. The parallel is mine and
  methodological only. These are judgements about vignettes, not reports
  by stimulated participants, and they give nothing toward the
  expectation-matched test [THEORY-766](../theory.d/THEORY-766.md) asks for.
- **[THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md) and interoception.** The paper does not mention
  interoception. No bearing.
- **[THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md) (felt affect is the experience of the body's
  regulatory state).** The bodily view, as Borg et al. state it, makes
  awareness of pain "a perception of something external to the mind - the
  bodily pain state" (p. 6). That has the same shape as [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md)'s
  claim that what is felt is a state of the body. The IASP definition
  the authors defend makes pain "an unpleasant sensory and emotional
  experience", which is [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md)'s felt layer with its valence. Both
  pairings are mine. Neither paper studies what is felt, so neither
  bears on [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md)'s promotion test, which asks for manipulations of
  interoceptive signalling. The folk concept could be bodily, mental or
  polyeidic with [THEORY-045](../theory.d/THEORY-045.md) true or false.
- **[QUESTION-059](../questions.d/QUESTION-059.md).** The pilot vignettes ask whether damage that is
  never felt is pain, and the answers lean to no. That is a fact about
  the folk use of "pain". It is not a case of a regulatory role met
  without feeling, which is what the question would need. No bearing.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** The paper makes the distinction the record keeps.
  Folk judgements about vignettes are evidence about a concept and its
  pragmatics, and the paper's whole argument is that they shift with
  framing. They establish how people classify pain under a given
  question. They do not establish where pain is or what it is. Borg et
  al. do not draw a metaphysical conclusion from their pilot data, and
  their discussion of reduction (p. 27) is conditional on the polyeidic
  model.
- **Self and persons ([TERM-045](../terms.d/TERM-045.md), [THEORY-417](../theory.d/THEORY-417.md), [ADR-024](../decisions.d/ADR-024.md)).** The privacy
  studies ask whether two people can share one pain. That is a question
  about individuating pains, not persons or selves. No bearing.
- **[LIT-635](../literature.d/LIT-635.md) ([NOTE-511](NOTE-511.md)).** Block's "phenomenism" holds that pain has
  phenomenal character beyond any representation. Borg et al. appeal to
  the intuition that "there is a particular way that they feel" (p. 18).
  This is an appeal to the same intuition, not support for Block.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **Its own data are a footnote.** The pilot gives two means, no sample
  size, no tests and no list of the eight scenarios. Each vignette also
  says outright that the person reports no pain, so "has pain" may be
  read as asking whether he or she felt any. That is the kind of loaded
  probe the paper objects to in its opponents.
- **Not a reply to Reuter, Sienhold & Sytsma.** The pain-in-mouth
  studies are not discussed. The objections in 3.iii and 3.iv do not
  reach them: they do not ask whether pain is "in the mind", and their
  means for pain are well below neutral.
- **The polyeidic view has no test.** Its dimensions are suggested, not
  derived. The criteria for a "genuine dimension" are deferred (fn 27).
  The choice between models is left open, and so is whether the
  dimensions are in the concept or in the pragmatics.
- **Version.** Read from a revise-and-resubmit manuscript. The published
  text may differ, including in the pilot figures.

## Open questions

- Does a probe without the "in the mind" implicature, run with the same
  participants and both a bodily and an experiential framing, move the
  same people along a mental/bodily scale? That is the test of clauses
  (c) and (d).
- Do people with chronic or diffuse pain answer the bodily view's
  vignettes differently from people without?
- Does the pain-in-mouth result (Reuter, Sienhold & Sytsma 2019) survive
  a framing that stresses the experience of the pain over the injury?
