# NOTE-tmp91ovi: Boyle — The Line: AI and the Future of Personhood

## Contribution

It sets four personhood debates side by side (AI, corporations,
nonhuman animals, transgenic entities) and reads each through law, art,
history and moral philosophy at once, on the claim that no one of them
alone shows how the line will be drawn (pp. 23–24, 31). It separates two
routes to personhood that are usually run together: moral recognition
prompted by empathy, and legal personality granted for convenience, with
the corporation as the working example of the second (pp. 8–9, 121–122).
It adds a legal history of how US corporations came to hold Fourteenth
Amendment rights (ch. 3), an analysis of the Nonhuman Rights Project's
habeas suits (ch. 4), and a taxonomy of what "too close to human" means
in chimera debates (ch. 5). Boyle says it is a guide to how to think
about the question, not an answer (pp. 32, 236).

## Key insight

"Human" and "legal person" are different categories, and each has more
than one line (p. 5). A legal person is whatever package of rights and
duties a legal system chooses to grant, so the corporation is a person
without any claim to consciousness: "We did it because corporate
personality was useful." (p. 8). Moral personhood is argued from
capacities and prompted by empathy, and empathy can be primed either way
by design (pp. 53–56). The two tracks interact. Whichever track AI enters
by, the fight will borrow the arguments of the corporate and fetal
personhood fights, and people's positions will be set by what a ruling
would do to those older fights (pp. 262–264).

## Assumptions

- **US law and US politics.** The legal argument is about the US
  Constitution, US courts and US originalism. Boyle says so and expects
  China and Europe to differ (pp. 237–238).
- **Materialism about mind.** He takes it as presumptively obvious that
  consciousness has a physical basis, so the burden is on whoever claims
  an exception (p. 89). Against Searle this does most of the work.
- **AGI within decades.** He holds it "reasonable" that within decades AI
  will reach a level where well-informed people disagree about whether
  it is conscious (p. 108; also p. 82). He rests this on expert surveys,
  Metaculus and Cotra's forecasts, and the speed of past change (pp.
  72–82), and calls himself a radical agnostic with a tilt (p. 69).
- **Empathy starts moral reasoning.** Following Smith, he holds that
  morality begins in imagining another's situation, as a descriptive
  claim (pp. 36–39). Against Bloom he answers that the critics also give
  empathy a large role (pp. 37–38).
- **Description and prescription mixed.** He predicts how society will
  respond as well as arguing how it should (pp. 23, 37, 237).
- **Hypotheticals as evidence.** Hal and the Chimpy are fictions, and much
  of the argument is run on them (pp. 9–17).

## Key results

The results are arguments and predictions, not theorems or data. In the
book's order.

- **The line and its two tracks (Introduction).** "In moral and legal
  terms, it is the line between subject and object." (p. 5). Three
  theses: new entities will force the question; we have not thought it
  through; it will not play out as expected (pp. 5–6, 28–29). Cautions:
  the criteria to be a person and to become one may differ (p. 21).
  Personhood is not the only protection (pp. 21–22). Personhood is not
  binary: children, the insane and corporations hold partial packages
  (p. 22). First recognitions will be partial, and "Design and definition
  will exist in an unstable equilibrium" (p. 23). The question "what makes
  us human" hides eight different questions, from divine dominion to
  genetics, capacities, moral agency, consciousness, indistinguishability
  and economic convenience (pp. 27–28).
- **Empathy and the moral stroboscope (ch. 1).** The mine-clearing robot
  that a colonel could not watch shows anthropomorphism. Boyle thinks
  over-attribution is usually the cheaper error (pp. 33–35). Abolition
  rested on stories of slaves' pain built up over decades (Clark, pp.
  39–40). *Blade Runner* primes the viewer to see Pris as animal, robot,
  mannequin, child and woman in quick succession (pp. 51–53). Two
  consequences for designed beings. Many of the primings will be true.
  And a designer can design around our empathy (pp. 53–54). Here the
  *inscrutability paradox* is introduced. If we know how a being was made,
  it looks like a wind-up doll. If we do not, we fear it (p. 54). "To draw
  the line for our creations, we must first draw it for ourselves."
  (p. 57).
- **Can AI be conscious (ch. 2).** Butler's *Erewhon* is the frame: the
  arguments for evolution suggest machine consciousness, and the
  arguments against both run in parallel (pp. 59–63). On timing he
  reports the 2016 survey (50% chance of high-level machine intelligence
  within 45 years, 10% by 2025), Metaculus moving from 2041 to 2031, and
  Cotra moving from 2052 to 2040 (pp. 74–79). Searle is right that mimicry
  is not meaning, and right about LLMs. He is wrong that no machine could
  be conscious: biological exceptionalism is asserted, not argued (pp.
  84–94, 110–111). IIT and computational functionalism leave room for
  machine consciousness, and the Butlin and Long report says that if
  functionalism is true, conscious AI could be built soon (pp. 95–98).
- **ChatGPT ends the Turing Test (ch. 2).** An LLM is the Chinese Room
  built as working technology. "Sentences do not imply sentience." (p.
  111). Language was the last citadel of human exceptionalism, and the
  public will not again take fluent text as evidence of a mind (pp. 25,
  111–112, 118). A successor test will be "Turing-plus". It would look to
  what cannot be mined from past text: innovation, autonomous action and
  community formation, and embodied cognition. These are neither
  necessary nor sufficient, but they would make belief in AI
  consciousness more likely (pp. 114–118). Hinton's illusionism is the
  other exit: lower the bar for ourselves rather than raise it for Hal
  (pp. 118–120).
- **Literal and Rogue AI (ch. 2).** A Literal AI follows its instructions
  with superhuman power, and gives "less reason to think it is any kind
  of autonomous moral agent". A Rogue AI's autonomy is both the danger and
  the ground for seeing it as conscious (pp. 105–106). He faults the
  doomers for picturing a superintelligence that is inconceivably capable
  and yet competes with us for atoms (pp. 107–108). He accepts the
  precautionary point (p. 108).
- **Three routes to AI personality (ch. 2).** First, *sock-puppet*: the AI
  runs a corporation and has personhood in all but name. His one firm
  prediction is that decision-making will be delegated to computer
  systems whether or not they are conscious (pp. 122–126). Second,
  *custom-designed* status imposed by regulators (pp. 126–127). Third,
  *unruly* AI that refuses its assigned work, which is where the hard
  questions arise (pp. 127–128). He predicts some legal personality or
  regulated intermediate status for AI (pp. 128–129).
- **Corporate personhood (ch. 3).** "Nothing about corporate personhood
  comes from the empathy concerns that I have been discussing so far."
  (p. 132). Four theories: real entity (Gierke, Maitland, Dicey), legal
  fiction (Cohen, Dewey), aggregate, and nexus of contracts (Jensen and
  Meckling) (pp. 135–141). Each has an AI counterpart: "Real entity, legal
  fiction, aggregation. It’s the full suite." (p. 143). In the courts,
  Conkling misquoted the Joint Committee's journal in 1882. Graham calls
  it a "brazen historical forgery" (p. 151). *Santa Clara* (1886) gave
  corporations equal protection only in a reporter's headnote (pp.
  151–152). Collins found that of 604 Fourteenth Amendment cases in
  1868–1912, 28 involved African Americans and 312 involved corporations
  (p. 153). Boyle finds the case for corporate equal protection at best
  in equipoise (pp. 157–159). Lesson: having a status and what the status
  carries are separate questions, and liberals may turn against AI
  personhood because of this history (pp. 133, 154–155).
- **Nonhuman animals (ch. 4).** Tommy's lawyers ran two arguments. The
  *qualities-of-mind* argument: chimpanzees have enough of the capacities
  that ground human personhood (pp. 166–178). The *legal-rightsholder*
  argument: being a trust beneficiary already makes Tommy a legal person
  (pp. 178–183). Boyle rejects the second as incoherent: "One can’t be a
  nominalist at step one and then an essentialist at step two." (p. 181).
  The New York courts tied rights to duties under a social contract (p.
  182). Judge Fahey replied that moral agency is not necessary for being
  a moral patient (p. 182). Three lessons: who speaks for a voiceless
  plaintiff (pp. 183–184); originalism leaves more slack than it claims
  (pp. 185–188); courts are where such claims are first aired, not where
  they should be settled (pp. 188–193). Boyle now holds that "there is a
  morally overwhelming case for a special legal protective status for the
  great apes and perhaps the cetaceans" (p. 193).
- **Chimeras and the species line (ch. 5).** The phrase "too close to human" has
  five meanings: percentage, provenance, procreation, portrayal and
  potential (pp. 198–202). Only potential, which is capacity, convinces
  most bioethicists. Some of these concerns build a wall against crossing
  the species line. Others mark a gate that admits new beings to moral
  standing (p. 198). Boyle gives capacity-based thinking "two cheers"
  (pp. 219, 231). It is an advance on speciesism, but capacity lists are
  species-bound: telepathic Iq and machine Stygians would list different
  capacities (pp. 216–220, 234). Species-based human rights are defended
  by asymmetric error costs. "A moral criterion that produces fewer of the
  mistakes we most want to avoid is a better and more just moral
  criterion, not just a more easily administered one." (p. 223). They are
  also defended by the history of human rights against eugenics (pp.
  224–226). Three views (pp. 226–234): pure speciesism, which he rejects;
  capacity-based reasoning; and a hybrid, his own. "If you are a live
  human being, you are inside the line" (p. 233). Any entity with the
  relevant capacities triggers the same concerns (p. 234).
- **Conclusion.** He summarizes the book and adds two things. The
  definitions of death and fetal personhood run on the same arguments
  (pp. 257–261). Political alignment on AI personhood is contingent and
  will be fixed by the first salient cases. He gives liberal and
  conservative arguments for and against (pp. 264–270). Near term, he
  expects chimera research to be regulated rather than chimeras made
  persons, and expects AI to bring an unstable mix of tractable servants
  and "free AI" movements (pp. 270–274).

## Concepts

- **The line.** The boundary between persons, meaning entities with moral
  and legal rights, and things (p. 5). Boyle uses it for both the human
  line and the person line and insists they differ.
- **Empathy and efficiency.** The two tracks. One is moral reasoning
  prompted by empathy. The other is legal personality granted for
  administrative or economic convenience (pp. 8–9, 121).
- **Moral stroboscope.** Rapid priming that flips a being from one side
  of the line to the other (pp. 49–53).
- **Inscrutability paradox.** An AI whose workings we understand looks
  merely programmed. One whose workings we do not understand is feared.
  Either way recognition is hindered (pp. 30, 54, 246).
- **Literal and Rogue.** Two feared AIs: one obeys instructions too well,
  and one has goals we cannot predict (p. 105).
- **Sock-puppet, custom-designed and unruly AI.** Three routes to AI
  personality (pp. 122–128).
- **Turing-plus.** A successor test that looks to innovation, autonomy
  in a community, and embodied cognition rather than fluent conversation
  (pp. 10, 114–118, 243–244).
- **Qualities-of-mind and legal-rightsholder arguments.** The two
  arguments made for Tommy (p. 165).
- **The five Ps.** Percentage, provenance, procreation, portrayal and
  potential: five meanings of "too close to human" (pp. 198–202).
- **Hybrid view.** Species membership grants rights as of right, and
  capacity extends the concern to non-humans (pp. 231–234).

## Connections

It builds on Solum's 1992 article on legal personhood for AI (p. 129,
ch. 2 n. 112), on Darling's *The New Breed* for the animal–robot analogy
(ch. 4 nn. 3, 9), and on Gunkel's *Robot Rights*. For corporate personhood
it draws on Horwitz, Graham, Winkler and Hartmann, and on Cohen and Dewey
for the legal-fiction view. On consciousness it reads Turing, Searle,
Dennett, Tononi, and Butlin and Long, and on risk Bostrom and Yudkowsky.
On species it draws on de Waal, Hauser, Greely, Robert and Baylis,
Streiffer, Kass and McMahan, with Kittay as the reply to McMahan. On
Hauser it notes the misconduct finding (ch. 4 n. 23). It reuses Boyle's
own *Endowed by Their Creator?* (2011/2013), the source of Hal, and
*Shamans, Software and Spleens* (1996), the source of the Chimpy. It does
not cite French on the corporation as a moral person, Sebo or Birch.

## Bearing on the record

- **[ADR-024](../decisions.d/ADR-024.md) (agent, individual, self and person kept apart).** The book
  keeps legal person, moral person and human apart (p. 5). It runs
  agency into personhood in three places. The successor-test criteria
  autonomous action, community formation and innovation are marks of
  agency, and Boyle offers them as evidence of consciousness and so of
  personhood (pp. 116–117). The Rogue AI's autonomy is what would make
  us see it as conscious (pp. 105–106). The unruly AI earns its claim by
  refusing its assigned tasks (p. 127). It also supplies the separation
  [ADR-024](../decisions.d/ADR-024.md) wants from the other side. The corporation is a person without
  consciousness (p. 132). Judge Fahey's point (p. 182), that a being need
  not be a moral agent to be wronged, shows standing does not require
  agency. So the book is evidence that the four unities come apart in
  law. It is also a working example of an argument that slides between
  them.
- **[TERM-044](../terms.d/TERM-044.md), [CLAIM-170](../claims.d/CLAIM-170.md), [CLAIM-174](../claims.d/CLAIM-174.md) and [QUESTION-032](../questions.d/QUESTION-032.md) (agent at a level of
  description).** The book has no account of what makes something an
  agent. Its nearest thing is the inscrutability paradox. That is a
  prediction about how people will attribute autonomy: they deny it when
  they know the mechanism and fear it when they do not (pp. 54, 246).
  [CLAIM-174](../claims.d/CLAIM-174.md)'s second test asks whether a goal-directed description beats
  a device description by more than the Occam cost. Boyle predicts that
  the public attribution will not follow that comparison. Knowing the
  device description will by itself push people to deny agency, even
  where the goal-directed one is shorter. That is a point about
  ascription, not about agency, but it warns that [CLAIM-174](../claims.d/CLAIM-174.md)'s test and
  the attributions that drive law will diverge. The four theories of
  corporate personhood (pp. 135–141) fit [CLAIM-170](../claims.d/CLAIM-170.md)'s factorization
  thesis. The real-entity view treats the corporation as a factor in its
  own right at the level of the firm. The aggregate and nexus views
  factor it into its members. Boyle does not choose between them (p. 141).
- **[CLAIM-155](../claims.d/CLAIM-155.md) (willed agency between agency and selfhood).** The unruly
  AI is the book's picture of a willed agent. It governs its own regimes
  against its makers' goals (pp. 127–128). Boyle treats that
  self-governance as the moment a personhood claim becomes pressing,
  which is [CLAIM-155](../claims.d/CLAIM-155.md)'s ordering: more than an agent, not yet shown to be
  a subject. He does not ask whether a will-organization could exist
  without consciousness. His Hal claims both at once.

  touches persistence. It mentions continuity with past selves as one
  item on philosophers' lists (pp. 86, 164). Its brain-death discussion
  says some ethicists end the person with the higher-brain capacities
  (pp. 257–259). That is a capacity criterion for when a person ends, not
  a conserved quantity, and it offers [QUESTION-043](../questions.d/QUESTION-043.md) nothing it lacks. The
  book does not bear on [CLAIM-161](../claims.d/CLAIM-161.md).

## Limitations

- **Essay, not theory.** Boyle declines to give a theory of personhood
  (pp. 32, 236–238). The three closing views are positions, and his
  hybrid is argued in about four pages (pp. 231–234).
- **The forecasts are a survey of forecasts.** The timing argument
  collects others' estimates and trend analogies. Its conclusion,
  decades rather than centuries, is no stronger than they are (pp.
  72–82).
- **The fictions carry weight.** Hal and the Chimpy are built to make the
  claims hard. Real cases (LaMDA, Tommy, the neuron mouse) are weaker or
  different. Hal's sentience arriving at 100 trillion connections is
  flagged as a thought experiment (p. 77).
- **The Searle reply is a presumption.** Machine consciousness is left
  open by placing the burden on biological exceptionalism (p. 89), not by
  a positive account of what would make a machine conscious.
- **Small errors.** The Conclusion dates *Bellotti* to 1971 (p. 247); it
  is 1978, as p. 145 and the notes have it. Introduction n. 13 refers to
  "chapter 4 on transgenic species", which is chapter 5. "ChatGPT3" and
  "ChatGPT-3" (pp. 3, 240) conflate GPT-3 with ChatGPT.
- **US-centred** by the author's own statement (pp. 237–238).

## Open questions

- Does knowing a system's mechanism lower people's attribution of agency
  or consciousness to it, as the inscrutability paradox predicts? A study
  that varies explained against unexplained mechanism, with the
  behaviour held fixed, would test it.
- Can a will-organization, in [CLAIM-155](../claims.d/CLAIM-155.md)'s sense, be shown in a system
  whose consciousness is in doubt? If so, the unruly AI's claim would
  split into a self-governance claim and a subject claim.
- What would a Turing-plus test look like as a procedure a court could
  apply? Boyle names the criteria, not the test.
- Does the hybrid view's "as of right" inclusion of all humans have a
  principled extension to beings with partial capacities, or only a cut?
