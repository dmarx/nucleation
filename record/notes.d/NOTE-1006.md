# NOTE-1006: Botvinick & Cohen — Rubber hands 'feel' touch that eyes see

## Contribution

The report gives a simple, repeatable procedure that makes people feel a
rubber hand as their own, and shows that the procedure works only when
what is seen and what is felt are synchronous. It adds a behavioural
measure, the drift of the felt position of the real hand toward the
rubber one, that tracks how long the illusion was reported. It turns the
question of what makes a body part feel like mine into an experimental
one.

## Key insight

Seeing a touch and feeling one at the same moment is enough, on its own,
to move where touch is felt, where one's hand is felt to be, and which
hand is felt to be one's own. Ownership of a body part is not fixed by the
body: it follows the correlation among the senses, and when the senses
conflict the system resolves the conflict by moving the felt hand.

## Assumptions

- **The questionnaire measures the experience.** The illusion is
  established by self-report: an open-ended description and agreement on
  a seven-step visual-analogue scale with nine statements "presented in a
  random order" (Fig. 1). Three describe the predicted effects and six
  serve as controls.
- **Intermanual reaching measures position sense.** "With eyes closed,
  the right index finger was drawn along a straight edge below the table
  until it was judged to be in alignment with the index finger of the
  left hand". Displacement is the difference between the means of three
  reaches before and three after exposure.
- **Reported prevalence is a usable predictor.** Figure 2's x-axis is
  "the percentage of the 30-min viewing period during which the ownership
  illusion was experienced". How subjects reported this over 30 minutes
  is not stated.
- **A brief communication.** Ten subjects are named for the first
  experiment. The size of the second experiment's group and of the
  asynchronous control group is not given in the text, and neither are
  the statistical tests behind the P-values. Figure 2 plots ten points
  (my count from the rendered page).

## Key results

All on p. 756.

- **Setup.** Each subject's left arm rested on a small table, hidden by a
  standing screen; "a life-sized rubber model of a left hand and arm was
  placed on the table directly in front of the subject". Two small
  paintbrushes stroked the rubber hand and the hidden hand, "synchronising
  the timing of the brushing as closely as possible".
- **The illusion (Fig. 1).** After ten minutes, subjects "seemed to feel
  the touch not of the hidden brush but that of the viewed brush, as if
  the rubber hand had sensed the touch". The three underlined, predicted
  statements drew affirmative responses: "It seemed as if I were feeling
  the touch of the paintbrush in the location where I saw the rubber hand
  touched", "It seemed as though the touch I felt was caused by the
  paintbrush touching the rubber hand" and "I felt as if the rubber hand
  were my hand" ("P<0.002 for underlined questions, P<0.018 after
  correcting for multiple comparisons"). On the rendered figure the first
  has every response at "agree strongly", and the other two have means
  near ++ with no response below +. The six control statements (the real
  hand drifting, a second left hand, touch felt between the hands, the
  real hand turning "rubbery", the rubber hand drifting or coming to
  resemble one's own) do not reach significance. Five have means on the
  disagree side; the last, that the rubber hand "began to resemble my own
  (real) hand", sits slightly above neutral. Their ranges are wide.
- **Ownership (final paragraph).** Subjects who referred touch to the
  rubber hand "also consistently reported, in both sections of the
  questionnaire, experiencing the rubber hand as belonging to
  themselves". "Eight of ten subjects spontaneously employed terms of
  ownership", for example "I found myself looking at the dummy hand
  thinking it was actually my own."
- **Proprioceptive drift (Fig. 2).** After a prolonged (30-minute)
  exposure, "Subjects' reaches after experiencing the illusion were
  displaced rightward toward the rubber hand, the magnitude of this
  displacement varying significantly in proportion to the reported
  duration of the illusion". The fitted line is y = 0.09x − 1.4 (cm
  against % of 30 min), "r2 0.47, p<0.03".
- **Asynchronous control.** "A small asynchrony" in the brushing gave
  "low prevalence of the illusion (mean 7% of the exposure period compared
  to 42% for the synchronously brushed group; P<0.01)" and no drift toward
  the rubber hand: "mean displacement 13 mm away from the rubber hand,
  compared with 23 mm towards it in the experimental group; P<.04".
- **The model.** "We hypothesized that this illusion involves a
  constraint-satisfaction process operating between vision, touch and
  proprioception — a process structured by the correlations normally
  holding among these modalities." A connectionist model "suggested that
  the illusion's spurious reconciliation of visual and tactile inputs
  relies upon a distortion of position sense"; the drift experiment
  tested that prediction. The model is not described or cited. Its layer
  of bimodal units is compared with premotor cells in monkeys that respond
  to touch on a body region and to a seen object approaching it (Graziano
  and Gross 1994).
- **Conclusion.** "While the rubber hand illusion does not tell us
  precisely what ingredient might make only certain forms of intermodal
  correlation relevant to the self, it does show that intermodal matching
  can be sufficient for self-attribution."

## Concepts

- **Intersensory bias** — the class of effects in which one sense shifts
  what another reports; the authors place the illusion in it, citing
  prism adaptation (Hay, Pick and Ikeda 1965), Tastevin (1937) and others.
- **Referred touch** — touch felt at a location other than the stimulated
  one, here at the seen rubber hand.
- **Self-attribution / bodily self-identification** — experiencing a body
  part as belonging to oneself. The paper does not use "ownership" as a
  technical term; it says "terms of ownership" and, in Fig. 2's caption,
  "ownership illusion".
- **Intermodal perceptual correlation** — the proposal, credited to Lewis
  and Brooks-Gunn (1979) and Bahrick and Watson (1985), that the body is
  told apart from other objects by its "participation in specific forms
  of intermodal perceptual correlation".

## Connections

The authors place the illusion beside Ramachandran's mirror-box work, in
which amputees who watched their intact arm touched in a mirror felt the
touch in the phantom, and beside bimodal premotor neurons in monkeys
(Graziano and Gross). The developmental literature on infants' detection
of contingency between their own movement and what they see (Bahrick and
Watson) supplies the idea that intermodal correlation marks the body as
self. The paper builds on these by citation only; none was read here.

## Limitations

- Ten subjects, one laboratory, one page. No effect sizes beyond the
  regression, no named tests, and the control group's size is not given.
- The questionnaire's predicted items were known to the experimenters,
  and subjects saw the rubber hand brushed in a design that makes the
  intended answer guessable. The six control items, five with means on the
  disagree side, guard against blanket agreement but not against
  suggestion specific to the touch and ownership items. The authors do
  not discuss demand characteristics.
- The drift experiment changes the exposure (30 minutes against ten) and
  measures prevalence by a procedure not described, so its relation to the
  questionnaire result is indirect.
- The connectionist model that motivated the drift prediction is neither
  shown nor cited, so C5 cannot be assessed from the paper.
- In Figure 1's caption the corrected threshold (P<0.018) is larger than
  the uncorrected one (P<0.002); this reads as two different statistics
  given without explanation.
- The conclusion concerns attribution of a hand. It does not show that
  whole-body ownership, self-location or the self works the same way.

## Open questions

- Is the drift a measure of ownership or only of visuo-proprioceptive
  recalibration? The paper ties it to reported duration but does not
  separate the two.
- What makes "only certain forms of intermodal correlation relevant to
  the self"? The authors leave this open in their own words. Does the
  rubber hand need to look like a hand, be placed where a hand could be,
  or only be touched in synchrony?
- Is the self-report of ownership independent of the referred touch, or
  a description of it? The questionnaire items were affirmed together.
