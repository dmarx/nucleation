# NOTE-tmpy5k5m: The Moral Status of Animals (SEP)

## Contribution

The entry asks whether anything distinctive about humans justifies granting them moral status while denying it to other animals. It reviews the main proposed grounds for moral considerability — species membership, human-exceptional capacities, personhood, sentience and agency — and reports a growing philosophical consensus that human–animal differences do not justify denying animals moral consideration. It then turns to what interests animals have and how their claims are to be counted against competing ones, and closes with approaches (care, relational, empathy-based) that reject the standard argumentative framing altogether.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1 defines moral considerability as being a being that can be wronged, and asks why only humans would qualify.
- §1.1–1.3: speciesism (Ryder's coinage, Singer's popularisation), human exceptionalism by capacities, and Kantian personhood are each presented with the objection that no capacity is both shared by all humans and absent in all other animals.
- §1.4–1.5: sentience (the utilitarian ground — lives can go better or worse for the being) and agency (Sebo's 2017 distinction between propositional and perceptual agency) are the newer proposed grounds.
- §2.1–2.2 separates having moral status from how strong a claim is ("showing up on a moral radar screen" vs. where and how strong the signal is), and surveys subjective and objective accounts of animal interests.
- §3 reports Clark's and Diamond's view that argument misframes the issue, and ends with Gruen's "entangled empathy" as an alternative mode of moral attention.
