# NOTE-tmp57s5w: Block & Fodor — What Psychological States are Not

## Contribution

It separates functionalism from its first formal statement. Putnam's
machine-state theory said psychological states are machine-table states of
an automaton; the paper shows that identity fails on several independent
counts, and it shows which counts leave broad functionalism standing (all
but the qualia argument). It also states, in 1972, the productivity and
structure arguments that later carry the representational theory of mind.

## Key insight

Ask what a machine table individuates. A machine-table state is fixed by its
outputs and successor states, so it is a single, occurrent, unstructured
item on a finite list. Psychological states are none of these: some are
never occurrent, several co-occur, there are indefinitely many, and they
contain one another. So whatever functional story is true of minds, its
states are computational states of the system ("has just proved Fermat's
last theorem"), not entries in its table.

## Assumptions

- **Target (§II).** FSIT: every organism that satisfies psychological
  predicates has a *unique best description* as a probabilistic automaton,
  whose inputs are sensory transducers and outputs motor instructions, and
  each psychological state is one of its machine-table states (p. 165).
- **Type identity** means the biconditionals "O is in psychological state
  type X at t iff O is in machine-table state type Y at t" (p. 165). The
  paper sidesteps the identity conditions of abstract objects.
- **Physicalism** here is type physicalism (n. 2).
- **Empirical premises.** Lashleyan equipotentiality, convergent evolution of
  psychology, and the buildability of psychologically similar machines
  (pp. 160–161); the productivity of human belief is "an empirical claim,
  albeit an overwhelmingly plausible one" (p. 176).
- **Methodological conservatism.** One should resist models requiring
  changes "not independently motivated" (p. 170), used to keep the
  everyday dispositional/occurrent distinction.

## Key results

All are informal arguments.

- **Behaviourism (pp. 160, 162–164).** Behaviour depends on the interaction
  of beliefs, desires, inputs and memories, so the one-to-one pairing fails;
  the disjunctive (many-to-one) rescue fails three ways: no distinct
  disjunctions, no lawlikeness (Davidson), and contradiction when one
  behaviour expresses different states at different times.
- **Physicalism (pp. 160–161).** Equipotentiality, convergent evolution of
  psychology, and machines that are psychologically like an organism but
  physiologically unlike it.
- **FSIT is not trivial (pp. 165–166).** Every organism is simulable by some
  Turing machine and realises the "null" Turing machine (Putnam, in
  conversation); FSIT needs a *unique best* description whose states
  correspond to psychological states.
- **Argument 1 (pp. 168–170).** Every machine-table state is a possible
  occurrent state; "believes that P" has no occurrent "is believing that P".
  Psychological states may correspond to "some abstract property of the
  organization of machine tables", but that is "very much weaker".
- **Argument 2 (pp. 170–172).** An automaton is in one state at a time, so
  FSIT models sequential but not simultaneous interaction; organisms need
  "a set of intercommunicating automata operating in parallel". An
  equivalent single automaton exists, but it and the parallel set cannot
  both be best descriptions.
- **Argument 3 (pp. 172–174).** Inverted qualia: verificationist
  counterarguments "are not persuasive". Two replies are open: nomological
  impossibility of functionally identical, qualitatively distinct states;
  or ruling qualia irrelevant to type identity. The second risks saying "an
  organism might be in pain even though it is feeling nothing at all". This
  argument alone threatens functionalism in the broad sense.
- **Argument 4 (pp. 174–175).** "Damn" versus "darn" makes your pain
  type-distinct from mine; with paths between all states, "any two automata
  which have less than all their states in common will have none of their
  states in common".
- **Argument 5 (pp. 175–177).** Machine-table states form a list; a
  person's possible psychological states are productive, specifiable "at
  best ... by finite axiomatization". They may still correspond to
  *computational* states, for which countability suffices.
- **Argument 6 (pp. 177–179).** Believing P is "somehow" a constituent of
  believing P & Q (n. 8: "Very much 'somehow'"); lists have no notion of
  constituency, and productivity and structure are "two aspects of the
  same phenomenon".
- **§IV (pp. 179–181).** "The basic condition upon type identity in science
  is that it makes possible the articulation of the domain of laws."
  Behaviourism and physicalism type states by a subset of the relevant
  laws; FSIT adds the machine-table constraint, which "cannot be
  satisfied".

## Concepts

- **Machine-table state** — a state "specified by columns in its machine
  table" (p. 177).
- **Computational state** — "any state of the machine which is
  characterizable in terms of its inputs, outputs, and/or machine table
  states" (p. 177).
- **Unique best description** — the one automaton description whose states
  correspond to the organism's psychological states (p. 165).
- **Type identity** of psychological states — being in states of "exactly
  the same type" (p. 159).

## Limitations

- The arguments are aimed at one formulation; each comes with an escape
  that keeps functionalism (abstract properties of machine tables,
  computational states). They show FSIT's machine-table clause fails, not
  that a functional theory of mind fails.
- The qualia argument is explicitly prima facie and not developed.
- Argument 1 rests on English grammar ("is believing" is not said), backed
  by a conservatism principle; a theory might posit occurrent belief states.
- The empirical premises are stated, not evidenced.

## Open questions

- Which "abstract property" of an automaton could a disposition such as
  belief correspond to (argument 1)? The paper names the option and leaves
  it.
- Is argument 4's fineness objection answered by Psychofunctional typing
  at a level of abstraction, as [LIT-407](../literature.d/LIT-407.md) later suggests, or does it recur
  for any functional individuation?
