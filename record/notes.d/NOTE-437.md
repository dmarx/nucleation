# NOTE-437: The Future of Intervention Science: Process-Based Therapy

## Contribution

The paper turns a family of complaints about DSM-era psychotherapy research
into one programme with a name, process-based therapy (PBT), and one
foundational question. It is written jointly by leading figures of the two
camps it reconciles: Hofmann as the Beckian, traditional CBT side and Hayes
as the acceptance and commitment therapy, "new generation" side. The
programme makes the process of change, not the syndrome or the named
protocol, the unit of evidence-based care. The paper adds no data. Its
contribution is the framing and a list of testable predictions about where
the field will go.

## Key insight

Gordon Paul's 1969 question already asked "how does it come about?". The
field answered the rest of the question with "protocols for syndromes" and
dropped that part. The authors' bet is that syndromes are the wrong unit,
because they never resolved into diseases. The right unit is the
modifiable process that a procedure moves and that in turn moves the
outcome. Once that is the unit, a therapy's name, its school and even the
diagnostic category become secondary. Two problems that change by the same
processes under the same procedures need not be distinguished.

## Assumptions

- **Processes are real, modifiable and measurable at the level the paper
  needs.** A therapeutic process is defined as "a set of theory-based,
  dynamic, progressive, and multilevel changes that occur in predictable
  empirically established sequences oriented toward the desirable
  outcomes".
- **Mediation and moderation can carry the evidential load**, with the
  stated caveat that "a statistically significant mediator is not
  synonymous with a generative mechanism because third variables can
  always exist". Mediators and moderators are "first steps" toward causal
  and functional accounts (Kazdin 2007; Nock 2007).
- **Pragmatic outcomes are common ground across philosophies of science.**
  Contextualists take outcomes as truth criteria and "elemental realists"
  take them as the outgrowth of ontological knowledge; both value them
  (Biglan & Hayes 2016).
- **Change runs through context.** "If a dependent variable is going to
  change in psychology, ultimately it needs to be done by changing history
  or situational circumstance."

## Key results

The paper has no theorems or experiments. Its results are the definitions,
the argument against the syndrome programme, and the predicted trends.

- **Foundational question:** "What core biopsychosocial processes should be
  targeted with this client given this goal in this situation, and how can
  they most efficiently and effectively be changed?"
- **Definition of PBT:** "the contextually specific use of evidence-based
  processes linked to evidence-based procedures to help solve the problems
  and promote the prosperity of particular people". Procedures are
  techniques. Processes are the change mechanisms that procedures act
  through.
- **The case against the latent disease model.** It assumes symptoms
  reflect latent disease entities. The DSM-5 itself concedes that
  validators are insufficient and falls back on clinical utility, and the
  authors say there is "scant evidence" the model met even that. NIMH
  funded protocols for syndromes after DSM-III (1980) and began
  withdrawing from them after 2010 toward RDoC.
- **Cited process-level findings** (as the paper reports them):
  - In panic, verbal information and perceived control changed anxiety
    under CO₂ challenge, supporting Clark's catastrophic-misinterpretation
    model over a purely organic one.
  - Exposure-based CBT for anxiety "works in part through cognitive
    defusion" (Arch et al. 2012).
  - Patients high in catastrophic misappraisal did better with
    capnometry-assisted respiration training than with CBT (Meuret,
    Hofmann & Rosenfield 2010).
  - Anxiety-only patients may do better with traditional CBT and those
    with comorbid mood disorders with ACT (Wolitzky-Taylor et al. 2012).
  - Psychological flexibility can account for part of the effect of the
    therapeutic alliance as well as of ACT (Gifford et al. 2011).
- **Admission rule.** "If a procedure reliably produces gains and
  manipulates a process that mediates these gains, then it is ready to be
  admitted into the armamentarium of PBT." If the specified process "cannot
  be shown to be consistently applicable, the underlying model is
  incorrect and needs to be set aside". Conversely, if a procedure fails to
  move a process, the model "remains untested".
- **Predicted trends**: decline of named therapies and of general schools;
  rise of testable models; rise of mediation and moderation studies; new
  forms of diagnosis and functional analysis, including complex networks;
  a move from nomothetic to idiographic methods; processes that specify
  modifiable elements; the importance of context; component studies
  returning to the laboratory; new training; integration with the life
  sciences; new delivery forms (apps, books, calls); a science of the
  therapeutic relationship; and attention to culture.

## Concepts

- **therapeutic procedure**: a technique or method the therapist uses to
  reach the client's agreed goals.
- **therapeutic process**: the change mechanism through which a procedure
  produces the outcome, defined as theory-based, dynamic, progressive and
  multilevel. It may include common factors such as the alliance, if they
  meet the same theoretical and empirical standards.
- **process-based therapy (PBT)**: defined above. **PB-CBT** is its CBT
  form.
- **latent disease model / medical illness model**: the assumption that
  symptoms reflect underlying, latent disease entities, each to be targeted
  by a specific protocol.
- **functional analysis**: idiographic identification of "important,
  controllable, causal functional relationships" for a client's target
  behaviours (Haynes & O'Brien 1990). It is presented as behaviour
  therapy's original foundation, which psychiatry's structural nosology
  displaced.
- **mediator / moderator**: as Baron and Kenny define them, with the
  caveat that clinical use blurs baseline moderators into ongoing
  reassessment.

## Connections

- **Network theory ([LIT-547](../literature.d/LIT-547.md)).** The paper's "complex network approach"
  is Hofmann, Curtiss and McNally (2016). It describes the approach as "an
  extension of functional analysis" in which "psychological problems are
  not expressions of underlying disease entities but rather are
  interrelated elements of a complex network". That is Borsboom's theory
  in all but citation. Borsboom's network interventions are a natural
  reading of what a process is in network terms.
- **The single-mechanism alternative ([LIT-538](../literature.d/LIT-538.md)).** Salkovskis,
  Sighvatsson and Sigurdsson ask the same Paul question, in its 1967 form
  without "how does it come about". They propose one mechanism for all
  effective therapies: a shift from a stuck negative meaning ("Theory A")
  to a tested alternative ("Theory B"), which they call psychological
  flexibility. PBT expects many processes in a hierarchy, and it suggests
  that reappraisal might sit under a broader cognitive flexibility. The
  two converge on "flexibility" as the candidate at the top of that
  hierarchy, from opposite starting points.
- **Engel ([LIT-548](../literature.d/LIT-548.md)).** The foundational question's "biopsychosocial"
  is Engel's word. The paper does not cite him, and its target is the DSM's
  latent-disease model rather than the biomedical model of medicine.
- **Meditation's adverse effects ([LIT-066](../literature.d/LIT-066.md)).** The paper lists mindfulness
  among the processes CBT has absorbed and warns that "few processes are
  always positive regardless of context". Lindahl et al.'s taxonomy of
  meditation-related difficulties is an instance of the moderation that
  warning calls for, though the paper does not discuss it.

## Limitations

- **A position paper.** Its evidence for the field's direction is a
  selection of cited studies, and its trends are predictions.
- **It names few processes concretely.** Flexibility, defusion, reappraisal
  and the alliance appear as examples; the hierarchy of processes is
  explicitly left to future research.
- **The mediator problem is acknowledged, not solved.** The paper grants
  that statistical mediation is not mechanism, then makes mediation the
  admission test. Its answer is the laboratory component study and
  idiographic measurement, both described rather than shown.
- **Interest.** The authors disclose book royalties and advisory work. The
  programme also favours models with a "tight link" to processes, and the
  paper names ACT, Hayes's own therapy, as a possible example.

## Open questions

- What are the core processes, how many are there, and how do they nest?
- Can moderated mediation in fact recover a nosology, and would it agree
  with network clusters ([LIT-547](../literature.d/LIT-547.md)) or with the single mechanism of
  [LIT-538](../literature.d/LIT-538.md)?
- Is psychological flexibility, as ACT measures it, the same construct as
  the meaning-making flexibility of [LIT-538](../literature.d/LIT-538.md)?
