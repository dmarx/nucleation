### Summary
Tacit bargaining is bargaining where communication is incomplete or impossible. Schelling studies it first in the pure-coordination case: the lost couple in a store, parachutists with maps, and questionnaire puzzles such as heads or tails, numbers, meeting in New York, and dividing $100. People coordinate far better than chance by converging on a focal point, whose marks are prominence and uniqueness, relative to who the parties are. Mixed-motive versions (biased heads/tails, letter orderings, X/blank, troop lines on the map, tax shares, a mediator's split) show that the party disfavoured by the focal point usually accepts it, because no other choice can coordinate expectations. He then argues that explicit bargaining also needs convergent expectations ("If not here, where?"), so round numbers, splitting the difference, precedent, mediators, the status quo and natural boundaries settle outcomes. A fair result's moral force is partly its power to focus expectations. For limited war (no gas in WWII, the 38th parallel, the Formosa Straits, atomic versus non-atomic), limits must be qualitatively distinct, and unilateral prior proposals can serve as focal points.

### Contribution
It introduces the focal point (later "Schelling point"), with experimental questionnaire evidence that tacit coordination of expectations works. It extends the idea to explain the determinacy of explicit bargaining and the shape of tacit limits in war. This is the analysis of coordination by salience that Lewis's Convention (LIT-473) builds on.

### Summary
To be a rational animal is just to have propositional attitudes, and these come only as a holistic set: one belief requires a dense network of mostly true beliefs, and so a largely correct logic. Against Malcolm's dog "barking up the wrong tree", Davidson argues that without speech there is no way to fix the semantic opacity of a creature's supposed belief or the general beliefs it would need. Our success in explaining animals by attributing attitudes does not show they have them (the heat-seeking missile). His two-step argument is as follows. (1) To have a belief one must have the concept of belief: surprise, which any believer must be liable to, requires a belief about one's own belief. (2) The concept of belief is the concept of objective truth, and the subjective-objective contrast comes only from the concept of intersubjective truth, through communication. He concedes that he cannot show this last step and offers an analogy instead: objectivity is a triangulation between two creatures and a shared object, whose baseline is language. Conclusion: rationality is a social trait, and only communicators have it.

### Contribution
It gives an explicit argument for the thesis that thought requires language, which he notes has been widely asserted but little argued. The core is the surprise argument from belief to the concept of belief, together with the first published statement of triangulation as the source of the concept of objectivity.

### Key results (Dialectica page)
1. Rationality is having propositional attitudes, p. 318: "to be a rational animal is just to have propositional attitudes"
2. Holism, p. 318: "the intrinsically holistic character of the propositional attitudes makes the distinction between having any and having none dramatic."
3. Irrationality presupposes rationality, p. 321: "Irrationality is not mere lack of reason, but a disease or perturbation of reason."
4. The thesis, p. 322: "My thesis is rather that a creature cannot have a thought unless it has language."
5. Attribution can be justified and still be wrong (the missile), p. 323: "This uninformed observer might be justified in attributing a desire and beliefs to the missile; but he would be wrong."
6. Step one, p. 324: "First, I argue that in order to have a belief, it is necessary to have the concept of belief."
7. Step two, p. 324: "Secondly, I argue that in order to have the concept of belief one must have language."
8. Surprise, p. 326: "Surprise requires that I be aware of a contrast between what I did believe and what I"
9. Belief and objective truth, p. 326: "To have the concept of belief is therefore to have the concept of objective truth."
10. Learning and discrimination are not enough, p. 326: "Yet none of this, no matter how successful by my standards, shows that the creature commands the sub-" (quote stops at the break)
11. Admitted gap, p. 327: "I confess I do not know how to show this."
12. Triangulation, p. 327: "Our sense of objectivity is the consequence of another sort of triangulation, one that requires two creatures."
13. Conclusion, p. 327: "The conclusion of these considerations is that rationality is a social trait."
14. The moral question is set aside, p. 319, fn. 1: "I see no reason to be less kind to those without thoughts or language than to those with; on the contrary."

### Assumptions
- Semantic opacity is the mark of attitude attribution.
- An observer can in favourable circumstances tell what others think (an observability assumption, which he says is neither verificationism nor behaviourism).
- Every believer is liable to surprise of the belief-about-belief kind.
- Holism: no fixed list of beliefs, but much true belief is required.

### Concepts
Rational animal; propositional attitude; holism of the mental; semantic opacity; de re attribution; the concept of belief; surprise; subjective-objective contrast; intersubjective truth; triangulation; "dumb" (= incapable of interpreting or engaging in linguistic communication).

### Bearings
- LIT-440 and THEORY-596 (Dennett, Intentional Systems: to ascribe is to predict assuming rationality): contrast. Davidson grants the predictive success of attributing attitudes to animals and still denies that they have them (the missile, pp. 323-324). Dennett makes successful prediction sufficient.
- LIT-398 and THEORY-538 (Dennett, True Believers): contrast, as above.
- THEORY-616 (the bearer of an LM's attitudes is the instance, on the intentional-strategy criterion): qualifies. On Davidson's criterion the question becomes whether the system interprets others' speech and commands intersubjective truth, not predictive adequacy.
- THEORY-646 and LIT-112 (realism about folk psychology): background.
- THEORY-495 and LIT-125 (Tomasello): contrast/parallel. Both locate the human difference in sociality. Davidson denies non-linguistic creatures any beliefs ("rationality is a social trait. Only communicators have it", p. 327). Tomasello attributes intentional states and individual rationality to apes and adds shared intentionality on top. Davidson's triangulation (two creatures and one object) is a structural parallel to joint attention, presumably why Tomasello cites it.
- THEORY-151 (objectivity belongs to a community's critical practices, Longino): parallel. Objectivity is grounded intersubjectively, though at the level of science, not of belief.
- THEORY-089 and LIT-648 (Block, two-factor content): background, on content and holism.

### Limitations
The scan is clean apart from OCR spacing. The argument is explicitly incomplete: Davidson concedes he cannot show that the subjective-objective contrast requires intersubjective truth (p. 327), and he presents the steps as an "argument" in scare quotes that "can be challenged" (p. 324). There is no empirical content; he says the question "is not empirical" (abstract, p. 317).

### Candidate THEORY
Sentence: Davidson argues that a creature has beliefs, and so any propositional attitude and any rationality, only if it has the concept of belief, which liability to surprise shows every believer must have, and that this concept, being the concept of objective truth, comes only from triangulating with another creature in linguistic communication, so that only communicators are rational animals.
promote_when: A published argument supplies the step Davidson concedes he cannot show, that the subjective-objective contrast can be had only through the concept of intersubjective truth gained in communication. That argument must be defended in the literature against the objection that surprise needs only a revised first-order belief.

### How far the stand-ins stand in
A is Keller's own popular restatement of the book's whole theory: phenomena of the third kind, micro and macro levels, maxims, the footpath model, and the Ferguson definition. B is verbatim text from the German original of the book on the three-stage invisible-hand explanation and the pejoration case. Together they cover the book's central account (Part Two, ch. 4) in German. They do not cover Part One's history of the problem (conjectural history and the natural-versus-artefact dichotomies, which A touches briefly at S. 7-8), ch. 5 "Discussion" (on evolution and teleology), or the English wording and page numbers.

### Summary (from A and B)
Language change is everywhere, and contemporaries perceive it as decay, because every change of a convention begins as a violation of it, a "mistake", that stops being a mistake once it is general (A, S. 4). Changes in the world are neither necessary nor sufficient for language change (S. 7). Nineteenth-century linguistics failed because it was trapped in the dichotomy of natural organism versus human artefact (S. 8). Between these lies a third kind, illustrated by the campus network of footpaths: made by people, but neither intentionally nor knowingly. Languages are "phenomena of the third kind", following Ferguson's "the result of human action, but not the execution of any human design" (S. 9). Explaining one requires separating the micro level of individual action, aimed at local goals, from the macro level of structure it produces. Only systematic deviations leave traces, and the system comes from maxims that speakers follow (save energy, be understood, be noticed, be polite) (S. 9-10). Examples are the [ham] reduction, the short half-life of youth slang, and the pejoration of words for women (wîp, frowe, Frauenzimmer, Dame) through the galanterie maxim "choose one storey too high" (S. 10-11; B). The book's invisible-hand explanation has three stages: the motives that produce the acts, the cumulative process, and the resulting structure. It explains its explanandum as the causal consequence of individual intentional actions serving at least partly similar intentions (B). Such explanations give structural, not specific, predictions (B, p. 2).

### Contribution
It brings invisible-hand explanation (Ferguson, Smith, Mandeville, Menger) to historical linguistics as the proper form of explanation for language change. A language is analysed as a phenomenon of the third kind, the unintended macro-level result of micro-level communicative acts guided by maxims.

### Assumptions
- Speakers act intentionally for local communicative goals and do not aim at the language.
- Maxims are shared by many speakers, which makes their deviations similar.
- A language is a system of conventions.
- The ideal-typical three-stage model is adequate to explain cultural phenomena (custom, morality, markets, inflation).

### Concepts
Phenomenon of the third kind; invisible-hand explanation (three stages: motives, process, structure); micro and macro levels; maxims (energy saving, being understood, being noticed, politeness or galanterie); systematic versus random violation; pejoration as inflation; structural prediction; Mandevillean paradox.

### Bearings
- LIT-473 (Lewis, Convention): background. Keller treats a language as a system of conventions, and change as convention breach becoming general. Lewis supplies the account of convention, and Keller the dynamics of its change.
- THEORY-414 and LIT-472 (Guala, rules in equilibrium): parallel. Both explain an institution as an undesigned equilibrium of individual choices.
- THEORY-561 (Floridi, distributed morality, whose definition invokes an "invisible hand", LIT-901) and THEORY-207 (responsibility for outcomes emerging from neutral interactions, LIT-899): parallel. A macro outcome is produced by micro acts that do not aim at it.
- LIT-771 (Kirby et al., cumulative cultural evolution of language in the lab): supports. It gives experimental evidence that linguistic structure arises without design through use and transmission, which is the kind of test Keller's account invites.
- LIT-141 (Emergence) and LIT-424 (Dennett, From Bacteria to Bach, Unavailable): background.
- QUESTION-009 (when does coordination constitute an additional agent?): contrast case. An invisible-hand structure is coordinated and adaptive (in translation: the footpath network is more intelligent than the paved paths the architects planned, A S. 8) but no agent.
- Tomasello codes (THEORY-495 and LIT-125): background only. Keller supplies the cultural-evolutionary, undesigned route by which conventions, linguistic ones included, arise and change, which Tomasello's account of conventional communication presupposes.
