# NOTE-tmpb1ult: Petkova and Ehrsson — If I were you: perceptual illusion of body swapping

## Contribution

Before this paper the ownership illusion was a limb illusion: the rubber
hand, judged by questionnaire, proprioceptive drift or a response to
threat. This paper extends it to a whole body, by putting the participant's
eyes, through cameras and head-mounted displays, where a mannequin's eyes
are. It adds a physiological measure for the whole body (skin conductance
to a knife), shows the effect spreading from a stroked part to an
unstroked one, and runs a swap with a living person whom the participant
faces and can see.

## Key insight

Ownership of a body follows the viewpoint and the correlations, not the
knowledge of which body is mine. Look down from a body's eyes, feel what
it is seen to feel, and the body seen is felt as one's own, even while
one's real body is in view.

## Assumptions

- **Threat-evoked skin conductance indexes ownership.** "There is a
  direct linear relationship between the strength of ownership of a hand
  and the degree of anxiety experienced when the hand is being subjected
  to physical threats [25]" (p. 6). Taken from the authors' earlier
  rubber-hand fMRI work, not tested here.
- **Synchronous minus asynchronous isolates ownership.** Asynchronous
  stroking matched for number and length of strokes is the control for
  attention, salience and arousal; a spoon controls for an approaching
  object; a box controls for associative learning (Exp. 4).
- **Control statements catch suggestibility.** Four of the seven
  statements (S4–S7) "served as controls for suggestibility and
  task-compliance" (p. 2).
- **Perception settles on one body.** The discussion leans on "the
  perceptual systems tend to produce single resolutions when resolving
  sensory conflicts" and on priors from "a lifetime of experiences of
  seeing the world from the first person perspective" (pp. 6–7).

## Key results

- **Experiment 1 (n = 32, 16 women).** HMDs fed by two cameras at a male
  mannequin's eyes; participants tilt their heads down and see its body
  where theirs should be. A rod strokes both abdomens for two minutes,
  synchronously or asynchronously; then a seven-statement questionnaire,
  −3 to +3. Read from Fig. 2: synchronous S1 "I seemed to feel the touch
  given to the mannequin" ≈ +1.6, S2 (touch caused by the stick on the
  mannequin) ≈ +2.4, S3 "It felt like the mannequin's body was my body"
  ≈ +1.3; asynchronous ≈ −1.0, −0.2, −0.55. Reported: synchronous
  F(1, 223) = 125.4, "p = .000"; asynchronous F(1, 223) = 1.513,
  "p = .703"; illusion statements between conditions F(1, 95) = 107.5.
  *Checked:* F(1, 223) = 1.513 gives p = 0.22, not .703 (both
  non-significant). The denominator degrees of freedom fit 224 = 32 × 7
  ratings and 96 = 32 × 3, so the ratings of one participant are treated
  as independent observations. The paper does not say what the
  within-condition F compares; illusion against control statements is
  the reading that fits.
- **The control statements move too (Fig. 2, by eye).** Synchronous
  against asynchronous: S4 "I felt naked" ≈ −0.45 against −1.45; S6 "It
  felt as if my body had turned into a plastic body" ≈ +0.15 against
  −0.75; S7 (the mannequin came to resemble me) ≈ −1.0 against −1.45; S5
  "two bodies" ≈ −1.1 against +0.05. Three of four controls rise under
  synchronous stroking, by up to a scale point. No test of the controls
  between conditions is reported.
- **Experiment 2 (n = 10, 2 women).** After a minute of stroking, a knife
  is slid into a hidden cleft in the mannequin's abdomen so that it looks
  as if it cuts; a same-sized spoon is the neutral object. Three sessions
  of four one-minute periods. Synchronous-knife SCR exceeded
  asynchronous-knife (Z = −2.599, p = .009) and synchronous-spoon
  (Z = −2.191, p = .028); knife and spoon did not differ after
  asynchronous stroking (p = .484). *Checked:* all three match. The text
  says Wilcoxon; Fig. 3's caption says "two-tailed t-test".
- **Experiment 3 (n = 13).** Stroking the hands, or the abdomen, in time
  raised the response to a knife at the abdomen over the matching
  asynchronous condition (Z = −2.511, p = .011; Z = −2.132, p = .033).
  *Checked:* Z = −2.511 gives p = 0.012 by the normal approximation; an
  exact test may give .011. Hands and abdomen were not compared with each
  other, so "generalised" means each produced an effect, not that they
  produced the same one.
- **Experiment 4 (n = 12).** A dark green box of the mannequin's height
  and width, stroked and threatened at the same height. Synchronous
  mannequin over synchronous box, p = .008; synchronous over asynchronous
  for the mannequin, p = .04, and not for the box, p = .819 (t-tests, no
  statistics given). No Object × Synchrony interaction is reported. Read
  from Fig. 5: mannequin ≈ 1.0 and 0.66 (axis labelled mmho), box ≈ 0.42 and 0.39, so
  the box draws less response than the mannequin even after asynchronous
  stroking. Part of the "only a human-like body" result is a difference
  between threatening a body and threatening a box, whatever the
  stroking. The knife threat to the mannequin was changed for this
  experiment to touch without appearing to cut, to match the box.
- **Experiment 5 (n = 20, 13 women).** Cameras on the experimenter's
  helmet; the participant stands opposite, sees the scene from the
  experimenter's eyes, including their own body from shoulders to knees,
  and the two squeeze right hands for two minutes, to a metronome, in time
  or in a "semi-random" alternation. A knife passes over one wrist or the
  other. Illusion condition: experimenter's wrist over own wrist,
  Z = −3.099, reported p = .0002; experimenter's wrist, synchronous over
  asynchronous, Z = −2.091, p = .037; asynchronous, own over
  experimenter's, n.s. (p = .737); Hand × Condition F(1, 19) = 17.08,
  p = .001. *Checked:* Z = −3.099 gives p = 0.0019, so ".0002" is a slip
  for .002. Fig. 7's bars carry p = .002 on the within-illusion
  comparison and p = .037 on the experimenter's hand across conditions,
  as the text has it; the caption's prose assigns them the other way
  round. F(1, 19) = 17.08 gives p = 0.0006. Read from Fig. 7: illusion
  ≈ 0.95 (experimenter's hand) and 0.68 (own); control ≈ 0.74 and 0.76.
  Own-hand response in the illusion condition is not lower than in the
  control by any test reported.
- **Experiment 5's subjective evidence** is pilot interviews with ten
  other people, six more with the cameras tilted down, and spontaneous
  remarks ("I was shaking hands with myself!"). Experiment 5 has no
  questionnaire. "Several of the participants" reported a weaker illusion
  in the asynchronous condition (p. 5), so asynchrony weakened it without
  abolishing it.
- **Gender.** No difference between women and men in Experiment 1
  ratings (F(1, 223) = .257, p = .613, again on ratings) or in pooled SCR
  from Experiments 3–4 (F = .004, p = .952). *Checked:* both match their
  statistics.
- **Methods.** Separate naive groups per experiment. SCR is peak-to-trough
  within 5 s of threat onset, averaged including zero responses.
  "Null responders" (zero response on more than two-thirds of trials)
  were excluded; how many is not said. Each condition has a handful of
  threats per person (Exp. 2: three sessions of four conditions).
  Parametric or non-parametric tests were chosen per experiment by a
  Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Stroking and threats were by hand; no blinding
  of the experimenter is mentioned.

## Claims

| C1 | Synchronous visuotactile stimulation of a mannequin seen from its own eye position produces reported ownership of its body | moderate | Exp. 1, n = 32; large effect on S1–S3, but ratings analysed as independent and controls drift with condition |
| C2 | Threat to the mannequin after synchronous stroking raises skin conductance over asynchronous and neutral-object controls | moderate | Exp. 2, n = 10, checked; replicated as a synchronous–asynchronous effect in Exps. 3 and 4 |

| C4 | Only a human-shaped object can be owned | weak | Exp. 4, n = 12; no interaction test, and the box draws less response under both stroking conditions |

## Concepts

- **Body swap illusion** — the perceptual experience of owning another
  body, a mannequin's or a person's, induced by a first-person view from
  that body with correlated touch or movement. Not a swap of identity.
- **Whole-body ownership** — ownership of the entire seen body produced
  by correlated stimulation of one part (p. 6).
- **Null responder** — a participant with no threat-evoked SCR on more
  than two-thirds of trials; excluded.
- **Cognitive impenetrability** — the paper's test of a "genuine
  perceptual illusion": it should survive seeing one's own body (p. 4).

## Connections

It extends the rubber hand illusion (Botvinick and Cohen 1998, which
Reader C files in this batch) and Ehrsson's own threat and fMRI work on it
(Ehrsson, Spence and Passingham 2004, filed by Reader D; Ehrsson et al.
2007), and the 2007 out-of-body illusions it cites: Ehrsson's and
Lenggenhager, Tadi, Metzinger and Blanke's "Video ergo sum" (Reader A).
Its first-person mannequin and Lenggenhager et al.'s body seen from
behind are the two designs the later full-body literature compares.
Kilteni et al. 2015 ([NOTE-tmpy8fky](NOTE-tmpy8fky.md)) review it as the founding full-body
ownership illusion and report the later test it lacks: third-person views
of a mannequin or virtual body abolish the illusion at 40 to 100 cm
(Petkova et al. 2011b, Slater et al. 2010, Maselli and Slater 2013, 2014;
cited there, not read here). Its discussion invokes Bayesian priors and
cites Körding et al. 2007 on causal inference, which Kilteni et al. build
into a model. It cites Metzinger's *Being No One* in its first paragraph
and nowhere after.

## Bearing on the record

- **[NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md), C6** ("Body ownership can be manipulated by multisensory
  conflict and is the simplest form of self-awareness"). For the first
  half this paper is better evidence than the sources C6 cites: it
  manipulates ownership of a whole body with a physiological measure and
  controls. But it qualifies how. What moves ownership here is
  multisensory *agreement* from a body's own viewpoint, not conflict
  alone, and the paper claims, and the later literature finds, that a
  body seen from outside, as in Lenggenhager et al., does not support
  ownership in the same way. So C6's citation of Lenggenhager et al. for
  ownership mixes two phenomena that Kilteni et al. classify apart:
  ownership of a body seen as one's own, and drift of self-location
  toward a body seen from behind. The paper says nothing on "simplest
  form of self-awareness". My reading.
- **Metzinger ([LIT-450](../literature.d/LIT-450.md), [LIT-568](../literature.d/LIT-568.md)).** The swap survives the participant
  seeing and shaking their own hand. That fits a transparent self-model
  as [NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md) reports it: knowing which body is mine does not undo the
  experience of owning the other one: the content of the bodily
  self-model is set by current correlations, not by self-knowledge. It
  does not answer [NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md)'s open question, how to make a self-model
  opaque. Whether that shows transparency or just a perceptual
  module's encapsulation, the paper cannot say. The pairing is mine; the
  paper does not use the word.
- **[NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md)'s "centre of awareness".** The paper's last line says the
  illusion moves "a human centre of awareness from one body to another".
  It measured ownership, not self-location; read with the self-location
  measures of Lenggenhager et al. (Reader A), the claim should be held as
  untested here.
- **Active inference ([LIT-1181](../literature.d/LIT-1181.md), [NOTE-960](NOTE-960.md)).** Little. The paper's account
  is priors from a lifetime of first-person viewing plus multisensory
  integration in egocentric frames. Experiment 5 has active squeezing,
  but the visuomotor correlation is not manipulated apart from the
  touch. The persistence of a weaker illusion under asynchrony is the
  kind of result a precision-weighted account would predict; the paper
  does not test it.
- **Demand characteristics (Lush 2020, Reader C).** The paper's guard is
  four control statements, three of which rise under synchronous
  stroking. Its stronger guard is skin conductance, but a participant can
  tell synchronous from asynchronous stroking and knows a knife is coming,
  so SCR differences are not immune to expectancy. Nothing here tests
  that.
- **Apps and Tsakiris (Reader D).** Its "priors" paragraph is the
  informal version of what a predictive account of self-recognition
  would formalise.
- **[NOTE-325](NOTE-325.md) (phantom pain as body-schema prior).** Opposite direction:
  that note has a prior that persists against the senses; this paper has
  a body representation re-assigned within two minutes by the senses.
  Both can be true for different components of the body model; the
  record should not read either as the whole of it.
- Filed as [THEORY-tmpvh682](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpvh682.md).
- **For the Anthology of the SOTA:** no instruction for ML practice. The
  discussion's tele-robotics and virtual-reality "applications" are
  speculative and not about learning systems.

## Limitations

- Small, separate samples (10, 13, 12, 20; 32 for the questionnaire), each
  experiment run once.
- The questionnaire is analysed with each participant's ratings as
  independent observations, and is used only in Experiment 1.
- Two p-values do not match their statistics (.703 for F = 1.513; .0002
  for Z = −3.099), and Fig. 3's and Fig. 7's captions disagree with the
  text on the test or on which comparison is which.
- Excluded null responders are not counted.
- No third-person condition, no self-location measure, no proprioceptive
  drift.
- Manual stroking and threats by an experimenter who knew the condition.
- The shape result lacks an interaction test.

## Open questions

- Does the swap's SCR effect survive a control for expectancy, such as
  asking a separate group what response each condition is meant to
  produce (Lush's method) and matching it?
- With a first-person view held fixed, how much of the illusion is
  visuotactile synchrony and how much the seen body's position? Kilteni
  et al. report that a spatially coincident realistic virtual body is
  owned even under asynchronous touch.
- What did self-location do in the swap? A walking or pointing measure of
  felt location would test the "centre of awareness" claim directly.
