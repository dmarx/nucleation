# THEORY-tmp6tk5g: Praiseworthiness is compatible with being psychologically determined by good reasons, but blameworthiness requires that the agent could have done otherwise, that is, could have acted on the right reasons

## The evidence is second-hand

**The primary texts are unread.** No lawful copy of "Asymmetrical Freedom"
or of *Freedom Within Reason* could be found (see their status notes). The
asymmetry is reported here through McKenna and Coates's SEP entry, the only
read source the record holds that states it. Nothing is quoted from Wolf.
The owner authorised filing it on this evidence on 2026-10-03.

## Source

- Wolf, "Asymmetrical Freedom" (1980), [LIT-tmpbc48b](../literature.d/LIT-tmpbc48b.md), Deferred, unread: the
  article in which, by the SEP's report, the asymmetry is first argued.
- Wolf, *Freedom Within Reason* (1990), [LIT-tmp2u6df](../literature.d/LIT-tmp2u6df.md), Deferred, unread:
  pp. 79–81 for the asymmetry and chapter 5 for the ability it needs.
- McKenna and Coates, SEP *Compatibilism*, [LIT-291](../literature.d/LIT-291.md), §4.3, read in [NOTE-270](../notes.d/NOTE-270.md).

## What the source reports

McKenna and Coates ([LIT-291](../literature.d/LIT-291.md), §4.3) report that Wolf "develops a surprising
asymmetry thesis according to which praiseworthy conduct does not require
the freedom to do otherwise but blameworthy behavior does", citing
"Asymmetrical Freedom" ([LIT-tmpbc48b](../literature.d/LIT-tmpbc48b.md)) and *Freedom Within Reason*
([LIT-tmp2u6df](../literature.d/LIT-tmp2u6df.md), pp. 79–81). They restate it in Fischer's vocabulary: "only
blameworthy conduct requires regulative control. Guidance control is
sufficient for praiseworthy conduct."

**Praise.** By their report, if an agent acts in accord with the True and
the Good and is "so psychologically determined that she cannot but act in
accord with" it, her inability to do otherwise does not threaten the
freedom responsibility needs: "how could her freedom be in any way enhanced
simply by adding an ability to act irrationally?"

**Blame.** If she acts against the True and the Good and "is so
psychologically determined that she cannot act in accord with it, then,
being unable to act as reason requires, it would be unreasonable to blame
her". So the "otherwise" blame needs is specific: the ability to have
acted on the right reasons, not an ability to do anything at all.

**Determinism.** Because blame needs that ability, the view is exposed to
incompatibilist arguments. They report that Wolf answers by arguing that
physical determination does not entail psychological determination, and
that the ability blame needs requires only the falsity of psychological
determinism, a thesis she takes to have no support. Their gloss: she is "a
compatibilist about blameworthiness and physical determinism" and an
incompatibilist about blameworthiness and psychological determinism.

Talbert's SEP *Moral Responsibility* ([LIT-tmppa1o1](../literature.d/LIT-tmppa1o1.md), preamble) confirms only
that Wolf (1980, 1990), with Nelkin, questions the assumption that praise
and blame need the same capacities. It does not state the asymmetry, so it
is not listed as a source.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say blame requires indeterminism.** On the report, the
  ability blame needs is compatible with physical determinism and
  incompatible only with psychological determinism.
- **It does not restore PAP in general.** The principle of alternative
  possibilities holds for blame and fails for praise. Robb's PAP map
  ([LIT-294](../literature.d/LIT-294.md), [NOTE-269](../notes.d/NOTE-269.md)) reports that the moral arguments for PAP (fairness,
  "ought implies can", the W-defense) cover blame at most, and the
  asymmetry has exactly that shape.
- **It does not yet answer Frankfurt cases for blame.** In a Frankfurt-style
  case of wrongdoing, an agent does wrong on her own while an idle
  intervener ensures she could not have done otherwise. Fischer and
  Ravizza's account ([THEORY-tmpmjijs](THEORY-tmpmjijs.md)) rests on the judgement that such an
  agent is blameworthy. None of the record's sources reports Wolf's answer.
  This is the open point of the rivalry, and the `promote_when` asks for it.
- **It does not say who is praiseworthy.** It states a condition on the
  freedom praise and blame need, not their other conditions.
- **It does not escape manipulation.** An agent manipulated into acting
  well, who cannot do otherwise, is praiseworthy on this view as reported.
  [LIT-tmpbc48b](../literature.d/LIT-tmpbc48b.md) draws this out as my inference from the report. It is
  [THEORY-040](THEORY-040.md)'s forced choice taken on the bullet-biting side for praise.

## Relations

- **`extends` [THEORY-tmpp1sl4](THEORY-tmpp1sl4.md) (the Reason View).** The asymmetry is the
  Reason View's competence condition applied to praise and to blame
  separately. McKenna and Coates ([LIT-291](../literature.d/LIT-291.md), §4.3) present it as how Wolf
  makes free will "track moral reasons": what responsibility needs is the
  ability to act on the True and the Good. An agent who does act on it has
  that ability whatever else she can do. An agent who does not has it only
  if she could have acted otherwise. That builds on the same condition and
  adds what it requires case by case, so it is a refinement, not a rival.
- **`rivals` [THEORY-tmpmjijs](THEORY-tmpmjijs.md) (Fischer and Ravizza).** The two accounts agree
  that praise needs no alternatives. They cannot both be right about blame.
  This account says blame requires that the agent could have acted on the
  right reasons, which is regulative control in Fischer's sense, as
  McKenna and Coates put it. Fischer and Ravizza hold that responsibility
  for actions, omissions and consequences requires only guidance control.
  The opening of their concluding chapter says so, as [LIT-tmp1m893](../literature.d/LIT-tmp1m893.md)
  records, and Fischer's own summary does too ("Stories and the Meaning of
  Life", [LIT-tmpgaaah](../literature.d/LIT-tmpgaaah.md), §I, read). So their account counts as blameworthy
  some agents this one excuses, the Frankfurt-style wrongdoer above being
  the clear case: she acts from her own mechanism with the intervener idle, so she
  has guidance control, and she could not have acted otherwise. Where the
  inability is due only to physical determinism the two may agree, since
  Wolf holds that it leaves the psychological ability intact. Neither account corrects the other in the
  record's sources, so the relation is `rivals`. If a reading shows how
  Wolf handles the Frankfurt-style wrongdoer, it should become `corrects`
  on whichever side that settles.

- **Rivals [THEORY-tmpejn3z](THEORY-tmpejn3z.md)**, the claim that the PAP is false. Frankfurt's
  1969 case ([LIT-tmpwts2i](../literature.d/LIT-tmpwts2i.md), pp. 835–837) is stated for blame as well as for
  praise: the agent who could not have done otherwise is blameworthy. The
  asymmetry denies exactly that.
