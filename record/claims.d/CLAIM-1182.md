# CLAIM-1182: Kinship designs cannot split DNA from marks

## The claim

Let a child's outcome depend on what the gamete carries from its
biological parent: DNA, G, and any epigenetic mark the parent's exposure
left in the germline, E. It also depends on what the carrying mother
supplies (the womb, W) and what the rearing family supplies (R). A
donor-gamete or adoption design compares pairs in which the parent whose
exposure is measured is the biological parent with pairs in which that
parent is not.

- In related pairs, the measured parent supplies G and E, and W and R
  where they carried or raised the child.
- In unrelated pairs, the measured parent supplies only W and R. G and E
  come from a donor or birth parent whose exposure is not the one
  measured.

The related–unrelated contrast therefore estimates the part of the
association carried by G and E together. Nothing in the contrast varies E
while G is held fixed, because both are fixed by the same fact: whose
gamete it was. So the contrast can say "inherited", in the design's sense
(Rice et al.'s "maternally inherited factors", [THEORY-2297](../theory.d/THEORY-2297.md)), but not
"germline epigenetic" as against "genetic".

## The derivation

1. Within the design, G and E are both functions of the identity of the
   gamete's source, with E also a function of that source's exposure.
2. The design's contrast varies only whether the measured parent is that
   source (related) or not (unrelated).
3. So any difference between the contrasts is a function of the pair
   (G, E) jointly. A difference that came from E alone and one that came
   from G alone, correlated with the exposure (a genetic liability to be
   exposed, say), give the same pattern.
4. To separate them, E must vary while G is held. That happens when one
   person's gametes are formed both before and after the exposure.
   Siblings conceived before and after the exposure share their parent's
   genotype, and their own genotypes differ only by chance segregation,
   which is uncorrelated with the exposure.

Step 3 is the whole point. Rice et al. say as much from the other side:
in their design, epigenetic changes induced by the prenatal environment
"will emerge as environmental effects". A mark carried in the donor's egg
would emerge as inherited.

## Relation to what the record holds

- **[CLAIM-644](CLAIM-644.md)** says no design now in use separates the germline route.
  This claim gives one reason, for the design that comes closest.
- **Horsthemke** ([THEORY-1618](../theory.d/THEORY-1618.md)) holds that in humans genetic inheritance
  "should and can be excluded". That is by examining the DNA, a
  measurement this claim sets aside. The two are compatible: the
  comparison alone cannot divide G from E, and sequencing or gamete
  measurement may.
- The way out in step 4 is the within-family timing contrast that Costa
  et al. use for fathers (born before against after captivity;
  [NOTE-1255](../notes.d/NOTE-1255.md)). Their sons were raised by the father, so that design leaves
  rearing in. [CLAIM-1017](CLAIM-1017.md) sets out the combinations.

## What it does not say

- Not that donor-gamete designs are useless here. With an exposed donor
  and unexposed carrying and rearing families, they would show that the
  gamete carries the effect, which no human study of trauma has shown.
- Not that a germline epigenetic effect exists or not.
