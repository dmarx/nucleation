# NOTE-2330: Malik-Moraleda, Mahowald, Conway and Gibson 2023 — Concepts restructured during language contact

## Contribution

A measured case in which the terms of one language's colour field change
their extensions together when its speakers acquire a language with more
colour contrasts, and change them by restructuring native words rather
than borrowing. Before it, bilingual colour studies compared languages
that differ by one term (Greek and Japanese blues); this one compares a
language with few consensus terms and one with about eleven, and finds a
larger effect.

## Key results

- **Three outcomes distinguished (Introduction).** Tsimane' concepts may
  "remain unaffected", be remapped "by Tsimane' terms taking on new
  meanings", or be remapped "by borrowing Bolivian-Spanish terms". The
  result is the second: bilinguals "remapped Tsimane' concepts without
  importing Bolivian-Spanish terms into Tsimane'" (Abstract).
- **Consistency.** Average surprisal over the 84 chips: Tsimane'
  monolinguals 5.16, bilinguals in Tsimane' 4.42 (p < .001), bilinguals
  in Spanish 4.02, Spanish monolinguals 3.70.
- **Modal terms.** Monolinguals have five majority terms (jäinäs red,
  tsincus black, chames yellow, itsidyeisi purple, cafedyeisi brown);
  bilinguals in Tsimane' add shandyes (green) and yụshñus (blue).
  celeste (light blue) does not appear in bilinguals' Tsimane'.
- **Narrowing.** "the number of chips that are encompassed by each of the
  modal color terms is larger for the Tsimane' monolingual group compared
  to the Tsimane' bilingual group". Between monolinguals and bilinguals in
  Tsimane', "all color terms had different distributions, barring yụshñus
  (blue) and jäinäs (red)"; itsidyeisi/morado (purple) "was not different
  between any of the four groups".
- **Neighbour overlap.** KL divergence between neighbouring pairs rises
  from monolinguals to bilinguals: shandyes-yụshñus 0.10 against 1.18
  (Spanish monolinguals' verde-azul 10.65); yellow-brown 0.45 against
  1.39; red-purple 1.52 against 4.58. "the confusability between all
  neighboring color pairs diminishes from monolingual to bilingual
  Tsimane' speakers" (Discussion).
- **The confound, stated (Discussion).** All bilinguals lived in San Borja,
  "more industrialized than their Tsimane' communities", so "it might not
  be the exposure to the second language ... it might be the interactions
  in the culture".
- **A caution on Nafaanra (Discussion).** "it is plausible that
  bilingualism, instead of (or in addition to) a pressure for efficiency,
  led to an increase in color terms in the Nafraana" [sic].

## Assumptions

- The three groups differ in colour vocabulary because of bilingualism
  (or its cultural correlates), not because of prior differences between
  people who became bilingual and people who did not.
- Chip selection and naming in a light box measure the terms' extensions;
  P(c) is uniform in the surprisal.

## Concepts

- **Restructuring against borrowing**: native terms take new boundaries
  rather than foreign terms entering.
- **Average surprisal** S(c) = Σ_w P(w|c) log 1/P(c|w), the expected
  number of guesses for a chip from its name.
- **Neighbour overlap** as KL divergence between the chip distributions of
  two terms adjacent in colour space.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-159](../theory.d/THEORY-159.md), promote_when.** Partly of the kind asked for: the
  neighbouring terms' use is measured, and every studied term but one
  (purple) shifts, with neighbour overlap falling, when the field's
  contrasts change. Three things fall short. The inventory of Tsimane'
  words does not change; what changes is how many contrasts speakers draw
  with the same words, so this is a split of a shared category (the
  reverse of a merger), not an addition. There is no set of terms outside
  the colour field measured as a control; the control is a group of
  monolingual speakers. And the change may be driven from outside the
  language (Spanish boundaries, or a more colour-talking culture), while
  the account concerns relations "inside a language state". Bearing:
  evidence of the shape the account predicts, without its control.
- **[CLAIM-082](../claims.d/CLAIM-082.md).** The pattern is what the claim's value account predicts
  and the opposite of its defeat condition's second clause: no term's
  range stays fixed while its neighbours' contrasts change, except
  itsidyeisi, whose neighbours' overlap with it (red-purple) also fell.
  Its warrant is interpretive, so this is corroboration of the shape, not
  a test ([ARG-205](../arguments.d/ARG-205.md), Proposed).
- **[CLAIM-242](../claims.d/CLAIM-242.md).** A contrast only: the bilinguals' Tsimane' terms take
  contrasts from Spanish, which is something crossing between languages,
  but it crosses in one speaker, not between speakers' utterances; it says
  nothing about transport of an utterance's contrast with its
  alternatives. No relation written.
- **[THEORY-163](../theory.d/THEORY-163.md) (Zaslavsky et al. 2018).** Consistent background: the
  surprisal analysis is an efficiency measure of the same family.

## Limitations

- Cross-sectional groups; no individual is measured before and after.
- n = 30 bilinguals, not preregistered; the chi-square tests and full KL
  table are in the unread supplement.
- Colour only; no terms outside the field.
- A gap in its own argument: the Discussion calls the result "the
  color-word boundaries being borrowed in Tsimane' from Spanish", but the
  bilinguals' Tsimane' grue split falls between the two monolingual groups
  (1.18 against 0.10 and 10.65), so the data show partial convergence;
  how far the boundaries are Spanish's is not measured directly.
