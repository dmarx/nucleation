# THEORY-149: Users can govern a shared resource durably without either privatisation or central control, and the institutions that last are marked not by particular rules but by structural regularities: user-made and user-monitored rules, graduated sanctions, cheap local conflict resolution, recognised rights to self-organise, and nested layers of governance

## Source

Ostrom (2009), Nobel lecture, [LIT-719](../literature.d/LIT-719.md), read in [NOTE-563](../notes.d/NOTE-563.md). The
1990 book, [LIT-737](../literature.d/LIT-737.md), is unread.

## What was actually shown

Ostrom's lecture ([LIT-719](../literature.d/LIT-719.md)) reports results from several kinds of
evidence, and each of them could have gone the other way.

- **Self-governance happens and lasts.** Coded case studies found many
  user-governed common-pool resource systems that survived for long
  periods. That contradicts the prediction that users trapped in a commons
  dilemma cannot escape it without an outside authority or property
  rights.
- **Design principles, not rules.** The specific rules of successful
  systems "varied extensively across sites". What the long-surviving
  systems shared, and the failures lacked, were eight structural
  principles. In their updated form these are boundaries, local
  congruence, collective choice, monitoring of users and resource by those
  accountable to users, graduated sanctions, conflict resolution,
  recognised rights, and nested enterprises. Two thirds of over 100 later
  studies confirmed them.
- **Experiments.** Without communication, laboratory subjects overused the
  resource. With face-to-face talk they cooperated more. When they chose
  their own sanctioning system they reached 90% of the optimum. In a field
  experiment, a regulation imposed from outside with weak enforcement
  raised extraction compared with talk alone.
- **Field studies.** Farmer-governed irrigation in Nepal outperformed
  government systems on all three measures (229 systems). Across forests,
  local monitoring correlated with forest condition, and formal protected
  status did not.
- **Polycentricity.** In US metropolitan policing, areas with many
  independent producers had more efficient producers, and duplication was
  rare.

## What this does not say

- **The principles are not shown to cause survival.** They are regularities
  of survivors, and the lecture calls them "best practices" that users did
  not consciously apply.
- **Not that self-governance always works, or that polycentric is better.**
  The lecture's last lesson is "no panaceas". What matters is fit to the
  local system, adaptation of rules over time, and whether users find the
  arrangement legitimate and equitable.
- **Not shown for firms.** The evidence concerns users of a resource they
  share and depend on. A corporation governing itself, or employees
  governing a firm, is a different structure, and the principles' transfer
  to it is untested here.

## Connections

- **Self-governance of a will.** The owner's essay on organizational will
  cites Ostrom for distributed monitoring and authority. Principles 4 and 8
  are the specific support. The record holds no account linking these
  institutional principles to individual self-governance; none is declared.
- **Group agency ([THEORY-127](THEORY-127.md)).** List & Pettit ask when a group has
  attitudes of its own. Ostrom asks when a group's rules last. A commons
  regime can be durable without being a group agent, and the two accounts
  do not depend on each other.
