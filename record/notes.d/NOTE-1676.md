# NOTE-1676: Holl et al. — a call for practical spatially patterned forest restoration methods

## Contribution

A short review, by the senior author of Corbin & Holl (2012), of the
applied-nucleation literature since, and of what keeps the method from
practice. It states the mechanism as the authors now hold it, separates
nuclei of planted trees from inert faunal attractants, and gathers what is
known and unknown about minimum nucleus size, planted share and cost. It
recommends comparisons with natural regeneration and plantation at
realistic scale, monitoring outside the planted area, and reporting costs.

## Key insight

A planted tree is support that becomes the capacity: "once established,
trees attract dispersers and modify microsite conditions continuously",
whereas a perch only stands in for that attraction and then rots (p. 2).
But establishment is not enough; a nucleus must also spread, and whether
it does is "slow and highly unpredictable" and seldom measured.

## Assumptions

- **Seed sources and dispersers remain** in the landscape; the authors
  name their loss as a case where the method does not fit (p. 4).
- **Planted trees survive establishment**, which takes early weeding and
  protection from livestock and fire (pp. 2–3).
- **Recovery is judged across the whole site**, not only within nuclei
  (pp. 2–3).
- **Secondary evidence.** An opinion article: its figures are cited from
  Zahawi & Augspurger (2006), Holl et al. (2020) and unpublished data.

## Key results

1. **Mechanism (p. 2).** Initial clusters facilitate recovery by
   "attracting seed dispersing animals thereby enhancing seed dispersal,
   creating favorable conditions for seedling recruitment both within and
   at the edge of nuclei" and by "spreading over time". The definition
   cites Corbin & Holl (2012).
2. **Tree against perch (p. 2).** Perches and bat boxes "do not improve
   local conditions for seedling establishment and growth (Reid & Holl
   2013) and have short-term impacts (e.g. many bird perches decompose
   within a few years). In contrast, once established, trees attract
   dispersers and modify microsite conditions continuously."
3. **Spread (p. 2).** "key to the nucleation model of succession is that
   vegetation nuclei not only establish but also spread over time, which
   may be slow and highly unpredictable"; "few studies have monitored
   seedling establishment adjacent to actively restored patches to assess
   whether they increase in size".
4. **Two kinds of nucleation (p. 2).** Michaels et al. (2024) and Eppinga
   et al. (2023) distinguish "“analogy with nucleation” (i.e. nucleation
   that depends on outside inputs, such as seed dispersal)" from
   "“autocatalytic nucleation” (i.e. creating positive feedbacks for the
   establishment and growth of species within the patch)". The authors:
   "We contend that these mechanisms are not mutually exclusive, as
   recovery of all ecosystems depends on colonization by the many plant,
   animal, and microbial species that are not actively reintroduced".
5. **Minimum nucleus (p. 3), cited.** "a minimum nuclei size of
   approximately 64 m2 is needed to attract seed dispersing birds and
   shade out pasture grasses in tropical moist forests (Zahawi & Augspurger
   2006; Holl et al. 2020)", varying "with ecosystem type and disperser
   behavior".
6. **Minimum share (p. 3), unpublished.** Some Brazilian practitioners are
   "only planting 1–5% of the overall restored area, which is unlikely to
   catalyze forest recovery within a reasonable time frame".
7. **Cost, contested (pp. 3–4).** "One argument favoring applied
   nucleation is that it is cheaper for a given seedling spacing than
   fully planting a site"; but on flat, mechanized Brazilian ground "the
   cost of planting nuclei was 1.5–1.7 times greater than planting a
   similar area in strips" (unpublished), while manual planting on
   mountainous terrain favours nuclei. "costs are rarely reported".
8. **The unplanted matrix (p. 3).** Unplanted areas carry "a dense cover
   of invasive grasses, ferns, and other ruderal vegetation", raising "the
   risk of accidental or intentional fire" that "can set back forest
   recovery in both unplanted and planted areas".
9. **Scaffolding that pays and is removed (p. 4).** Brancalion et al.
   (2020): "harvesting eucalyptus after 4–5 years defrayed 44–75% of
   restoration implementation costs without inhibiting recovery in the
   native tree strips."
10. **Evidence base (pp. 1, 3).** The methods "can be effective in catalyzing
    forest recovery over the first decade or two in some systems", but
    non-tree methods have mostly been tested "in plot sizes less than 1–2
    m2".
11. **Where it does not fit (p. 4).** "when rapid carbon sequestration is
    the primary goal; where key seed dispersing fauna have been
    extirpated; where natural regeneration is dominated by invasive
    species".

## Concepts

- **applied nucleation**: planting or seeding patches ("nuclei", "tree
  islands", "woodland islets") to accelerate forest recovery, after the
  nucleation model of Yarranton & Morrison (1974).
- **analogy against autocatalytic nucleation**: nucleation fed by outside
  inputs against nucleation by positive feedback within the patch; the
  authors hold them not exclusive.
- **minimum nucleus size** and **planted share**: the area below which a
  nucleus, or a planting, does not catalyze recovery.
- **spatially patterned restoration**: nuclei, strips and other partial
  plantings (Shaw et al. 2020).
- **temporary income scaffolding**: an exotic crop interplanted and
  harvested once the native planting is established.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md)** (independent of the founder, never of the feed):
  qualifies its scope. Every colonist comes from outside the nucleus, as
  in "analogy with nucleation", but the supplier is the surrounding
  landscape, not the intervener; the planting can be withdrawn and the
  supply cannot (the method fails where dispersers are gone). This fits
  the claim's own split between founding and feeding.
- **[CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md)** (upkeep drawn in proportion to the intact state, with
  an ignition threshold): supports it in kind. A nucleus's draw on
  dispersers grows with its own state, and the cited ~64 m² minimum reads
  as an ignition threshold. No evidence on collapse or hysteresis.
- **[CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md)** (supply that does the work first lowers the capacity
  kept): the perch against the tree is close to its distinction. The
  perch does the attracting and decays, leaving nothing; the tree becomes
  the capacity. No evidence of crowding out or of dependence on duration.
- **[CLAIM-603](../claims.d/CLAIM-603.md)**: nothing directly; dependence on dispersers is
  mutualistic, which leans to the reciprocal case, but the paper does not
  argue it. [CLAIM-558](../claims.d/CLAIM-558.md): nothing.
- **[CLAIM-566](../claims.d/CLAIM-566.md)** (assemble, persist, regenerate): supports separating
  establishment from spread, and says spread is rarely measured.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md)**: a mechanism but no measure of independence; trees
  that facilitate "continuously" are the condition under which withdrawal
  could cost nothing.
- **[QUESTION-399](../questions.d/QUESTION-399.md)**: bears directly, through the analogy/autocatalytic
  split and the authors' rejoinder; minimum size, minimum share and spread
  are partial answers.
- **[TERM-117](../terms.d/TERM-117.md)** (productive nucleus): a ready instance, with natural
  regeneration as comparator and a minimum that "will vary with ecosystem
  type and disperser behavior".
- **[QUESTION-380](../questions.d/QUESTION-380.md)**: qualifies it; on the authors' account the effect is
  predicted from site and landscape, not from the planting's design alone.
- **[CLAIM-463](../claims.d/CLAIM-463.md)** (restoring conditions need not restore the capacity): the
  weedy, fire-prone matrix is the case where removing the pasture use does
  not by itself bring the forest back; the paper does not test it.
- Filed from it: [THEORY-1477](../theory.d/THEORY-1477.md).

## Limitations

- An opinion article of five pages; its key numbers are cited (64 m²) or
  unpublished (1–5% share, 1.5–1.7× cost).
- Table S1 was not read.
- Nothing on what happens after maintenance ends; it discusses only the
  early-years burden.

## Open questions

Whether nuclei spread, at what rate, and how large they must be in a given
system; settled by monitoring outside planted patches over decades, with
island size analysed as a factor.
