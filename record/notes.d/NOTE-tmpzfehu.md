# NOTE-tmpzfehu: Sales Practices Investigation Report

## Contribution

The company's own causal account of the Wells Fargo sales-practices
episode, written for its independent directors by outside counsel. It had
access to 35 million documents and 100 interviews, and it reviewed 1,341
internal investigation records of terminated employees (pp. 2–3, 35). It
answers where the harm came from, how the company explained it to itself
at the time, and why the people with authority did not act on what the
organization knew.

## Key insight

The report rejects the account that the company's leaders gave at the time,
in which a few bad employees caused the harm. In its place it puts a
system: unattainable sales goals, enforced by rankings, scorecards and fear
for one's job, sustained by a structure in which the business line ran
itself, "run it like you own it". The report's own words for the
leadership's error: "Effect was confused with cause" (p. 6).

## Assumptions

- **The investigator's position.** The board's independent directors
  commissioned it, and the board is one of its subjects. Shearman & Sterling
  was "determined to be independent of Wells Fargo" (p. 2).
- **The company's data.** FTI Consulting "relied on data provided to it by
  Wells Fargo" (p. 26, n. 8) and "did not independently test" the
  population of potentially unauthorized accounts (p. 26, n. 7).
- **Missing witnesses.** Tolstedt "declined, on advice of counsel, to be
  interviewed" (p. 8, n. 1). So did Paterson, the Community Bank's HR head
  (p. 80, n. 24), and Bacon, head of Corporate Security (p. 90, n. 27).
- **A non-random sample.** The 1,341 records were not statistically
  sampled, and the review "disproportionately focused on managers"
  (p. 36).

## Key results

- **Root cause.** "The root cause of sales practice failures was the
  distortion of the Community Bank’s sales culture and performance
  management system, which, when combined with aggressive sales
  management, created pressure on employees to sell unwanted or unneeded
  products to customers and, in some cases, to open unauthorized accounts"
  (Overview). None of the ingredients is condemned alone: "there is nothing
  necessarily pernicious about sales goals, a sales-oriented culture or a
  decentralized corporate structure", but they "coalesced and failed
  dramatically here" (p. 4).
- **Pressure, not pay.** "Employees who engaged in misconduct most
  frequently associated their behavior with sales pressure, rather than
  compensation incentives" (p. 7). Plans were "commonly referred to as
  50/50 plans", expected to be met by only half the regions (p. 19).
  Turnover ran at least 30% a year from 2011 to 2015 (p. 27).
- **Misconduct rose with the goals.** Sales-practice allegations rose from
  288 in Q2 2007 to 1,469 in Q4 2013, and terminations or resignations
  from 61 to 447, then fell after 2013 as goals were moderated (pp. 33–34).
  The rate at which new accounts were funded fell from about 90% in 2005 to
  below 80% in 2012 and exceeded 95% by 2016 (p. 6).
- **Scale.** About 5,300 employees were fired for sales practice violations
  between 2011 and March 2016 (p. 16). The CFPB order's population is
  "1,534,280 deposit accounts that may not have been authorized" (p. 26,
  n. 7). Only 1.7% of the unfunded accounts incurred fees that were not
  reversed (p. 43, n. 18). Removing all the suspect accounts moved the
  cross-sell metric by at most 0.04 in any quarter (p. 43, n. 19).
- **Little explicit direction.** Most terminated employees admitted the
  misconduct and blamed sales pressure. Branch managers "only rarely" said
  they were explicitly told to misbehave, and in nine of the 1,341 records a
  district manager was accused of explicitly directing it (p. 37). The
  report also documents regional leaders in Los Angeles, Florida and
  Arizona who applied extreme pressure and urged selling regardless of
  need (pp. 22–26).
- **Leadership's explanation.** "It was convenient instead to blame the
  problem of low quality and unauthorized accounts and other employee
  misconduct on individual wrongdoers and poor management in the field
  rather than on the Community Bank’s sales model" (p. 5). Stumpf's
  "reaction invariably was that a few bad employees were causing issues"
  (p. 10). In 2015 he wrote "This is not systemic" (p. 56). In May 2015 the
  Risk Committee was told that "the root cause was intentional employee
  misconduct, not systemic issues arising from sales goals or
  compensation" (p. 105).
- **Structure and control functions.** The Chief Risk Officer "did not have
  any line authority or directive power to enforce changes on the lines of
  business" (p. 60). The control functions took a "transactional" approach,
  case by case (p. 13). Corporate HR had "no coordinated effort … to track
  or size the sales practice issues" (p. 79). Audit rated the Community
  Bank's culture "Strong" in 2013 (p. 94). The term "customer harm" was "narrowly
  construed to mean only financial harm such as fees and penalties" (p. 14).
- **Board.** Sales practices were not reported to the board as a noteworthy
  risk until 2014 (p. 15). The board found its own conduct could have been
  "improved in three respects": earlier centralization of risk, more
  concrete plans demanded of management, and more pressure on Stumpf to
  replace Tolstedt (pp. 16–17).
- **Sanctions and remedies.** Product sales goals were eliminated from
  October 1, 2016 (p. 51), and incentives were rebuilt around customer
  service (pp. 8, 52). Risk and HR were centralized, 4,100 risk employees
  in 2016 and 1,100 more in 2017 (p. 12). Five Community Bank executives
  were fired for cause (pp. 8–9, Overview). The board recorded
  forfeitures and clawbacks of $19 million plus about $47.3 million in
  options for Tolstedt (p. 9), about $41 million plus a $28 million
  clawback for Stumpf (p. 10), and about $32 million for eight Operating
  Committee members (p. 14). In total that is "more than $180 million"
  (Overview).

## Signals and authority

The report traces, repeatedly, the path from the people who knew to the
people who could act:

- **Known early, not escalated.** In August 2004 Internal Investigations
  reported that "The incentive to cheat is based on the fear of losing their
  jobs for not meeting performance expectations" and recommended cutting
  or eliminating sales goals. "There is no evidence that the report and its
  recommendations were further escalated" (pp. 89–90).
- **Reaching the top without being acted on.** Stumpf "received numerous
  customer and employee complaints about sales practices and sales
  pressure, which he or his assistants referred to appropriate subordinates
  without further follow-up" (p. 55). Told that about 1% of Community Bank
  employees were fired each year, he "reacted positively" and does not
  appear to have "initiated any follow-up investigation or inquiry"
  (p. 55).
- **Dissent overruled.** Regional leaders' objections to the goals were
  viewed "as simply a cover-up for poor management" (p. 41). Tolstedt told
  Stevens "not to talk to Loughlin anymore and that she needed to 'toe the
  line'" (p. 65).
- **Reported, then filtered.** Bacon wrote in 2012 of "a concerning trend in
  the area of Sales Integrity – from the increase in EthicsLine reports, to
  the increase in executive complaint letters". Russ Anderson told him his
  reporting made the problem sound "so much worse than it is" (p. 50).
  Audit received the same email and "did not follow up" (p. 95). Before the
  May 2015 Risk Committee meeting, the 1% figure was deleted (p. 105).
  Internal Investigations had counted 1,229 and 1,293 terminations for 2013
  and 2014, and those numbers "went unmentioned" (p. 106).
- **Seen, but filed as minor.** In the A&E Committee's materials before
  2014, sales integrity matters sat with internal matters "viewed as having
  minimal, if any, customer impact" (pp. 99–100).
- **Supplied from outside.** The press, the Los Angeles City Attorney and
  the regulators forced the issue. The board "learned for the first time"
  of the 5,300 terminations through the September 2016 settlements
  (p. 16).
- **Retaliation not found, so far.** A "limited review completed to date"
  found no "pattern of retaliation" against employees who complained, and
  the review was ongoing (p. 87, n. 26).

## Concepts

- **sales practice (sales integrity) violation** — the report's umbrella
  for gaming the sales system, from false referral credits to opening
  accounts without consent, simulated funding and falsified records
  (pp. 35–36).
- **simulated funding** — moving money from one customer account to
  another, sometimes unauthorized, account so that it looked funded
  (p. 26).
- **Rolling Funding Rate** — the share of new accounts funded with more
  than a de minimis amount, the Community Bank's own quality metric
  (p. 21).
- **"run it like you own it"** — the decentralized model's mantra, giving
  lines of business control of their own risk and HR functions (p. 19).

## Bearing on the record

The report supports the narrower reading of the "psychopathic corporation"
thesis on several points. The harm is traced to the sales model and the
system that enforced it, not to bad character. The CEO and the head of the
Community Bank are faulted for clinging to that model and for misreading
it as a few bad apples. Within the company, the costs to customers were
narrowed to fees and seen case by case. The board learned the full scale
from regulators.

It also complicates the half of the reading on which nothing in the
corporation registers the costs it imposes. The company did register them, in its investigators' 2004 report,
its HR project of 2011–2012, its regional leaders' objections and its
EthicsLine data. What failed was the route from those signals to anyone
with authority, and the authority to act on them. That is [CLAIM-349](../claims.d/CLAIM-349.md)'s
separation of access, authority and action, documented in one firm. The
report does not attribute the harm to the pursuit of profit as such. The
leaders it faults were protecting a sales model and its growth figures
(p. 5), and the unauthorized accounts made little money (p. 43, nn.
18–19).

## Limitations

- **A self-report.** It is the company's board reporting on the company,
  the board included. Its findings on the board are mild. The same
  committee was weighing shareholders' derivative demands on the board
  (p. 2), and the report was filed with the SEC as proxy material.
- **No intent established.** It infers motive: Tolstedt and her risk
  officer "likely" kept information from the board "to give themselves
  freedom to address these issues on their own terms, rather than to
  encourage improper behavior" (p. 8). But Tolstedt was not interviewed.
- **Not a finding of no individual fault.** It names leaders, fires five
  for cause and claws back pay. Thousands of employees were fired, and the
  report does not say they were blameless.
- **Scale is the regulators'.** The account and termination counts come
  from the settlements and from company data, untested by FTI.
- **Retaliation review incomplete.** Its retaliation review was "ongoing"
  when the report was published (p. 87, n. 26).

## Open questions

Whether the outside investigations confirm this report's allocation between
system and individuals, such as the later regulatory and criminal actions
against named executives, none of which is read here. And
whether the company's later conduct bears out the remedies it lists.
