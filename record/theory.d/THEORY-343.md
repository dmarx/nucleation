# THEORY-343: In laboratory choice tasks, people offload cognition more as a task's internal demand rises, but the choice follows their evaluation of the demand rather than its benefit: they offload where it gives no gain and more when less confident at equal ability, and offloading can lower memory for what was offloaded

## Source

Risko & Gilbert (2016), [LIT-1059](../literature.d/LIT-1059.md) (Trends in Cognitive Sciences 20(9)),
read in [NOTE-887](../notes.d/NOTE-887.md) from the authors' manuscript; the typeset version's
Trends and Outstanding Questions boxes were not seen. The studies the
review summarises were not read, so every result below is the review's
account of them, with values read off its Figure 2.

## What was actually shown

**Definition.** Cognitive offloading is "the use of physical action to
alter the information processing requirements of a task in order to reduce
cognitive demand", split into offloading onto the body (here-and-now:
tilting the head to read rotated text, pointing, gesture) and into the
world (writing down, setting reminders, saving to a device).

**Offloading tracks demand.** In three paradigms the share of trials on
which people offload rises with the load that impairs unaided performance.
Head tilting rises with display rotation and with the number of items
(Risko et al. 2014). Reminder setting rises from about 33% to about 73% of
intentions as load and interruption push unaided accuracy from about 95% to
about 79% (Gilbert 2015). Writing items down rises from about 42% at 2
items to about 87% at 10, while no-choice accuracy falls from near 100% to
near 0 (Risko and Dunn 2015).

**Offloading departs from demand.** Three results could have come out the
other way and did not.

- Words rotated in a rotated frame and in an upright frame gave similar
  rotation costs and a similar physiological measure of demand, "Yet
  spontaneous rates of external normalization are much higher when both the
  words and frame are rotated" (Dunn and Risko 2016), and people misjudged
  the former as harder.
- People wrote items down "about 40% of the time when they had to remember
  only two items, a memory load at which performance was already at ceiling
  without offloading", and "erroneously judged that offloading would improve
  their performance" there.
- "Individuals with lower confidence in their memory abilities were more
  likely to spontaneously set reminders, even after controlling for any
  influence of objective ability". Lower confidence in a perceptual task,
  with accuracy held constant, also went with more reminders.

The review offers three explanations for superfluous offloading (an
undetected benefit, a bias against effort, a mistaken belief) and favours
the third without a test that separates them.

**Offloading changes what is remembered.** People who typed statements
expecting them saved recalled them worse than people expecting them erased
(Sparrow et al. 2011), and saving can improve memory for where information
is "at the expense of remembering what it actually is". Photographing
museum objects impaired memory for them a day later (Henkel 2014). A
turn-by-turn navigation group drove a route better, remembered its scenes
worse, and drove it worse a second time without the aid (Fenech et al.
2010).

## What this does not say

- **That people offload when it pays.** The review's point is the reverse:
  when people offload and when offloading pays come apart. It gives no
  account of when it pays, no cost of offloading set against the cost of the
  internal route, and no effect sizes.
- **That the metacognitive model is shown.** Figure 3 is a box diagram with
  three arrows; it is not formalised and makes no quantitative prediction.
  Belief is never manipulated apart from ability in the studies as reported;
  the confidence results are correlational after statistical control.
- **That reliance drifts.** The self-reinforcing drift away from internal
  capability ("the model predicts a kind of self-reinforcing pattern") is a
  prediction of the model, illustrated by the GPS example. No study reviewed
  tracks it over time.
- **That offloading is computation reused over time.** Every study reviewed
  offloads for one use within a task or session. The number of times an
  offloaded result is used is not a variable anywhere, so nothing here
  prices expected reuse.
- **That the effort being minimised is known.** Box 2 leaves open whether
  the choice minimises time (Soft Constraints) or memory load specifically
  (Minimal Memory).
- **That the memory cost generalises.** The saved-or-erased and photo
  results are single studies as reported; the record holds no replication.

## Connections

- [QUESTION-095](../questions.d/QUESTION-095.md) asks whether one cost model covers leaving a result in the
  world and amortizing a computation. This account adds two constraints on
  any answer: a model of when offloading pays is not a model of when people
  do it, and offloading erodes the internal route it replaces, which a
  fixed reconstruction cost ([CLAIM-303](../claims.d/CLAIM-303.md)) leaves out. It also names a
  retrieval cost: "you no longer need to remember the content of the file,
  but you do need to remember where to find it."
- [THEORY-359](THEORY-359.md) supplies a benefit-minus-cost objective under which the
  decision to offload could be written; this account says the decision as
  people make it is driven by belief about the cost.
- Automation bias, in the review's Box 3, is the nearest relative of the
  staleness cost [QUESTION-095](../questions.d/QUESTION-095.md) names: reuse of an external aid's output
  without checking it. The review does not frame it as staleness.
