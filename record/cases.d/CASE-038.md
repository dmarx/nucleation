# CASE-038: TV Tropes: a community-curated catalogue of narrative tropes and their instances, such as the Gilligan Cut

## The case

TV Tropes (https://tvtropes.org/) is a wiki that names recurring narrative
devices and lists works that use them. The owner, at U44: "I'm wondering if
the TVTropes project might be relevant reference case?"

A155 §1 took the Gilligan Cut. Its core pattern, in A155's words: "A
character insists that something will not happen. An edit immediately
reveals that the predicted event has happened or is happening. The contrast
produces a comic or ironic effect." A155: "The recognizable structure is a
relation between assertion, expectation, juxtaposition, and contradiction."

The site relates tropes to one another by their "variations, inversions,
subversions, and deconstructions", and its editors compare, contrast and
split related tropes (A155 §2). A155 §3 turned these into a vocabulary of
transformations: recasting ("Same relational pattern, different characters
or setting"), inversion, subversion, deconstruction, and parody ("A
recognizable pattern is preserved while its communicative purpose
changes"). It tied parody to the Villon–Henley case ([CASE-010](CASE-010.md)). "The
particularly interesting distinction is between **preserving a trope's
structural pattern** and **preserving its communicative function**."

Gala, Khursheed, Lerner, O'Connor and Iyyer 2020 (doi:10.18653/v1/2020.nlpcss-1.23,
not held) compiled "approximately 30,000 tropes and 1.9 million documented
instances across film, television, and literature", as A155 §4 says, linked
to IMDb and Goodreads metadata. The paper scores gender skew in tropes. It
measures association, not trope preservation under transformation.

A191 §8 put the Gilligan Cut ("a character refuses some possibility,
followed by a cut showing the contrary outcome") under five manipulations:

1. Preserve the events but remove the decisive juxtaposition.
2. Preserve the juxtaposition but change the assertion.
3. Reverse the outcome.
4. Preserve the sequence while changing its humorous purpose.
5. Produce a parody that remains recognizable as an instance.

"These interventions separate narrative structure from narrative function."
v7 made the case Study B (A178): "Distinguish preservation of the trope's
recognizable relational pattern from preservation of its rhetorical or
pragmatic function." A203 §29 keeps it without naming the Gilligan Cut, and
A218 Study 5 keeps the design without naming TV Tropes ("independently
curated trope instances").

## What it can show

A trope's instances are related by shared function and convention, not by
descent. That isolates [CLAIM-002](../claims.d/CLAIM-002.md)'s third relation, similarity of
communicative function, from its second, continuity of transmission.
Parody keeps the structure and changes the function, which is
[CLAIM-034](../claims.d/CLAIM-034.md)'s point that fidelity is one goal among several. If the
manipulations were run, the case could show whether a relational pattern is
recognized across realizations that share no surface form, and where a
trope's boundary is sharp or audience-dependent ([CLAIM-147](../claims.d/CLAIM-147.md)). A191:
"**A trope might be a real pattern without every classification decision
being objectively unique.**"

## What it cannot show

The annotations are the community's. A trope's description was written to
fit its examples, and examples are added that fit the description
([CLAIM-145](../claims.d/CLAIM-145.md)). So agreement with the catalogue's labels measures
editorial agreement, and the catalogue gives candidates for testing, not
validation. That is [QUESTION-005](../questions.d/QUESTION-005.md) for this corpus. A155 calls the trope
"a candidate communicative invariant", and nothing yet shows a stable core
of relations across a trope's instances. None of the manipulations has been
run, so the case bears no weight yet.

The analysis is not new in kind. Propp's functions ([THEORY-167](../theory.d/THEORY-167.md)) and
Lévi-Strauss's transformations ([CLAIM-019](../claims.d/CLAIM-019.md)) are the same move on folktales
and myths. No reply in the exchange names either.

A155 also says that TV Tropes identifies the Gilligan Cut as its earliest
trope entry. That is unverified and is not filed. The two other trope papers
A155 cites (Chaudhary and Jhala 2022; Zhang et al. 2026, arXiv:2606.19499)
are left to the anthology, which could hold them.
