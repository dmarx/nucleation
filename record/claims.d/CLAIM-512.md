# CLAIM-512: The diachronic thesis faces two objections: a dilemma, that read narrowly it fails for judgments not about any organization's continuation, such as that suffering is bad, and read broadly every judgment is met vacuously as a preference between continuations unless the organizational description adds causal specificity, transfer or counterfactual discrimination; and a distinction, that evaluating a history is not prescribing its continuation, so a judgment can be determinate with no future transformation fixed

## The objection

**Narrow horn.** If the relatum must be an organization's persistence or
change, judgments about experiences are counterexamples. That suffering is
bad does not obviously concern any organization's continuation. Bringing
in the sufferer as the organization makes the relatum carry the judgment
rather than fix it.

**Broad horn.** If any difference between possible histories counts as a
change in some organization, every judgment has a diachronic relatum
trivially, and [CLAIM-518](CLAIM-518.md) says nothing [CLAIM-363](CLAIM-363.md)'s standard did not.

## The second objection: evaluation is not prescription

A judgment that something went wrong need not say what should be done
about it. "This institution violated its agreement" is fully determinate
without fixing whether the institution should reform, compensate,
dissolve or keep anything. Evaluating a history, J_N(γ), can be
determinate while its admissible continuations, 𝒜_N(γ), are not. Version 1
of the thesis made the second a condition of the first. That was the
owner's objection at review, and the thesis was narrowed to meet it. It
now claims only that some judgments need diachronic relations, and treats
prescription as a further question.

## What an answer would need

A27 §10 proposed three tests by which the organizational description would
earn its place:

- **causal specificity:** it identifies which relations a remedy must act
  on, beyond what the standard says;
- **transfer:** the same description predicts the verdict in a new context;
- **counterfactual discrimination:** it separates cases the standard alone
  does not, as [CASE-110](../cases.d/CASE-110.md) does for coarse-grainings.

None was carried out. Until one is, this stands unanswered.

## Note of 2026-10-31: the parent paper's ethics on both horns, and a pair of cases that does not answer

Bencherki, Brummans and Vézy's responsibility ([LIT-1902](../literature.d/LIT-1902.md), pp.
1366–1367) is a diachronic normative thesis of the kind this objection
is aimed at, and it falls on the broad horn. It speaks of "the
collective duty to foster each being-in-becoming, regardless of the
'level' on which they are operating". The paper's beings-in-becoming
include a piston that "communicates movement" (p. 1364) and a virus that
"is itself a process of becoming" (p. 1356). So the duty applies to
every change, and it cannot arbitrate between individuations that block
each other. The paper's own cases are such conflicts: the health
department and MSF (pp. 1359–1363), the army and the spouses ([LIT-1901](../literature.d/LIT-1901.md)).
It also makes prescription the content of the judgment, which this
objection's second part separates from evaluation.

Bencherki (2017)'s checklist and daycare (pp. 789–792; note of this date
on [CLAIM-518](CLAIM-518.md)) might seem to meet the third of A27's tests, counterfactual
discrimination. They are two cases with one end state that the verdict
separates. But what separates them is a standard of authorship,
"whose action it was", and not the individuation description, which
[THEORY-1285](../theory.d/THEORY-1285.md) says needs that standard and disclaims it. The pair
supports [CLAIM-518](CLAIM-518.md)'s narrowed claim that some judgments need histories.
It does not show that the organizational description adds anything to
the standard, which is what this objection asks.
