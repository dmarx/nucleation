summary: >-
  Using U.S. daily newspaper data (1996–2015; 1,596 newspapers in 1,266
  counties; 296 exits) and municipal bond data (MSRB, 1999–2015), the
  authors study the long-run effect of local newspaper closures on municipal
  borrowing costs. Focusing on closures that leave a county with two or
  fewer dailies (204 "Closure" counties), they find offering yields rise by
  about 5.5 bp and secondary yields by 6.4 bp three or more years after a
  closure, more for revenue bonds (about 10 bp), costing about $650 thousand
  per average issue. Identification: comparison with population-matched
  neighbouring counties (7.3 bp); closures in low- vs high-newspaper
  counties (10.3 bp vs no effect); Craigslist entry as an instrument for
  closures (4–6 bp). Effects are larger in states with isolated capitals
  (worse governance) and do not differ by internet use (online news not a
  substitute). Mechanisms: government inefficiency (more costly advance
  refundings and negotiated sales; higher government wage share, employees
  per capita, taxes and deficits per capita) and informational frictions
  (price dispersion up about 5%).

# NOTE-tmpf5h7u: Gao, Lee and Murphy (2018 working paper) — Financing dies in darkness? The impact of newspaper closures on public finance

## Contribution

Evidence that the local press's monitoring of government has a measurable price in public finance, with several identification strategies, extending the media-and-governance literature from corporate to municipal finance.

## Key results

1. Abstract: "Following a newspaper closure, municipal borrowing costs increase by 5 to 11 basis points, costing the municipality an additional $650 thousand per issue."
2. Introduction: "following the three year period after a newspaper closure, municipal bond offering yields increase by 5.5 basis points, while yields in the secondary market increase by 6.4 basis points".
3. Introduction: "a newspaper closure in a low newspaper county increases offering yields by 10.3 basis points, while a newspaper closure in a high newspaper county has no significant effect on offering yields".
4. Introduction: "Craigslist-induced newspaper closures increase municipal bond yields by four to six basis points".
5. Introduction: "online sources do not act as a perfect substitute for local newspapers, at least for our sample period."
6. Introduction, mechanism: "a newspaper closure leads to a 1.3 percentage point increase in the government wage ratio"; county deficit per capita "increases by about $53".
7. Introduction: the authors argue media capture by local government is "unlikely" because the municipality "is typically not an important source of revenue".
8. Conclusion: online outlets "do not necessarily provide a good substitute for high-quality, locally‐sourced, investigative journalism."

## Assumptions

- Closures are not driven by local economic conditions (supported by matched-county, newspaper-count and Craigslist-IV designs, and by balanced growth rates).
- Craigslist entry affects bond yields only through newspaper closures (exclusion restriction).
- Bond-market investors are local and price local governance risk.
- Lost "monitoring" is the channel (supported by inefficiency and liquidity measures), not, e.g., loss of the newspaper as an advertiser of bond issues.

## Concepts

Media as government watchdog; local newspaper closures; municipal bond offering and secondary yields; advance refundings; negotiated vs competitive sales; informational frictions and price dispersion; Craigslist as an instrument; substitutability of online news; state capital isolation as a governance measure.

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** A's account ("municipal borrowing costs rose 5–11 basis points after local newspaper closures, consistent with weakened monitoring") is **accurate** for the working paper, and matches the authors' abstract. The published version's exact figures were not checked.

- **CLAIM-tmpifp69** (attention-optimized news works against investigative journalism; findings cannot show demand before they exist; value falls largely to non-payers): **supports the externality step (step 2) and bears on the claim's defeater; does not bear on attention optimization.** The paper measures a public value of local news that the newspaper does not capture: taxpayers pay about $650 thousand more per bond issue after closure, a cost borne by people who were not (or not all) subscribers. That is exactly the "value falls largely to people who never pay" premise, quantified for one channel (accountability reporting on local government). It also bears on the claim's strongest rival (A33 §10: loss of investigative capacity reflects collapse of older advertising subsidies): the paper's instrument is Craigslist's erosion of classified advertising, i.e. the advertising-subsidy collapse itself causes the closures whose costs it measures. So it supports the rival's account of *why* papers close, not the claim's attention-optimization account; the claim's defeater (attention dependence, apart from subsidy collapse, predicting capacity loss) is untouched. The paper is about closure, not clickbait.
- **CASE-tmpd5eqf** (found on recheck: the newsroom that cuts investigations because it sees low traffic on investigations it no longer funds): the paper's anecdotes (Bell, California; Rocky Mountain News) illustrate the cost side of missing monitoring, not the case's demand-reading loop.
