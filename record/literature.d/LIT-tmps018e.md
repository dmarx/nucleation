# LIT-tmps018e: The Impact of Simulation-Based Spaced Training for Skills Acquisition on Learning and Performance Outcomes Among Healthcare Professionals: A Systematic Review

Patocka, C., Anderson, I., Brennan, E., Lacroix, L., Pandya, A., Ganshorn, H. & Hall, A. K. (2026). The Impact of Simulation-Based Spaced Training for Skills Acquisition on Learning and Performance Outcomes Among Healthcare Professionals: A Systematic Review. *Simulation in Healthcare* 21(4): 241–246. doi:10.1097/SIH.0000000000000898. Published online 5 December 2025.

Not read. The takeaways are the abstract's, checked by string match against the Crossref abstract, and unverified until the review is read.

## Key takeaways

- **Scope.** "Of 4572 citations screened, 15 met inclusion criteria":
  randomized or prospective comparative studies of resuscitation and
  surgical procedures, most with spacing intervals of about a week.
- **Equivalence.** "spaced training was generally as effective as massed
  training."
- **Retention.** "Some evidence suggested advantages for spaced training
  in skill retention, particularly for time to complete procedures."
- **No patient-level evidence.** "No studies demonstrated improvements in
  patient care practices, patient outcomes, or broader educational
  effects."

## Standing in the record

The pasted bibliography on emergency readiness cites it, with no
description, under maintaining capabilities that are rarely exercised.
From its abstract, it is about how to schedule the initial training of a
skill, mostly over intervals of about a week, not about keeping a rarely
used capability over long non-use or about refresher training; it bears
on A's theme only as evidence on how training schedules affect retention.
The record held nothing on spacing effects before this filing.

**The neighbour.** Martinengo et al. (2024), [LIT-tmpu5jbd](LIT-tmpu5jbd.md), an independent
Cochrane-method review of spaced digital education for health
professionals, read in full, stands in only partly. It shares the
comparison of spaced with massed simulation, the surgical skills
outcomes, the heterogeneity, the absence of patient outcomes for
simulation, and the lack of any rationale for spacing intervals. It does
not stand in for this review's retention finding: it analyses immediate
post-training results only. Its pooled simulation result favours spacing
(two studies, low certainty) where this abstract reads "generally as
effective", and its scope differs (digital or high-fidelity simulation
only, and RCT designs, but also online education). Cite this review's
results only from its abstract until it is read. Filed from the neighbour:
