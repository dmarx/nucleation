# NOTE-tmpsnlxv: Review of Samuel Scheffler, Death and the Afterlife

## Contribution

A short, admiring review. Its use to the record is as a report of the
parts of the book the Tanner text lacks: the third lecture, the four
commentaries and, barely, the replies.

## Key insight

The book holds two theses that pull against each other, and Scheffler
says so. Death gives our values their status, so immortality would not
help. Yet our confidence in those values depends on others living after
us. Cottingham quotes Scheffler calling the conclusion "strange and
unsettling" (book p. 109).

## Assumptions

- **A reviewer's selection.** What the review reports of each part is
  what Cottingham chose to report.
- **Cottingham's own stance.** He wants religious reflection on
  finitude admitted to the discussion, and calls the empirical question
  of how we would react "philosophically less interesting" than the
  normative one.

## Key results

- **What the book is.** The two 2012 Tanner Lectures at Berkeley and a
  third paper "delivered at Chicago the previous year"; four commentaries
  (Wolf, Frankfurt, Shiffrin, Kolodny) and Scheffler's replies. 210 pp.
- **Lectures I and II, as reported.** The conjecture; the
  nonconsequentialist point; "we need humanity to have a future for the
  very idea that things matter to retain a secure place in our conceptual
  repertoire" (p. 60); non-believers in personal immortality go on valuing
  (p. 71); the infertile world's "apathy, anomie, and despair" (p. 40);
  and "there is a very specific sense in which our own survival is less
  important to us than the survival of the human race" (p. 73).
  Cottingham glosses this as "a more altruistic construal of the human
  condition", reminiscent of Parfit.
- **The third lecture** ("final lecture", on death). As reported:
  - Unlike Parfit, Scheffler calls "incredible" any view that denies a
    personal egocentric subject (p. 103).
  - He holds the fear of death reasonable, or not unreasonable, while
    agreeing with Williams that personal immortality would not be
    desirable (p. 107).
  - "it is essential to our idea of a life that it is temporally bounded"
    (p. 100), and what we cherish has the status of values "because of
    their role in our finite and bounded lives" (p. 100).
  - So death is needed for confidence in what we value, yet that
    confidence depends on others being alive after we die (p. 109). The
    Cunning Little Vixen's frog closes it: life continues.
- **Frankfurt.** Questions whether the desire for humanity to continue is
  as altruistic as Scheffler implies, and suggests its significance may
  come to little more than that "natural selection has ensured that we
  have those desires" (p. 138).
- **Wolf** ("the most interesting of the critiques"). Questions the key
  premise that impending doom would undercut our faith in our projects.
  Extinction would disorient at first, but we could "snap out of it"
  (p. 128). Against Scheffler's reading of Alvy Singer, there would still
  be time and reason to "create beauty, gain wisdom and help each other"
  (p. 127). Cottingham credits her with turning the debate to how we
  ought to react.
- **Shiffrin and Kolodny** are named only. **The replies** are mentioned,
  not summarized.
- **Cottingham's objection.** Scheffler and his critics leave out
  religious thought, though finitude, not personal immortality, is
  central to much of it; the problems are approached through recent
  science fiction alone.

## Concepts

- **doomsday and infertility scenarios**, as in Scheffler.
- **temporal boundedness of a life**: the third lecture's claim that a
  life's having a beginning, middle and end is essential to its goods
  being values.

## Connections

- **Scheffler's lectures (NOTE-tmpsnlxv-scheffler).** The review's pages 40,
  60, 71 and 73 match passages in Lecture I, part 8 and Lecture II, parts
  2 and 5 of the Tanner text, which fits Crossref's placing of the two
  lectures at pp. 14–82.
- **Williams on the Makropulos case**, which the third lecture accepts.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** Two points, both inferences.
  - Cottingham reads the book as "a more altruistic construal" and says
    Scheffler finds us "far less selfish than is often supposed". The
    Tanner text says the opposite about altruism: the result is
    dependence on future people, not unsuspected altruism. A reader of
    the review alone would overstate the book as a case against the
    thesis.
  - Frankfurt's doubt is the thesis's side of the argument, made from
    evolution rather than from the self; Wolf's is the opposite: that the
    value of our projects does not depend on the collective afterlife at
    all, which would remove the case altogether.
- **The legacy question ([QUESTION-tmpruhu4](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md)).** The third lecture's pairing
  is relevant: we need our own death for our values and others' lives
  after it for our confidence in them. Legacy, on this picture, is not a
  substitute for survival.

## Limitations

- **A review**, under 2,000 words, of a 210-page book. Shiffrin and
  Kolodny are only named, the replies not summarized.
- **A misreport.** The review puts the asteroid "thirty years after the
  life of our generation has come to an end". In Scheffler it is thirty
  days after one's own death.
- **The book's text is not checked**: page numbers and quotations are the
  reviewer's.
- **Typo**: "Scheffler's s two 2012 Tanner lectures".
