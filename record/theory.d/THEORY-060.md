# THEORY-060: A life story constructed from adolescence on is the form a person's diachronic identity takes, and its content shapes later well-being

## Which unity, and which word

This account is about **narrative unity**: the unity a life has as a story.
"Diachronic identity" here is identity in Erikson's sense, "a sense of
sameness and continuity across different social roles and over time"
([NOTE-470](../notes.d/NOTE-470.md), Assumptions). It is a person's psychosocial identity, so the
account bears on **personhood**: McAdams adopts Schechtman's claim that "we
constitute ourselves as persons" by narrative ([LIT-595](../literature.d/LIT-595.md), p. 6). It is
not a claim about numerical identity over time. Whether the storyteller of
forty is the same person as the child of four is Parfit's question, and the
story neither answers it nor needs to.

Nor is it about the **self** in the record's sense of a subject. McAdams
calls narrative identity the third layer of a "self", but his self is a
developing psychological organisation: traits, then goals, then story. The
subject who tells the story is presupposed. That is why the account does not
carry `self`. Where it meets accounts of the self as subject, below, the
difference is kept.

## Source

- McAdams (2019), [LIT-595](../literature.d/LIT-595.md), read in full in [NOTE-470](../notes.d/NOTE-470.md). Page
  references are to the journal's pagination.

## What was actually shown

**The construct.** "A narrative identity is a person's internalized and
evolving story of how he or she has become the person he or she is becoming
(McAdams and McLean 2013)" (p. 2). It gives a life "unity, moral purpose,
and temporal coherence" (p. 1). Once a person has one, "there is no going back"
to being only an actor or an agent (p. 8).

**When it arrives.** Development runs from social actor (self-recognition by
about age 2) to motivated agent (goals and theory of mind, the school years)
to autobiographical author, in "adolescence and young adulthood" (pp. 6–8).
Autobiographical memory, parental scaffolding of children's self-narration,
and autobiographical reasoning, which grows from adolescence into midlife,
are cited as the prerequisites ([NOTE-470](../notes.d/NOTE-470.md), C2).

**Its content matters.** Stories with agentic protagonists go with
well-being. In Adler's (2012) longitudinal study of psychotherapy clients,
"the changes in the story predict and precede the decline in symptoms and
the enhancement of psychological well-being. In other words, the story
changes first, and then the storyteller gets better" (p. 10). Midlife adults
whose stories resemble "the redemptive self" score high on generativity
(p. 10).

**Its limits, as McAdams states them.** Studies "generally assume that nearly
everybody 'has' a narrative identity to be analyzed", while "some people are
more oriented toward narrative identity than are others", measured by the
ANIQ (p. 10). And the idea that lives "may readily assume a narrative form ...
would appear to be a cultural construction that resonates well with the
sensibilities of the modern world" (p. 13).

The first half of the title is McAdams's framework, supported by cited
developmental findings. The second half rests, as a causal claim, on one
longitudinal study the record holds only through this citation. The rest is
correlational.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say the story is true.** Narrative identity is "an invented
  construction", not "a veridical testimony" ([NOTE-470](../notes.d/NOTE-470.md)). The claim is
  that it is causally consequential, not accurate.
- **It does not say everyone has one.** McAdams's own concessions (the ANIQ,
  the cultural-construction remark) make universality the account's weakest
  point, and `promote_when` says how to test it.
- **It does not say storytelling is an adaptation.** The essay's evolutionary
  framing is labelled speculative and is not part of this account.
- **It does not say what makes agency over time self-governed.** That is a
  different unity (see Bratman, below).

## Connections

- **Strawson, "Against Narrativity" ([LIT-452](../literature.d/LIT-452.md), [NOTE-383](../notes.d/NOTE-383.md)).** Strawson denies
  the psychological Narrativity thesis in any non-trivial version, and says
  many normal people are Episodic: they do not experience the self they are
  as having been there in the further past ([NOTE-383](../notes.d/NOTE-383.md), C1, C5). He also
  denies the ethical thesis: Episodic lives can be good (C6). That rivals
  both halves. If the first half is true, Episodics either have a life story
  they do not experience as theirs, or lack the identity it constitutes. If
  the second is true, they should fare worse unless their story-less lives
  are offset otherwise. The two works already `rival` each other in the LIT
  scheme. No THEORY states Strawson's account, so no `rivals` is declared
  here. Strawson's evidence is testimony, and McAdams does not cite him;
  the ANIQ comparison in `promote_when` is where they would meet.
- **Bratman's self-governance over time ([THEORY-076](THEORY-076.md)).** Bratman grants
  that narrative unity "may apply to many cases" and holds that the "more
  basic" unity at work in diachronic self-governance is acting together with
  oneself. That is not a rival claim. The two accounts are about different
  unities in the record's sense: this one about the unity a life has as a
  story, that one about what makes conduct over time governed by the agent.
  They would be rivals only if each claimed to be the basic unity of the
  same thing, and McAdams makes no claim about self-governance. Bratman's
  remark is a claim about which unity self-governance needs, and both can
  be true. No `rivals` is declared.
- **The RFT and ACT self ([THEORY-044](THEORY-044.md), [THEORY-050](THEORY-050.md), [THEORY-051](THEORY-051.md)).** RFT's
  conceptualized self, the content of verbal relations about oneself, is
  the narrative self described from outside ([THEORY-044](THEORY-044.md)), and McAdams's life
  story is a developed version of that content. ACT treats domination by it
  as inflexibility ([THEORY-050](THEORY-050.md)). This account treats constructing it as a
  developmental achievement whose content can help. The two agree if
  holding a story and being fused with it are different things. Adler's
  finding that more agentic stories precede improvement is compatible with
  ACT only if the change is in what the story says and not in how tightly it
  is held. Neither source draws the distinction, and nothing in the record
  tests it.
- **Metzinger ([THEORY-064](THEORY-064.md)).** Metzinger's self is pre-reflective and
  present. He attributes the experience of an enduring self to "the
  invariance of bodily self-awareness and autobiographical memory"
  ([NOTE-460](../notes.d/NOTE-460.md), §3.5), which is where McAdams's author begins. The two
  are accounts of different things, a subject and a life story, and do not
  compete.
