# THEORY-572: Where an effect is not yet well conceptualised, replication cannot say which effect exists: direct replication bears on random error, conceptual replication presupposes that two operationalisations pick out the same effect, and an effect is delineated instead by probing for systematic error

## Source

Feest (2019), [LIT-161](../literature.d/LIT-161.md) (*Philosophy of Science* 86(5), 895–905), read in full
in [NOTE-196](../notes.d/NOTE-196.md) from the PhilSci-Archive manuscript. Page references are the
manuscript's.

## What was actually shown

**Similarity needs individuation (§2).** No two experiments are identical, so
replication aims at similarity, and similarity is always in some respect.
Judging it takes conceptual assumptions (what the manipulation and measure
are, *qua* what) and material ones (that confounders were controlled). Even a
replication that "simply imitates" relies on unstated auxiliaries.

**Direct replication (§3.1, pp. 6–7).** It "can (perhaps) provide evidence for
the existence of something, but it cannot say existence of what". Described
locally, the effect is operationist caution and says almost nothing.
Described broadly, its conceptual scope is in question, and repetition cannot
answer that.

**Conceptual replication (§3.2, pp. 8–9).** Comparing two operationalisations
"of the same construct" presupposes they pick out the same effect, which is
the point at issue, so a match or a mismatch is "underdetermined by the
experimental data".

**The Mozart effect (§4, pp. 10–11).** On Feest's reading the debate between
Rauscher and her critics turned on the choice of dependent variable (a
spatial-reasoning subtest) and of independent variable (a Mozart sonata, or
up-beat music and mood; Chabris 1999), not on whether the result repeated.
That work, probing hypotheses about systematic error to delineate a
phenomenon, is what she calls exploration.

**What could have come out otherwise.** The Mozart debate could have been
about whether the effect repeated. On her reading it was not. It is one case.

## What this does not say

- **That replication is useless.** Direct replication still bears on random
  error. The claim is that it is "less useful and important than is widely
  assumed" in this kind of research (§5).
- **That the replication crisis is unreal.** Feest concedes it is real and "to
  a large degree a crisis that turns on questionable research practices with
  regard to the use of statistical methods" (§5, p. 13). Her claim is that it
  is misnamed.
- **That it holds where constructs are well formed.** By her own reasoning,
  conceptual replication should not beg the question in psychophysics or other
  domains with settled constructs.
- **That direct replication is blind to all systematic error.** The paper says
  so (pp. 7, 10) but does not consider multi-lab replications, which vary
  some unstated auxiliaries ([NOTE-196](../notes.d/NOTE-196.md), C3: weak).
- **Anything about external validity.** Only internal validity is treated
  (fn. 4).
- **How exploration differs in practice from a run of conceptual
  replications.** The positive proposal is about a page long.

## Connections

- The reading draws the analogy to benchmarks: whether a result repeats across
  seeds or labs speaks to reliability, and whether it is the capability it is
  named for is a question of conceptual scope ([NOTE-196](../notes.d/NOTE-196.md), Bearing). That is the
  reading's extension, not the paper's.
- Leonelli on open science ([LIT-189](../literature.d/LIT-189.md)) is the record's other work on
  reproducibility. How the two compare has not been checked.
