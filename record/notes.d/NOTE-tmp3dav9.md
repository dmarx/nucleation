# NOTE-tmp3dav9: Social Identity Theory and Self-categorization Theory: A Historical Review

## Contribution

A review, not new work. It separates the two theories that are cited
under one name and tells how each changed: which claims were central,
which were dropped, and which critiques still stand. For this record its
use is as a map. It says which of the fandom papers' borrowed claims
belong to social identity theory (SIT) and which to self-categorization
theory (SCT), and which were revised.

## Key insight

The two theories answer different questions with one shared mechanism,
categorization. SIT, from the minimal group paradigm, explains why groups
favour themselves: people want a positive self-concept, the group's
standing is part of it, and the group's standing is judged by comparison
with other groups. SCT drops SIT's continuum from interpersonal to
intergroup behaviour and asks when a person acts as a group member at
all. Its answer is levels of self-categorization, salience by fit, and
depersonalization. Several popular readings of SIT, such as "high
identifiers are more biased" and "bias repairs low self-esteem", are
simplifications that the theory's architects disowned.

## Assumptions

A review; its assumptions are the approach's.

- **Groups are psychologically real only by comparison**: "Groups are not
  islands" (p. 207).
- **Motive**: SIT assumes a desire for a positive and secure self-concept
  (p. 207). SCT "offered no explicit motivational analysis" (p. 215).
- **Context**: categories and their content are relative to a perceiver
  and a frame of reference (p. 208).

## Key results

- **SIT (pp. 205–207).** It began from the minimal group paradigm (Tajfel,
  Billig, Bundy & Flament 1971). Participants assigned to meaningless
  groups favoured their in-group in allocating points they could not
  themselves receive, sometimes at the cost of absolute gain, to maximize
  the difference. Tajfel and Turner put interactions on a spectrum from
  purely interpersonal (rare) to purely intergroup. Categorization
  accentuates similarities within and differences between groups, and
  shifts the self-concept from personal identity towards social identity.
  Social identity is "those aspects of an individual's self-image that
  derive from the social categories to which he/she belongs, as well as
  the emotional and evaluative consequences of this group membership"
  (p. 206). Members seek *positive distinctiveness*. Members of low-status
  groups use exit, flattering comparisons, chosen dimensions, revaluation
  or social change, depending on the perceived permeability, stability and
  legitimacy of the hierarchy. "For Tajfel, social identity theory was at
  its heart a theory of social change" (p. 207).
- **SCT (pp. 207–209).** After Tajfel's death in 1982, Turner and
  colleagues elaborated the cognitive element and turned to intragroup
  processes (Turner et al. 1987). Three levels of self-categorization:
  human, social and personal, with finer gradations allowed at the
  middle. A **functional antagonism** between levels: as one becomes more
  salient, the others less. Salience is set by accessibility and fit;
  comparative fit is the **meta-contrast ratio**, and normative fit is
  consistency with stereotypical expectation. **Depersonalization**:
  under salience, self and others are seen as interchangeable exemplars of
  a context-dependent prototype that both describes and prescribes.
- **Reinterpretations (pp. 209–211).** Group polarization comes from the
  perceptual shift of categorization (Turner, Wetherell & Hogg 1989).
  Cohesion is depersonalized liking based on prototypicality, not
  interpersonal attraction (Hogg & Hardie 1991). Stereotype content shifts
  with the comparison set (Haslam et al. 1992), and stereotypes have
  social functions rather than being the shortcuts of a cognitive miser.
  Deindividuation is a shift from personal to social identity, so
  anonymity makes people more responsive to the local norm, prosocial or
  antisocial (Reicher, Spears & Postmes 1995), with meta-analytic support
  (Postmes & Spears 1998). Crowds come with shared norms, and their
  violence is often aimed at symbolic intergroup targets (Reicher 1987).
  The normative/informational distinction in influence is dissolved
  (Turner 1991). Leaders influence to the extent they are prototypical
  (Hogg 2001). Turner (2005) reverses the usual order of power:
  prototypicality → influence → power → control of resources (p. 211).
- **Evidence and its limits (pp. 211–213).**
  - Minimal-group bias survived demand and similarity explanations.
  - Rabbie's interdependence critique was the strongest challenge.
    Participants favour the out-group when they believe it controls their
    outcomes (Rabbie, Schot & Visser 1989). Bias disappears when outcomes
    are explicitly independent (Karp et al. 1993). Many now read the
    behavioural interaction model as a finer analysis within SIT, not a
    rival.
  - In the 1990s the research moved from minimal to real groups and, by
    Hornsey's account, consolidated the socio-structural hypotheses.
  - SCT tests manipulated salience or measured identification. High
    salience or identification increased self-stereotyping and perceived
    in-group homogeneity, and prototypicality affected influence only
    then.
- **Dead ends (pp. 213–215).**
  - The weak correlation between identification and bias was read as
    against SIT (Hinkle & Brown 1990). Turner (1999) denied the theory
    ever predicted it, and "the field appears to have accepted" that it is
    not diagnostic.
  - Hogg and Abrams's (1990) two self-esteem hypotheses got more
    meta-analytic support for "bias raises self-esteem" than for "low
    self-esteem causes bias" (Rubin & Hewstone 1998). The approach was
    criticized for measuring personal self-esteem to predict a collective
    outcome. Turner distanced SIT from it.
- **Later directions (pp. 215–217).**
  - Distinctiveness and uncertainty reduction (Hogg 2000) replaced
    self-esteem as the most studied motive.
  - A "subtle withdrawal" from functional antagonism, and attention to
    individuals shaping norms (Hornsey 2006; Postmes & Jetten 2006).
  - Group-based emotions (Smith 1993), collective guilt and intergroup
    schadenfreude.
  - The human level of identity first studied (Wohl & Branscombe 2005).
  - Organizational psychology.
- **Critiques, not answered (p. 217).**
  - Functional antagonism and salience look rigid beside subgroup,
    cross-cutting and relational identities (Abrams & Hogg 2004).
  - Depersonalization obscures tolerance of dissent.
  - It is too broad to be falsifiable (Hogg & Williams 2000).
  - It is itself reductionist (Farr 1996).
  - It explains in-group favouritism better than out-group hostility
    (Brewer 1979; Brown 1995).
- **Growth.** PsycInfo publications naming either theory: 11 in 1986,
  47 in 1996, 568 in 2006 (endnote 2).

## Concepts

- **social identity**: the part of self-image from category memberships,
  with its emotional and evaluative consequences (p. 206). In SCT, the
  intermediate level of self-categorization.
- **positive distinctiveness**: favourable difference between in-group and
  relevant out-groups, which members are motivated to achieve or keep
  (p. 207).
- **socio-structural variables**: perceived status, permeability,
  stability and legitimacy of an intergroup hierarchy (pp. 207, 212).
- **functional antagonism**: as one level of self-categorization becomes
  more salient, the others become less so (p. 208).
- **meta-contrast ratio**, **normative fit**, **depersonalization**,
  **prototype**: as in Turner et al. 1994 ([NOTE-tmpf9mzh](NOTE-tmpf9mzh.md)). Hornsey adds
  that the prototype is prescriptive as well as descriptive (p. 209).
- **social identity approach**: SIT and SCT together.

## Connections

- **Turner et al. 1994 ([NOTE-tmpf9mzh](NOTE-tmpf9mzh.md)).** The two agree on SCT's core.
  Hornsey reports that functional antagonism was later softened, while the
  1994 paper still describes a "continual competition" between personal
  and group levels (Turner et al. 1994, p. 456). The two are not in
  conflict, because a competition can be graded.
- **The record's fandom papers.** Čater et al. 2026 ([LIT-1138](../literature.d/LIT-1138.md)) and Berendt
  & Uhrich 2016 ([LIT-1147](../literature.d/LIT-1147.md)) are framed by SIT's distinctiveness and
  collective self-esteem. Wegner et al. 2020 ([LIT-1140](../literature.d/LIT-1140.md)) concerns identity
  threat. Biel 2026 ([LIT-1133](../literature.d/LIT-1133.md)) concerns recategorization into a common
  in-group. All are unread. Per this review, "collective self-esteem"
  findings belong to SIT's contested self-esteem strand (C4), not to SCT.

  and Brown & Hewstone's contact theory, which Hornsey cites (p. 215), are

## Limitations

- **A review by an insider.** Hornsey works within the approach and says
  he will "not seek to defend" it against its critics (p. 217). The
  balance of evidence is his summary, with few effect sizes.
- **Brief.** About 14 pages of text for some 35 years; each strand gets a
  paragraph.
- **Dated 2008.** It predates the replication debates in social
  psychology, so none of the cited effects has been checked against
  them here.

## Open questions

- Has minimal-group bias held up in large preregistered replications, and
  does the Karp et al. independence result replicate? A replication study
  read in full would settle it for the record.
- Is the approach falsifiable as a meta-theory (p. 217)? The record could
  hold one of its specific predictions to a test, as [THEORY-tmpo85ke](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpo85ke.md)
