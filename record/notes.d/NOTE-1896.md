# NOTE-1896: Prest (2017) — Performing the Racial Scale: From Colonial Saint-Domingue to Contemporary Hollywood

## Contribution

An account of the theatre as an instrument of the racial scale, with Minette's career as its test; a second half on colourism in Hollywood casting.

## Key results

1. "the theatre could uphold the racial scale by keeping the whites white and offer a modest degree of movement within that scale by whitening the disproportionately small number of free people of colour present in the auditorium."
2. Port-au-Prince: "the free people of colour were required to sit in fifteen second-tier boxes (seven on either side of the auditorium and one at the back)."
3. Le Cap: free people of colour "sat in the upper boxes at the back of the auditorium."
4. "Throughout the period, the small number of black roles in the repertoire were customarily performed by white actors in blackface. Indeed, we have no evidence of any actors of colour until the 1780s."
5. Minette "had only one grandparent of African origin and was probably – though not certainly – relatively pale-skinned."
6. Abstract: "Her ability to move up the racial scale onstage is significant, though ultimately limited."
7. "For Minette, then, her success in the theatre allowed for some social mobility but this did not necessarily shield her from daily prejudice in other areas of her life."
8. "Minette was not an active pioneer for actors of colour in 1780s Saint-Domingue".
9. "While the inclusion of people of colour in the theatre audience was systematic, although restricted, during the late 1770s and 1780s, the appearance of performers of colour onstage remained exceptional."

## Assumptions

The sources are incomplete and biased; Moreau de Saint-Méry is a hostile but informative witness; titles ("Demoiselle", "la nommée") index status.

## Concepts

Racial scale; colourism; degeneration; whitening through civilization; cross-casting.
