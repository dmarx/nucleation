# NOTE-tmpwcnyu: Choroszewicz (2026) — Positioning public sector practitioners as 'moral crumple zones': Mechanisms in the early use of generative AI work support tools

## Contribution

Extends Elish's moral crumple zone (LIT-tmp4gcqt) from accidents to the prospective allocation of
responsibility during trials of LLM tools. "MCZ positioning" is "the disproportionate allocation
of responsibility to LLM-based tools' users relative to their actual authority, control and
capacity" (p. 3).

## Key results

1. Abstract, p. 1: five mechanisms, "(1) the assumption of human oversight and liability, (2) the
   immaturity of tested tools marketed as ‘sparring buddies’, (3) trial-and-error learning
   offloaded to users, (4) limited transparency of AI tools' development and (5) persuasive
   fallibility and the burden of vigilance placed on users".
2. §4.1, p. 6: "human oversight and responsibility as liability were explicitly codified as a user
   burden"; innovators: "We [innovators] are unable to guide you on how to formulate effective
   questions, which is why we are experimenting with the tool."
3. §4.2, p. 6: "flawed and low-quality outputs tended to be portrayed as the result of poor user
   interaction rather than issues with the tool's design and development".
4. p. 6: "This expectation of prolonged engagement can function as users' invisible work to
   compensate for the limitations of AI tools".
5. §4.3, p. 7: negative feedback was "frequently dismissed as temporary, cited as a reason for the
   tools' further development or redirected back to users as a need for their additional effort".
6. §4.5 and §5, p. 9: "This vigilance became a form of continuous cognitive and emotional labour";
   "creating a paradox where tools intended to save time instead generated additional workload".
7. §5.2, p. 10: "Codifying responsibility as a user burden insulates technologies and their
   developers from accountability".

## Assumptions

Responsibility allocation is read from innovators' statements, training and feedback sessions;
no outcome or sanction data; partial tester samples (about 43 of about 900 Copilot testers).

## Concepts

MCZ positioning; human-in-the-loop; engaged and unengaged augmentation; epistemic,
interpretative and operational opacity; persuasive fallibility; burden of vigilance; invisible
work.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpnfjln**: corroborates its allocation clause (control upstream, the obligation to
  secure acceptable outputs downstream) and, weakly, its different principles for credit and blame:
  the tools' promise is protected while poor outputs are put down to users (results 3, 5). It
  documents the unseen compensation as "invisible work" (result 4). It does not show blame after a
  visible failure: none was observed.
- **CLAIM-tmp0tg7y**: management expected saved time while the tools added work (result 6),
  covered by users' invisible effort. The loop's later step, cutting resources to the observed
  level, is not observed.
- **CLAIM-678**: the users' lack of epistemic power over the tools is the paper's opacity finding.
- Related held reading: THEORY-1519 (generative AI shifts users to evaluating output) supports
  mechanism 5 from the human-factors side.
- **The exchange** (A17 §5, A19) reports the paper accurately. Its projection that a worker who
  misses an error "may be blamed" goes beyond what was observed.

## Limitations

Early trials, not deployment; no harms or sanctions observed; one country; the author notes that the
MCZ lens "may nevertheless oversimplify the complexity of varying levels of human involvement and
control" (p. 10).
