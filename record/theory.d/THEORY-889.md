# THEORY-889: In Acharya, Blackwell and Sen's analysis, white Southerners surveyed in 2006 to 2011 who live in counties where a larger share of the 1860 population was enslaved are, with state fixed effects and 1860 county controls, somewhat less Democratic, less supportive of affirmative action and more racially resentful, by about 0.09 to 0.13 standard deviations per standard deviation (about 20 points) of slave share, and in 1984 to 1998 survey data colder toward black Americans, by about 0.32; a cotton-suitability instrument gives larger estimates; the direct effect estimated with present black share held fixed is about as large; and in county presidential vote, which counts black voters until disenfranchisement, the difference appeared after Reconstruction rather than before the war

## Source

Acharya, Blackwell and Sen (2016), [LIT-1388](../literature.d/LIT-1388.md), read in full in [NOTE-1089](../notes.d/NOTE-1089.md)
from *The Journal of Politics* 78(3): 621–641, the typeset PDF posted by the
second author. The online appendix, which holds many of the robustness
checks, was not read.

## What was actually shown

- **The association.** Pooled CCES surveys (over 40,000 white respondents
  in 1,329 Southern counties) and ANES thermometer scores. A 20 point
  increase in 1860 slave share goes with a 2.3 point fall in the share of
  whites identifying as Democrats and comparable shifts on affirmative
  action and racial resentment: "approximately a 0.09–0.13 standard
  deviation change in the CCES outcomes" (p. 626). With state fixed effects
  and 1860 economic, demographic and geographic controls (Table 1).
- **The causal reading.** Instrumenting slave share with cotton suitability
  gives larger estimates (Table 2). The authors call the exclusion
  restriction "a strong assumption" (p. 628). Holding present black share
  fixed by sequential g-estimation leaves the direct effect about as large
  (Table 3), against racial threat. Migration in 1935–40 and present income
  inequality do not account for it (Figure 3, Table 4).
- **What could have come out otherwise.** The timing could have shown the
  difference before the war; it did not. High- and low-slave counties voted
  alike before the war, and in the 1851 Georgia and Mississippi elections
  fought over slavery and secession (Figures 4–5); the difference grew
  with disenfranchisement after Reconstruction. The mechanization test
  could have shown no attenuation; slavery's effect on partisanship nearly
  vanishes where tractors spread fastest in 1930–40 (Table 5). Lynchings
  in 1882–1930 were higher where slavery had been.
- **Transmission.** Southern parents' 1965 racial thermometer gaps correlate
  with their children's into 1997, and the slavery effect is as large among
  whites born after 1965 (Table 6). The authors conclude that political
  culture, "rather than exclusively institutions", plays a role (p. 638),
  and do not rule out institutions.

## What this does not say

- **Not that slavery's attitudes were carried forward unchanged.** On the
  paper's own timing the difference was produced after emancipation. The
  persistence is of a post-emancipation divergence, not of antebellum
  opinion.
- **Not that most Black Belt whites differ.** The effects are about a tenth
  of a standard deviation.
- **Not party persistence.** "it is sociopolitical attitudes, rather than
  partisanship, that are passed down from parent to children" (fn. 23); the
  same counties were more Democratic before about 1950 and are more
  Republican now.
- **Not a family-level finding.** Respondents are assigned the slavery of
  the county where they live now. Family descent is not observed.
- **Not that institutions played no part.** Families and local institutions
  are not separated.
