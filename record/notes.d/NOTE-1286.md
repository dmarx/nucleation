# NOTE-1286: Recategorization and Prosocial Behavior: Common In-Group Identity and a Dual Identity

## Contribution

A chapter-length review of the common in-group identity model as it
bears on helping, with two new studies. The model says bias falls when
members of two groups come to see themselves as one group. The chapter
shows this extends to helping, not only to attitudes. It then adds the
model's second shared representation, the dual identity (subgroups within
one superordinate group), and reports that a minority member who
expresses it is helped less by majority members, not more.

## Key insight

A shared identity extends help across a group line, but only in the form
the helper prefers. The authors' White participants valued a common
identity with a Black student. When he described himself as "a Black
Colgate student", which includes the common identity, they treated him
like one who described himself only as Black. The enlarged "we" that
carries helping is the majority's assimilationist "we": "The responses of
White participants ... were positive and prosocial when a Black person
identified himself in a way compatible with an assimilation ideology"
(p. 204).

## Assumptions

- **Social identity and self-categorization theory as the basis**
  (p. 193). Bias is in-group favouritism, so moving out-group members
  inside the boundary moves favouritism to them.
- **Majority help as the case that matters** (p. 191), since majority
  members' help "is more often required".
- **Dual identity cannot be tested from the minority side** (pp. 198,
  205). A White person saying "I see myself primarily as a White person"
  would read as racist, so the authors did not run the mirror condition.
- **Helping measures are low-cost acts**: posters placed, hours
  volunteered on a form, agreeing to an interview.

## Key results

- **The 1989 experiment, as reported** (p. 194). Relative to a two-group
  condition, decategorization (separate individuals) reduced bias by
  making former in-group members less attractive; recategorization (one
  group) reduced it by making former out-group members more attractive.
  No numbers are given.
- **Dovidio et al. 1997, helping** (pp. 194–195). Two laboratory
  subgroups (overestimators and underestimators), induced to a one-group
  or two-group representation. A taped student, ill and behind on a
  project, asked for help placing posters.
  - Two Group: more posters for an in-group than an out-group member,
    Ms = 4.83 vs 2.25.
  - One Group: "no bias against original out-group members"
    (Ms = 3.08 vs 3.92).
- **Commons dilemmas** (p. 195). Kramer and Brewer 1984: a salient
  superordinate identity (same city) raised conservation when resources
  became scarce. Wit and Kerr 2002: contributions to a six-person fund
  were highest when participants were framed as one group of six, lowest
  when two three-person subgroups were salient.
- **Field study** (Nier et al. 2001, Study 2; pp. 195–196). At a
  Delaware football game, Black and White interviewers in either
  university's hat asked fans for an interview on food preferences.
  - White fans and a Black interviewer: 59% complied with a shared
    university identity, 36% without.
  - White fans and a White interviewer: 44% vs 37%, not significant.
  - Too few Black fans to analyse.
- **Dovidio, Gaertner & Johnson 1999** (pp. 198–201). White Colgate
  students watched a scripted, favourable interview with a Black
  confederate who answered "And how do you see yourself?" four ways.
  - Prejudice (Brigham's Attitudes toward Blacks Scale, higher is more
    prejudiced): common university 1.68, different racial 2.24, dual
    2.47, unique individual 2.32. Only the common condition differed from
    the others.
  - Hours volunteered for a Black student organization: common 0.85,
    unique 0.55, dual 0.30, different 0.25.
  - Attitudes did not mediate volunteering; they correlated r = .25 with
    it.
- **The follow-up study** (pp. 201–204). The same design with three
  conditions (common, dual, different), a request from the confederate
  himself, and empathic concern measured (Batson's adjectives).
  - Attitudes toward him: 5.00 common vs 4.33 different and 4.55 dual
    (p = .08 across conditions).
  - Empathic concern: 3.35, 2.58, 2.47 (p = .04).
  - Posters agreed to: 2.73, 0.57, 0.46 (p = .01).
  - Different and dual did not differ on any measure; common was higher
    than the two combined on each (ps < .04).
  - Mediation: with empathic concern added, its effect on helping was
    β = .34, p < .01, and the condition effect fell from β = .46 to .35
    (Sobel z = 1.99, p < .05), still significant. Attitudes did not
    mediate.
- **Proposed mechanisms** (pp. 205–206), untested here.
  - In-group projection (Mummendey and Wenzel 1999): majority members
    take their group's standards as the superordinate category's, so a
    minority member stressing his subgroup looks deviant.
  - Identity threat: a dual identity undermines the dominant culture's
    hegemony.
  - Culture: in Portugal, one-group recategorization reduced bias more
    for Black children and dual identity more for European Portuguese
    children (Rebelo et al. 2005).

## Concepts

- **recategorization**: changing who counts as in-group by making a more
  inclusive category salient, so former out-group members are inside it.
- **decategorization**: reducing the salience of group boundaries so
  members are seen as separate individuals.
- **common in-group identity (one group)**: both groups seen as one
  superordinate group.
- **dual identity**: original subgroup identities and the common identity
  active at once ("Black Colgate student").
- **assimilation versus multiculturalism**: the ideologies the one-group
  and dual-identity representations parallel (p. 197).
- **in-group projection**: taking one's own group's standards as those of
  the superordinate category.

## Connections

- **Gaertner et al. 1989 ([LIT-1710](../literature.d/LIT-1710.md)) and Gaertner et al. 1993
  ([LIT-1709](../literature.d/LIT-1709.md))**, both unread. The chapter gives one sentence of the
  first and none of the second. It restates the model, the four
  representations and the two routes of the 1989 study.
- **Self-categorization theory ([THEORY-1114](../theory.d/THEORY-1114.md)).** The model draws on
  it. Recategorization is a change in the level of self-categorization,
  induced by the experimenter. The chapter does not use metacontrast or
  measure depersonalization.
- **Biel ([NOTE-1299](NOTE-1299.md))** studies European common in-group identity
  among football fans by survey. Nier et al.'s stadium study is the

- **Cialdini et al. ([LIT-1728](../literature.d/LIT-1728.md)) and Batson et al. ([LIT-1700](../literature.d/LIT-1700.md)).** The
  chapter measures empathic concern with Batson's items and finds a shared
  identity raises it.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).** The chapter bears on it in
  three ways. These are inferences; the authors do not discuss
  selflessness.
  - It supports the thesis's prediction that helping follows the "we".
    Moving an out-group member inside a shared category raised help to
    the in-group level, in the laboratory and at a stadium. The boundary
    was moved by the experimenter, not by what participants cared about,
    which is the kind of manipulation the thesis needs.
  - It qualifies what "larger self" means. The "we" that extends help is
    the helper's own representation of it. A minority member who claims
    the common identity on his own terms is helped as if outside it. If
    the larger self is the helper's self, the thesis predicts this; but
    then the extended self is not a fact both parties share. It is one
    party's category, and it may extend only to those who fit its
    prototype.
  - It shows a shared identity working through empathy. In the second
    study the common identity raised empathic concern and empathy carried
    part of the effect on helping.
- **Batson's objection ([CLAIM-494](../claims.d/CLAIM-494.md)).** The mediation runs from
  identity to empathy to helping. That is the reverse of Batson's design,
  which holds merging fixed and finds empathy still predicts helping. The
  inference: identity and empathy are not independent routes. A shared
  identity raises empathy, so controlling for merging may remove part of
  empathy's effect. The residual direct effect of condition (β = .35)
  shows identity also acts by another route.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md)).** Recategorization is a
  manipulation of the self's extent that does not go through caring: the
  experimenter changes the category, and helping follows. That is the
  shape of answer the objection asks for. But no study here measures
  self-categorization; the manipulation is assumed to work, and caring
  (empathy) is downstream of it.
- **The group entity versus a category ([TERM-096](../terms.d/TERM-096.md)).** The model's
  common identities are categories (a university, a city, "Americans"),
  not group entities.
- **A dated note should be added to [CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md).** Proposed text:
  "Dovidio et al. (NOTE-1286) review experiments in which recategorizing
  two groups as one removed in-group bias in helping, in the laboratory
  and at a football stadium. The shared identity raised empathic concern,
  which carried part of the effect. But White participants helped a Black
  student who called himself a Black Colgate student no more than one who
  called himself Black. If helping follows an extended self, the self it
  follows is the helper's category, and a dual identity can fall outside
  it."

## Limitations

- **No sample sizes, test statistics or design details** for most
  studies; the chapter reports means and p-values selectively. The 1997
  One Group means are not labelled in-group and out-group.
- **One confederate, one campus**, Colgate, for both dual-identity
  studies.
- **Cheap helping.** Posters, hours promised on a form, a short
  interview.
- **The mirror condition is missing**, so whether the effect is
  discordance or the dual identity reading as exclusionary is open
  (p. 205).
- **Not the originals.** The 1989 and 1993 papers' designs, measures and
  data are not reported.
- **Typos.**
  - "majority and majority groups" for majority and minority (p. 197).
  - "handle a sealed envelope" for hand (p. 202).
  - "Westchester State University" (pp. 195–196); the university is
    West Chester.
  - Wolsko, Park and Judd is cited as 2006 (p. 197) and 2000 (p. 207);
    these may be two papers, but the copy has no reference list to check.

## Open questions

- Does recategorization extend costly help, not only cheap help?
- Would measuring self-categorization (for instance with the overlap
  circles) show that it, not empathy, carries the effect of a shared
  identity on helping, as the thesis would predict?
- Do minority members extend help across a group line under a dual
  identity, as the model would predict from their preference?
