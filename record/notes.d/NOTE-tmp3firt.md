# NOTE-tmp3firt: Intergenerational transmission of paternal trauma among US Civil War ex-POWs

## Contribution

A large human test of whether a father's severe, time-limited hardship
shortens the lives of children conceived after it. The setting gives two
natural controls: men captured when prisoners were exchanged quickly,
against men captured when exchanges stopped; and, within families,
children born before the war. It adds a test of reversibility, by the
child's quarter of birth, and argues for a mechanism by eliminating
others.

## Key insight

The timing of capture, not the fact of it, separates fathers who starved
from fathers who did not. Exchanges stopped in mid-1863 over the treatment
of black soldiers and their white officers, and camps filled. Among
survivors to 1900, wartime records mention scurvy for 11% of non-POWs,
14% of POWs captured before July 1863, 23% of those captured between July
1863 and July 1864, and 14% of those captured after June 1864 (Data and
Methods). Only the sons of the 23% group show the effect, so capture as
such, and whatever selected men into it, is controlled.

## Assumptions

- **Exchange-period POWs are a fair control** for no-exchange POWs:
  similar men, captured at a different time. Ex-POWs did differ from
  non-POWs (earlier enlistment, more labourers, fewer farmers), so those
  traits are controlled.
- **Quarter of birth indexes maternal nutrition in utero.** Second-quarter
  births are taken to have had inadequate late-gestation nutrition, and
  fourth-quarter births adequate; the justification is in the SI,
  following Doblhammer and Vaupel (Discussion, "Mechanisms").
- **Proof by elimination.** If socioeconomic, family, selection and
  maternal channels each fail to explain the result, and the pattern
  (sons only, by quarter of birth) fits epigenetics, then epigenetics is
  the best explanation.
- **Mortality after 45 is the outcome** because the linked data suit it
  best (SI). Earlier deaths are not studied.

## Key results

- **Sample** (Data and Methods). 2,342 children of 732 no-exchange POWs,
  2,416 children of 715 exchange-period POWs, and 15,145 children of 4,920
  non-POW veterans, all born after 1866 and surviving to 45: 19,903
  children of 6,367 fathers. Fathers from two longitudinal Union Army
  samples drawn from service and pension records; children linked to
  death records and censuses.
- **Model.** Cox proportional hazards for years lived after 45, clustered
  on family, stratified on enlistment year; father's POW group
  interacted with child's sex.
- **Between families** (Table 1). Sons of no-exchange POWs:
  - Specification 1, controls for birth year and father's characteristics
    at enlistment: HR 1.110 (SE 0.045), P = 0.009. Sons of exchange-period
    POWs: 1.016, P = 0.663. No-exchange against exchange: χ²(1) = 4.06,
    P = 0.044.
  - Adding postwar paternal wealth, occupation, county density, birth
    order and siblings: 1.104, P = 0.012.
  - Sons alive at 21 in 1910, with their own occupation and marriage
    controlled: 1.115, P = 0.021 (8,427 sons).
  - Adding maternal lifespan and maternal grandfather's wealth: 1.106,
    P = 0.011.
  - In the last two, the difference from exchange-period POWs' sons is
    P = 0.062 and 0.077.
  - Daughters: every hazard ratio between 0.959 and 0.997, none
    significant.
- **Within families** (Table 2; 1,067 sons of 342 fathers, 855 daughters
  of 275 fathers, war-born children excluded). Sons of no-exchange POWs
  born after the war against their brothers born before: HR 2.225
  (SE 0.932), P = 0.056. No-exchange against exchange: χ²(1) = 4.71,
  P = 0.030; against non-POW: χ²(1) = 2.20, P = 0.138. Daughters: no
  effect. The authors ask that the magnitude "be interpreted with
  caution".
- **Quarter of birth** (Table 3; 10,313 sons of 5,615 fathers). Sons of
  no-exchange POWs, relative to non-POWs' sons born in the second
  quarter: first quarter 1.090 (P = 0.198), second 1.204 (P = 0.008),
  third 1.101 (P = 0.134), fourth 1.013 (P = 0.849). Second against
  fourth, χ²(1) = 4.28, P = 0.039.
- **Channels examined and, by the authors' account, excluded** (Results;
  mostly SI Tables S11–S16, not seen).
  - Sons' socioeconomic outcomes and family structure were barely
    affected; sons of no-exchange POWs were, if anything, less often
    divorced.
  - POW status did not predict the wife's family wealth or the mother's
    longevity.
  - No-exchange POWs alive in 1900 themselves had a hazard of 1.14 against
    non-POWs, so there was "little attenuation" from father to son.
    Controlling for the father's lifespan changed the sons' estimates
    only modestly.
  - Excess deaths among sons were mainly from cerebral haemorrhage and,
    marginally, cancer; none from suicide or accidents. Cause of death
    comes only from the few states with online indexes; the text says the
    SI examines excess mortality "for the 26% of children with this
    information".
- **Comparisons offered.** The effect is comparable to that of low
  childhood socioeconomic status or urban residence then (SI); the
  Överkalix hazard ratio for paternal undernutrition, 1.11, is of
  similar size (Discussion).

## Concepts

- **exchange and no-exchange periods**: captured before July 1863 or after
  June 1864, when prisoners were soon exchanged, versus between, when
  they were held in worsening camps.
- **hazard ratio**: the ratio of instantaneous death rates, here for years
  lived after 45. The paper glosses 1.11 as "1.11 times more likely to
  die".
- **reversibility**: an inherited effect undone by a later exposure, here
  maternal nutrition, as in the animal studies the authors cite.
- **proof by elimination**: arguing for one mechanism because measured
  alternatives fail to explain the pattern.

## Connections

- **The question it is filed for (QUESTION-tmp8bttd).** It is one of the
  question's two epidemiological pieces on the germline route, with
  Överkalix (Pembrey et al., LIT-tmpmxksg), which the paper leans on for
  its sex-specific, Y-linked reading.
- **The womb (Heijmans et al., LIT-tmpkgr1s).** The paper cites the Dutch
  Hunger Winter methylation work, and its quarter-of-birth result puts
  the womb inside the paternal story: the mother's late-gestation
  nutrition is what decides whether the father's hardship shows.
- **Parenting and silence (Danieli, LIT-tmp881f5).** The paper cites
  chapters from Danieli's handbook for the psychological channel, then
  argues against it: an effect on boys born in one season "suggests that
  psychological transmission to boys only is an unlikely explanation".
- **Dias and Ressler (LIT-tmpk0wlg).** Not cited. Both papers exclude
  social transmission by design and arrive at a biological route, one in
  mice with a molecular result, one in men with none.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** The question's framing, that on
  the biological route no self is needed, fits this paper's account of
  itself. What passes is a mortality risk, not distress, memory or
  meaning. These are inferences; the paper does not discuss identity or
  the self.
  - The outcome is death from stroke and cancer after 45. It is the
    least self-involving outcome one could choose, and the authors find
    no excess suicides or accidents, which they take as evidence against
    a psychological route.
  - The effect depends on the son's season of birth, which no
    identification with the father or his cause could track.
- **It bears on the identity route at one point, and against it.** In the
  Conclusion the authors suggest that psychological transmission may be
  commoner among children of POWs held by Japan, Korea or Vietnam, or of
  genocide survivors, because Civil War POWs "returned home to a grateful
  nation. Their status as heroes may have given their experiences
  meaning." That is a hypothesis that a group's narrative, a shared
  meaning given to the injury, *protects* descendants rather than
  injuring them. It is untested here. If it held, the identity route
  would be a moderator of harm, not only a carrier of it. That is an
  inference from one sentence.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** Not addressed.
- **QUESTION-tmp8bttd.** The paper does not supply what the question asks
  for, a human study that separates germline from uterine, parenting
  and cultural routes. It separates socioeconomic and family-fixed
  channels well. It has no data on parenting, on PTSD (unrecognized
  then) or on family interaction, and its strongest mechanistic result
  shows the womb mattering.
- **A dated note is proposed for QUESTION-tmp8bttd** (section 3). None on
  CLAIM-tmpjylq8.
- **No THEORY filed.** One study whose mechanism is argued, not measured.
- **No ML instruction.**

## Limitations

- **Mechanism by elimination only.** The authors: "we cannot reject
  definitively cultural or psychological explanations", and even if the
  route is epigenetic "we cannot disentangle the impact of the stress of
  captivity from that of starvation" (Strengths and Limitations).
- **No data on what would test the psychological route**: interactions
  in the family, paternal PTSD, children's physical or mental health.
- **Older-age mortality only**, and cause of death sparse.
- **The within-family estimate is not significant at .05**, and the
  within-family test against non-POW families is not significant at all.
- **Quarter of birth is a proxy** for maternal nutrition, not a measure.
- **Most robustness checks are in the SI**, which was not read.
- **Generalization unknown**, by the authors' own statement; Civil War
  POWs faced "neither torture nor murder".
- **Inconsistencies and typos.**
  - The introduction gives "4,593 children of 1,407 former POWs and
    15,310 children of 4,960 non-POW veterans"; the analytical sample and
    Table 1 give 4,758 children of 1,447 ex-POWs and 15,145 children of
    4,920 non-POWs. The difference is not explained in the main text.
  - Results: second-quarter sons of no-exchange POWs were 1.2 times
    likelier to die than sons "of ex-POWs during the no-exchange period";
    it should be the exchange period, as the abstract has it.
  - Results: second-quarter sons of no-exchange POWs "were 1.1 times more
    likely to die than sons of non-POWs born during the fourth quarter".
    I could not reconcile this with Table 3 (1.204 against 0.933).
  - The exchange period is defined as after June 1864, but exchanges are
    said to have resumed in December 1864.
  - "childrens' years lived", "programing", "geneontology",
    "veterans'children". In the references: Heijmans et al. 2008 given as
    PNAS volume "104" (it is 105); "adpative" (ref. 19); "Pennsylvannia"
    and "edisserations" (ref. 43).

## Open questions

- Does the effect appear in grandsons, as in Överkalix? A third-generation
  result would separate germline transmission from the son's own
  exposure through his mother.
- Would sons of men from the same units who escaped capture, or adoptees,
  separate the father's biology from the household he made?
- Did the heroic meaning of captivity in the North protect sons
  psychologically, as the authors speculate? A comparison with
  Confederate veterans held in Union camps, who returned to defeat, would
  test it.
