# THEORY-tmpx9sfw: Gender and race, defined as a feminist and antiracist theory needs them, are hierarchical social positions: a woman is someone subordinated in part because she is taken to have bodily features presumed to show a female's reproductive role, a racialized group is one subordinated or privileged in part because its members are taken to have features presumed to show ancestry from a region, and on these definitions both are real, social and contingent

## Source

Sally Haslanger (2000), "Gender and Race: (What) Are They? (What) Do We
Want Them To Be?", *Noûs* 34(1):31–55, [LIT-480](../literature.d/LIT-480.md), read in full in [NOTE-394](../notes.d/NOTE-394.md).
Page numbers are the journal's.

## What was actually shown

**The method (pp. 32–35).** "What is gender?" has three readings.
Conceptual: what our concept is. Descriptive: what kind our usage tracks.
Analytical: what concept serves our legitimate purposes. The paper's
project is analytical, linked to the descriptive claim that gender and race
talk tracks social kinds "typically obscured by the manifest content" of
everyday concepts (p. 34). Appropriating an ordinary term is warranted when
its central functions remain (p. 35, n. 6) and the politics are acceptable,
a condition the paper leaves general.

**The definitions.**

- *Woman* (p. 42). S is a woman iff (i) S is regularly observed or
  imagined to have bodily features presumed to be evidence of a female's
  biological role in reproduction; (ii) having them marks S, within the
  dominant ideology, as someone who ought to occupy positions that are in
  fact subordinate; (iii) satisfying (i) and (ii) plays a role in S's
  systematic subordination along some dimension. *Man* is the privileged
  counterpart. One can *function as* a woman in a context without being one
  (pp. 42–43).
- *Racialized group* (p. 44). Parallel, with features presumed in a
  context to show ancestral links to a region, and subordination or
  privilege. Italians, Germans and the Irish have been racialized in the US
  and are not now (p. 45). Note 17 lists four qualifications.
- *Gender* and *ethnicity* (pp. 45, 50–51) are the non-hierarchical genera,
  so a just society's categories can be described in the same terms.

**Two objections answered by the project's values.** Commonality: women
share a kind of position, sexually marked subordination, whose content
varies (p. 45). Normativity: choosing which facts matter on explicit values
is "both inevitable and important" (p. 46).

**The conclusion (pp. 51–52).** Gender and race are real and social, not
chosen, hierarchical as we know them, and contingent. "The world itself
can't tell us what gender is" (p. 52).

**What could have come out otherwise.** Little, by design. The definitions
are stipulated and defended by their purpose. The one claim that could fail
is the descriptive one, and the paper does not put it to a test.

## What this does not say

- **That this is what "woman" means.** The author is "less committed to
  saying that this is what gender is and what race is, than to saying that
  these are important categories that a feminist antiracist theory needs"
  (p. 52).
- **That every female is a woman.** Non-oppressed females, if any, are not
  women in this sense, and the paper accepts it: "It is part of the project
  of feminism to bring about a day when there are no more women" (p. 46).
- **That the empirical premises are shown.** That there are no racial genes
  and that classification differs between Brazil and the US are given as
  "matters of established fact" without citation (p. 43).
- **That oppression or "dominant ideology" is analysed.** Both are
  placeholders (pp. 39–40; n. 14).
- **That it handles intersectional categories as categories.** Only the
  content of a position varies with race and class (p. 39).
- **That trans women are covered.** Ritchie's n. 30 ([LIT-486](../literature.d/LIT-486.md)) reports
  Jenkins's charge that this account excludes them. [NOTE-394](../notes.d/NOTE-394.md) does not
  examine it, and neither does this document.

## Connections

- **Ritchie 2018** ([LIT-486](../literature.d/LIT-486.md), [THEORY-tmpll6jt](THEORY-tmpll6jt.md)) reads these definitions as
  identifying *women* with a node in a structure of subordination, with the
  marker as a requirement on occupiers. On her terms these are feature
  social groups, kinds, not organised wholes.
- **Ásta** ([LIT-491](../literature.d/LIT-491.md), unread) is the rival account of the same categories:
  conferred constraints and enablements.
- **Epstein's survey** ([LIT-487](../literature.d/LIT-487.md), [THEORY-tmpavyju](THEORY-tmpavyju.md)) distinguishes what
  grounds a category from what anchors it. Haslanger's definitions say
  what grounds being a woman; the dominant ideology is the anchor. The
  placement is the record's.
- **Durkheim** ([LIT-484](../literature.d/LIT-484.md), [THEORY-tmpv1zbf](THEORY-tmpv1zbf.md)). Both locate a category's reality
  in social relations rather than individuals' properties ([NOTE-394](../notes.d/NOTE-394.md)).
- No bearing on the group-agency strand: nothing here treats a gender or a
  race as an agent.
