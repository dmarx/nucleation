summary: >-
  A memorial lecture, not a study. Volkan defines a large group as thousands or millions of people who mostly never meet but share sentiments, and pictures large-group identity as a tent's canvas worn over each member's individual identity, with the leader as its pole. Membership in ethnic, national and religious groups begins in childhood, through identification with parents' investment in the group and the images and "ego tasks" they deposit in the child; after adolescence a core large-group identity cannot be changed. From unofficial dialogues between conflicting groups (1980s to early 2000s), he reports that the central psychological factor in large-group conflict is the need to protect large-group identity, and that a participant who felt that identity threatened would speak with emotion of an injury suffered by ancestors centuries before. The shared image of such an event, "real, fantasized or even mythologized", undergoes a change of function as it passes down the generations and becomes an identity marker: a chosen trauma. Chosen traumas feed entitlement ideologies and, in a "time collapse", make the old event feel recent, as Milošević did with Kosovo 1389, with dehumanization and purification following. Recent traumas, the Holocaust among them, are not chosen traumas but undigested ones, bound up with large-group mourning.
