# CLAIM-tmpi98gg: A corporation can be an agent fit to be held responsible while the costs its conduct imposes on others gain authority over what it does only through its decision procedures, its members' role duties, its regulators and its markets, so that it can hold signals of a harm without acting on them; "psychopathic" fits it only in that functional sense, naming a design rather than a lost capacity, which puts the remedy in its structure rather than in any member's character

## The claim

Three parts.

1. **Agent and responsible.** A corporation whose decision structure
   makes members' acts its own is an agent (French, [THEORY-131](../theory.d/THEORY-131.md)), and one
   that forms its own judgments by authorised avowal is fit to be held
   responsible (Pettit, [THEORY-139](../theory.d/THEORY-139.md)). Its reasons need not be any member's
   ([CLAIM-271](CLAIM-271.md)).
2. **No standing authority for others' costs.** A person's harm to
   another normally weighs with them as more than a cost to themselves:
   through empathy, guilt, the anticipation of another's resentment.
   Nothing in a corporation does this as standing equipment. A group can
   have functional, unfelt emotions at most ([THEORY-125](../theory.d/THEORY-125.md), with no actual
   case yet shown), and List doubts it is conscious ([NOTE-340](../notes.d/NOTE-340.md)). The
   corporation can still *hold* information about the harm it does: its
   investigators, complaint lines and control functions collect it. What
   it lacks is anything that gives that information authority over its
   decisions by default ([CLAIM-349](CLAIM-349.md) separates access to a signal from its
   authority over action). Others' costs gain that authority through four
   routes: its decision procedures (a rule that weighs a harm), its
   members' role duties (Collins, [LIT-tmp3qow8](../literature.d/LIT-tmp3qow8.md): members "use their role
   in the collective with a view to the collective doing its duty"), its
   regulators (law and liability that turn a harm into a cost), and its
   markets (customers and workers who leave). Where none of the four
   carries a harm, the signal can sit in the corporation unacted on.
   This is Bakan's "can neither recognize nor act upon moral reasons to
   refrain from harming others" ([LIT-tmp8tm3e](../literature.d/LIT-tmp8tm3e.md)), corrected: the
   corporation can recognize them, in the sense of holding them; what it
   lacks by default is a route from them to action.
3. **Functional, not clinical.** On the clinical construct, psychopathy
   is a deficit in a being whose kind normally has the capacity. A
   corporation's kind has no such default. So "psychopathic" fits only
   in a functional sense: the corporation's conduct can match the
   lifestyle and antisocial features of the construct (exploitation,
   failure to accept responsibility, indifference to harm) without
   anything in it corresponding to a damaged affect.

**What follows.** The remedy lies where the authority would come from: in
procedures, role duties, liability and exit, not in finding members with
better character, which a corporation's reasons need not reflect. That
matches Bakan's prescription, regulation, and parts from Boddy's
([LIT-tmpcyd6d](../literature.d/LIT-tmpcyd6d.md)), whose remedy is screening out psychopathic individuals.

**The documented case.** Wells Fargo's Community Bank, 2002–2016
([CASE-tmpuxgzy](../cases.d/CASE-tmpuxgzy.md), from the board's own investigation). The report puts the
root cause in the sales culture and performance management and calls the
leadership's bad-employee account a confusion of "effect" with "cause".
The bank held the signals for twelve years: a 2004 internal investigation
tied cheating to fear of job loss and was not escalated, complaints that
reached the CEO were passed down without follow-up, and the termination
figure was deleted before the board's risk committee saw it. The board
learned the scale from regulators. The remedies were structural. The
case also corrects a mechanism Bakan's description gives: the accounts
earned little (1.7% incurred fees that were not reversed), so the harm
was not done because its benefits outweighed its costs. The structure
pursued its own metric, sales growth, and nothing gave the harm authority
against it. The claim needs only that.

## What it does not say

- It does not say a corporation is not responsible. Others' costs lacking standing
  authority in it is compatible with being fit to be held
  responsible on Pettit's conditions, which ask for normative judgment
  and control, not feeling.
- It does not say corporations generally harm, or that their members are
  blameless. Members' role duties are one of the four routes, and a
  member who fails theirs is answerable for it.
- It does not say the four routes are equal, or that any of them is
  sufficient.
- It does not say a corporation cannot know of its harms. The case shows
  one that knew, in parts, for years.
- It does not settle whether a corporation could be built with standing
  authority for others' costs (a constitution that weighs them, as
  some benefit-corporation charters claim to). If one could, the claim
  says it would be a design, not a recovery.
