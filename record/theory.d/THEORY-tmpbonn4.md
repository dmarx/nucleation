# THEORY-tmpbonn4: Among 100 Chapel Hill voters not yet committed to a candidate in September–October 1968, the aggregate emphasis they gave the campaign's main issues correlated +.967 with the emphasis of the major news items, and +.979 with that of the minor items, in the nine media serving them; and voters leaning to a candidate agreed more with all the news than with news about their own side in 18 of 24 comparisons

## Source

McCombs and Shaw (1972), *Public Opinion Quarterly* 36(2): 176–187,
[LIT-tmpulqti](../literature.d/LIT-tmpulqti.md), read in full in [NOTE-tmpr2fo5](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpr2fo5.md).

## What was actually shown

One hundred registered voters from five Chapel Hill precincts, filtered to
those "who had not yet definitely decided how to vote", were asked between
18 September and 6 October 1968 to name "the two or three main things which
you think the government should concentrate on doing something about",
"regardless of what politicians say". The news and editorial content of the
nine media that the spring pretest found to serve the community was coded,
for 12 September–6 October, into fifteen categories, and split into major
and minor items by space, position and time (intercoder reliability above
.90).

The correlation between the media's emphasis on the main campaign issues
and the voters' was +.967 for major items and +.979 for minor items. For
the 45 respondents who leaned to a candidate, Table 3 compares, for four
media, the correlation of their issue emphasis with all the news against
its correlation with news about their own party. All the news correlates
higher in 18 of the 24 comparisons, own-party news in 5, and one is a tie.

What could have come out otherwise: the voters' issues could have matched
the media poorly, or matched their own party's news better than the whole.
Selective perception predicts the second, and it lost 18 to 5.

## What this does not say

- **Not that the media caused the voters' ranking.** The design is a
  cross-sectional aggregate correlation. The authors: "The existence of an
  agenda-setting function of the mass media is not proved by the
  correlations reported here." The causal hypothesis is
  [THEORY-tmp98xm5](THEORY-tmp98xm5.md), Proposed.
- **Not that any voter's attitudes changed.** Only the ranking of issues
  was measured.
- **Not a statistical result in the usual sense.** No test is reported,
  and the correlations run over a handful of issue categories. The
  coefficient type is not stated.
- **Not independent of the candidates.** The media's emphasis reflected
  "to a considerable degree the emphasis by candidates".
- **Not beyond the sample.** One community, undecided voters only, one
  campaign window.
