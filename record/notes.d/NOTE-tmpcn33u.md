# NOTE-tmpcn33u: Leaving a Legacy: Intergenerational Allocations of Benefits and Burdens

## Contribution

An experimental paper from the legacy programme of Wade-Benzoni and
colleagues. It asks whether passing on burdens (debt, waste, disease)
differs from passing on benefits (energy, money, food) when the recipient
lives later and has no voice. It argues that intergenerational decisions
are distinctive because they join a time delay to a self–other trade-off,
and that this combination is what lets a decision bear on one's legacy.
Three studies measure attitudes and three measure allocations. For the
record it is also the only available account of Wade-Benzoni 2006
([LIT-tmpcpdlk](../literature.d/LIT-tmpcpdlk.md)), which it cites three times.

## Key insight

Bad outcomes weigh more than good ones, but which way that pushes depends
on the context. In negotiation, burdens make people more selfish. Here,
where the recipient is in the future and powerless, burdens made people
less selfish. The authors explain the reversal by legacy: "people care more
about avoiding leaving a burdensome negative legacy than about leaving a
beneficial positive legacy" (p. 13). Legacy, in turn, is a matter of the
self: "the motivation underlying legacies is largely based on the desire to
extend the self into the future" (p. 26).

## Assumptions

- **Legacy needs others.** "One cannot create a legacy by having a fleeting
  impact, or by affecting merely one's own future self" (pp. 11–12,
  citing Wade-Benzoni 2006b). A legacy is an enduring effect on other
  people who come later.
- **Legacy is symbolic immortality.** Leaving a legacy "provides a symbolic
  form of immortality" (p. 7) and expresses "a desire to extend the self
  beyond mortal life" (p. 12, citing Becker, Kotre and McAdams).
- **Bad is stronger than good** (Baumeister et al. 2001, quoted at length
  on p. 10).
- **Organizational successors stand in for generations.** A replacement
  employee or another subsidiary in five years counts as a future
  generation (pp. 8, 20, 24).

## Key results

- **Experiment 1** (pp. 13–15; 76 undergraduates, waiting for a chance at
  basketball tickets). A global-warming passage framed as diminished food
  and water for future generations (benefits) or as spreading disease
  (burdens). A nine-item "modified" generativity scale (α = .86) was higher
  after burdens, M = 4.90 (SD 1.01), than after benefits, M = 4.10 (SD .96),
  F(1, 74) = 12.44, p = .001. Its items are about oneself living on: "I want
  to be remembered after I die", "I hope that in some way, part of me will
  live on after I die", "I feel that I have done something that will
  survive after I die".
- **Experiment 2** (pp. 15–17; 101 MBA students, class exercise). Consuming
  beneficial resources versus minimizing burdensome substances.
  - Legacy concern, two single items analysed separately because they
    correlated only r = .191: higher after burdens (5.89 vs 5.31),
    F(1, 99) = 9.42, p = .003. Avoiding a negative legacy mattered more than
    leaving a positive one (5.79 vs 5.44), F(1, 99) = 4.72, p = .03; no
    interaction.
  - Responsibility to future generations, one item: 6.0 vs 5.5,
    F(1, 99) = 7.22, p = .008.
  - Affinity for future generations, one item: 5.62 vs 5.0,
    F(1, 99) = 9.62, p = .003.
  - Ethical concerns, four items (α = .88): 5.5 vs 5.1, F(1, 99) = 4.64,
    p = .03.
- **Experiment 3** (pp. 17–19; 64 MBA students). Recalling a time one
  allocated a burden, rather than a benefit, between oneself and a future
  person who could not reciprocate raised moral emotions (disgust, regret,
  guilt, shame, embarrassment; α = .88): 2.18 vs 1.54 on a 1–5 scale,
  t(62) = 2.63, p = .01.
- **Experiment 4** (pp. 19–23; 32 university staff, paid $5 plus up to
  $10). A departing employee splits a $100 sales fund between profit now
  (5% to oneself) and marketing that pays a successor 50% more, and decides
  how many of 20 boxes to deliver now or leave to the successor at 50% more
  cost. Everyone made both decisions. On a common metric, self-interest was
  higher for benefits (M = 13.10, SD 5.51) than for burdens (M = 8.03,
  SD 7.61), F(1, 28) = 10.58, reported p < .001. Having made the sales
  oneself made no difference.
- **Experiment 5** (pp. 23–26; 131 MBA students). An energy executive
  keeps "Delta" energy or passes it to another subsidiary that will use it
  50% better, either now (dictator context) or in five years
  (intergenerational context); or neutralizes Delta's waste or passes it on
  at 50% more cost.
  - Present self-interest (Table 1, higher = more for oneself now):
    benefits 47.00 to a present other and 64.80 to a future other; burdens
    44.03 and 37.91. Valence main effect F(1, 127) = 6.06, p = .02;
    interaction F(1, 127) = 2.89, reported p = .05. Only benefits with a
    future recipient differed from the other three cells.
  - "There were ethical considerations that came into play", one item
    (Table 2): benefits 4.58 present and 3.80 future; burdens 4.26 and
    5.37. Interaction F(1, 128) = 7.50, p = .007. Ethics predicted lower
    self-interest, B = −4.52, and with it entered the valence × timing
    interaction dropped to B = −16.91, p = .17; the test of the reduction
    was marginal, z = 1.84, p = .065.
- **Experiment 6** (pp. 26–27; 92 undergraduates). The same task, all with
  a future recipient, but the present party was either oneself or another
  member of the other subsidiary (Table 3): benefits 52.43 with self and
  38.48 without; burdens 32.18 and 37.59. Valence F(1, 88) = 4.50, p = .04;
  interaction F(1, 88) = 3.78, p = .05. Removing the self lowered present
  favouritism for benefits, t(88) = 2.91, p < .01, and did nothing for
  burdens.
- **Practical recommendations** (pp. 29–30). Have managers identify with
  their successors, as in common-ingroup work (Gaertner et al. 1989) and
  minimal groups; highlight ethics and the burdensome side of decisions.

## Concepts

- **legacy**: an enduring effect of one's behaviour on other people who
  come later; a route to symbolic immortality.
- **intergenerational dilemma**: a choice in which what serves future
  others requires a sacrifice now, marked by power asymmetry, no direct
  reciprocity, a self–other trade-off, a time delay, role transition and
  growing consequences (p. 19).
- **intergenerational generosity (beneficence)**: allocating more benefit,
  or less burden, to future others than self-interest would.
- **resource valence**: whether what is allocated is a benefit or a
  burden.
- **generativity**: as used here, a nine-item scale of wanting to be
  remembered and to make a lasting mark, presented as "concern for and
  commitment to the well-being of future generations" (p. 14).

## Bearing on the record

- **The legacy question ([QUESTION-tmpruhu4](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md)).** These are inferences; the
  paper does not discuss egoism.
  - The authors take the thesis's reading as their premise. Legacy is the
    wish "to extend the self beyond mortal life" (p. 12) and "to extend the
    self into the future" (p. 26). But they do not test it. Nothing
    measures how far the self extends, and legacy concern is never measured
    in a study that measures generosity.
  - What extends is the self, not the self's circle. The generativity items
    are about oneself being remembered and living on. Future people figure
    as the medium of that survival, not as part of the self. That is the
    temporal extension in [QUESTION-tmpruhu4](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md), symbolic immortality, rather
    than a social extension to include those who come after.
  - Experiment 6 cuts against an extended-self reading of the generosity.
    Putting the self into the trade-off raised self-favouring with benefits
    and left burdens unchanged. What the self's presence did was make
    people keep more, not give more. The generosity with burdens is as well
    explained by a moral brake on imposing harm on the powerless, which the
    authors themselves invoke (p. 12), as by legacy.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** The paper's framing is the
  thesis in time: acting for future generations as acting for a self that
  outlives the body. Its data do not separate that reading from plain
  self-interest restrained by ethics.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** "Affinity for future
  generations" rose with the burden framing (5.62 vs 5.0), alongside
  responsibility and ethical concern. One item, moving with every measure of
  caring, cannot show that affinity comes apart from caring.
- **Batson's objection ([CLAIM-tmpybc4k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpybc4k.md)).** Not addressed.
- **A dated note on [QUESTION-tmpruhu4](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md) is proposed.** No note on [CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md) itself: the paper names the
  thesis's reading but gives it no test. Proposed text:

  > Wade-Benzoni, Sondak and Galinsky (NOTE-tmpcn33u), read in place of
  > Wade-Benzoni 2006, define legacy as the wish to extend the self beyond
  > death and show that leaving burdens, rather than using up benefits,
  > raises concern with being remembered and generosity to future others.
  > But legacy concern and generosity are never measured in the same study,
  > and the generosity effect is people keeping benefits for themselves
  > when the other person will get them later. Including the self in the
  > trade-off made people keep more, not give more. The experiments show
  > self-interest discounting future others and a moral brake on passing
  > harm forward, more than a self that includes them.

## Limitations

- **Two halves never joined.** Experiments 1–3 measure legacy, affinity
  and emotion; Experiments 4–6 measure allocation. No study has both, so
  the claim that legacy concern drives allocation is inferred across
  studies.
- **Small samples and class exercises.** Experiment 4 had 32 participants
  in four cells; Experiments 2, 3 and 5 were MBA class exercises debriefed
  in class.
- **Hypothetical stakes**, except Experiment 4's few dollars. Future
  generations are replacements and subsidiaries, five years on at most.
- **Single items** for legacy, responsibility, affinity and ethics. The two
  legacy items correlated only .19.
- **The generativity scale is a selection.** The nine items kept are about
  one's own survival and mark, not care for the next generation, so it
  measures legacy striving more than generativity in McAdams's sense.
- **Reported p-values that do not match their statistics.**
  - Experiment 4: F(1, 28) = 10.58 is printed as p < .001; it is p ≈ .003.
  - Experiment 5: the interaction F(1, 127) = 2.89 is printed as p = .05;
    two-tailed it is p ≈ .09. The paper does not say it tested one-tailed.
    This is the result behind Hypothesis 8.
- **Typos.**
  - "we a conducted" (p. 16); "a greater a sense" (p. 20); "SE = 12,31"
    for 12.31 (p. 25).
  - Eisenberg is cited as 2000 in the text (p. 17) and 2001 in the
    references, with volume 51 (the 2000 volume).
  - Taylor 1991 is given as *Psychological Bulletin* 11 (the volume is 110).
  - "Organizational Behavioral and Human Decision Processes" (twice, for
    *Organizational Behavior*).

## Open questions

- Does legacy concern, measured in the same participants, predict or
  mediate the allocation? A study that measured both would test the
  paper's central inference.
- Is the burden effect legacy or the moral asymmetry between imposing harm
  and withholding benefit? Telling participants that no one will ever know
  who left the burden would remove the legacy route and leave the moral
  one.
- What did Wade-Benzoni 2006 argue about whether intergenerational
  beneficence is altruism or egoism? The 2010 paper does not say;
  Wade-Benzoni and Tost 2009, "The egoism and altruism of intergenerational
  behavior", which the record does not hold, is the likely place.
