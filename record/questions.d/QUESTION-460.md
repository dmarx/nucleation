# QUESTION-460: Does an organized whole ground norms concerning its own constituent parts, and if it does, who bears the obligations those norms establish: the whole, where it is the kind of thing that can owe, or the agents and roles that maintain, control or benefit from the arrangement; and on what ground: that the parts constitute it, contribute to it, are vulnerable to it or are governed by it, its commitments to them, or its history with them?

## The question

The owner, U5 of the exchange "About Plantation Paradise" (2026-11-12),
after an hour on colonial theatre, dependency and exploitation:

> is there anything in this neighborhood that investigates the question
> from  the abstract perspective of probing the nature of obligations a
> whole might have towards its constituent parts?

The question is the owner's. Its direction is what makes it distinct:
not what members owe a collective, nor what a collective owes outsiders,
but what a whole owes the parts it is made of.

## Ground and bearer: two inquiries

The owner's review of [#488](https://github.com/dmarx/nucleation/issues/488) on 2026-11-13: "A whole can be the ground or
object of a normative judgment without necessarily being the bearer of
the corresponding obligation."

The review found two inquiries in the question, to be kept apart:

- **(a) The ground.** "One asks whether organization itself grounds a
  norm concerning constituent parts."
- **(b) The bearer.** "The other asks who could bear an obligation
  established by that norm. For a corporation and its employees, the
  corporation may be a plausible duty-bearer. For an ecosystem and its
  populations, or an organism and its cells, the answer is much less
  obvious. A relational arrangement might have normatively relevant
  properties without itself being an agent capable of owing anyone
  anything. Obligations might instead fall upon the agents who maintain,
  control, or benefit from the arrangement."

So the candidate bearers are the whole, where it is an agent ([TERM-074](../terms.d/TERM-074.md)
sense 3, often), and the agents or roles that maintain, control or
benefit from the arrangement. The nearest held distinction is [TERM-083](../terms.d/TERM-083.md)'s
split between moral agents, who can be responsible, and moral patients,
who are owed; it is about who is owed, not about whether what grounds an
obligation must also bear it. [QUESTION-128](QUESTION-128.md) asks what moral authority needs
beyond an organization's own dynamics, and [CLAIM-104](../claims.d/CLAIM-104.md) names a downward
direction from "an encompassing agent"; neither separates ground from
bearer. The review's verdict on where the work lies: "not in establishing
that institutions have obligations toward members, but in explaining how
normative relationships can be constituted by organized processes even
when the organization itself is not the kind of entity to which moral
agency or moral standing can straightforwardly be attributed."

## A discriminating test

The owner's, from the review: "Does being a constituent of a particular
whole generate obligations beyond those already arising from the
constituent's vulnerability, interests, rights, and exposure to another's
power?" Hold fixed a person's vulnerability, the controlling actors'
capacities and the relevant institutional consequences. In one case the
person is a constituent of the organization; in the other, the person is
affected by precisely the same arrangement but is not a constituent.
"Would the organization owe something additional in the first case?"

| Result | What it would show |
|---|---|
| Obligations arise specifically from constitution or membership | a genuinely whole–part ground of obligation; what gives membership that significance must then be explained |
| Obligations arise from vulnerability or control, regardless of membership | a relational responsibility theory that applies across boundaries; "The mereological relationship would help identify where the obligations apply without independently grounding them" |
| The arrangement creates normative constraints but is not itself a duty-bearer | obligations must be attributed to agents or roles maintaining the arrangement |

The review: "The third possibility is especially relevant to Nucleation's
broad ontology of organizations." The test is filed as a thought
experiment, [CASE-256](../cases.d/CASE-256.md), bearing on this question.

## Which sense of "organized whole"

The owner, correcting the reading pass on 2026-11-12: "keep in mind, my
use of 'organization' is more abstract than standin for 'institution'.
check the TERM." The term is [TERM-074](../terms.d/TERM-074.md). "Organized whole" here is its
sense 1 by default: a relational arrangement of processes, components,
roles or constraints, whose arrangement matters to some operation or
outcome. A whole in that sense need not be an institution (sense 3) or an
agent, and need not maintain itself (senses 2 and 4): an organism and its
cells, an ecosystem and its populations, an arrangement of processes. Its
parts need not be persons.

The question therefore has a prior part: whether, and in which sense of
obligation, a whole that is not an agent can bear obligations at all.
A12's three senses (functional, constitutive and moral) are where it
divides. A functional "obligation" of a non-agent whole, what it must
maintain to go on functioning, is [QUESTION-128](QUESTION-128.md)'s first half, norms of
function and failure defined from an organization's own dynamics. A moral
obligation needs a bearer that can owe; the record's only statement of the
downward direction, [CLAIM-104](../claims.d/CLAIM-104.md), is about "an encompassing agent", and
[TERM-083](../terms.d/TERM-083.md) defines standing on the side of the one owed, not the one who
owes. No entry asks whether a whole that is not an agent can owe its
parts anything in a sense beyond the functional, so that part stays here,
inside the question, and is not filed apart.

## Why it is a question

The record holds pieces near it and no question of it.

- **The direction was named once, and set aside.** The owner's U75 of an
  earlier exchange held that authenticity "gives us an account for
  normativity at least from the downward direction of
  superagent-->constituents" ([CLAIM-104](../claims.d/CLAIM-104.md)), and asked what grounds
  normativity across agents at one level ([QUESTION-007](QUESTION-007.md)). Both are
  Deferred with the authenticity line. [SYNTHESIS-023](../syntheses.d/SYNTHESIS-023.md) finds the content of
  the downward norm unstated. "On the reading that the whole owes its
  constituents non-domination it runs against" [CLAIM-362](../claims.d/CLAIM-362.md)'s own
  disclaimer ("We are proposing an organizational criterion, not
  asserting rights for neural modules"), and "on the other reading it
  needs a premise no component holds."
  This question asks that open part directly, for any whole, without the
  authenticity line's ownership criteria.
- **The organizational-normativity line holds the limits.** Viability
  does not establish what ought to persist ([CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md)). A description
  adequate to an institution's performance can merge states its members'
  claims distinguish, and the target cannot be justified by the
  description that serves it ([CLAIM-348](../claims.d/CLAIM-348.md), [CASE-110](../cases.d/CASE-110.md)). The line's one duty
  is a second-order duty of answerability, to keep violations knowable,
  contestable and correctable, a verdict only given an independently
  justified standard ([CLAIM-624](../claims.d/CLAIM-624.md)), and whether corrigibility is desirable
  at all is not established ([CLAIM-511](../claims.d/CLAIM-511.md)). None says to whom the duty is
  owed, or whether being a part is a ground of it.
- **Standing is defined, its ground left open.** [TERM-083](../terms.d/TERM-083.md) defines moral
  standing as being owed consideration for one's own sake and not only
  for one's effects on others. On that definition a part's functional
  importance to the whole is not its standing. What a whole owes a part
  that has standing, because it is that whole's part, is not asked.
- **[QUESTION-128](QUESTION-128.md)** asks whether norms can be defined from an
  organization's own dynamics and "what more do practical and moral
  authority need". This is the part of it that concerns the whole's
  obligations to its own parts.

## A's sharpening

A12 answered with literatures and turned the question several ways. The
sharpening is A's; adopting any of it is the owner's call.

- **Three senses of obligation**: "Functional obligation: What must the
  whole maintain to continue functioning?"; "Constitutive obligation:
  What commitments arise from the relationships that make the whole the
  kind of organization it is?"; "Moral obligation: What does the whole
  actually owe its constituents, regardless of whether honoring those
  claims improves the whole's performance or survival?" A: "The third
  cannot straightforwardly be derived from the first", citing [CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md)
  and [QUESTION-128](QUESTION-128.md).
- **Four directions.** A whole to an outside party, a constituent to the
  whole, the whole to a constituent, and one constituent to another. A:
  "Much collective-responsibility theory concentrates on A and B. Your
  question is principally about C. And C should not be presumed to
  follow from A, B, or the existence of the whole." Collins ([LIT-1426](../literature.d/LIT-1426.md))
  gives "a downward transmission of duties to members, not an account of
  duties owed to members", and Mellor ([LIT-2543](../literature.d/LIT-2543.md)) contests even that
  transmission.
- **Six candidate grounds**, each with its problem: constitution ("Why
  does being constitutive confer a moral claim?"), causal contribution
  ("Contribution alone need not generate entitlement"), vulnerability
  ("Which vulnerabilities generate duties, and why?"), governance ("What
  makes this authority legitimate or accountable?"), commitment ("What
  obligations exist without consent or promises?") and history ("Which
  obligations persist after the relationship changes?"). A separates
  obligations "constituted by a relationship" from those "triggered by
  the consequences of a relationship".
- **Parts differ in standing.** "A human constituent can possess moral
  claims that a cell, enzyme, or organizational subprocess does not
  possess in the same sense." A general account must tell parts with
  standing of their own from processes whose normativity is only
  functional, or it treats every constituent as a rights-holder, or every
  one as a means. The record's wholes range over both ([CLAIM-362](../claims.d/CLAIM-362.md)'s neural
  modules; the persons of an institution in [CASE-110](../cases.d/CASE-110.md)).
- **Holism against individualism.** From environmental ethics (Leopold,
  Callicott, Regan; Wardrope, [LIT-2538](../literature.d/LIT-2538.md)): "How can the flourishing or
  continued existence of a higher-order organization be morally important
  without permitting its interests to absorb or override all constituent
  claims?"
- **A's formulation** (A12 §8), offered "as a candidate research question
  rather than a thesis already established by the record":

  > Under what conditions does a self-maintaining organization acquire
  > obligations toward constituent processes or individuals, beyond the
  > requirements of its own functioning, in virtue of its role in
  > constituting, maintaining, governing, or transforming their
  > conditions of existence and agency?

  It narrows the owner's question to grounds in the whole's role. The
  owner's asks the nature of the obligations, and leaves the ground open.

A's own candidate principle, that responsibilities grow as the whole
governs more of what its parts need, was filed as a claim conditional on
a vulnerability principle ([CLAIM-671](../claims.d/CLAIM-671.md)). Since the owner's review
of 2026-11-13 that claim's mechanism is increased vulnerability to a
responsible agent's discretion, and its bearer is that agent, not the
whole.

## What would count as an answer

An account that says, for a whole and a part with standing of its own,
which relation between them (constitution, contribution, vulnerability,
governance, commitment or history) grounds a norm concerning the part,
with the further normative premise that ground needs; who bears the
obligation the norm establishes, the whole where it can owe or the agents
and roles that maintain, control or benefit from it; and that separates
that obligation from what the whole must do to persist. [CASE-256](../cases.d/CASE-256.md)'s
three results are the shapes such an answer can take. A
demonstration that no relation of part to whole grounds anything the
part is not already owed by any agent with the same power over it would
dissolve the question.

## Note of 2026-11-12: Lovett and Riedener, Thompson, Fineman and Wardrope, read

Readings of the reading pass of 2026-11-12, each placed by the [TERM-074](../terms.d/TERM-074.md)
sense it is about. None answers the question in its default sense 1.

- **Lovett and Riedener** ([THEORY-1819](../theory.d/THEORY-1819.md), [NOTE-1891](../notes.d/NOTE-1891.md)): sense 3,
  organizations as group agents with persons as members. They ask the
  opposite direction, what is owed to organizations, and answer that
  organizations have no moral status ("they themselves can make no claims
  on us at all", p. 223), so what seems owed to them is owed to members.
  For sense-3 wholes that gives a members'-side ground, members' interest
  in willing participation in successful group agency, not a duty of the
  whole to its parts. It does not transfer to sense 1.
- **Thompson** ([THEORY-1814](../theory.d/THEORY-1814.md), [NOTE-1895](../notes.d/NOTE-1895.md)): sense 3, states, tribes
  and families as intergenerational group agents. Her §10 is a direct
  ground A12 missed: such groups answer "to needs intrinsic to human
  life", and members' children "acquire entitlements to its benefits",
  resources, respect and protection (p. 18). The premise is persons'
  needs, and the bearers are members through the group. It supports the
  history and commitment grounds as well, for sense-3 wholes, and does not
  transfer to sense 1, where the parts need not be persons with needs.
- **Fineman** ([THEORY-1818](../theory.d/THEORY-1818.md), [NOTE-1899](../notes.d/NOTE-1899.md)): sense 3, the state toward
  persons. It supports the vulnerability, governance (constitution of
  resource-conferring institutions) and contribution (benefit from care
  work) grounds for that whole. It does not transfer to sense 1. On
  [CLAIM-671](../claims.d/CLAIM-671.md), see [ARG-099](../arguments.d/ARG-099.md).
- **Wardrope 2019** ([THEORY-1822](../theory.d/THEORY-1822.md), [NOTE-1898](../notes.d/NOTE-1898.md)), the stand-in for
  Wardrope 2020: the biotic community is closer to sense 1, a whole that is
  not an agent, with members that need not be persons. It answers A12's
  holism sub-question one way, community value "extending, not replacing,
  values assigned to individuals". But the bearer of the obligation is the
  members, as agents, not the whole: the Land Ethic asks "respect for his
  [sic] fellow members, and also respect for the community as such." Nothing
  is owed by the community to its parts.
- **Van der Weele** ([THEORY-1821](../theory.d/THEORY-1821.md), [NOTE-1892](../notes.d/NOTE-1892.md)), the weak stand-in
  for Kittay 1995: sense 3, carers and society; the dependency worker's
  claim on the society that relies on the work.
- **Not read**: Mellor ([LIT-2543](../literature.d/LIT-2543.md)) and Pettit ([LIT-2545](../literature.d/LIT-2545.md)) are
  Unfetched; Kittay 1995 ([LIT-2523](../literature.d/LIT-2523.md)) and Wardrope 2020 ([LIT-2538](../literature.d/LIT-2538.md))
  are Unavailable.

Each reading carries `bears_on` this question. The prior part, whether a
whole that is not an agent can owe anything beyond the functional, is
untouched by all of them. The question stays Open.
