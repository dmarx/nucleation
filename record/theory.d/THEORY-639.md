# THEORY-639: Inductive risk arises not only when a hypothesis is accepted but in choosing a significance level, in characterising data and in interpreting results: in the dioxin cancer studies each of these choices traded false positives against false negatives, three panels scored the same rat-liver slides differently, and threshold and no-threshold readings of the same data underlie regulatory limits about 100 times apart

## Source

Douglas (2000), [LIT-139](../literature.d/LIT-139.md) (*Philosophy of Science* 67(4), 559–579), read in
full in [NOTE-145](../notes.d/NOTE-145.md). Page references are the journal's.

## What was actually shown

**Inductive risk** is "the chance that one will be wrong in accepting (or
rejecting) a scientific hypothesis" (p. 561, after Hempel 1965). Douglas
extends it to accepting methodologies, data and interpretations (p. 565).

**Significance level (§4, pp. 565–569).** For a fixed test, the significance
level trades false positives against false negatives. In dioxin bioassays a
false positive leads to overregulation and a false negative to
underregulation. Dose groups are about ten-fold apart, so a false negative
under the threshold model sets the "safe" dose "at least one order of
magnitude less protective than it should be" (p. 568). Reducing both errors
needs more power, and one two-year study of 200 rats costs about $3 million
(p. 569).

**Characterising data (§5, pp. 569–572; Table 1).** The same female
Sprague-Dawley liver slides from the 1978 Kociba study were scored in 1978,
1980 and 1990. Total tumours at 100 ng/kg/day: 34/50, 33/47, 18/50. At 10
ng/kg/day: 20/50, 27/50, 9/50. Controls: 9/86, 16/86, 2/86. The 1990 panel
voted by majority. Borderline calls trade the two errors, and the 1990
re-evaluation led Maine to relax its regulation. Blinding was impossible,
because liver toxicity marked the high-dose slides.

**Interpreting results (§6, pp. 573–577).** With 50 animals per group a 1%
excess cannot be detected, so an apparent threshold at 1 ng/kg/day may be "a
limit of detection, not a limit in the activity of dioxins". But every
tumour-bearing liver at 100 ng, and 8 of 9 at 10 ng, showed acute toxicity,
which makes a threshold plausible if the tumours come from cytotoxic
regeneration. Countries assuming a threshold set acceptable levels about 100
times higher than no-threshold countries such as the US (p. 577).

**What could have come out otherwise.** The panels could have agreed, and the
data could have favoured one dose-response reading. Neither happened.

## What this does not say

- **That every internal choice carries inductive risk that matters.** Douglas
  says "not all scientific decisions in the internal stages of science will
  require the consultation of non-epistemic values" (§7, p. 577). Uncertainty
  may be negligible, or error may have no outside consequences.
- **That the conventional significance levels are wrong.** She sets the
  conventions aside: "I will not argue that such conventions have not worked
  reasonably well" (p. 566). The argument is about what a deliberate choice of
  level would involve.
- **That values tell which reading is true.** On her account they bear on
  which error to risk, not on whether evidence confirms a hypothesis (p. 565).
- **That the pattern holds outside toxicology.** All three cases come from one
  domain where the policy consequences are unusually direct.
- **That rodent results transfer to humans.** That extrapolation is set aside
  (p. 567).
