# THEORY-1518: Cohen and Levinthal argue that a firm's ability to recognize, assimilate and apply outside knowledge rests on its prior related knowledge, built largely as a byproduct of its own R&D and not quickly bought, and that because this capacity accumulates and shapes what the firm expects of a field, a firm that stops investing in a fast-moving field may be locked out of it even after major advances; with 1975–1977 data on 1,719 business units they test only the static part, that R&D intensity rises more with the relevance of harder-to-learn, less targeted knowledge

## Source

Cohen and Levinthal (1990), [LIT-2228](../literature.d/LIT-2228.md); read in [NOTE-1712](../notes.d/NOTE-1712.md).
Griffith, Redding and Van Reenen (2004), [LIT-2229](../literature.d/LIT-2229.md), estimate the
absorptive face of R&D at the country-industry level ([THEORY-1523](THEORY-1523.md))
and complement this account.

## What was actually shown

- **The argument.** Absorptive capacity is "largely a function of the
  firm's level of prior related knowledge" (p. 128) and "may be created as
  a byproduct of a firm's R&D investment" (p. 129). Its critical part "is
  often firm-specific and therefore cannot be bought and quickly
  integrated" (p. 135). "Accumulating absorptive capacity in one period
  will permit its more efficient accumulation in the next" (p. 136). Hence
  lockout: "once a firm ceases investing in its absorptive capacity in a
  quickly moving field, it may never assimilate and exploit new
  information in that field, regardless of the value of that information"
  (p. 136), and it may not pay to rebuild, "even after major advances in
  the field" (p. 137). These are argued from cognitive psychology and from
  illustrations, not tested.
- **The test.** On FTC Line of Business data for 1975–1977 and the Yale
  survey, "1,719 business units representing 318 firms in 151 lines of
  business" (p. 142), R&D intensity rises more with the relevance of basic
  science, taken as harder to learn, than of applied: constrained
  coefficients .189 against −.080 in the GLS, different at p < .01 under
  every estimator (p. 145). The ranking of outside sources by how targeted
  they are is largely confirmed. Both could have come out the other way.
- **Spillovers.** Where applied science is more relevant, the effect of
  appropriability on R&D intensity is greater (p < .05; p. 147), as the
  model predicts when learning is harder.

## What this does not say

- That any firm that stopped or outsourced R&D was locked out. No firm is
  followed over time; lockout and path dependence are argued, not
  measured.
- That absorptive capacity was observed. The test reads R&D intensity's
  response to proxies for ease of learning; the use of outside knowledge
  is never measured.
- How long substitution must last to do harm, or how long recovery takes.
- That spillovers raise own R&D in general. That result rests on two SIC
  groups and the authors say it is not "a direct test of our model"
  (p. 147).
- Its bearing on [CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md) and [CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md) is the record's reading, not the
  authors'; they write about firms and R&D, not about supply withdrawn.
