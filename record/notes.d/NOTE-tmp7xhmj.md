# NOTE-tmp7xhmj: Keller (2016) — Fiduciary Duties and Moral Blackmail

## Contribution

A named concept and an argument that it is coherent and real: "To commit moral blackmail is to perform a wrongful act that makes a person do something by making her alternatives morally unacceptable" (p. 20). Fiduciary work (doctor, nurse, teacher, lawyer) is one predictable route into exposure to it. Conceptual analysis by stylized cases; no empirical evidence.

## Key results

1. p. 20: "if I have the power to change your circumstances, then I may have the power to change the moral demands you face."
2. p. 21: "you can have a genuine moral obligation because you have been treated wrongly." The victim keeps a legitimate complaint, which Keller puts as "I see that it is my moral obligation to perform this act, but it is morally wrong that I have this moral obligation" (p. 26).
3. p. 22: "Moral blackmail works by way of the victim’s moral sensibility, just as ordinary blackmail works by way of the victim’s conception of her own best interests." What matters is that the victim believes the alternatives unacceptable and is motivated to avoid them (p. 21).
4. p. 23: "It may even be common knowledge between us that I have deliberately manipulated your circumstances. But for all that, my manipulations may succeed."
5. p. 17: fiduciary relationships tend "naturally and predictably to lead to circumstances under which the fiduciary has a special moral relationship with the beneficiary"; its obligations "can require acts that the fiduciary obligations do not require" and "can outlast the fiduciary obligations".
6. p. 24, third parties: a law firm that cuts time per client: "I can increase your workload without having to increase your pay and without losing clients for the firm." An official who announces changes to teachers' conditions "in the weeks before students take their final exams and graduate", so that strikers can be made to "look heartless and selfish".
7. p. 25: "It really is more difficult to go on strike if you are a nurse or a teacher than if you are a policy analyst or a software developer."
8. p. 26: those in caring roles may reasonably "expect greater pay or improved conditions, as compensation for the moral burden that comes with her role"; because such roles fall disproportionately on women, the burden can become one of the "instruments of systemic injustice" (p. 27).

## Assumptions

What morality requires depends on circumstances, and special obligations can be acquired unchosen (p. 12). Blackmail is wrongful by definition (p. 18); what makes a particular act wrongful is not analysed. Keller does not choose among the four accounts of partiality he surveys. Note 27 makes his notion wider than McConnell's (1981): the blackmailer need not threaten evil.

## Concepts

Moral blackmail; emotional and ordinary blackmail; fiduciary duty; special relationships; obligations of partiality; moral sensibility; third-party moral blackmail; "moral freedom" (p. 27).

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpd6uxt**: corroborates its core in the source it was attributed to: the obligation is genuine (result 2), the target is wronged in discharging it, and seeing the manoeuvre does not release the target (result 4). Two qualifications. Keller puts the mechanism in the target's moral beliefs and motivation, and success depends on what the target believes, so he does not say it works "rather than [through] a defect". The claim's limit, that the obligation is "no larger than the circumstances support", is A's, not Keller's.
- **CLAIM-tmpyjzi8**: corroborates its mechanism in part: the law firm (result 6) captures extra work without extra pay through the lawyers' obligations to clients, and result 8 names the unpaid moral burden. Keller's agents are individual strategists; he does not say an institution comes to run on the saving.
- **QUESTION-tmpgyn11**: bears on it. Timing teachers' conditions so that a legal right to strike becomes morally costly is the question's "moral cost of withdrawal" acting as leverage, and result 7 is the asymmetry it asks about. What separates it from ordinary reliance, for Keller, is only that the act is wrongful.
- **CLAIM-404**: Keller's result 1 is the general form of the third route, through circumstances, that the claim's dated note marks.
- **CLAIM-671**: a person-level case of power over a vulnerable beneficiary generating special obligations, from an account of partiality rather than Goodin's principle.
- **The exchange**: A's Alice and Bob case is Keller's own opening case (pp. 1-2), not one close to it. A13 §3's teachers are Keller's, with a government official in place of school administrators. A's limit on the created obligation and its Type A/B/C typology are not Keller's.

## Limitations

Read in pre-print. The cases are stylized (p. 25), and nothing measures how common moral blackmail is. Because "wrongful" is left unanalysed, the account does not sort moral blackmail from legitimate reliance on a person's conscientiousness.
