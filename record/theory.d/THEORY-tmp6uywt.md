# THEORY-tmp6uywt: Simkute, Tankelevitch and colleagues argue that generative AI repeats an irony of automation: because easy tasks are the easier to automate, it makes easy tasks easier and hard tasks harder, leaving the user the hard share of the work with the added task of evaluating the system's output

## Source

Simkute, Tankelevitch, Kewenig, Scott, Sellen and Rintel (2024),
[LIT-tmpaw2id](../literature.d/LIT-tmpaw2id.md); read in [NOTE-tmpndiw7](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpndiw7.md).

## What was actually shown

No new study; a synthesis of cited findings.

- **For automation** (§2.4): it "often makes easy tasks easier but fails to
  reduce the workload during cognitively demanding tasks, and in fact, often
  makes them harder", and the explanation offered is that "easy tasks are
  easier to automate, and so the more difficult tasks tend to remain under
  manual control, albeit alongside the additional task of monitoring
  automation". Pilots' workload fell on autopilot in straight flight and
  rose during landing.
- **For generative AI** (§2.4): user studies in which programmers trust the
  tool for simple tasks and in which it "led to more task failures in medium
  and hard tasks", as one cited study puts it; "whereas GenAI succeeds at
  making easy tasks even easier, current systems are less effective at
  supporting harder tasks."
- **The added task** (§2.1): users' roles shift from producing output to
  evaluating it, and monitoring can "paradoxically impose a considerable
  workload on humans".

## What this does not say

- That the user's skill at the easy share decays; the paper is about
  productivity, not retention.
- That Bainbridge stated the polarization; the paper credits it to others.
- How large the effect is, or whether it holds outside the programming
  studies it draws on.
