# NOTE-548: Moral Responsibility

## Contribution

A survey of moral responsibility as a question partly independent of free
will. It separates responsibility from obligation and from causal
responsibility, then covers the free-will background (§1), three
approaches (forward-looking, Strawsonian, reasons-responsiveness, §2) and
ten contemporary debates (§3). For this record it is the lawful report with
the most page references of Fischer and Ravizza's book, and the only report
the record holds of Wolf's JoJo case and of her case against Real Self
views.

## Key insight

The conditions of responsibility have come apart into families that can
each be held without the others. These are a control condition (Frankfurt
cases moved it from alternatives to the actual sequence), a competence
condition (the ability to recognise and respond to moral considerations,
Wolf's JoJo), a historical condition (manipulation) and an epistemic
condition. Fischer and Ravizza and Wolf both appear under more than one of
them.

## Assumptions

- "Responsibility" means moral responsibility in the accountability sense:
  "whether a person bears the right relation to their actions to be
  properly held accountable for them" (preamble).
- The entry focuses on blame and says so, because blameworthiness is often
  taken to involve liability to sanction (preamble).

## Key results

As a survey it reports and does not prove. The reports this record uses:

- **§1, Frankfurt cases.** The Black and Jones case in one paragraph. The
  dilemma objection (Ginet 1996, Widerker 1995), defences (Fischer 2002,
  2010) and refined cases (Mele and Robb 1998, Hunt 2000, Pereboom 2000,
  2001) are named. Frankfurt's later gloss is quoted: "making an action
  unavoidable is not the same thing as bringing it about that the action
  is performed" (2006, 340). Even without the ability to do otherwise,
  determinism "might threaten responsibility in other ways", citing
  Fischer and Ravizza 1998, 151–168, the Direct Argument chapter.
- **§2.2, Strawson.** Reactive attitudes respond to the quality of will.
  Excuses differ from exemptions, a distinction due to Watson. The
  response-dependence worry is that practices would be beyond criticism
  "simply because they are that group's practices", citing Fischer and
  Ravizza 1993, 18. Wallace's fairness-based reply is reported.
- **§2.3, Fischer and Ravizza.** Frankfurt cases show alternatives are not
  necessary, and what is required is guidance control (1998, 29–34). The
  hypnotised or compelled agent "is not responsive to reasons—his
  behavior would be the same, no matter what reasons there were" (37).
  Because the Frankfurt agent would also act the same whatever the
  reasons, responsiveness is put in the mechanism, "the process that leads
  to the relevant upshot" (38). Guidance control: "the mechanism that
  actually issues in the action is his own, reasons-responsive mechanism"
  (39). Moderate responsiveness (69–85): "an understandable pattern of
  (actual and hypothetical) reasons-receptivity" (71); understanding "how
  reasons fit together", so that "acceptance of one reason as sufficient
  implies that a stronger reason must also be sufficient" (71); a pattern
  of regular receptivity including moral considerations (77). Criticism is
  named: of the receptivity/reactivity contrast (McKenna 2005, Mele 2006a,
  Watson 2001), and of the focus on mechanisms (Brink and Nelkin 2013,
  McKenna 2013, Sartorio 2016).
- **§3.1.1, Wolf and Watson.** A Real Self view, quoted from Wolf (1990,
  33): behaviour is attributable to the real self "if she is at liberty (or
  able) both to govern her behavior on the basis of her will and to govern
  her will on the basis of her valuational system". Wolf's central example
  of the view is Watson 1975; her 1987 paper calls Watson 1975, Frankfurt
  1971 and Taylor 1976 "deep self views". Against them: they are silent on
  how the agent came to be so, and a real self from a traumatic upbringing
  makes the agent responsible "only in a superficial sense" (1990, 37–40).
  Deeper assessment requires that "she is able to form her values on the
  basis of what is True and Good" (1990, 75). Watson's reply (1996) splits
  responsibility into attributability and accountability.
- **§3.2, moral competence.** JoJo, raised by an evil dictator, becomes a
  sadistic tyrant, is "happy to be the sort of person that he is", and is
  moved by the desires he wants to be moved by. He meets structural
  conditions, but his upbringing left him "unable to appreciate the
  wrongfulness of his behavior", and it is this impaired competence "that
  does the real excusing work", which extends to those who "could not help
  but be mistaken about their [bad] values" (Wolf 1987, 57). Joining Wolf:
  Benson 2001, Fischer and Ravizza 1998, Fricker 2010, Levy 2003, Russell
  1995 and 2004, Wallace 1994, Watson 1987.
- **§3.8, ultimate responsibility.** Galen Strawson's Basic Argument and
  Kane's undetermined self-forming choices. Fischer (2006) is cited for the
  reply that the argument sets the bar too high.
- **§3.9, history and manipulation.** Real Self views are non-historical.
  Fischer and Ravizza (1998, 196–201) object that a mesh produced by
  "brainwashing or subliminal advertising" would not make the agent
  responsible, because the mechanism would not be "the agent's own" (197);
  "taking responsibility" (207–239) is their historical answer. Mele's
  Beth/Ann case, Pereboom's four-case argument and the replies follow.
- **§3.10, the epistemic condition.** Tracing (Fischer and Ravizza 1998,
  49–51, among others), and the regress argument for skepticism from
  culpable ignorance (Rosen, Zimmerman, Levy).

## Concepts

- **guidance control** (Fischer and Ravizza): control manifested when an
  agent guides behaviour in a direction, whether or not she could have
  guided it otherwise.
- **moderate reasons-responsiveness**: an understandable pattern of
  regular reasons-receptivity, with some moral receptivity.
- **Real Self / deep self views** (Wolf's label): responsibility as the
  attributability of behaviour to the agent's valuational system or
  higher-order desires.
- **moral competence**: the ability to recognise and respond to moral
  considerations.
- **attributability / accountability** (Watson): responsibility as
  disclosure of the agent's evaluative orientation, and as liability to
  moral demands and blame.

## Bearing on the record

It is a lawful, read source for the THEORY documents on Wolf's Reason View
and on Fischer and Ravizza filed in the same batch. It gives page
references for guidance control, the JoJo case for the Reason View, and the
moral-competence agreement between the two accounts, which is why the
record does not treat them as rivals on that point.

## Limitations

- A survey. Its quotations of Fischer and Ravizza and of Wolf are
  second-hand, and their page numbers are the entry's.
- It says almost nothing about Wolf's asymmetry. For that the record
  depends on [LIT-291](../literature.d/LIT-291.md) §4.3.
- It does not use "weak reactivity". The receptivity/reactivity contrast
  is named only as a target of criticism.
