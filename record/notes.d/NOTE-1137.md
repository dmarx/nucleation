# NOTE-1137: Craske et al. — Maximizing exposure therapy: an inhibitory learning approach

## Contribution

The inhibitory learning model of extinction (Bouton) was well supported
in the laboratory, but "there has been little discussion of how to
implement this model in clinical practice" (Abstract). This paper turns
the model into instructions for clinicians, and shows where those
instructions part from the two older rationales for exposure: habituation
(stay until fear declines) and belief disconfirmation (test and refute a
stated cognition). It reports no new data. Its case studies are
"exemplary" composites: "This is not intended to be exhaustive but rather
exemplary" (Case Studies).

## Key insight

Extinction does not undo what was learned, so exposure does not either.
"the original CS-US association learned during fear conditioning is not
erased during extinction, but rather is left intact as new, secondary
inhibitory learning about the CS-US develops" (Inhibitory Learning
Model). Fear returns with time, a change of context, an unrelated
aversive event or a single new pairing. So the goal is not to make fear
fall in the session. It is to build an inhibitory expectancy strong
enough, and retrievable widely enough, to win when the old one is cued.
The lever is mismatch: "The more the expectancy can be violated by
experience, the greater the inhibitory learning" (Expectancy Violation).

## Assumptions

- **Fear conditioning is a valid model** of panic disorder, social
  anxiety, specific phobia, OCD and PTSD (Inhibitory Learning Model,
  after Grillon 2008), and exposure is "the clinical proxy of
  extinction".
- **Error-correction learning.** Rescorla–Wagner: associative change is
  driven by the discrepancy between expected and actual outcome, and
  directed to the most salient cue (Expectancy Violation).
- **Anxious people learn inhibition less well.** "anxious individuals
  show deficits in the mechanisms that are believed to be central to
  extinction learning" (Deficits in Inhibition), citing imaging and
  conditioning studies. This motivates the whole programme.

## Key results

These are the paper's arguments and the evidence it cites. It contributes
no data of its own.

- **Return of fear (Inhibitory Learning Model).** Spontaneous recovery,
  renewal, reinstatement and rapid reacquisition each show the original
  association surviving extinction, and each has a clinical analogue: a
  return of fear after treatment, with time, in a new place, after an
  unrelated adverse event, or after one re-pairing.
- **Fear reduction does not predict outcome (Inhibitory Learning vs
  Habituation).** "the amount by which fear reduces by the end of an
  exposure trial or series of exposure trials is not predictive of the
  fear level expressed at follow-up assessment in fearful human samples"
  (citing Baker et al. 2010; Culver et al. 2012; Kircanski et al. 2012).
  This is read as "divergence in response systems": "outward expression
  of fear on the one hand, and conditional associations indicative of
  underlying learning on the other hand, may not always change in
  concordance".
- **The strategies.** The abstract lists eight. The body numbers eight
  under the heading for enhancing inhibitory learning and its retrieval: expectancy
  violation, deepened extinction, occasional reinforced extinction,
  removal of safety signals, variability, retrieval cues, multiple
  contexts and reconsolidation. Affect labeling follows as a separate
  strategy "for enhancing inhibitory regulation". Table 6 lists another
  eight, adding attentional focus and dropping multiple contexts and
  reconsolidation. The three lists do not match.
- **Cognitive restructuring before exposure can backfire (Expectancy
  Violation).** "cognitive interventions may reduce the expectancy of a
  negative outcome before exposure and thereby lessen the mismatch
  between initial expectancy and actual outcome." The authors therefore
  confine cognitive work to after exposure, for consolidation.
- **Safety signals: mixed evidence (Removal of Safety Signals).** The
  detrimental effect is shown in some studies (Sloan & Telch 2002;
  Salkovskis 1991). "However, recent data have presented contradictory
  findings": hygienic wipes after contamination exposure (Rachman et al.
  2011), and claustrophobia (Deacon et al. 2010; Sy et al. 2011). The
  reconciliation is Rescorla–Wagner. Inhibitory stimuli "will mitigate
  extinction learning inasmuch as they decrease the expectation of the
  US". So the cost of a safety behaviour depends on how much inhibition
  it carries against the excitatory cues present. The advice is still to
  remove them, gradually only to limit attrition: "If willing, immediate
  removal of safety signals is preferred."
- **Distraction** is called "such a pernicious safety behavior" because it
  lowers awareness of the CS–no-US relation. The footnote then reports a
  meta-analysis (Podina et al. 2013) under which some distraction
  outperformed focused exposure.
- **Reconsolidation** may weaken the original memory, but results that
  hold whether the reminder comes before or after extinction suggest "the
  results may relate more to enhancing the retrieval of the extinction
  learning rather than erasing acquisition learning" (Reconsolidation).
- **Case studies (Tables 1–5).** OCD (Roberto), PTSD (Julia), social
  phobia (Deandre), dog phobia (Sharon) and panic disorder (Charlie). Each
  exposure is set up as a stated feared outcome with a rated probability
  (60–99%), carried past the point at which the client predicts it, and
  followed by "Did what you were most worried about occur?" and "What did
  you learn?".

## Concepts

- **Inhibitory learning**: new learning that the CS no longer predicts
  the US, stored alongside the original excitatory association, so the CS
  has two meanings (Inhibitory Learning Model, after Bouton).
- **Safety signal**: a stimulus directly associated with the
  non-occurrence of the US. A **retrieval cue**, by contrast, retrieves
  the CS–no-US relation as an occasion setter (Retrieval Cues).
- **Deepened extinction**: extinguishing cues separately, then together,
  so that their compound overpredicts the US (Deepened Extinction).
- **Occasional reinforced extinction**: occasional CS–US pairings during
  extinction (Occasional Reinforced Extinction).

## Connections

It builds on Bouton's contextual account of extinction, Rescorla and
Wagner's error-correction rule, Rescorla's deepened extinction and
Bjork's work on variability in learning. It contrasts itself with
habituation models (Foa & Kozak 1986) and with belief disconfirmation by
behavioural testing (Salkovskis et al. 2006). It carries forward the
authors' earlier comparisons (Craske et al. 2008, 2012). De Kleine et al.
([LIT-1490](../literature.d/LIT-1490.md)), with Vervliet as co-author, cite it for the clinical
translation of fear learning. Goodson et al. ([LIT-1491](../literature.d/LIT-1491.md)) cite the
inhibitory-learning account of safety behaviours through Blakey and
Abramowitz 2016.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-446](../claims.d/CLAIM-446.md) (governance-maintained anxiety).** The exchange cited
  the paper for exposure as new inhibitory learning that competes with
  the old association rather than erasing it, with expectancy violation
  and the removal of safety signals as its levers. That is accurate. Two
  additions: the paper reports the evidence on safety signals as
  contradictory, and its list of levers runs to eight or nine, differently
  given in three places. Its bearing on the claim:
  - **Identifiability, in clinical form.** The claim says that under
    avoidance the dangerous and safe hypotheses predict the same
    observation. The paper's version is that avoidance "can interfere
    with new learning by preventing any violation in expectancy" (OCD
    case), and that exposure must be carried past the point where the
    feared outcome is predicted. Its case tables are designs for a
    discriminating observation. Supports the clause's clinical premise.
    It is the authors' rationale, not a test.
  - **"The regime can outlast a corrected belief."** The inhibitory
    model gives a mechanism the claim does not name. The old association
    is never deleted, so the defence it drives can be re-cued after the
    new learning. That is a third kind of persistence, beside belief and
    authority: retrieval competition between two learned expectancies.
    The claim should be read against it. A governance account must say
    what it adds to the explanation that the inhibitory memory
    lost the retrieval contest here.
  - **"Belief correction is not behavioural correction."** The paper
    goes further, in a direction the claim does not take. Lowering a
    stated expectancy *before* exposure is predicted to reduce the
    learning exposure produces, because it shrinks the mismatch. On this
    account the order matters: belief correction first can blunt
    behavioural learning. It is derived from Rescorla–Wagner, not shown
    here.
  - **The five quantities.** "divergence in response systems", outward
    fear and underlying learning not changing together, is a cited
    instance of the dissociation the claim posits between threat belief,
    anxious affect and defence ([CLAIM-283](../claims.d/CLAIM-283.md), [CLAIM-349](../claims.d/CLAIM-349.md)). It is not a
    measure of authority.
  - **"Not every safety behaviour blocks learning."** The claim's own
    caveat is the paper's reported finding. The Rescorla–Wagner
    reconciliation (a safety behaviour costs learning in proportion to
    the inhibition it carries) is a specific, testable version of it.
- **[QUESTION-045](../questions.d/QUESTION-045.md).** Return of fear after successful extinction is the
  laboratory signature of a regime that can be re-entered after it seems
  to be left. That fits "outlasts its trigger", but through memory,
  without the degrading capacity to leave that [QUESTION-045](../questions.d/QUESTION-045.md) asks about.
- **[THEORY-285](../theory.d/THEORY-285.md).** The paper's account of distraction, which reduces
  awareness of the CS–no-US relation, parallels that model's attenuation
  of the evidence that would correct the prior. The footnote's
  qualification applies.
- Filed from this reading: [THEORY-914](../theory.d/THEORY-914.md) (extinction leaves the
  original association intact) and [THEORY-921](../theory.d/THEORY-921.md) (fear reduction during
  exposure does not predict follow-up).
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- **No data of its own.** Every empirical statement is a citation, and
  the review is not systematic. The case studies are illustrations, not
  reported cases with outcomes.
- **Several strategies are flagged by the authors as weakly supported.**
  Multiple contexts: "the effects are unstable". Occasional reinforced
  extinction: "just gaining evidence", "further evidence is warranted".
  Reconsolidation: "a need for further evidence", not replicated in all
  cases. Retrieval cues: "very weak" in one study.
- **Fear-conditioning as the model of all five disorders** is assumed,
  not argued (Grillon 2008 is the only support given).
- **Accepted manuscript, not the version of record.** It says itself
  that the manuscript "will undergo copyediting, typesetting, and review
  of the resulting proof".
- **Inconsistent strategy lists** across the abstract, the body and
  Table 6, as above.

## Open questions

- Does pre-exposure cognitive restructuring in fact reduce long-term
  outcome, as the model predicts? A trial crossing restructuring before
  against after exposure would show it.
- Which safety behaviours impede learning, and is it the ones carrying
  the most inhibition, as the Rescorla–Wagner reconciliation predicts?
- Do the inhibitory-learning deficits in anxious people predict who
  relapses after exposure?
