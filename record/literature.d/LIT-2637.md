# LIT-2637: Erased but Not Forgotten: How Backdoors Compromise Concept Erasure

Braun, T., Grebe, J. H., Mohr, P., Rohrbach, M. & Rohrbach, A. (2026). Erased but Not Forgotten: How Backdoors Compromise Concept Erasure. *Proceedings of the 43rd International Conference on Machine Learning* (ICML 2026), PMLR 306. arXiv:2504.21072.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-1980](../notes.d/NOTE-1980.md) (skimmed).*

- **The attack** (abstract): "an adversary binds a backdoor trigger to a
  concept slated for removal, and this malicious link survives subsequent
  erasure".
- **How often** (§1): the deepest attack "circumvents erasure in 71.6% of
  the cases on average, and in up to 94.4%"; without model access, "up to
  80.2% success in the celebrity erasure, up to 92.7% success in the
  object erasure, and a 6× increase in explicit content".
- **Against robust erasure** (§1): it holds "even against erasure methods
  that claim adversarial robustness and explicitly search for residual
  trigger-to-target representations".
- **What helps the attack** (§5): "modifications that are spread across a
  larger set of parameters make backdoors harder to erase".

## Standing in the record

Filed from A's turn on concept erasure, which says that deliberately
established alternative triggering associations could survive several
erasure methods (paraphrased; the exchange was not rechecked by the
filer). The reader found that right: "several" is six, three standard and
three robust, on three architectures; the per-method values were not
read. A's ICML 2026 is right. Not to be confused with Jiang et al.,
arXiv:2510.00635. [THEORY-1911](../theory.d/THEORY-1911.md) carries its central account.
