# NOTE-tmpqu2kh: Schwitzgebel — Is the United States Phenomenally Conscious? Reply to Kammerer

## Contribution

The first published reply to Kammerer's Sophisticated Anti-Nesting
Principle (SAP; [LIT-409](../literature.d/LIT-409.md), [NOTE-362](NOTE-362.md)). It grants the SAP its two virtues,
keeping the antheads and Block's inhaled tiny organism conscious and
capturing the intuition that a car-and-driver system is not a second
mind, and then argues that its verdict on the US is unsecured, that its
condition A has unintuitive consequences, and that its justification is
either too strong or ad hoc. It also restates what Schwitzgebel's own
argument is for: that some actual or easily arranged groups are
conscious if materialism is true, with the US chosen only as the most
plausible case.

## Key insight

The SAP makes a group's consciousness depend on what its members think
about the group, and why they keep doing their part. Both are facts about
individuals that can change while the group's processing stays exactly
the same. So the SAP either makes group consciousness flicker with
members' moods, or, if "required" is made strict enough to stop that,
becomes a condition nothing meets. Separately, the SAP needs members to
represent the very entity whose consciousness is at issue, and it is not
clear that anyone does.

## Assumptions

- **"The United States" as defined in the original paper**: "a spatially
  distributed, concrete entity with people as some or all of its parts"
  (§2, citing Schwitzgebel 2015 pp. 1698, 1708). The author's "best
  guess" adds residents' artefacts, computers especially, and perhaps
  the air between people, but not terrain or unharvested crops.
- **Kammerer's statement of the SAP** is quoted in full (§1, from p. 1051)
  and glossed: "If the reason a larger entity acts as if it's conscious is
  that it contains smaller entities within it who have conscious
  representations of that larger entity, then that larger entity is not
  in fact conscious."
- **Every theory here will have sharply unintuitive consequences** (§2,
  citing Schwitzgebel 2014). So the second concern is offered as a cost
  to be weighed, not a refutation.

## Key results

- **Concern 1 (§2): the principle may not exclude actual groups.**
  - Kammerer allows group consciousness. If he allowed some actual
    conscious groups, the two would agree on the intended main idea and
    differ "only about a detail". Schwitzgebel doubts Kammerer would allow
    this, but "nothing he says seems to preclude" it.
  - Condition A requires members to consciously represent W. When people
    use "the United States" they may refer to the government, the nation,
    the land mass. Whether they represent the concrete entity at issue
    depends on reference magnetism, "a rather risky
    ontological-cum-referential proposition". If they do not, condition A
    is not met and the US is not excluded.
- **Concern 2 (§2): unintuitive dependence on members' motives.**
  - The Leo case. Monday: Leo processes video feeds for Sector 27A of the
    Tonga Trench without knowing of the group. Tuesday: he learns of it
    and carries on; his representation is not required. Wednesday: he is
    depressed and keeps working only because he represents the group and
    feels obligated; condition A is arguably met, so the group loses
    visual consciousness of the sector. Thursday: he recovers and it
    returns. Group-level report, behaviour and processing never change.
  - The dilemma. Read loosely, "required" yields such "flickering-qualia
    cases" (cf. Chalmers 1996). Read strictly, Kammerer's own view lets
    the US's population be replaced by non-conscious "people" who are
    functionally identical (because constituted by lower-level people),
    so no conscious representation is ever required, and condition A
    becomes "nearly impossible to meet".
  - General form: any theory that dissociates higher-level functional
    organisation from consciousness invites flickering or fading qualia
    cases.
- **Concern 3 (§2): the justification.**
  - Kammerer's justification is quoted (from p. 1055): ascribe
    consciousness only when behaviour cannot be explained by the mental
    states of other subjects, because one should prefer the simpler
    explanation.
  - If general, it is the overdetermination or causal-exclusion problem
    (Kim 1993; Merricks 2001; Thomasson 2007). It would put normal human
    consciousness in doubt, since behaviour is in principle explicable at
    the cellular level, and baseballs breaking windows. Whatever answer
    saves those, emergent or not, saves group consciousness too.
  - If special to consciousness, it is unmotivated and "appealing to us
    only because it seems to rule out U.S. consciousness".
  - And group-level explanation is often the simpler one: why the army
    disembarked here is explained by the route to the capital, not by the
    motives of each general and private.
- **Conclusion (§3).** "I have not encountered a better defense of an
  anti-nesting principle than Kammerer's." Anti-nesting principles should
  appeal to anyone wanting to avoid unintuitive verdicts on groups and on
  levels below the organism.

## Concepts

- **Anti-nesting principle** — conscious beings cannot have conscious
  subparts, nor belong to larger conscious systems. *Restricted*
  principles forbid nesting only under stated conditions (§1).
- **Sophisticated Anti-Nesting Principle** — Kammerer's restricted
  principle, quoted in full in §1 (see [NOTE-362](NOTE-362.md)).
- **"Required"** — the word in condition A whose reading the second
  concern turns on: motivationally, emotionally or for the part's
  functional contribution.
- **Flickering-qualia case** — a case in which conscious experience at
  one level switches on and off while that level's functional
  organisation is held fixed (§2).

## Limitations

- **The Leo case depends on one reading of "required".** The author
  says so ("on one possible flat-footed reading") and offers the
  dilemma for the case where Kammerer reads it more strictly; where
  Kammerer would "drive the stake" is left open.
- **The strict horn is sketched.** The replacement of the population by
  functionally identical non-conscious "people" is asserted to follow
  from Kammerer's allowances, not worked through.
- **The reference argument rests on the author's own ontology of "the
  United States".** He says he is "not at all committed" to it.
- **Short.** A seven-page reply with no positive principle of its own.

## Open questions

- Is there a reading of "required" that blocks motive-driven flicker but
  can still be met by actual citizens? The answer decides whether the SAP
  survives concern 2.
- Can the simplicity principle be restricted non-arbitrarily, for
  example to cases where the lower-level subjects' states are *about* the
  higher-level behaviour, which is Kammerer's condition A again?
- Does the Leo case also apply to IIT's exclusion postulate, where a
  member's change of state can change which set of elements has maximal
  Φ? The book's "Max goes to sleep" case ([NOTE-188](NOTE-188.md)) suggests it does.
