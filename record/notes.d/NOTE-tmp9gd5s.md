# NOTE-tmp9gd5s: Baedke et al. — Unknotting reciprocal causation

## Contribution

Biologists and philosophers have urged evolutionary theory to take "reciprocal causation" seriously: feedback loops in which organisms change how they experience the environment or alter its physical properties, with niche construction central because it can shift selection pressures. Comparing early-twentieth-century approaches with current niche construction theory, the authors find two recurring problems: failure to keep meaningful boundaries between organism and environment, and failure to integrate experiential and physical kinds of reciprocal causation. Drawing on niche construction case studies, they offer a model that solves both, distinguishing interacting organisms and environments in complex scenarios and integrating experiential and physical niche construction.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- Introduction: the historical diagnosis is meant as a guide to unsolved problems in current practice; the authors explicitly do not offer a new taxonomy of niche construction types.
- "Organism-environment reciprocity in historical context" / "Theoretical problems and decline": early organism-centred reciprocity was marginalised as externalism and gene-centred reciprocity took over, partly because of the two problems.
- "Niche construction theory and reoccurring problems": the same problems recur in NCT and in developmental-systems hybrids.
- "Unknotting reciprocal causation in physical niche construction": a visual model that first separates within-organism and within-environment causal paths (Fig. 2b), then unrolls the loop into a time sequence (Fig. 2c).
- "Experienced environments…": brings the experienced environment into the model without merging the whole environment with the organism, against inseparability views (Levins & Lewontin, Sultan, Walsh).
- "Integrating different types…" / Conclusions: maps physical, constitutive, mediational, relational and relocational NC onto one framework, illustrated by bumblebees damaging leaves to hasten flowering (Fig. 5); the organism–environment boundary is called an "epistemic necessity".
