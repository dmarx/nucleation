# QUESTION-380: When does establishing a small, internally compatible subsystem make global organization more likely, and how can its effectiveness be predicted from the constraint architecture?

## Why it is a question

A's notion: an organizational nucleus is "a localized intervention or
substructure whose formation biases the subsequent evolution toward a
coherent global organization", which A marks as "not automatically a
nucleation site in the strict thermodynamic sense". Internal
compatibility can be made exact with the record's apparatus: the
subsystem's demands are a face of its compatibility complex ([CLAIM-347](../claims.d/CLAIM-347.md)).
The question is when realizing such a face locally raises the chance
that the whole reaches a coherent organization, that is, raises reliable
emergence and not only feasibility ([CLAIM-567](../claims.d/CLAIM-567.md)).

The readings point both ways.

- **Seeds can predict.** Stern, Pinson and Murugan ([NOTE-1571](../notes.d/NOTE-1571.md);
  [THEORY-1418](../theory.d/THEORY-1418.md)): a folding island is "the largest subset of the pattern that
  will fold in the desired branch, if that subset is cut out and actuated
  at the chosen crease", and whether the islands of the actuated creases
  cover the sheet predicts success far better than their number
  ([CLAIM-562](../claims.d/CLAIM-562.md)). The predictor is computed from the constraint structure,
  approximately, by mountain–valley propagation.
- **Seeds can mislead.** In the same paper a badly placed seed locks in a
  distractor, there being no backtracking at zero temperature. DeLuca et
  al. ([NOTE-1561](../notes.d/NOTE-1561.md); [THEORY-1398](../theory.d/THEORY-1398.md)) find a persistent locally ordered precursor
  in DNA origami folding, yet forming it early "is not a perfect predictor
  of overall folding performance", and one run, best folded early, ends
  "caught in a global topological trap". The paper does not call its
  mechanism nucleation, and should not be cited as a nucleation case.

The record's nearest entries are about arguments, not organizations:
[CLAIM-185](../claims.d/CLAIM-185.md) holds that a persistent structure is available as an anchor
and [CLAIM-184](../claims.d/CLAIM-184.md) that anchors entrench; [QUESTION-038](QUESTION-038.md) asks what turns a
persistent structure into an anchor. [QUESTION-090](QUESTION-090.md) asks when a population
develops self-maintaining governance, of which a nucleus would be one
route.

## What would answer it

A predictor computed from a constraint architecture, like the union of
folding islands, that says which local subsystems raise the probability
of reaching a coherent global organization, which lower it, and by how
much, with the conditions (temperature or noise, the possibility of
backtracking, the order of formation) under which it holds; tested in a
system other than the one it was built for.

## What would not answer it

A case in which some local structure formed first and global order
followed. Precedence is not bias: DeLuca et al.'s trapped run had a
precursor too.

## Note of 2026-11-05: the DNA origami readings

Read on the owner's request to connect the record's realizability inquiry to DNA origami; the works were chosen by the assistant.

**Majikes et al., "Revealing thermodynamics of DNA origami folding via affine transformations".** Majikes et al. (2020), [LIT-tmp0hqya](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0hqya.md), offer a mechanism by which a small nucleus helps. A staple's longest domain, a "nucleation seed subsequence", binds first. The short folds that follow lower the loop entropy cost for their neighbours, a gain of more than 10 °C in melting temperature for short against long folds. This is predicted from architectural quantities (loop distance and scaffold stiffness), not sequence. Measured on one fold, inferred for many. A test would be a structure with several coupled folds in which a fold's melting or folding temperature rises once a neighbouring fold has closed. No status changes.

**Dunn et al. 2015 and Schneider, Möritz and Dietz 2019, two DNA origami readings.** Two partial answers for origami. Schneider, Möritz and Dietz ([NOTE-tmpqi66a](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpqi66a.md)): nucleating clusters
of segments joined by long-range crossovers form a compacted intermediate; removing the 20
earliest segments costs hours of delay but does not forbid folding; what predicts a nucleus is
connectivity, not binding strength ("little correlation between the free energy of hybridization
of entire staple strands with their mean folding time"). Dunn and colleagues ([NOTE-tmpvgzvb](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpvgzvb.md)): an
early partial structure restricts which targets remain reachable, seam patterns before 50%
occupancy predict the final fold in their model, and seam staples compatible with one target only
bias toward it. Dannenberg and colleagues ([NOTE-tmpyo994](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpyo994.md)) add a caution: the crystal-nucleation
analogy "is not so obvious" for origami. Each is one design; generality is open.

No status changes.

## Note of 2026-11-05: A's synthesis on DNA origami

From the synthesis on DNA origami by another assistant (A), pasted by the owner on 2026-11-05 and filed with its four further works in this pull request.

A offers a five-stage sequence, which A flags as "a synthesis of observed and simulated mechanisms, not a universal sequence": unassembled scaffold, nucleating interactions, cooperative growth, intermediate organization, then success or trap. The works A cites qualify where the nucleus forms and what it does.
- **Concentration can move the nucleus.** Wah et al. 2016 ([NOTE-tmp059hz](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp059hz.md)) moved the start of folding from the edges to the centre by raising central and cutting edge staple concentrations. The authors give an equilibrium reading: folding starts where staples melt highest.
- **Short fold distances may set it.** Majikes et al. 2021 ([NOTE-tmpetbc9](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpetbc9.md)) hypothesize that origami nucleates where fold distances are short, and grows along a front as folding displaces blocking strands.
- **Moving the nucleus is not yet shown to change the outcome.** Neither reading shows that moving the nucleus changes the final yield, which is what the question asks.

No status changes.

## Note of 2026-11-05: the further DNA origami readings

The works cited in A's two syntheses on DNA origami, pasted by the owner on 2026-11-05, read in this pull request.

**Lee Tin Wah et al., "Observing and controlling the folding pathway of DNA origami".** Lee Tin Wah et al. (2016), [LIT-tmps9gx3](../literature.d/LIT-tmps9gx3.md), are directly relevant. The
nucleus sits where local staple melting temperature is highest and can be
moved by changing staple concentrations alone; this is an observed,
movable nucleus whose location sets the growth path. Whether seeding a
small compatible nucleus raises global yield is not measured. No status
changes.

**Majikes et al., "Failure Mechanisms in DNA Self-Assembly".** Majikes et al. (2021), [LIT-tmplg4lb](../literature.d/LIT-tmplg4lb.md), bear on the question as a hypothesis:
origami should nucleate where fold distances are short, each closed fold
raising its neighbours' j-factor and letting blocking strands be displaced
along a growth front, while tiles, lacking these cooperativities, stall.
The effectiveness of a nucleus is thus predicted from the fold-distance
distribution. Untested at the multi-fold level.

No status changes.
