# NOTE-tmpyjyrt: Frischmann and Benesch (2023) — Friction-In-Design Regulation as 21st Century Time, Place, and Manner Restriction

## Contribution

Two things. A legal argument: much regulation that requires friction in
digital design is a content-neutral restriction on the time, place or
manner of communication, so the First Amendment does not bar it but
subjects it to intermediate scrutiny (Part III). And a descriptive
framework: six parameters, each with questions, for comparing friction
measures (§II.A). Part IV sketches regulations as thought experiments,
some of which the authors expect to fail. The account of why friction is
needed is summarized from Frischmann and Selinger, *Re-Engineering
Humanity* (2018), not argued afresh.

## Key results

As argued; the article reports no study of its own.

1. **Definitions.** Friction-in-design regulation is "regulation by the
   state that either directly requires friction-in-design measures or
   requires certain functional outcomes that are best achieved by
   friction-in-design measures"; for digital systems it is "regulation that
   requires friction with a functional purpose" (§II.B, p. 417).
   Friction-in-design needs no state action; the regulation does (p. 417
   n. 120). Not every added friction is friction-in-design: shadowbanning
   "might generate friction but not qualify as friction-in-design, at least
   in so far as the actions are not design features" (p. 415 n. 115).
2. **What friction is for.** "Friction is resistance. It slows things
   down. ... friction affords us opportunities to stop and think, to
   deliberate and even second-guess ourselves and others" (§I.B, p. 388).
   It is instrumental, not an end: "We do not, however, hold friction to be
   an End worth pursuing" (p. 379 n. 1); "We want neither to eliminate
   friction nor to have too much of it" (p. 389).
3. **Friction and governance.** "Interconnection often should be seamful,
   as that friction provides opportunity/space for governance" (p. 387).
   Infinite scrolling "eliminates what would otherwise be a seam for
   self-governance" (p. 394). "Friction can improve governance of the
   boundaries, or seams" (p. 429). A border delay could aim at "enabling a
   governance seam at the jurisdictional border" (p. 437).
4. **Speed bump against checkpoint.** A speed bump nudges drivers "to
   internalize costs associated with speed" (p. 400). A sobriety checkpoint
   "requires a complete stop. It is mandatory (not a nudge)"; "drivers must
   demonstrate that they are sober and thus entitled to use the shared
   resource" (p. 404). CAPTCHAs and passwords "are more like checkpoints
   ... than speed bumps" (p. 405). Footnote 89 quotes Frischmann and Ohm,
   "Governance Seams": CAPTCHAs "govern behavior at an important interface
   (the relevant seam), create friction with a functional purpose
   (authentication), and enable governance in the enforcement of a
   membership rule", while speed bumps "do not involve a seam or enable
   governance" (p. 405). Footnote 75 records Lemley's distinction between
   friction "imposed on everyone as a means to prohibit activities" and
   friction that only imposes "delays designed to cause reflection but
   with no intent to stop considered decisions" (p. 401).
5. **The six parameters** (pp. 399–403): type of friction; direct effect
   on subjects; architectural design; purpose; scope of application
   (always on or triggered); governance ("Who decides when and how to
   deploy and how to assess impacts?").
6. **Friction used against users.** The iPhone location setting puts
   friction only on the path to privacy: "A slippery slide in one direction
   and a steep climb in the other" (p. 410, quoting Frischmann 2021).
   "Friction-in-design can be used strategically to fatigue consumers and
   raise impediments to behaviors that challenge business models"
   (p. 412). Regulation could "require friction in some cases but limit its
   strategic or asymmetric use in others" (p. 412).
7. **Reported effects, others' data.** Speed bumps reduce speed and
   pedestrian injury (p. 397, citing Tester et al. 2004). WhatsApp reported
   that highly forwarded messages "dropped 70%" after its forwarding limit
   (p. 413, a press report). Twitter reported more reading before
   retweeting (p. 413). "Interstitial warnings" that "interrupt users and
   require interaction" change behaviour more than contextual ones (p. 442,
   citing Kaiser et al. 2021).
8. **Content neutrality as the constraint.** A trigger defined by content
   (TikTok's sensitive-content warning) would not qualify (p. 443 n. 190);
   a trigger by number of forwards, geography or social-graph distance
   could (pp. 437–42).
9. **Evidence.** The article "primarily aims to open the regulatory
   possibilities and frame and motivate systematic empirical research"
   (p. 434); digital friction measures "are rarely studied rigorously, with
   the exception of privacy- and security-by-design research" (p. 446).

## Assumptions

- That more deliberation by individuals is the main good friction serves,
  grounded in a capabilities view of human flourishing the authors state
  and do not defend here (pp. 417–18).
- That the instantaneous reach of online communication has little
  marginal value for most communications (p. 435, "no doubt contestable").
- That a regulator choosing a delay or trigger can meet narrow tailoring
  with evidence that does not yet exist (p. 438 n. 183, pp. 442–43).
- That content neutrality, a First Amendment test, is also a fair guide to
  which frictions are legitimate (p. 418, "the First Amendment supplies
  guidance").

## Concepts

Friction-in-design; friction-in-design regulation; friction with a
functional purpose; governance seam (Frischmann and Ohm); seamful
interconnection; asymmetric friction; digital speed bump; checkpoint
friction; frictional reframing; time, place and manner restriction;
content neutrality; freedom of reach; interaction effects (a kind of
externality); active choosing by default.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-710](../claims.d/CLAIM-710.md)** (the owner's: procedural friction exposes, or perhaps
  characterizes, opportunity for intervention). The article's sentence
  "that friction provides opportunity/space for governance" is the owner's
  "exposes" in another vocabulary, stated as a premise, with no evidence.
  Its friction is mostly individual and cognitive, not the handoffs and
  approvals between parties the claim is about. What bears more is the
  seam distinction it reports: an impedance (the speed bump) offers no
  point of governance, a gate (the checkpoint, the CAPTCHA) does. That is
  the claim's own caveat that not all friction offers a point of
  intervention, and it leans against reading "characterizes" as true of
  friction as such. It also shows something the claim does not say: the
  gates these authors describe let the party that set them check the
  person passing (sobriety, humanity, identity), not the person check the
  party. Whose intervention a gate exposes is a separate question from
  whether it exposes one. Bearing: a parallel and a qualification; no
  relation, the article giving no evidence.
- **[TERM-125](../terms.d/TERM-125.md)** (independent execution gate, distinct from execution
  impedance and from an intervention opportunity). The speed bump and the
  checkpoint are the term's impedance and gate, and Frischmann and Ohm's
  "governance seam" is a precedent in the literature for the line the term
  draws, made on the same grounds: the checkpoint stops passage until a
  condition another party checks is met. A neighbouring sense the term
  could list: friction-in-design, friction "with a functional purpose" set
  by a designer. Whether to cite the seam as the term's precedent is the
  owner's call.
- **[CLAIM-715](../claims.d/CLAIM-715.md)** (cognitive friction differs from friction that makes the
  institution answerable). Corroborates its premise from the regulatory
  side. Nearly every measure here acts on the user's deliberation (alerts,
  comprehension checks, delays to "stop and think"); none gives users a
  way to stop or redirect the platform. Even where the state imposes the
  friction, what it imposes is on users' thinking. [THEORY-tmp2j2zm](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp2j2zm.md) carries
  `corroborates`.
- **[CLAIM-731](../claims.d/CLAIM-731.md)** (friction moves from imposing a decision to contesting
  it). A parallel, not evidence: the iPhone setting, where only the path
  away from the default carries friction, and friction used "to fatigue
  consumers and raise impediments to behaviors that challenge business
  models", are consumer-design cases of the same asymmetry. No relation.
- **[CLAIM-711](../claims.d/CLAIM-711.md)** (standing constraints set slowly in advance can keep a
  process interruptible without delaying it). Background: the
  content-neutral triggers the article proposes (a forward count, a
  border, social-graph distance) are standing rules set in advance; they
  delay what they catch and leave the rest alone, the shape [CLAIM-711](../claims.d/CLAIM-711.md)
  describes. The article does not discuss interruptibility. No relation.
- **[QUESTION-471](../questions.d/QUESTION-471.md)** (which of a record's checks are points at which an
  incorporation can still be stopped). The seam distinction is the
  question's own: a check that blocks passage until a condition is shown

## Limitations

- An argument, not a study. The effects it reports are others' or
  companies' own claims (the 70 percent drop is from a press report of
  WhatsApp's statement).
- The governance-seam distinction, the part that matters most for the
  record, is borrowed and stated in footnotes; this article does not
  develop it. In the quoted passage speed bumps "directly govern use of
  shared roads" though they do not "enable governance" (p. 405 n. 89):
  the line is between friction that governs and friction that opens a
  place where someone governs.
- Gap in its argument: it assumes that more individual deliberation is
  what friction should buy and never asks for whom a seam opens, so a
  checkpoint that serves the platform and one that serves the user fall
  under the same name.
- US First Amendment law only, as the authors say (p. 379 n. 6).

## Open questions
