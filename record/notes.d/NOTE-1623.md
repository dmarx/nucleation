# NOTE-1623: Rockafellar and Uryasev — Optimization of conditional value-at-risk

## Contribution

CVaR, the expected loss beyond the VaR, is defined through the VaR, a
quantile that is awkward to optimize. The paper shows that CVaR is the
optimal value of a simple convex minimization in one extra scalar, so that
CVaR-minimizing portfolio selection and hedging can be done with standard
stochastic programming, linear programming or nonsmooth methods on scenario
data, and the VaR comes out as a byproduct. It also argues that CVaR is the
better objective because CVaR ≥ VaR.

## Key insight

Write the tail expectation as α plus a scaled expected excess over α. As a
function of α this is convex, and minimizing it picks α at the quantile, so
the quantile and the tail expectation are found together. A decision then
enters only through the expected excess, which keeps whatever convexity or
linearity the loss has in the decision.

## Assumptions

- **No atoms.** "We assume however in what follows that the probability
  distributions are such that no jumps occur" (§2, p. 4), for simplicity:
  "We prefer to leave such technical issues for a subsequent paper."
- **A density p(y)** for the random vector, though in practice only a
  sampler for it is needed.
- **Convexity** of the loss in x and of the feasible set X, for the
  convex-programming claim; the losses in both examples are linear.
- **For the equivalence Proposition**: normal losses, β ≥ 0.5 and an
  active return constraint.
- **In the applications**: a fixed required return, long-only fully
  invested weights (portfolio), or box constraints on positions (hedging).

## Key results

- **Definitions (§1, p. 2).** "the β-VaR of a portfolio is the lowest
  amount α such that, with probability β, the loss will not exceed α,
  whereas the β-CVaR is the conditional expectation of losses above that
  amount α."
- **Why CVaR (§1, p. 2).** VaR has "a lack of subadditivity and convexity",
  and "CVaR is known to have better properties than VaR". The paper credits
  Pflug (2000) with proving CVaR a coherent risk measure.
- **Theorem 1 (§2, p. 5).** "As a function of α, Fβ (x, α) is convex and
  continuously differentiable." Its minimum is the CVaR and the left
  endpoint of its argmin interval the VaR, so CVaR can "be calculated
  without first having to calculate the β-VaR on which its definition
  depends" (p. 6).
- **Theorem 2 (§2, p. 6).** "Minimizing the β-CVaR of the loss associated
  with x over all x ∈ X is equivalent to minimizing Fβ (x, α) over all (x,
  α) ∈ X × IR", a convex program "when f (x, y) is convex with respect to
  x".
- **Linear programming (§3, p. 9).** On sampled scenarios F_β becomes
  piecewise linear, with one auxiliary variable per scenario, and "the
  possibility of such reduction to linear programming does not depend on y
  having a special distribution, such as a normal distribution".
- **The normal case (§3, Proposition, p. 10).** "If β ≥ 0.5 and the
  constraint (15) is active at solutions to any two of the problems (P1),
  (P2) and (P3), then the solutions to those two problems are the same":
  minimum CVaR, minimum VaR and minimum variance agree.
- **Sampling (§3, pp. 11, 15).** With pseudo-random samples, convergence to
  the minimum-variance values "is slow at best" (p. 11); at β = 0.99 with
  1000 samples the CVaR deviates by −5.12% (Table 5). Sobol quasi-random
  samples show "relatively fast convergence to the values for the Minimum
  Variance problem" (p. 15).
- **Hedging (§4, p. 19).** An 11-instrument NIKKEI options portfolio, from
  Mausser and Rosen, with a non-normal loss, solved by a variable-metric
  nonsmooth method. One-instrument CVaR hedges come out close to the
  published VaR hedges; adjusting four instruments together lowers CVaR
  much further. One position has β-VaR −205,927 and β-CVaR 1,183,040: "This
  example clearly shows, by the way, the superiority of CVaR over VaR in
  capturing risk", since VaR does not say "how serious the outcome might be
  the rest of the time."
- **Conclusion (§5, p. 19).** "Although, formally, the method minimizes
  only CVaR, it also lowers VaR because CVaR ≥ VaR."

## Concepts

- **β-VaR α_β(x)**: the least α with Ψ(x, α) ≥ β, Ψ the loss distribution
  function (eq. 2).
- **β-CVaR φ_β(x)**: (1−β)⁻¹ times the expected loss over the event that
  the loss is at least α_β(x); "also called Mean Excess Loss, Mean
  Shortfall, or Tail VaR" (abstract, p. 1).
- **F_β(x, α)**: α + (1−β)⁻¹ ∫ [f(x, y) − α]⁺ p(y) dy (eq. 4), and its
  sample-average version (eq. 9).
- **A_β(x)**: the set of minimizers of F_β(x, ·), a closed bounded interval
  whose left endpoint is the VaR.

## Connections

It builds on the stochastic-programming literature (Ermoliev and Wets, Kall
and Wallace, Pflug, Prekopa) to which Theorem 2 opens CVaR, on Markowitz's
mean-variance problem as the benchmark of the normal case, and on Mausser
and Rosen (1999) for the hedging example and its VaR hedges. It cites Pflug
(2000) for the coherence of CVaR.

## Bearing on the record

- **The pasted bibliography's use of it.** Listed under tail risk with no
  gloss; implicitly the formal tool for optimizing against the worst
  (1 − β) of outcomes. That fits the paper, and nothing A says of it is
  wrong. What A cannot lean on it for: the paper concerns portfolio losses
  with a known or simulable distribution and a fixed β, and has nothing on
  rarely exercised capabilities, readiness or slack. Using CVaR to value
  capacity kept for rare events needs the loss of a capacity decision in
  each scenario, and a sample that contains the rare scenarios, which the
  paper's own Table 5 shows is costly at β = 0.99.
- **[CLAIM-517](../claims.d/CLAIM-517.md) (viability at boundary contexts; fidelity in ordinary
  operation certifies nothing).** Qualifies, by analogy only. CVaR is the
  standard formal way to make an objective depend on the tail of a
  distribution rather than its bulk, the same stance as the claim's
  attention to boundary contexts. The paper proves nothing about viability
  or invariance.
- **[CLAIM-357](../claims.d/CLAIM-357.md) and [CLAIM-365](../claims.d/CLAIM-365.md) (governance regret and the frustration
  residual).** Qualifies, loosely. Those are min–max constructions over
  admissible updates; CVaR is another, convex, way to fold a distribution of
  outcomes into one number. They sit side by side as scalarizations.
- **[LIT-1975](../literature.d/LIT-1975.md) (adjustable robust linear programs, [NOTE-1503](NOTE-1503.md)).** The nearest
  held neighbour: worst-case linear programming under uncertainty, where
  this paper is risk-averse linear programming under a distribution. Two
  accounts of choosing under uncertainty in the same machinery.
- **[NOTE-068](NOTE-068.md) (mean-field-type games)** names risk measures beyond variance
  as open; CVaR is one, with no direct link.
- **[TERM-055](../terms.d/TERM-055.md), [CLAIM-079](../claims.d/CLAIM-079.md), [CLAIM-347](../claims.d/CLAIM-347.md), [QUESTION-132](../questions.d/QUESTION-132.md)**: unrelated. [QUESTION-132](../questions.d/QUESTION-132.md)
  asks when equivalent adaptive operators differentiate into specialists
  with positive growth when rare, not, as A glosses it, about capabilities
  whose contribution shows in rare contexts.
- Filed: [THEORY-1391](../theory.d/THEORY-1391.md). No instruction for machine-learning practice;
  nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **The authors'.** The no-atom assumption, put off to a later paper; the
  examples "have relatively low dimensions and are offered here for
  illustrative purposes" (p. 19); "the assumption that there is a joint
  density of instrument returns can be relaxed" (p. 19); VaR constraints and
  specialized algorithms left open.
- **Not a VaR minimizer.** The paper does not solve the minimum-VaR problem
  in general; it argues only that CVaR-optimal decisions are good for VaR.
- **Thin numerical evidence**: two small examples, with pseudo-random
  deviations of about 5% in CVaR at β = 0.99.
- **One period, fixed β, known or simulable distribution.** Nothing on
  rare events whose probability is itself unknown, or on decisions over
  time.
- **A manuscript.** Read from the 1999 manuscript; the journal text may
  differ in wording.
