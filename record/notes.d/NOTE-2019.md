# NOTE-2019: Elish (2019) — Moral Crumple Zones: Cautionary Tales in Human-Robot Interaction

## Contribution

A named concept for the attribution side of the gap between control and
responsibility: it says where blame lands when an accountability gap opens
in an automated system, and that the landing protects the system's
integrity. Its object is "cultural perceptions of blame and
responsibility" (p. 52), not legal liability.

## Key results

- **The metaphor** (p. 40): "the moral crumple zone protects the integrity
  of the technological system, at the expense of the nearest human
  operator". Responsibility "may be misattributed to a human actor who had
  limited control over the behavior of an automated or autonomous
  system".
- **Accidental or intentional** (p. 41): the human may become a component,
  "accidentally or intentionally", "that bears the brunt of the moral and
  legal responsibilities"; operators tend to become "liability sponges".
- **Partly responsible operators** (Three Mile Island): "Without a doubt,
  actions taken by the plant operators led to the accident and
  exacerbated its severity" (p. 44); yet "Even as their actions were
  constrained, their culpability was focused on and singled out" (p. 46).
- **Control distributed, responsibility not** (p. 49), on the rule that
  keeps the pilot in command (14 CFR 91.3): "While control has been
  effectively distributed, responsibility has not scaled accordingly."
  Regulators, engineers and managers "have created contradictory dynamics
  in which automation is seen as safer and superior in most instances".
- **Certification fixes the frame** (p. 50): "Because the autopilot did not
  malfunction in a way recognized through its certification process, the
  only possible malfunction, systemically, is the human pilot, becoming
  the moral crumple zone."
- **Not an explanation of the gap** (p. 50): "The concept of a moral
  crumple zone is not a way to explain why these gaps occur."
- **Not universal** (pp. 50–51): "moral crumple zones are not a property
  of every complex and automated system." In the Therac-25 case fault went
  to the manufacturer. They "are likely to take shape in the immediate
  aftermath of a highly publicized event or accident", and the operators'
  epistemic position matters: those at Three Mile Island "did not have
  sufficient information or authority to take corrective actions".
- **More than a scapegoat** (pp. 51–52): "a moral crumple zone is more than
  just the articulation of a scapegoat"; "The technology is maintained as
  faultless, while the human operator becomes the faulty feature of the
  system."
- **Uber in Tempe** (p. 53), from the NTSB's preliminary report: "it is
  reasonable to question the appropriateness of the role and expectations
  of the safety driver in and of itself." Tesla's driver "is at once
  superfluous and essential".
- **Human in the loop** (p. 55): the rhetoric "suggests that keeping a
  'human in the loop' assures that human judgment will always be able to
  supplement automation as needed."

## Assumptions

- That press and official accounts shape formal accountability.
- That the relevant contrast is between operators and those who design,
  manage and regulate.
- An American frame; the evidence is case narrative and headlines, not
  systematic content analysis.

## Concepts

- **Moral crumple zone**: the human component that absorbs the moral and
  legal responsibility for a system's failure, protecting the technology.
- **Liability sponge**.
- **Handoff**: the last-minute transfer of control to a human, which people
  perform badly.
- **Certification** as the boundary of what can count as the machine's
  malfunction.

## Bearing on the record

Filed as [THEORY-1928](../theory.d/THEORY-1928.md).

- **[CLAIM-707](../claims.d/CLAIM-707.md)** is built on this concept, and the reading supports
  its blame part: blame lands on the nearest operator, sparing the system.
  It supports two further parts: credit to the arrangement (p. 49,
  automation "safer and superior in most instances") and upstream-set
  standards (p. 50, certification leaves the pilot as "the only possible
  malfunction"). It does not support "conscience does double duty", which
  is the exchange's. Its conditions (pp. 50–51) mark a limit the claim's
  text does not yet state: zones form after publicized accidents, where
  the operator lacked information or authority, and not otherwise
  (Therac-25).
- **[QUESTION-476](../questions.d/QUESTION-476.md)** (blameless post-incident practice): the Therac-25
  contrast is a case where fault went upstream.
- **[QUESTION-487](../questions.d/QUESTION-487.md)**: Elish names certification reform as a lever
  (p. 54), but no instrument by which operators hold principals to
  account. Weak.
- **[CLAIM-713](../claims.d/CLAIM-713.md)**: nothing on conscientiousness or moral subsidy. Left
  open.
- **The Uber case.** Elish wrote from the NTSB's preliminary report; the
  final report ([LIT-2664](../literature.d/LIT-2664.md), [NOTE-2034](NOTE-2034.md)) found the operator streaming
  video, not at the touch screen. The case is filed from the final report
  as [CASE-tmpnrxpi](../cases.d/CASE-tmpnrxpi.md).

## Limitations

- Three of the four cases rest on journalism and secondary sources.
- The Uber case predates the NTSB's final report.
- No account of how a zone could be prevented beyond research on
  certification.
