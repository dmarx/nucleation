# NOTE-tmp4y156: Jasanoff — Technologies of humility

## Contribution

It names a pair, technologies of hubris and of humility, that became a
standard frame in science-and-technology studies for the limits of expert
prediction in governance. Its argument is that participation, already
accepted in principle by 2003, is not enough: what must change is the
substance of what is asked, through four focal points that bring the
unknown, the normative and the distributive into deliberation.

## Key insight

Predictive methods fail in a patterned way. They "focus on the known at
the expense of the unknown", so "they downplay what falls outside their
field of vision, and overstate whatever falls within"; they hide judgment
behind claims of objectivity; and they learn from disasters only what fits
their initial assumptions. The answer is not better prediction but a
standing practice of asking, of any enterprise that would alter society,
what it is for, who will be hurt, who benefits, and how we can know.

## Assumptions

- **The 'Mode 2' account of science** (Gibbons et al. 1994) is taken as
  broadly right: knowledge is produced in contexts of application and
  quality control has merged with accountability.
- **Regulatory science's credibility rests on accountability**, its
  probity, representativeness and transparency, more than on peer
  judgment of quality (Jasanoff 1990, restated pp. 233–234).
- **Risk is a condition of modernity** (Beck), not a set of probabilities
  to be calculated away (p. 224).

## Key results

The paper is an argument, not a study.

- **The post-war contract has broken down** (pp. 227–235): fraud cases,
  charges of interest and bias, the collapse of the basic–applied
  distinction, Funtowicz and Ravetz's post-normal science.
- **The participatory turn is insufficient** (pp. 235–238). Formal
  participation can come too late, be ad hoc, or deepen controversy; the
  Shelby Amendment of 1998 turned transparency into a means of attacking
  findings; participation inside a risk-assessment frame "may not admit
  novel viewpoints". "Participation alone, then, does not answer the
  problem."
- **Three limits of the technologies of hubris** (pp. 238–239): peripheral
  blindness to uncertainty; pre-emption of political discussion; inability
  to internalize challenges outside their framing (single-chemical
  toxicity testing; Bhopal).
- **Four focal points** (pp. 240–242): *framing* (a problem framed wrongly
  gets a solution with the same defect); *vulnerability* (classifications
  by physical and biological indicators overlook "history, place, and
  social connectedness"); *distribution* (ethics committees as "end-of-pipe
  legitimation devices"; the end of OTA in 1995); *learning* (experience
  is "polysemic"; the cause of a failure may look different "to the
  marginal or the excluded").

## Concepts

- **Technologies of hubris**: predictive methods (risk assessment,
  cost–benefit analysis, climate modelling) that achieve power through
  claims of objectivity and serve management and control.
- **Technologies of humility**: "methods, or better yet institutionalized
  habits of thought, that try to come to grips with the ragged fringes of
  human understanding — the unknown, the uncertain, the ambiguous, and the
  uncontrollable".
- **Civic epistemology**: scattered and private knowledge "amalgamated,
  perhaps even disciplined" into a dependable public form (p. 240).

## Connections

It builds on Jasanoff's *The Fifth Branch* (1990) on regulatory science,
on Gibbons et al. (1994) and Nowotny, Scott and Gibbons (2001) on Mode 2
and socially robust knowledge, on Funtowicz and Ravetz on post-normal
science, and on Beck and Perrow. Longino's account of the critical
organization of a scientific community (THEORY-151), beside which this is
filed, is about how a community's knowledge is made objective; Jasanoff's
is about how a polity should decide under the uncertainty that knowledge
leaves.

## Bearing on the record

- **CASE-172.** The case uses the paper for the breadth of harms that are
  hard to quantify in advance. That fits, with one adjustment. Jasanoff
  writes about assessing a technology before it is adopted, not about
  counting the harm of a policy after it. Her first limit is directly the
  case's "success described at one level": a saving reported by a
  predictive, accounting frame overstates what falls within its field of
  vision and leaves out what falls outside, which is CLAIM-348's point that
  the description adequate to one target does not settle another. Her
  vulnerability point, that "history, place, and social connectedness"
  fall out of population-level indicators, names what the case says a
  reinstated grant does not restore: the cohort and the local research
  tradition. She gives no method for measuring either, so she supports the
  case's "what it shows" and not any size of loss.
- **THEORY-151.** Not a rival: Longino is about the social conditions of
  objectivity inside a community; Jasanoff is about governance between
  experts and publics. Her claim that regulatory science's credibility
  rests on accountability rather than peer quality is a point where the
  two meet and could be compared.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **An essay.** It proposes a frame; it does not test it, and says so.
- **US and European examples of 1980–2002**, mostly biotechnology,
  chemicals and BSE. Whether the focal points work as institutions is not
  shown; no technology of humility in operation is described.
- **Participation** is argued to be necessary and insufficient, but who
  participates and how disagreements are settled is left open.
- **Errors and slips.** Note 39 numbers the environmental-justice order
  "Executive Order 12298"; Executive Order 12898 is the order of 11
  February 1994 on environmental justice that the text describes.

## Open questions

- What would a technology of humility look like as a standing procedure
  rather than a habit of thought, and how would one tell whether it had
  changed a decision?
