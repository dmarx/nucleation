# LIT-422: Challenges to the Hypothesis of Extended Cognition

Robert D. Rupert (2004), *Journal of Philosophy* 101(8), 389–428 —
DOI-10.5840/jphil2004101826

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, as a critic of Chalmers's
extended-mind programme. [NOTE-343](../notes.d/NOTE-343.md) is the close reading of the same
day, and it placed the work: **Active**.

**How it bears on [LIT-097](LIT-097.md).** It accepts the case and attacks the arguments
for it. Its alternative is not that the mind stops at the skin by
definition, but that a conservative hypothesis (embedded cognition, HEMC)
explains the same cases. On [LIT-097](LIT-097.md)'s §5 criteria it builds the dilemma
[NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md)'s open question about "cognitive bloat" anticipated: without past
endorsement, anyone who can dial directory assistance believes every listed
number; with it, conscious endorsement inside the organism is "the
ultimate source of cognitive authority", and the notebook looks like a
tool. On [LIT-097](LIT-097.md)'s appeal to explanatory simplicity and natural kinds, it
answers from memory research. [NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md) recorded that simplicity "is power"
was assumed, not argued, and this paper argues the opposite case: the
complex, internal-plus-input explanation is the illuminating one.

**What the critics show and do not.** The paper shows that the 1998
argument from kinds needs a kind that does causal-explanatory work, and
that fine-grained memory kinds do not cover notebooks. It does not show
that no coarse kind is useful. Its case there is a challenge plus
conservatism. Chalmers's later answer to its first objection is one line,
calling it "an implausible human chauvinism about cognition"
([LIT-415](LIT-415.md) §2, citing Rupert's 2009 book); Clark's book answers it
under the chapter title "HEMC, HEC, HEMC…" ([LIT-412](LIT-412.md), unread).

**On consciousness and on groups.** The paper keeps consciousness out
except at one point. In footnote 38 it argues that making conscious
endorsement itself extended would need "an extended theory of conscious
acts", and would face the same bloat. It sets socially distributed
cognition aside, saying that with group mental states "many of us, quite
rightly, lose our bearings", and it notes that corporate bodies have
neither "a plausible theory of consciousness" nor "paradigm cases of
conscious acts". That is a point about extended belief in groups, and it
bears on [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) only as a reason why group agency is easier to
grant than group endorsement. It does not discuss phenomenal group
consciousness.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice. Its §IX argues
that AI research aims at self-contained systems that work in many
environments, as a reason to doubt that extended systems are cognitive
science's proper objects. That is a claim about the aims of AI as of 2004,
not a recommendation for practice.
