# NOTE-tmpqk41c: Ntoumanis et al. — SDT-informed health interventions, a meta-analysis

## Contribution

The first meta-analysis of SDT-based *experiments* in health that tracks
both the theory's constructs and health outcomes, and asks whether changes
in the one covary with changes in the other across studies. It extends
Gillison et al. 2019, which had motivation outcomes only, and Ng et al.
2012, which was mostly correlational (pp. 216–217).

## Key insight

Manipulating need support works on the proximal constructs and, modestly,
on behaviour: interventions move perceived support most, need satisfaction
and autonomous motivation by about a third of a standard deviation, and
health behaviour by about half. What they do not move is relatedness and
the controlled side of motivation, and the psychological-health benefit
does not outlast the intervention.

## Assumptions

- **Eligibility**: experimental or quasi-experimental; an intervention its
  authors say was (partly) designed on SDT; at least one SDT construct and
  one health behaviour, physical or psychological health outcome measured
  after the intervention. Motivational interviewing without reference to
  SDT excluded (p. 217).
- **Effect sizes**: Hedges' g, random effects, most-versus-least
  need-supportive arm, baseline-adjusted where possible, last follow-up
  only; r = .50 imputed to combine correlated outcomes; ICC 0.05 imputed
  for clustered designs (p. 219).
- **"Do changes in SDT constructs engender changes"** is tested by
  regressing one effect size on another across studies (p. 220). This is a
  between-study association, not mediation within studies, which the
  authors list as not done (p. 237).
- Searches to November 2018; study selection by one research assistant,
  not in duplicate (pp. 218, 237).

## Key results

### The studies (p. 222)

- 73 studies: 68 journal articles, 58 RCTs (20 cluster-randomized), 15
  quasi-experimental; 30,088 participants (mean 412), 36.6% male, mean
  age 35.4; intervention arms had 7.4 (SD 4.6) more SDT strategies than
  controls; mean duration 133 days; follow-up one week to 30 months.
- Blinding was rare: participants 26.0%, data collectors 11.0%, analysts
  8.2%, providers 4.1%.

### Effects (Table 1, p. 222)

| outcome | end: k, g [95% CI] | follow-up: k, g [95% CI] |
|---|---|---|
| need support | 21, 0.64 [0.35, 0.93] | 6, 1.13 [−0.35, 2.61] |
| competence | 22, 0.31 [0.12, 0.49] | 11, 0.55 [−0.05, 1.14] |
| autonomy | 17, 0.37 [0.15, 0.60] | 6, 0.25 [−0.01, 0.51] |
| relatedness | 14, 0.20 [−0.04, 0.45] | 6, 0.03 [−0.20, 0.25] |
| combined need satisfaction | 23, 0.37 [0.19, 0.55] | 11, 0.49 [−0.05, 1.02] |
| autonomous motivation | 37, 0.30 [0.17, 0.42] | 14, 0.18 [−0.00, 0.36] |
| controlled motivation | 18, 0.07 [−0.04, 0.18] | 6, 0.02 [−0.24, 0.27] |
| amotivation | 14, −0.07 [−0.28, 0.14] | 5, −0.26 [−0.54, 0.03] |
| health behaviour | 49, 0.45 [0.33, 0.57] | 28, 0.28 [0.17, 0.38] |
| physical health | 16, 0.04 [−0.15, 0.23] | 14, 0.28 [0.03, 0.53] |
| psychological health | 22, 0.29 [0.14, 0.45] | 10, 0.14 [−0.09, 0.36] |

- I² 43–99%. Without outliers (Table 2) competence (0.33), combined need
  satisfaction (0.28) and autonomous motivation (0.22) become significant
  at follow-up, and physical health at the end (0.13).

### Moderators (pp. 223–232, Tables 3–6)

- Neither the number of SDT techniques nor the difference in techniques
  between arms moderated any effect (p. 236).
- "Being positive that the individual can succeed" went with larger
  effects on several constructs and on behaviour at follow-up; its effect
  on autonomous motivation was confounded with non-specific reward (BCT
  10.3), which alone remained significant in a joint meta-regression
  (B = 0.78) (p. 230).
- Three competence-support techniques (identify barriers, problem solving,
  develop plans) went with *smaller* effects on psychological health.
- Allocation concealment reduced effects on autonomous motivation
  (B = −0.32) and on psychological health at follow-up (−0.74); RCTs had
  smaller behaviour effects than quasi-experiments (−0.57).
- Exploratory, "hypothesis generating", no correction for multiple tests
  (p. 236).

### Across-study covariation (Tables 7–8, pp. 228–234)

- With all studies, no SDT effect size predicted the behaviour effect size.
  After removing one multivariate outlier (Ha et al. 2017), autonomous
  motivation (β = 0.66) and need support (0.39) did; they also predicted
  behaviour at follow-up (0.67, 0.35). Need satisfaction effect sizes did
  not predict behaviour.
- Psychological health effect size covaried with the effect sizes of
  autonomous motivation (0.58), need satisfaction (0.41), relatedness
  (0.79), autonomy (0.57), competence (0.39) and need support (0.31), k ≤
  22. Nothing predicted physical health.
- Egger's test: possible small-study bias for health behaviour (p = .01)
  and psychological health (p = .006) (p. 230).

## Concepts

- **Need support** — behaviour of significant others that supports the
  three needs, as perceived by the recipient (pp. 215–216).
- **Combined need satisfaction** — overall or averaged satisfaction of the
  three needs (p. 219).
- **Psychological health** — e.g. "quality of life, depression" (p. 220);
  the breakdown is in Supplementary Table S4, not read.

## Connections

- **Gillison et al. 2019** found larger effects on autonomy (g = 0.84);
  the authors attribute the gap to inclusion criteria (p. 234).
- **Ng et al. 2012**, the correlational health meta-analysis, gave the path
  model this tests experimentally (p. 216).
- **Slemp et al. 2024** (LIT-tmpeapgr) cites this paper as the
  experimental counterpart of its correlations.
- The 93-item behaviour change technique taxonomy of Michie et al. 2013
  is coded alongside (p. 218).

## Bearing on the record

- **THEORY-052: the registration overstated what this meta-analysis
  offers.** LIT-tmpa0lm5 was registered as "whether manipulating need
  support changes outcomes, which THEORY-052's promote_when asks for". The
  promote_when asks for interventions that "reduce need frustration and
  reduce symptoms of several disorders, mediated by the change in
  frustration". This paper measures no need frustration, tests no
  mediation (p. 237), and pools "psychological health" outcomes in a
  general population mostly targeted at physical activity. Its psychological
  health effect is small at the end and gone at follow-up. It does not move
  THEORY-052.
- **It does answer a different question the registration also raised:**
  manipulating need support changes perceived support, need satisfaction
  and autonomous motivation, and behaviour. That is experimental evidence
  for SDT's contextual claim that the correlational syntheses (Slemp et al.,
  NOTE-tmpbntuj) cannot give.
- **QUESTION-tmpee04a.** Two findings bear on need-hood. Relatedness
  satisfaction did not respond to the interventions (g = 0.20, ns), so the
  experimental leverage is on autonomy and competence. And the
  across-study link between need satisfaction and psychological health
  (β = 0.41) is again a well-being outcome, so it does not escape the
  interdefinition.
- **No dose–response.** The number of SDT techniques, and the difference in
  techniques between arms, predicted nothing (p. 236); a rewards technique
  outperformed the SDT technique it co-occurred with on autonomous
  motivation (p. 230). Neither fits a simple story in which more need
  support gives more benefit.
- **THEORY-050** (inflexibility): no overlap; noted because the record pairs
  the two transdiagnostic accounts.
- Filed from it: THEORY-tmpdzmer (Active).
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- Selection not in duplicate; coders varied; no test of indirect effects
  (the authors', p. 237).
- Blinding rare; allocation concealment shrank effects; possible small-study
  bias on the two outcomes that matter most.
- Most studies are physical-activity promotion; few measured health
  outcomes or long-term motivation (p. 237).
- Intervention dose (frequency, duration of contact) could not be coded
  (p. 218).
- Follow-up estimates for the SDT constructs rest on k = 5–14 with very
  wide intervals.

## Open questions

- Does the change in need satisfaction or autonomous motivation mediate the
  change in behaviour within trials?
- Can an intervention reduce need frustration, and does that reduce
  symptoms (THEORY-052)?
- Why does relatedness not respond: the techniques used, or the target
  behaviours (p. 234)?
