# QUESTION-472: Can a party's ability to activate another's genuine moral obligations constitute control over the terms of that other's dependence, so that the moral cost of withdrawal does what a contractual or material barrier to exit does, and if so, what separates it from ordinary reliance on someone's conscientiousness?

## Why it is open

A30 §4 of the exchange
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afa2a8d-2598-83ea-8d1a-d9ceb992a99c), on
[CLAIM-690](../claims.d/CLAIM-690.md):

> Suppose a hospital is critically dependent on a particular nurse. That
> dependence might give the nurse considerable bargaining power. Or it might
> leave the nurse vulnerable to exploitation if the employer knows the
> nurse will not abandon patients and the nurse has no effective way to
> withdraw without violating their commitments.

[CLAIM-690](../claims.d/CLAIM-690.md) sorts a dependence by control over its terms: the capacity to
withdraw, renegotiate, set conditions of supply or invoke outside
enforcement. [CASE-264](../cases.d/CASE-264.md)'s workshops vary that control by contract and
discretion. A30's question is whether a third route belongs on the list:
the part can withdraw in law and in fact, and does not, because withdrawal
would breach a genuine obligation to third parties whom the controlling
party has placed in its care. If that counts as control over the terms,
[CLAIM-682](../claims.d/CLAIM-682.md)'s third condition (the dependent "lacks effective means of
protection, influence, challenge or justified withdrawal") can be met by a
conscience.

What turns on it, as the continuation's filing of the same turns put it
(share https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b):
[TERM-124](../terms.d/TERM-124.md) defines domination as a capacity to set or change the terms on
which a party can act, exit, contest or renegotiate. If the ability to
trigger an obligation is such a capacity, a party with no authority over
the person can dominate them through their own conscience; [CLAIM-703](../claims.d/CLAIM-703.md)
says how, and [CLAIM-713](../claims.d/CLAIM-713.md) says it can happen without intent. If it is not,
those are exploitation without domination, and the record's two terms
come apart there.

## What would count as an answer

- **Yes**, with a criterion that separates it from legitimate reliance:
  for instance, that the controlling party set the circumstances that
  activate the obligation and could fund their alternative (A15 §8's
  "Control" and "Exit" dimensions), as [CLAIM-703](../claims.d/CLAIM-703.md) and [CLAIM-713](../claims.d/CLAIM-713.md),
  from the same exchange, propose.
- **No**, with an argument that an obligation to third parties is not a
  term of the dependence between the two parties, so that the leverage is
  real but belongs to another relation (the third parties' claims), and
  [CLAIM-690](../claims.d/CLAIM-690.md)'s list is complete.

- **No**, by a second route: an argument that a capacity to set terms
  must act on the person's options and not on their reasons, so that a
  triggered obligation is a cost the person bears and not a term another
  sets.

A case varying only the source of the barrier to exit, contract in one
arrangement and the part's own obligation in the other, at the same
dependence, would test it as [CASE-264](../cases.d/CASE-264.md) tests control: two people of equal
indispensability who differ only in a commitment to those served, with
their leverage over their terms compared.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Freyd, read

Freyd's adaptive blindness is a mechanism by which dependence on another shapes what the dependent can afford to
know: "When the betrayer is someone we are dependent upon, the very mechanisms that normally protect us ... become a
problem" (p. 27). It is a neighbour of the question's concern with control over the terms of dependence, through
attachment rather than activated obligation, and leaves the question open. Relation: none (a note only; if the filer
writes it as evidence on the question, [ADR-093](../decisions.d/ADR-093.md) asks for bears_on from the THEORY).

## Note of 2026-11-16: Tessman, read

In Tessman's case (p. 14), A isolates B, cuts her off from support and undermines "her chances of
earning a living that would enable her to leave A". Her obligation to their child persists through
all of it. That is a case in which the moral cost of withdrawal and material control over exit work
together. Tessman does not ask whether A's ability to activate the obligation is itself control; the
question stays open. Relation: bears_on.

## Note of 2026-11-16: Keller, read

Keller's teachers case (p. 24) is a candidate instance: an official times changes to teachers'
conditions for "the weeks before students take their final exams and graduate", so that the right to
strike, though intact, carries a moral cost, and "It really is more difficult to go on strike if you
are a nurse or a teacher than if you are a policy analyst or a software developer" (p. 25). That is
the question's "moral cost of withdrawal" doing the work of a barrier. Keller's only line between this
and ordinary reliance on conscientiousness is that the act is wrongful, which he does not analyse, so
the question's second half stays open. Relation: bears_on ([THEORY-1917](../theory.d/THEORY-1917.md)).

## Note of 2026-11-17: Keller read: for fiduciaries, activating obligations weakens their hand in bargaining
<!-- inactive-ok-file: LIT-2682 LIT-2692 — new, Unfetched or Unavailable; the reading filed on 2026-11-18 and the works not reached -->

Keller 2016, read ([NOTE-2005](../notes.d/NOTE-2005.md), [THEORY-1917](../theory.d/THEORY-1917.md), bears_on). Evidence toward yes, for fiduciaries. Whoever can influence a fiduciary's circumstances "will be able to influence the facts about what special obligations you have" (p. 22), and the exposure makes it "more difficult, morally, to keep control over her time and energy, and to bargain for better conditions at work" (p. 26). His teachers case uses the moral cost of withdrawal as leverage in bargaining over pay and conditions: their "sense of obligation to their students ... will make it very difficult for them to exercise" the right to strike (p. 24). That is the question's moral cost of withdrawing doing the work of control over exit. Keller does not frame it as dependence, and his cases are deliberate.

Robillard's dissertation ([LIT-2682](../literature.d/LIT-2682.md), Unfetched) and Robillard and Strawser's book ([LIT-2692](../literature.d/LIT-2692.md), Unavailable), registered for this question, were not read.
