# THEORY-868: The structuralist methods and evidence in consciousness science support only that experiences have structure or stand in relations to other experiences, not that an experience is nothing but its relations; the stronger, metaphysical thesis implies that relational knowledge fixes what an experience is like, that every new experience changes all the others, and that a system with a single experience is impossible, and each consequence is doubtful

## Source

Negro (2025), [LIT-1346](../literature.d/LIT-1346.md), *Synthese* 206:8, read in full in
[NOTE-1054](../notes.d/NOTE-1054.md): §2 (the three structuralisms and three senses of
"structural", pp. 3–7), §3 (the three arguments, pp. 7–17) and §4 (what
survives, pp. 17–18).

## What was actually shown

- **A conceptual result.** "Consciousness is structural" has three senses:
  an experience has an internal structure; it is in a structure; it "just
  is the structure" (pp. 5–6). The first two are "fairly
  uncontroversial". Only the third yields metaphysical structuralism, and
  neither of the others entails it. Methodological structuralism (the
  structural constraint on neural correlates) and epistemological
  structuralism (aligning similarity structures across subjects) need only
  the first two: "thinking that an experience is within a structure is
  sufficient to do so – we do not need to think that the experience is the
  structure" (p. 18). Support from their success to the metaphysical
  thesis is "at best abductive" (p. 5).
- **Three valid arguments against the third sense.**
  - *Relational Mary* has seen every colour but orange and knows all of
    orange's relations. If she learns something on seeing it, relational
    facts do not fix character. If the reply is that the relations to
    orange are realized only once orange is, then "the relata are
    metaphysically prior to the relations" (p. 10).
  - *Novel experience.* If an experience is its place in the structure,
    adding blue to a system that has seen red, yellow and green changes
    what red is like. "A novel experience does not change other
    experiences in a subject's phenomenal repertoire" (p. 13). A reported
    change after expertise may be a change of judgement; making judgement
    constitutive "aligns metaphysical structuralism with the eliminativist
    claim that there is nothing more to experience than cognitive
    capacities" (p. 15).
  - *Lonely experience.* The view needs at least two related experiences;
    a system with one is conceivable, and a one-sensor workspace system is
    offered as nomologically possible (pp. 15–17).

What could have come out otherwise is the novel experience premise, which
is empirical. Nothing in the paper tests it.

## What this does not say

- **It is not a refutation.** Each argument's second premise is an
  intuition (what Mary learns; that old experiences stay as they were) or a
  conceivability claim. Lyre ([THEORY-880](THEORY-880.md)) accepts holism and predicts
  that dichromats experience every colour differently, so the novel
  experience argument's premise is the point at issue, not common ground.
- **It does not say experiences have intrinsic qualia.** The non-relational
  property Mary learns may be "fully reducible to the physical" (fn. 5).
- **It does not touch structuralisms that are not about external
  relations.** A view on which character is fixed by an experience's
  internal structure, or by causal organization rather than relations to
  other experiences, is outside its target.
- **The lonely experience system's consciousness is borrowed.** It is
  conscious only on the assumption of a global workspace theory, which the
  author doubts (fn. 9).
- **The eliminativism charge is short.** One paragraph, against the option
  of making judgement and other cognitive capacities constitutive. It is
  not argued that every such view denies phenomenal properties.

## Note of 2026-10-25: Isaac's methodological structuralism

Isaac ([LIT-1361](../literature.d/LIT-1361.md), read in [NOTE-1163](../notes.d/NOTE-1163.md)) is a structuralist of the kind this
THEORY allows. He holds structuralism as "a stance", not a "theoretical
assertation [sic] or result" (§1), and an isomorphism between experience and
brain as a regulative ideal of method. Nowhere does he say that an
experience just is its place in a structure. His evidence that quality-space
models target experience is convergence between models built by different
behavioural procedures ([THEORY-953](THEORY-953.md)), which needs only that experiences
stand in relations, Negro's second sense.

Two of his remarks touch Negro's arguments without meeting them. That a
shade "considered in isolation" is "not apt to play any representational
role" is about representation, not experience, so it does not make the
lonely experience impossible. And he leaves open whether bare sensations of
pleasure or pain are meaningful in isolation, calling it "a complex and
non-obvious phenomenological claim". He does not discuss holism or novel
experiences, so nothing in the paper bears on the empirical premise the
promotion test asks about. The comparison is the record's.
