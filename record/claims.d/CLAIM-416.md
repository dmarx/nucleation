# CLAIM-416: Competing perspectives compete over the tests that measure them as well as under them: success under an evaluative regime is evidence of competence relative to that regime, a fair test need not be neutral across competences, and a perspective that gains influence over the test can change what later success shows

## The claim

The owner, at U35: "various contemporaneous and geographically related
schools of martial arts vying for (regional/ideological/demographical/
physical) dominance might be another useful case to examine."

A35 drew from it "competing definitions of success, not merely competing
methods of achieving one agreed-upon success", and two forms of power:
winning under a rule, and influencing the rule. Its case is the rivalry of
Gracie jiu-jitsu and luta livre ([CASE-130](../cases.d/CASE-130.md)), where "a contest between
traditions requires them to negotiate which world their techniques will
be tested in". A36 put the distinction as a display formula, fairness of a
test is not neutrality across all possible competencies: "A competition
may offer equal procedural conditions while favoring capabilities better
suited to its chosen task. This is not an objection to athletic
competition. All meaningful tests must specify what is being tested. It
is an objection to generalizing from a result under one set of conditions
to universal superiority across every conceivable condition." A40 stated
the epistemic core: "Success under an evaluative regime is evidence of
competence relative to that regime. It does not by itself establish that
the regime adequately measures what matters."

Three parts, then:

1. **Relativity.** Success is evidence of competence under the regime that
   scored it.
2. **Non-neutrality.** A test can be fair, equal in its conditions for all
   competitors, and still select for some competences over others.
3. **Reflexivity.** A competitor that gains influence over the test,
   through rule-making, prestige, recruitment or institution-building,
   changes what later success under it shows. A36: "the activity being
   evaluated is altered by the evaluation system", and "competition can
   alter the competitors' characters, rather than merely selecting among
   fixed characters".

The third part is where it meets the record. [CLAIM-402](CLAIM-402.md) says a category
that institutions act on produces regularities that then confirm it, and
[THEORY-854](../theory.d/THEORY-854.md) (Espeland and Sauder's rankings) gives the mechanism for a
published rank. This claim is their companion for contests between
perspectives: the thing reacting to the measure is a competitor that can
also reshape the measure. A37 extended it beyond sport: Challenger, where
"outcomes evaluated under particular conditions can be overgeneralized
into claims about the competence of the organization itself"
([CASE-138](../cases.d/CASE-138.md)); elections and party realignment ([CASE-133](../cases.d/CASE-133.md)); and the
record's own measures, which [CLAIM-190](CLAIM-190.md) already says cannot certify
themselves.

## What it does not say

- **Not that success shows only competence under the exact rules.**
  Success under one regime can be evidence of competence beyond it, where
  the capacities it tests generalize across independently justified
  contexts. The methodological problem is which generalizations are
  warranted; a test that varies the regime while holding competitors'
  relevant differences fixed would separate capacities robust across
  conditions from those adapted to one test. (Added the same day after a
  review the owner relayed, against an over-relativist reading.)
- **Not that a test is unfair.** A36's own qualification above.
- **Not motive.** A36: "the rules shape behavior. That's well supported.
  The stronger claim is that a governing institution changes rules *in
  order to entrench particular competitors*. That requires separate
  evidence … We should not infer the second from the first."
- **Not that the historical particulars are settled.** The 1931 rules
  dispute and the later matches rest on Penglase ([LIT-1391](../literature.d/LIT-1391.md), unread)
  and on the replies' summaries. The sport studies A36 cited on judo and
  taekwondo rule changes were not identified and are not filed.

## Note of 2026-10-22: instances and a general mechanism

- **A contest over the rules, documented.** Penglase ([LIT-1391](../literature.d/LIT-1391.md)): "the
  Gracies often insisted on rules that would benefit them and their
  fighting style" (p. 86), and "Both of these rules – in particular
  insisting on the use of the judogi – also favored the Gracies in the
  ring" (p. 86). This is a competitor bargaining before a match, not an
  institution entrenching one, so the claim's "not motive" clause stands;
  it is an instance of the weaker form.
- **Lowering the test after passing it.** In the Rogers report ([LIT-1393](../literature.d/LIT-1393.md),
  p. 148), the reasoning the commission found: "We can lower our
  standards a little bit because we got away with it last time."
- **The judge has its own contest.** Abbott's precursor to [LIT-1366](../literature.d/LIT-1366.md)
  ([NOTE-1077](../notes.d/NOTE-1077.md)) finds New York's legislators setting the test of medical
  competence for reasons of their own politics.
- **The general mechanism.** Sewell ([LIT-1383](../literature.d/LIT-1383.md)) states the reflexive part in
  general: what a success shows is itself contested, and the reading that
  wins redistributes power. His example: the gains of a cavalry charge
  "may be attributed to the superior discipline and elan of the cavalry
  officers and thereby enhance the power of an aristocratic officer corps,
  or it may be attributed to the commanding general" (p. 19). The claim
  could cite this as the mechanism of which competition over tests is a
  case. Swidler ([LIT-1367](../literature.d/LIT-1367.md)) supports only the relativity half: ideologies
  survive by the situations they are enacted in, and she does not discuss
  competitors reshaping those situations.
