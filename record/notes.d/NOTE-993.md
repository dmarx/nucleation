# NOTE-993: Kalckert & Ehrsson — Moving a rubber hand that feels like your own

## Contribution

The paper builds a rubber hand illusion out of movement instead of touch,
with a physical model hand, and uses it to manipulate the sense of
ownership and the sense of agency separately in one paradigm. Each can be
present without the other: passive movement gives ownership without
agency, and an anatomically impossible hand gives agency without
ownership. Before it, studies of moving hands had always had agency
together with ownership, so "it was not possible to test for double
dissociation" (p. 2).

## Key insight

What makes a moving hand feel like mine and what makes me feel I am
moving it depend on different conditions. Ownership needs the seen hand
to be where and how one's own hand could be, and to move in time with it;
it does not need me to be the one moving it. Agency needs me to be the one
moving it, and the movement to come in time; it does not need the hand to
be mine. Both need synchrony. Yet when both are present they are not
simply added: agency over an owned hand is rated stronger, and only then
do the two ratings go together.

## Assumptions

- **The questionnaire measures the two senses.** Sixteen statements on a
  −3 to +3 scale: four for ownership, four for agency, and four control
  statements for each, which "served as controls for task compliancy,
  suggestibility, and expectancy effects" (p. 4, Table 1). Items were
  adapted from Botvinick & Cohen (1998) and Longo et al. (2008). Scores are
  the mean of each group of four, and a mean ≥ +1 counts as affirmed.
- **Drift measures ownership, and only with the questionnaire.** One
  pointing movement before and one after each induction, with the left
  index finger to the felt height of the right, eyes closed; three trials
  per condition. "We do not rely on experimental measures in isolation";
  the illusion requires both significant drift and affirmed ratings,
  citing Rohde et al. (2011) (p. 5). There is no objective measure of
  agency.
- **Passive means without efference.** In passive trials the
  experimenter moved the rod, with the link to the participant's finger
  kept, and checked by feel for "the lack of active or reactive forces"
  (p. 4). Both fingers then move together, so the participant gets
  kinaesthetic and visual feedback with no motor command.
- **The asynchronous and passive movements imitate the participant's.**
  The experimenter copied amplitude, frequency and double taps from
  earlier trials (p. 4). In the asynchronous condition the rod was
  detached and the experimenter moved the model finger by hand with
  "approximately 500 ms delay", so that the fingers "appeared to be moving
  in opposite phases". The participants did not learn the mechanism by
  debriefing (p. 4).
- **Between-group experiments.** Each experiment is a separate sample;
  conditions within an experiment are within-subject. The comparison of
  asynchrony with passive movement crosses experiments.

## Key results

**Setup (pp. 3–4, Fig. 1).** The participant's gloved right hand lay in a
box 30 cm in front; a gloved, life-sized wooden model hand lay on the box,
a blanket covering the gap to the shoulder. A rod through the box joined
the two index fingers. Participants tapped at about 1 Hz with occasional
double taps, for 3 minutes per condition in Experiments 1 and 3.

**Sample.** 104 naïve participants: 20 (Exp. 1), 20 (Exp. 2), 32 (Exp. 3),
32 (Exp. 4); no one took part twice (p. 3). Exp. 2 lost three
questionnaires, so its correlations have n = 17 (p. 6).

**Experiment 1: synchrony, questionnaire (p. 5, Fig. 2).**
- Synchronous: ownership 1.63 (SD 1.27), agency 2.44 (SD 0.56); "75% of
  the participants" affirmed ownership.
- Asynchronous: ownership −0.99 (SD 1.5), agency 0.66 (SD 1.19).
- Wilcoxon: ownership against its control, synchronous, Z = 3.792,
  p < 0.001; ownership synchronous against asynchronous, Z = 3.304,
  p = 0.001; agency against its control, Z = 3.825, p < 0.001; agency
  synchronous against asynchronous, Z = 3.767, p < 0.001 (pp. 5–6).
- On the rendered Figure 2, agency under asynchrony (0.66) is still
  marked above its control (about −0.8, "**"). Agency was reduced, not
  abolished, by asynchrony.

**Experiment 2: synchrony, drift (p. 6, Figs. 3–4).**
- Drift 1.73 cm (SD 2.05) synchronous against −0.35 cm (SD 1.04)
  asynchronous; Wilcoxon Z = 3.062, p = 0.002.
- Drift correlated with ownership ratings in the synchronous condition,
  "Spearman r = 0.557, n = 17, p = 0.020", and r = 0.532 (n = 16) without
  an outlier; pooled over conditions, r = 0.676 (n = 34). Not with agency
  ratings (synchronous r = 0.274, p = 0.287). "Thus, the proprioceptive
  drift is related to the experience of ownership but is not related to
  agency" (p. 6).

**Experiment 3: active/passive × congruent/incongruent, questionnaire
(pp. 7–9, Fig. 5).** All four conditions synchronous.

| condition | ownership | agency |
|---|---|---|
| active congruent | 2.12 (SD 1.03) | 2.52 (SD 0.55) |
| active incongruent | −0.62 (SD 1.62) | 2.2 (SD 0.78) |
| passive congruent | 1.57 (SD 1.3) | −0.61 (SD 1.5) |
| passive incongruent | −0.91 (SD 1.6) | −1.5 (SD 1.64) |

- Each affirmed score exceeded its control (Z between 4.920 and 4.940,
  p < 0.001) (p. 7).
- Ownership differed across conditions (Friedman χ²(3, n = 32) = 65.287,
  p < 0.001); congruent above incongruent for active (Z = 4.863) and
  passive (Z = 4.656), both p < 0.001.
- Active above passive ownership in the congruent position, Z = 2.765,
  p < 0.01, but not in the incongruent position, Z = 1.961, p = 0.050,
  against a Bonferroni threshold of 0.0125. The interaction test was not
  significant (t = 0.744, df = 31, p = 0.462) (p. 8).
- Agency differed across conditions (Friedman χ² = 75.247, p < 0.001);
  active above passive in both positions (Z = 4.863 and 4.772,
  p < 0.001). Agency was also higher in congruent than incongruent
  positions, for active (Z = 2.631) and passive (Z = 3.207, p = 0.001),
  "even between the passive conditions ... in which participants did not
  affirm experiencing agency per se". The interaction for agency
  approached significance (t = −1.950, df = 31, p = 0.060) (pp. 8–9).
- Responders: 87.5% and 78.1% for ownership (active and passive
  congruent), 96.9% and 93.8% for agency (active congruent and
  incongruent); McNemar p = 0.375 and 1.00. The authors conclude that the
  mean differences come from "the strength of these experiences among
  responders" (p. 9).
- Agency control statements rose "in the passive congruent and passive
  incongruent conditions" without reaching +1 (p. 9). On the rendered
  figure they sit near +0.3 and +0.8, above the agency scores in both
  passive conditions.
- Ownership and agency correlated only in the active congruent condition,
  "r = 0.503, n = 32, p = 0.003"; elsewhere r = 0.19 to 0.22, n.s.
  (pp. 9–10, Fig. 6).

**Experiment 4: the same design, drift (p. 9, Fig. 7).** Drift +0.77 cm
active congruent, +0.6 cm passive congruent, −0.35 cm active incongruent,
−0.004 cm passive incongruent. Repeated-measures ANOVA: position
F(1,31) = 7.878, p < 0.01; mode F(1,31) = 0.144, p = 0.707; no interaction.
In the incongruent trials "the position of the wooden index finger was
kept constant" (p. 4), so the moving finger was seen at the same place in
all four conditions.

**Asynchrony against passive movement (p. 9).** A post hoc unpaired t test
across experiments: agency under passive congruent movement (−0.61) was
lower than under asynchrony (0.66), t = 3.105, df = 50, p = 0.003,
"demonstrating that passive movement eliminated agency more completely
than visuo-somatic asynchrony".

**Interpretation (pp. 9–12).**
- Ownership: "meaningful correlations among all available sensory
  channels are critical for body ownership rather than signals from a
  specific sensory modality" (p. 10). The illusion took "approximately
  10–30 s" to develop, by anecdote.
- Agency: "as long as the intention to move is present, there exists an
  inherent tendency to attribute authorship of actions to the self, which
  is then cancelled by the detection of mismatches" (p. 11). "The sense of
  agency is best explained by a model in which both motor intentions and
  comparator mechanisms are essential contributing factors."
- Body agency: three observations (stronger agency when congruent, the
  near-significant interaction, the correlation only in the owned
  condition) "provide indirect evidence that body agency is distinct from
  agency over external objects" (p. 11). Participants said informally they
  moved the model "exactly like when I move my real hand".
- Ownership feeds agency: "even in the absence of an engaged agency
  mechanism, the presence of ownership still drives a residual sense of
  agency" (pp. 11–12).
- Agency and ownership: "Our data do not provide conclusive evidence that
  agency enhances ownership"; the active–passive difference in ratings
  may come from "general differences in task demands" (p. 12).
- Conclusion: "the first direct demonstration of the double dissociation
  of ownership and agency in a single rubber hand illusion paradigm"
  (p. 12).

## Concepts

- **Sense of ownership** — the experience of the body as one's own,
  measured by four statements such as "I felt as if the rubber hand was my
  hand".
- **Sense of agency** — "the sense of authorship over bodily movements"
  (p. 2), measured by four statements such as "I felt as if I was causing
  the movement I saw".
- **Body agency and external agency** — agency over one's own limb,
  constrained by the body's geometry, against agency over an object or a
  tool, which rests on "learned arbitrary mappings between an action and a
  goal" (pp. 2, 11). The distinction is the authors' and is offered as
  preliminary.
- **Moving rubber hand illusion** — ownership induced by correlated
  visual and somatic (proprioceptive, kinaesthetic, tactile) signals of
  movement, without brushing.
- **Responder** — a participant whose score is "> 1"; a non-responder
  scores "≤ 0" (p. 9).

## Connections

The paper takes the rubber hand illusion from Botvinick & Cohen (1998)
and the anatomical-incongruence manipulation from Ehrsson et al. (2004)
and Tsakiris & Haggard (2005). Its account of agency is Frith et al.'s
comparator, qualified by intention-based accounts (Haggard 2005; Wegner;
Sato). It answers Tsakiris et al. (2006), who compared active and passive
moving hands on drift alone and found them equal, but had no condition
with agency and no ownership. It draws on Farrer et al. (2008) for the
finding that delayed feedback is still judged self-generated or "biased"
rather than "other". Rohde, Di Luca & Ernst (2011), filed by a sibling
reader, is cited for the risk of relying on drift alone, and Longo et al.
(2008), also filed by a sibling, for the questionnaire and for a "feeling
of control" in the static illusion. None of these was read here.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md) (Proposed).** This is the direct experimental test of the
  dissociation half of Gallagher's split, and it is the one most affected.
  - Its refutation clause includes "if reports of ownership and agency
    could not be dissociated outside involuntary movement". The active
    incongruent condition dissociates them outside involuntary movement,
    in the direction involuntary movement cannot give: agency without
    ownership (C3). So this route to refutation is closed for reports of a
    model hand. The passive condition is involuntary movement and so
    repeats the known direction.
  - It fits the mechanism half without testing it. Ownership followed the
    sensory conditions (position and synchrony) and not the motor
    command; agency followed the motor command. That is the pattern
    [THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md) states: ownership from sensory feedback, agency from what
    "precedes action". But there is no forward-model or efference-copy
    measure, and the authors' own account adds intention to the
    comparator (C8).
  - It qualifies the split. The two are not independent: congruent
    position raised agency even in passive movement, and agency and
    ownership correlated only in the owned, active condition (C7).
    [THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md) says the two are "distinct and come apart"; this says they
    come apart and interact.
  - It does not touch thought insertion, the patient studies or the
    immunity principle, which are what [THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md)'s promote_when asks
    for. So it does not meet the promote_when. I would propose
    (proposal only) that [THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md)'s refutation clause cite this result
    as having addressed the dissociation outside involuntary movement for
    the hand, so that what remains open is the comparator and thought.
- **[THEORY-765](../theory.d/THEORY-765.md) (Proposed).** It **partly meets** the promote_when
  and **does not meet** its core.
  - Met: a first-hand reading of a replication, in a visuomotor rather
    than visuo-tactile form, with sample sizes, named tests (Wilcoxon,
    Friedman, ANOVA, Spearman) and means and SDs for both questionnaire
    and drift. No standardised effect sizes are given.
  - Not met: the suggestion control. The control statements are the same
    kind of guard the THEORY already says is insufficient ("control items
    guard against blanket agreement, not against suggestion specific to
    the touch and ownership items"). Expectations were not measured or
    matched, and trait suggestibility was not measured. Each participant
    saw all conditions of an experiment, and the predicted pattern (a
    rotated hand is not mine; a finger moved for me is not moved by me) is
    the obvious one. A double dissociation that matches what anyone would
    expect does not, on its own, rule out expectation. That is my reading;
    the paper does not discuss demand characteristics. The sibling filings
    of Lush 2020 and Lush et al. 2020 are the place this is argued.
  - On the drift refutation condition, it **bears against** the
    alternative. Drift did not appear under asynchrony (−0.35 cm), and in
    Experiment 4 the moving finger was seen at the same place in all
    conditions yet drift appeared only with the anatomically placed hand.
    So drift here does not track visual exposure. It also tracked
    ownership ratings and not agency ratings (C6). That is a stronger
    behavioural tie than Botvinick & Cohen's, though still on ratings
    collected after a separate, shorter induction.
  - Its scope is brushing; this result is movement. I would not change
    the THEORY's status. Proposal only: the promote_when could say whether
    a visuomotor replication counts, since it changes the stimulus the
    THEORY names.
- **[THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md) (Proposed).** **Does not meet** the promote_when,
  and is consistent with the account.
  - Nothing is varied parametrically and no model is fitted.
  - The responder analysis is suggestive of graded ownership: similar
    proportions of responders for active and passive congruent movement,
    with different means among them (p. 9). But the responder rule leaves
    scores between 0 and 1 unclassified, and the comparison is post hoc.
  - The anatomical-congruence effect is the prior the account invokes
    (a hand where no hand could be is not owned), measured here as an
    effect, not apart from it, which is the circularity the THEORY's own
    text names.
  - The real hand losing ownership, the second commitment, is not tested.
    The control statement "as if my right hand had disappeared" bears on
    it, but it was averaged into the ownership control score, which stayed
    negative in every condition; the item is not reported alone.
  - Drift did not differ between active and passive movement, which fits
    the account's treatment of motor signals as one more prediction with
    no special standing for ownership; the authors draw no such inference.
- **[THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md) (Proposed).** No bearing. The paper uses no
  interoceptive signal. It does show visuomotor correlation inducing
  ownership on its own, which is the signal Seth reports overshadowing the
  cardiac cue; it says nothing about the cardiac cue itself.
- **[THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) (Proposed).** **No bearing on promotion.**
  - First count: self-location and whole-body self-identification were
    not measured. The study is about part ownership only.
  - Second count: the passive condition shows ownership without agency
    in adults whose agency is "intact but unused", which the promote_when
    names as evidence that cannot settle the agency thesis.
  - Read at the level of the hand, the results fit "causally enabling,
    not constitutive": ownership arose without agency, and active
    movement raised ownership ratings only weakly and not drift (C9). But
    the THEORY's thesis is about minimal selfhood, which by its own
    terms the rubber hand does not reach. The finding that ownership
    raises agency (C7) runs the other way from the thesis, and concerns a
    hand.
- **[THEORY-322](../theory.d/THEORY-322.md) (Proposed).** Weak support for a feedback-independent,
  intention-based contribution to agency: with intention present and
  feedback mismatched, agency was 0.66; with feedback matched and no
  intention, −0.61 (C8). That fits the claim that agency draws on signals
  from action selection, "not only" on comparison. But it is a post hoc,
  cross-experiment comparison of explicit judgements, which [THEORY-322](../theory.d/THEORY-322.md)'s
  promote_when sets aside, and it does not vary outcome probability. It
  does not meet the promote_when.
- **[LIT-1009](../literature.d/LIT-1009.md), [NOTE-827](NOTE-827.md) (Haggard).** [NOTE-827](NOTE-827.md)'s C1, that involuntary movement
  gives ownership without agency, has here an experimental instance with
  a model hand (C2). Haggard's C8, that the comparator explains
  non-agency but not the positive experience, is close to these authors'
  reading that intention supplies a default that mismatch cancels; they
  reach it from data, he from argument.
- **[NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md), C6 (Metzinger, [LIT-450](../literature.d/LIT-450.md)).** Supports the manipulation half
  again, with visuomotor rather than visuo-tactile correlation. Nothing on
  "simplest form of self-awareness".
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** A worked case of report and behaviour diverging.
  Active movement raised ownership ratings (Z = 2.765) without raising
  drift, and the authors decline to trust the ratings alone, calling the
  combination of measures their "golden standard" (p. 12). The agency
  half of the dissociation rests on report alone, with no implicit
  measure such as intentional binding.
- **[LIT-453](../literature.d/LIT-453.md), [NOTE-377](NOTE-377.md) (Gallagher).** The paper cites Gallagher (2000) as one
  source of the distinction it tests.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **Report only for agency.** The double dissociation is in questionnaire
  scores. Drift is a measure of ownership; nothing objective measures
  agency.
- **No expectation control.** The control statements guard against
  agreeing with everything, not against expecting the predicted pattern.
  Conditions were within-subject, so participants could compare them.
  The experimenter, who moved the rod in the passive and asynchronous
  conditions, knew the hypotheses.
- **Asynchrony is not matched.** The asynchronous movements were made by
  hand by the experimenter, imitating the participant, at "approximately"
  500 ms; the delay was not measured or held fixed.
- **No standardised effect sizes.** Means and SDs are given; Z and F
  values are given; effect sizes are not.
- **A reporting slip.** The active congruent against active incongruent
  agency comparison is given as "Z = 2.631, p < 0.001" (p. 8). A
  two-tailed Z of 2.631 gives p ≈ 0.009, and Figure 5 marks the
  comparison "**", p < 0.01. It is still significant at the corrected
  threshold.
- **Agency controls in passive conditions.** The agency control scores
  rose in both passive conditions, and on the rendered figure exceed the
  agency scores there. Statements such as "the rubber hand was
  controlling my movements" may be partly true when an external force
  moves one's finger; that reading is mine.
- **Experiment 4's questionnaire is not reported.** The short
  questionnaire was given at the end of Experiments 2 and 4 (p. 5), but
  only Experiment 2's correlations appear.
- **Cross-experiment comparison.** The asynchrony against passive result
  (C8) compares different groups post hoc.
- **Small drift.** In Experiment 4 drift in the owned conditions was under
  1 cm, from one pointing before and one after each trial.
- **"Body agency" is preliminary.** The authors say themselves that it
  rests on "indirect evidence" and that "an important goal" is to test
  it objectively.

## Open questions

- Does the dissociation survive with expectations measured or matched,
  for example by asking participants beforehand what each condition will
  do to them?
- Is there an implicit agency measure, such as intentional binding, that
  shows the same pattern, so that the agency half does not rest on report?
- Is "body agency" a distinct process, or does ownership only raise
  ratings of agency, as it raised them in passive conditions where no
  agency was felt?
- Does the real hand lose ownership as the model gains it? The
  disappearing-hand item could be analysed alone.
- Does a moving rubber hand reach whole-body self-location or
  self-identification, which [THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) says the static one does not?
