# NOTE-1542: Lake, Salakhutdinov & Tenenbaum — Human-level concept learning through probabilistic program induction

## Contribution

People learn a new concept from one example and use it richly: to
classify, to generate new exemplars, to parse it into parts and to make
new related concepts. This paper gives one generative model that, from one
example of a handwritten character, matches people at one-shot
classification and produces exemplars and new characters that judges
mostly cannot tell from people's; lesions attribute the success to
compositionality and to priors learned from related concepts. It also
introduces the Omniglot data set.

## Key insight

"The model represents concepts as simple programs that best explain
observed examples under a Bayesian criterion." (p. 1332). A character is
the output of a drawing process; learning it is inferring the program,
and what transfers from earlier characters is the prior over parts and
their relations, the type/token structure "making BPL a generative model
for generative models" (p. 1333).

## Assumptions

- **The causal process is drawing**: strokes, sub-strokes and their
  relations. The structure is designed by the modellers: "Causal
  representations are prewired in our current BPL models" (p. 1337).
- **Background training on 30 alphabets**, images and stroke (motor) data
  both; the evaluation alphabets are disjoint: "Neither the production
  data nor any alphabets from this set are used in the subsequent
  evaluation tasks" (p. 1333).
- **Human-likeness as identification**: judges' accuracy at picking the
  machine in two-alternative choices, 50% being indistinguishable.
- **The deep baselines are of 2015**, pretrained on the same background
  images but not on the stroke data.

## Key results

- **Data (p. 1333).** Omniglot: "1623 handwritten characters from 50
  writing systems".
- **Task (p. 1335).** "One-shot classification was evaluated through a
  series of within-alphabet classification tasks for 10 different
  alphabets." Each is 20-way.
- **Classification (p. 1335).** "People were skilled one-shot learners,
  achieving an average error rate of 4.5% (N = 40). BPL showed a similar
  error rate of 3.3%"; "A deep Siamese convolutional network optimized for
  this one-shot learning task achieved 8.0% errors". A deep convnet erred
  13.5%, the hierarchical deep model 34.8%, modified Hausdorff distance
  38.8%.
- **Lesions (p. 1335).** "without learning to learn (token-level only) or
  compositionality (11.0% errors and 14.0%, respectively)".
- **Generating exemplars (p. 1335).** "Judges had only a 52% ID level on
  average for discriminating human versus BPL behavior." Shown as drawing
  movies, 59%.
- **Generating new concepts (p. 1336).** For a given alphabet, "The judges
  had only a 49% ID level on average"; unconstrained, 51% with a
  nonparametric prior reusing inferred parts. Lesioned models were easier
  to detect.
- **Less background (p. 1336).** With five background alphabets BPL erred
  "(4.3% and 4.0% errors, for the two sets, respectively)", while "the deep
  convolutional net performed notably worse than before (24.0% and 22.3%
  errors)".
- **Overall (p. 1336).** "For each visual Turing test, fewer than 25% of
  judges performed significantly better than chance."

## Concepts

- **Probabilistic program**: "probabilistic generative models expressed as
  structured procedures in an abstract description language" (p. 1333).
- **Type ψ**: "an abstract schema of parts, subparts, and relations";
  **token θ**: one execution of it, with motor noise, a start location and
  an affine warp; **image I**: its stochastic rendering. The joint is
  P(ψ)∏P(I|θ)P(θ|ψ).
- **Compositionality, causality, learning to learn**: the three named
  principles. Learning to learn is fitting the hierarchical priors to
  background characters; the lesions remove the learned hyperparameters at
  type or token level.
- **Visual Turing test and ID level**: judges' accuracy at picking the
  machine's production.

## Bearing on the record

- **A's characterization.** Right: characters as outputs of latent
  generative programs, stroke primitives composed into types, variation
  across drawings as tokens, human-level one-shot classification and
  human-like generation in several tests. Missing: that the hierarchy is
  type, token and image with priors learned from 30 background alphabets;
  the lesion evidence; the comparison with deep networks, including the
  five-alphabet test; that BPL learns from stroke data the baselines lack;
  that the generative structure is prewired.
  - **A's caveat** ("computational sufficiency and behavioral resemblance,
    not proof that people represent characters as the same programs") is a
    fair caution and A's, not the authors'. The authors go further: "our
    results suggest that people learn new handwritten characters in part by
    inferring abstract motor programs" (p. 1337), and they propose testing
    it in premotor cortex.
  - **The sentence A puts in quotation marks is A's paraphrase**, not the
    paper's.

  in-context learning in language models).** Unrelated in mechanism; a

## Limitations

- **The authors'.** "BPL still sees less structure in visual concepts than
  people do." (p. 1336): no parallel lines, symmetry, optional elements or
  stroke-end connections. Planning, explanation, communication and
  conceptual combination were not studied; causal structure is prewired;
  other domains (speech, gestures) are proposed, not shown.
- **One domain**, chosen because its causal process is known and recorded.
- **Not like for like**: BPL learns from stroke data the deep baselines do
  not see.
- **Judges.** Indistinguishability to naive judges depends on the judges;
  in the new-characters-for-an-alphabet test, 10 of 35 were significantly
  below chance, taking the machine for the human.
- **This reading** did not cover the Supplementary Materials, where the
  model's details and the controls are.
