# THEORY-327: Testimonial injustice is an ingenuous misjudgement of a speaker's credibility caused by prejudice, not a deliberate manipulation of it, and it can be structural and pre-emptive, when a speaker's word is never sought because of a structural prejudice that is no one's fault

## Source

- Fricker (2003), "Epistemic Justice and a Role for Virtue in the
  Politics of Knowing", *Metaphilosophy* 34, [LIT-1049](../literature.d/LIT-1049.md), read in full
  in [NOTE-860](../notes.d/NOTE-860.md), from the author-posted manuscript.
- Fricker (2017), "Evolving Concepts of Epistemic Injustice", Routledge
  Handbook, [LIT-1048](../literature.d/LIT-1048.md), read in full in [NOTE-853](../notes.d/NOTE-853.md), from the
  author-posted manuscript.

The book ([LIT-1004](../literature.d/LIT-1004.md)) was not read. Page references are to the
manuscripts.

## What was actually shown

**The core definition (2003).** Testimonial injustice "happens when a
speaker receives the wrong degree of credibility from his hearer owing to
a certain sort of unintended prejudice on the hearer's part"
(introduction). It arises because credibility is judged through a
socially trained "testimonial sensibility" that relies on stereotypes of
intellectual authority. These are a legitimate heuristic where founded,
and prejudice where not (pp. 9–12). Two cases illustrate it. Tom
Robinson's jury is culpable. Greenleaf, dismissing Marge as "female
intuition", is offered as plausibly non-culpable (pp. 14–18).

**Not deliberate (2017).** "In testimonial injustice the absence of
deliberate, conscious manipulation is definitive, at least in my
conception" (p. 1). The argument is from the concept's point: "the point
of the concept was to bring into focus a certain class of epistemic
misjudgements, and Ripley does not epistemically misjudge Marge at all"
(p. 3). Ripley, who deliberately works on Greenleaf's prejudice, does a
different wrong ("intentional gaslighting"). Greenleaf, whose judgement
Ripley's manipulation clouds, does the testimonial injustice. "Non-
deliberateness does not entail non-culpability" (p. 4).

**Structural and pre-emptive (2017).** Following Anderson: "a list of
expert witnesses that no one has refreshed for a good while, so that
(through no one's fault, let us add) the same old white male witnesses
tend to be drawn on". The others "are effectively silenced". "Their views
are not sought, and this is owing to a structural prejudice (the
un-refreshed list)." Any structural testimonial injustice "would have to
be pre-emptive". Once anyone speaks, "it would become transactional as
well as structural". Fricker calls the extension "very helpful" (pp.
8–9).

What could have come out otherwise: Fricker could have counted
deliberate discrediting as testimonial injustice, which is the wider use
she says the concept has partly drifted towards. She argues against it
and keeps that use for a "related but distinct" wrong.

## What this does not say

- **Not that every credibility deficit is an injustice.** An honest
  mistake, or a stereotype that is "empirically founded", does not
  qualify (2003, pp. 10–12).
- **Not that every unsought speaker is wronged.** The structural case
  needs a "structural prejudice". An omission from a list for reasons that
  track no misjudgement of the omitted is not covered. Whether an
  arrangement can be a prejudice without anyone's misjudgement is left
  open: the 2017 text calls the list itself the prejudice.
- **Not "identity prejudice".** Both texts say "prejudice". The 2003
  paper speaks of stereotypes of social types and of "social identity",
  but never uses the book's term, if the book uses it.
- **Not a claim about the book.** It is held at the strength of two of
  her papers, one of which is a short retrospective.
- **The virtue is not filed.** Reflexive critical openness and its
  historically contingent availability are in [NOTE-860](../notes.d/NOTE-860.md). No question
  in the record asks for them.
