# NOTE-049: Feinman et al. 2025, grand narratives of inequality

## Contribution

Established "grand narratives" tie inequality in large societies to roughly linear trends: domestication, then population growth or step-ups in hierarchical scale, with reversals only after catastrophes or collapse. These narratives were built mostly from historical or ethnographic snapshots and pay little attention to institutions or agency. The authors use quantitative, time-anchored archaeological and ethnographic house-size data from several world regions, most of which went through urbanization and hierarchy formation. Broad temporal patterns exist, but regional inequality trends are not linear or uniform, and they are not triggered immediately or mechanically by Malthusian dynamics or growth in scale.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- Methods: residences are the unit of analysis and house size is the wealth proxy. Site-level Gini coefficients come from the "SiteGiniLevel" compilation (1,176 sites, 47,019 houses). Two subsets are also used: apex sites (717 sites) and H50 (176 sites with at least 50 houses).
- Hierarchy (Fig. 1): number of political levels (1 to 6, with 6 for Roman England) against Gini gives r² = 0.22 for apex sites and 0.27 for H50 (P < 0.01). The authors read this as greater potential for inequality, not determination.
- Population (Fig. 2) and collapse: the log of polity population is a weak predictor. Inequality fell in England after Rome but rose in Central Mexico after Teotihuacan, contrary to the Malthusian "only catastrophe levels" expectation.
- Time (SI Fig. S2): in five macroregions there is no consistent trend. The largest upticks come centuries after the earliest cities (Southwest Asia after about 3500 BCE, Mesoamerica after about 500 BCE).
- Governance (Figs. 3–5): collective and autocratic apex sites are compared, and a composite "Hierarchical Clout" variable is built. Only 2 of 29 collectively governed apex sites in polities with three or more levels (Xochicalco, Tenochtitlan) show high inequality. The highest Ginis are in Southwest Asia, Europe and South America, which the authors associate with herd animals and metals while explicitly not claiming these caused it.
- Conceptual implications: stated limitations are that house size is one wealth metric, and the authors call for theory that drops assumptions of universality, linearity and progress.

## Open questions

- A large-N test of an influential narrative (inequality as the inevitable result of scale), with the authors arguing that institutions and governance mediate the outcome.
- Check the governance coding: it was done by regional experts, apparently at the same time as the other variables. Check also the "Polity-Scale Effect" correction and how robust the r² values are to sample choice.
- The paper is part of a PNAS series. The companion papers, for example on land-limited versus labor-limited production, may be the better entry point.
