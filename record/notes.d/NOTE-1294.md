# NOTE-1294: Death

## Contribution

A survey of the metaphysics of death and of whether, and when, death and
what follows it can harm the one who dies. Luper sets out each position's
premises and takes sides: for a compositional account of life, for the
loss-of-life account of death, against subsequentism and against
Williams on immortality.

## Key insight

Whether death and posthumous events can harm us turns on what welfare
is. If only experiences are good or bad for us, Epicurus wins. If the
fulfilment of desires counts, a desire I have now can be thwarted by
what happens after I die, and I am worse off now. "desire fulfillment
need have not experiential upshot" (§3.1).

## Assumptions

- **Comparativism** as the frame for harm: an event is overall bad for us
  if it makes our lifetime welfare lower than it otherwise would be
  (§3.2). The entry is neutral on what the intrinsic goods are.
- **The Epicurean presumption** (§4): what never makes us worse off at
  some time is not bad for us. Much of the timing debate depends on it;
  atemporalism rejects it.
- **What we are** is left open between animalism, personism and mindism
  (§2.5); several results depend on it.

## Key results

- **Life and death** (§§1–2).
  - The compositional account: an object is alive if and only if it is
    composed of things that can engage in self-modifying activities
    integrated under information some of them carry (§1.3).
  - The loss of life account: to die is to lose the capacity for those
    activities. Frozen embryos are viable though not vital, so alive
    (§2.2). Life can in principle be restored, not just revived (§2.4).
  - Terminators hold that a person ceases to exist at death;
    anti-terminators that one can persist as a corpse (§2.6).
  - "Mortal" should mean that one's life will end (§2.8).
- **Epicurus reconstructed** (§3.1) in nine steps. The key premises are
  intrinsic hedonism, that death is not an experience and causes none,
  and extrinsic instrumentalism (something is extrinsically bad only by
  making us have intrinsic evils). Two repairs defeat it:
  preferentialism, on which a thwarted desire is bad for us whether or
  not we notice; and Nagel's point that things can be bad by *precluding*
  goods.
- **Deprivationism** (§3.2). Dying at a time is bad for us when our
  lifetime welfare would have been higher had we not died then, and good
  when it would have been lower (the two Hilda cases).
- **The timing puzzle** (§4).
  - *Concurrentism*: a bad death harms while one dies. Lamont: deprivation
    harm occurs at the "ensuring event".
  - *Priorism* (§4.2): "Suppose that, as George Pitcher (1984) suggested,
    a desire that you have now may be thwarted by your death, even though
    you will die several months from now." Then it may be now that your
    death makes you worse off. "It may harm you retroactively."
  - *Subsequentism* (Feit, Bradley): harm while dead. Luper objects that
    a corpse, or a person who no longer exists, has no welfare level, not
    even zero.
  - *Indefinitism* (Nagel; Grey's blurry stretch) and *atemporalism*: that
    death is overall bad for Sam is a timeless truth (Feldman; Johansson's
    term).
- **Harmless preclusion and symmetry** (§5). Lacking genius makes life
  worse without harming; why is death different? Lucretius: we do not
  mind our prenatal nonexistence. Replies: Parfit's bias toward the
  future; our forward-looking pursuits (Kamm: we do not want our lives to
  be all over with); Nagel's essential-origins reply and Kaufman's
  defence of it for persons.
- **Posthumous harm** (§6).
  - Aristotle reports the popular belief; Nagel calls breaking a deathbed
    promise "an injury to the dead man" (§6).
  - Doubts: it seems to need backward causation (Partridge: the dead can
    be wronged, not harmed); and, if welfare is intrinsic and the person
    no longer exists, nothing after death can change it (§6.1). Taylor
    denies both wrong and harm.
  - Grover and Velleman: later events change the value or meaning of a
    life (§6.2).
  - **Pitcher** (§6.2), with Feinberg, Luper and Scarre, takes the
    preferentialist route. Posthumous events "harm us by being
    responsible for truths that thwart our desires". Slander after my
    death makes it true that my reputation is damaged. That harms me at
    the times I desired it untarnished, while I was alive. Likewise my
    desire that my child have a happy upbringing is thwarted, and I am
    worse off now, if she falls ill after I die. The posthumous event
    harms only indirectly.
  - Against it: Velleman holds well-being is "a fact intrinsic to the
    present"; Partridge, Vorobej and Suits that a desire one no longer has
    cannot be thwarted, or need not be cared about. Parfit replies that
    some desires are not conditional on their own persistence.
- **Immortality** (§7). Nagel: death is always bad, since experience is
  itself a great good. Luper: implausible, since sedation through surgery
  is good for us. Williams: immortality would be tedious; his categorical
  desires are not conditional on staying alive, like a father's desire
  that his daughter have a good start. Luper sides with Williams's
  critics: a gradually changing but continuous life could stay desirable.
- **Thanatizing** (§8). Conditionalizing every desire on one's surviving
  ("should I live on, let my project succeed") would not protect us:
  death also precludes having desires, so the strategy costs as much as
  it saves (Luper 2013). It is prudent only to avoid goals no lifetime can
  reach.

## Concepts

- **harm thesis**: death is at least sometimes bad for the one who dies.
- **deprivationism / comparativism**: death harms by precluding goods;
  something harms when it makes one's lifetime welfare lower than it
  would otherwise be.
- **priorism**: death, or a posthumous event, can make one worse off
  before it occurs, by thwarting desires one has then.
- **posthumous harm**: harm from events after one's death; on Pitcher's
  account, harm to the living person.
- **preferentialism**: desire fulfilment is intrinsically good for us,
  and thwarted desire bad, with or without experience of it.
- **categorical desire** (Williams): a desire not conditional on one's
  remaining alive.
- **thanatizing**: replacing desires death could thwart with versions
  conditional on one's survival.

## Bearing on the record

- **The legacy question ([QUESTION-301](../questions.d/QUESTION-301.md)).** The question's second
  reading is Pitcher's: acting for one's legacy can be self-interested in
  the ordinary sense, with no extended self. The entry confirms the
  mechanism. A desire about one's reputation or one's child after one's
  death is thwarted, on preferentialism, by what happens then, and the
  harm falls on the living person. These are inferences from the entry.
  - *It is self-interest only on a desire-based theory of welfare.* On
    hedonism there is no posthumous harm (§6.1), and acting for one's
    legacy cannot serve oneself. So Pitcher's reading of legacy is as
    strong as preferentialism.
  - *It meets the triviality objection at the level of welfare.* If the
    fulfilment of any suitably conditioned desire is part of my good,
    then whatever I act for is my good, and legacy is self-interested by
    definition. The entry says the conditions on such desires are
    "controversial" and sets them aside (§3.1). That is [CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md)'s
    worry moved from the self's extent to the theory of welfare.
  - *It does not reach Scheffler's case.* Pitcher's examples are desires
    about oneself (reputation) or one's own (one's child). Scheffler's
    infertility scenario concerns strangers who will never exist. A
    desire that humanity go on is thwarted by extinction on the same
    logic, but calling its frustration a harm to me is the triviality
    worry again.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).** Pitcher offers the thesis an
  alternative it does not need: legacy as self-interest of an ordinary,
  unextended person whose desires reach past death. The thesis and
  Pitcher can both be true, but either makes the other unnecessary for
  legacy.

## Limitations

- **An encyclopedia entry**, and an argumentative one: Luper takes sides
  (§§1.3, 4.3, 7.1, 7.2, 8) and is himself among the preferentialists
  he reports (§6.2).
- **Pitcher at second hand, briefly.** No ante-mortem/post-mortem
  distinction, no quotation from Pitcher, and no page reference.
- **Supplements not read** ("On Mortality"; the senescence supplement).
- **A wrong citation.** The bibliography gives Pitcher 1984 twice, as APQ
  21(2): 217–225, reprinted in Fischer 1993, 119–134. Those are the pages
  given for Rosenbaum 1986 in the same list. The LIT, from PhilPapers and
  Crossref, has APQ 21(2): 183–188 and Fischer pp. 157–168.
- **A dangling reference.** §4.2 asks the reader to "Recall the earlier
  case" of an infection whose symptoms come later; no such case appears
  earlier in the entry.
- **Typos**:
  - "desire fulfillment need have not experiential upshot" (§3.1);
  - "it brings us have pain" (§4.2); "Does a person's death makes her"

  - "Christopher Belsaw" for Belshaw (§2.2); "life-functionists" (§1.3);
    "mindist typically suggest" (§2.5); "according which" (§1.3);
