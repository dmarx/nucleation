# NOTE-266: Autonomy

## Contribution

A textbook overview of autonomy in four registers: history (§1), personal autonomy (§2), feminist philosophy (§3), and political theory and bioethics (§4). For this batch its value is §2.a, which presents the hierarchical model as "the basis upon which the mainstream discussion builds and reacts against". It sets out its standard objections under memorable names (Manipulation, Regress or Incompleteness, Ab Initio) and follows the line of critique into relational autonomy.

## Key insight

The hierarchical model is *procedural and content-neutral*. It asks only whether a first-order desire is endorsed by a higher-order volition, not where that volition came from or what it says. Every standard objection targets one of those two neutralities:

- *History* is ignored, so it cannot tell a hypnotised agent from an autonomous one (Manipulation).
- The *source of the endorser's authority* is left open (Regress, Ab Initio).
- A unified, wholehearted *self* is presupposed, which feminist and mestiza critics deny is the only form of autonomous agency.

## Assumptions

The premises and authorities the entry works from:

- **Personal autonomy is distinct from moral autonomy** (Kant) and from political autonomy. Contemporary accounts are "metaphysically neutral" and "procedurally individualist" (§2).
- **Content-neutral vs. substantive** is the main division (§2). Hierarchical, historical and coherentist accounts are content-neutral. Wolf, Benson, Korsgaard and Hill are substantive.
- **Autonomy can be a bare capacity or an ideal** (preamble), and it can be assessed locally (for actions) or globally (for agents).
- Authorities: Frankfurt 1988 (collection) and Dworkin 1988/1989 for the hierarchy; Watson 1975, Mele 2001, Christman 1991, Thalberg 1978 and Friedman 1986 for the objections; Meyers 2005, Barvosa-Carter 2007 and Oshana 2005 against wholeheartedness; Mackenzie & Stoljar 2000 and Nedelsky 1989 for relational autonomy.

## Key results

What the article argues or reports.

- **§1 History.**
  - Self-mastery in Plato and Aristotle.
  - Kant's moral autonomy versus heteronomy.
  - Romantic, Millian and existentialist individualisation.
  - Lévinas's preference for heteronomy.
  - Maslow, Rogers, Kohlberg, and Gilligan's critique.
  - Contemporary work is content-neutral and avoids developmental ideals.
- **§2.a.i The hierarchical model.**
  - It begins with Frankfurt and G. Dworkin in the 1970s. Their "insight" is that decisions are worth protecting if rooted in the agent's values and commitments, which contrasts the weak-willed person with the self-consciously valuing person.
  - Statement: "an agent is autonomous with respect to an action on the condition that his or her first-order desire to commit the act is sanctioned by a second-order volition endorsing the first-order desire (see Frankfurt 1988, 12-25)".
  - The model is neutral about the origins of higher-order desires, and so admits socially and relationally constituted ones. "The cause of such desires does not matter, solely the agent's identification with them (Frankfurt 1988, 53-54)."
  - Dworkin calls the higher-order preferences the "true self" (Dworkin 1989, 59).
- **§2.a.ii Criticisms.**
  1. **Manipulation.** Frankfurt's account is "ahistorical", so a hypnotist who installs second-order desires is not excluded (Mele 2001, 144–173). **Christman 1991** makes autonomy depend on "the means and historical process by which an agent reaches certain decisions", so that a brainwashed desire is nonautonomous. Its difficulties are an agent who freely gives up her autonomy, and an agent who endorses a desire but not the means by which it was forced on her (Taylor 2005, 10–12).
  2. **Regress or Incompleteness.** If second-order volitions need third-order ones, and so on, there is an infinite regress. If they are autonomous for some other reason, the model is incomplete (Watson 1975). Frankfurt acknowledges "no theoretical limit" to the series but ends it with a "decisive commitment" (Frankfurt 1988, 21). The entry objects that terminating the series is "arbitrary if there no reason behind it (Watson 1975)".
     - *Frankfurt's reply*, in "Identification and Wholeheartedness": a decisive commitment is one made "without reservation", where the agent feels no reason to continue deliberating (1988, 168–9). Stopping there is "hardly arbitrary". Possible error is a feature of all deliberation. Deciding seeks "to overcome or to supersede a condition of inner division and to make himself into an integrated whole" (174). It establishes "a constraint by which other preferences and decisions are to be guided" (175).
     - *Against wholeheartedness*: Meyers 2005 argues for a decentred, fivefold self. Barvosa-Carter 2007 argues that ambivalence is inescapable for mixed-race agents with conflicting inheritances. Oshana 2005 makes the point for life in a racist society.
  3. **Ab Initio.** It is "a puzzle how decisive commitments or higher-order desires acquire their authority without themselves being endorsed". If the source of autonomy cannot itself be reflectively endorsed, autonomy originates in something non-autonomous, outside the model.
  4. **Priority of higher orders.** The model gives "unjustified ontological priority" to higher levels (Thalberg 1978). It begs the question by positing an uncaused "true self" at the top (Friedman). Friedman 1986 proposes integrating desires of all orders instead.
- **§2.a.iii Coherentist accounts.** "For Frankfurt, we identify with a lower level desire if we have a second order volition endorsing it. However, our second order volitions don't necessarily represent us — we may have no reason for them, which Frankfurt acknowledges."
  - Ekstrom 1993: a self is a character of beliefs and preferences endorsed through reflection, where preferences are first-level desires "to be good", "similar to a second order volition for Frankfurt". A preference is authorised by coherence with the rest. This is meant to avoid the Regress and Ab Initio problems.
  - Bratman 2007, 5: plans and intentions partly constitute personal identity, and decisions are self-governing relative to them.
- **§2.b Substantive accounts.** Wolf's normative competence (strong). Benson's normative competence (strong, 1991), and his weak substantive condition of self-worth or self-trust. Korsgaard's practical identities, and Hill's version.
- **§3 Feminist.**
  - Critics: Gilligan, Benjamin, and Hoagland ("a thoroughly noxious concept"). Friedman and Christman reply that procedural autonomy is not substantive independence.
  - **Relational autonomy** distinguishes two levels of relationality: social sources of values, and social commitments themselves. On Friedman's view one can autonomously be a hermit. Donchin denies that one can opt out.
- **§4 Political and bioethics.**
  - Kant's principle of right. Mill's anti-paternalism. Gaus's liberal principle. Christman & Anderson on liberal neutrality. Sandel's communitarian critique. Okin and Weiss. Fineman on dependency. Forst's five conceptions of autonomy. Sen and Nussbaum's capabilities.
  - Belmont Report. Beauchamp & Childress's respect for autonomy. Disability rights ("Nothing about us without us"). Donchin's weak vs. strong relational autonomy in health care.

- **Frankfurt 1976** ("Identification and Externality"): nothing.
- **Frankfurt 1987** ("Identification and Wholeheartedness", named in §2.a.ii):
  - decisive commitment = made without reservation, with no felt reason to go on deliberating (pp. 168–9);
  - stopping there is not arbitrary, and fallibility is common to all deliberation;
  - decision aims to overcome inner division and make oneself "an integrated whole" (p. 174);
  - it sets "a constraint by which other preferences and decisions are to be guided" (p. 175).
- **Frankfurt 1992 and 1994** ("The Faintest Passion"; "Autonomy, Necessity, and Love"): nothing. No satisfaction, volitional necessity, caring or love.
- **Watson 1975** ("Free Agency"):
  - the regress: second-order volitions would need third-order ones, or else the model is incomplete (§2.a.ii);
  - ending the series by decisive commitment is arbitrary without a reason (§2.a.ii).

  **Not reported**: valuing vs. desiring, Watson's evaluational system, and any positive view of Watson's.

## Concepts

- **Content-neutral (procedural) vs. substantive autonomy.** Whether the account fixes any value the autonomous agent must hold (§2).
- **Hierarchical procedural account.** Autonomy is endorsement of a first-order desire by a second-order volition (§2.a.i).
- **Problem of Manipulation.** The ahistorical endorsement condition cannot exclude installed endorsements (§2.a.ii).
- **Regress or Incompleteness Problem.** Either an infinite regress of higher-order volitions, or authority from some non-hierarchical source (§2.a.ii).
- **Ab Initio Problem.** The un-endorsed source of autonomy is itself non-autonomous (§2.a.ii).
- **Decisive commitment / wholeheartedness.** A commitment made without reservation that ends deliberation and integrates the self (per "Identification and Wholeheartedness").
- **Coherentist account.** Ekstrom's and Bratman's accounts, *as opposed to* the hierarchical model (§2.a.iii). **This is narrower than the SEP "Personal Autonomy" entry's "coherentist", which includes the hierarchical model.**
- **Relational autonomy.** Autonomy compatible with, or partly constituted by, social relations. It works at two levels: social sources of values, and social commitments themselves (§3.b).

## Limitations

- It is short and introductory. §2.a, the part that matters here, is about 1.4k words.
- Frankfurt is cited by pages of a collection, with no essay titles except the 1987 one, so claims cannot be traced to specific papers without another bibliography.
- It stops at 1987 on Frankfurt and at 2007 on the literature overall. Frankfurt's satisfaction (1992), volitional necessity and love (1994, 1999) are absent, and so is Watson's valuing/desiring distinction.
- The desire/volition slippage in the regress paragraph obscures what, exactly, regresses.
- The attribution of an anti-paternalist motive to Frankfurt (C8) is unsupported.
- The page is undated; the dates here come from the site's CMS and the Wayback Machine.

## Open questions

- Does the regress objection belong to Watson 1975, as this article says? The SEP entry presents the regress anonymously and reads Watson as offering an alternative locus of authority. Only the original, or a reliable reconstruction such as Velleman 2002, can settle what Watson argued.
- Does decisive commitment stop the regress non-arbitrarily, or does it relocate the ab initio problem to the commitment? The article states both the reply and the ab initio puzzle but does not adjudicate.
- Can wholeheartedness be a condition of autonomy without excluding ambivalent agents (Barvosa-Carter, Oshana)?
