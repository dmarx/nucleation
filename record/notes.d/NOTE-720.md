# NOTE-720: Weber & Varela — Life after Kant

## Contribution

A philosophical essay, published after Varela's death, that reads three
lines as one. Kant's account of organisms as natural purposes in the
*Critique of Judgement*, read as non-reductive. Hans Jonas's philosophy of
the organism, built on metabolism. The autopoiesis theory of Maturana and
Varela. The authors argue that the last two, developed independently,
resolve the "unstable middle position" Kant left. Teleology in organisms
is intrinsic and real, not an observer's "as-if", and it is "inevitably
intertwined with the self-establishment of an identity which is the
living process". Nothing new is defined or measured. The autopoiesis
definition is quoted from Varela (1997).

## Key insight

A thing that makes itself has, by that fact, a purpose: to go on making
itself. Self-production "is already and inevitably a self-affirmation that
shows the organism as involved in the fundamental purpose of maintaining
its identity". The rest follows from that purpose. The organism's own
point of view, valence, meaning and the difference between environment and
world are all relative to it.

## Assumptions

- **Kant read against the reductionist reading.** The argument needs the
  KdU to hold "the possibility of a third way between a strong teleology
  and a brute materialism", and Kant's "subordination of the principle of
  mechanism under the principle of teleology" (KdU § 80) to be more than a
  maxim of inquiry. The authors admit that Kant's stance "may depend on the
  chosen quotations".
- **Phenomenological evidence counts.** Jonas's inversion is accepted: "we
  have the evidence of our intrinsic teleology in us", and "life can only
  be known by life". The argument from our own case to the bacterium is an
  argument by analogy and is not defended as one.
- **Metabolism is the defining quality of life** (Jonas): "every living
  being has it, no nonliving being has it".
- **The scope is the cell.** Autopoiesis is "explicitly formulated for the
  minimal living system, the cell". Its extension to multicellular
  organisms goes through a "neural identity", deferred to other work.

## Key results

The paper has no theorems. These are its theses, in its order.

- **Kant (§ 2).**
  - Organisms are natural purposes: "A thing exists as a natural purpose if
    it is (though in a double sense) both cause and effect of itself" (KdU
    § 64). Every part is "an organ bringing forth the other parts", and "an
    organized and self-organizing being can be called a natural purpose"
    (KdU § 65). The authors credit Kant with the first modern use of
    "self-organization".
  - Kant's interest is "intrinsic, and not relative purposiveness", which
    is the authors' distinction between internal purposes and Darwin's
    "external seemingly purposeful design".
  - Organism against artifact: artifacts "always point to an external
    purpose they are made or used for", while organisms "are purposes with
    the goal of keeping existent by organizing themselves".
  - Kant's position is unstable: an "ontological reductionist, but an
    epistemological anti-reductionist", a "transcendental agnosticist".
- **Jonas (§§ 3.1–3.2).**
  - Teleology becomes real through "our wish to exist further on as a
    subject, not our imputation of purposes on objects".
  - Metabolic identity: the organism "keeps its identity exactly by not
    keeping the same matter" (Jonas 1973). "Organisms are things whose
    existence is their own achievement" (Jonas 1992).
  - Four features of the living: exchange of matter from "a subject pole
    partially independent of the underlying matter"; precariousness and
    concern; a subjective perspective even in "the simplest forms of
    life"; the antinomies of freedom and necessity.
- **Autopoiesis (§ 3.3).**
  - Minimal autonomy is "a circular process of self-production where the
    cellular metabolism and the surface membrane it produces are the key
    terms".
  - The definition (Varela 1997, p. 75): "a network of processes of
    production (synthesis and destruction) of components such that these
    components: 1. continuously regenerate the network that is producing
    them, and 2. constitute the system as a distinguishable unity in the
    domain in which they exist".
  - "A multicellular organism ... is not in itself an autopoietic unit of
    second order, since its organization does not follow the same
    self-contructing principles." It "inherits its autonomous nature and
    sense-making qualities through the configuration of its neural
    identity", which "cannot detain us here".
  - What sets autopoiesis apart from dissipative structures and
    autocatalytic cycles is not a mechanism. It is that it gives "the
    origin of 'concern' based on its ongoing self-produced identity". The
    authors grant that "One could envisage the circularity
    metabolism-membrane entirely from the outside".
- **Intrinsic teleology (§ 3.4).**
  - Two modes: "a basic purpose in the maintenance of its own identity",
    and "a sense-creation purpose" that introduces a difference between
    environment ("the physical impacts it receives") and world ("how that
    environment is evaluated from the point of view established by
    maintaining an identity").
  - Valence is "dual at its basis: attraction or rejection, approach or
    escape". The sucrose example is quoted from Varela (1991).
  - Persistence is not the standard: Jonas, "If mere assurance of
    permanence were the point that mattered, life should not have started
    out in the first place. ... Not duration as such, but 'duration of
    what?' is the question."
  - "Subjectivity is the absolute interest the organism takes in his
    continued existence."
- **Conclusion (§ 4).** The thesis is double. Only an organism taken as
  "self-producing individuality" yields a strong intrinsic teleology, and
  admitting biological individuality, "and hence the precariousness of
  the living", commits one to it. Intrinsic teleology is "an empirical
  feature of an organism", objective "not in an absolute sense, only
  insofar as an organism is a center that organizes matter into a living
  being and its Umwelt". The "Newton of the grassblade" was not Darwin but
  "a historical convergence of philosophical and biological thinking".

## Concepts

- **Natural purpose (Naturzweck).** A thing that is "both cause and effect
  of itself", whose parts produce one another and the whole (KdU §§ 64–65).
- **Intrinsic against relative purposiveness.** Purposes immanent to the
  living thing, against the usefulness of things to one another (Kant's
  "relative finality", and Darwin's apparent design).
- **Teleonomy.** The "as-if" teleology of modern biology (Pittendrigh,
  Nagel), which the paper sets out to replace.
- **Autopoietic system.** A network of production of components that
  regenerates itself and constitutes itself "as a distinguishable unity in
  the domain in which they exist" (Varela 1997). Stated for the cell.
- **Concern (Sorge).** The need of a precarious system "to avoid
  perishing".
- **Environment and world (Umwelt).** The physical impacts on a system, and
  those impacts as evaluated from the point of view its self-maintenance
  sets up.
- **Leibapriori.** Apel's "apriori of the lived body", which the authors
  find in the late Kant.

## Bearing on the record

**For [CLAIM-194](../claims.d/CLAIM-194.md): the paper gives "produced" a definition and no test.**
The LIT expected this to be "the account in which 'produced' is defined".
In a weak sense it is. The definition's second clause says the components
"constitute the system as a distinguishable unity", and the cell's
"surface membrane" is one of the system's products. That is the
distinction [CLAIM-194](../claims.d/CLAIM-194.md) draws between a membrane the cell makes and a beam
joining two pendulums. The pendulums do not produce the beam, so they fail
the clause. But four things limit what the counter can take from it.

- **No operational criterion.** The paper gives no way to find out whether
  a given boundary was produced. It applies the definition only to the
  cell, where production is known from biochemistry. It has nothing to set
  beside the gap test as a measurement, so it does not answer the
  counter's own "What would answer it" and does not reduce [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md)'s
  unmet promote_when.
- **The scope is narrower than the counter needs.** "A multicellular
  organism ... is not in itself an autopoietic unit of second order".
  [CLAIM-194](../claims.d/CLAIM-194.md) is about combined forms: pairs, groups, firms ([CLAIM-157](../claims.d/CLAIM-157.md),
  [CLAIM-174](../claims.d/CLAIM-174.md)). If even an animal does not produce its own boundary in the
  autopoietic sense, a dancing couple does not. Strictly read, the
  produced/imposed distinction from this source would rule out every
  combined form the record cares about. Their individuality would have to
  come from the "neural identity" the paper defers. So the counter is
  stronger with this source than [CLAIM-194](../claims.d/CLAIM-194.md) states: on autopoiesis, no
  combined form has a produced boundary. But the source does not help
  anyone say which combined forms come close.
- **The difference is placed in the point of view, not in the dynamics.**
  The authors grant that the metabolism–membrane circle can be described
  "entirely from the outside". What they say autopoiesis adds over
  dissipative structures and autocatalytic cycles is "the instauration of a
  point of view" and "concern". That is a phenomenological claim, and the
  paper offers no observable that would carry it. [THEORY-071](../theory.d/THEORY-071.md) asks for the
  system to distinguish itself "and an observer does not do it for the
  system". This paper supports the idea and offers no measure of it.
- **Not the fiat/bona fide distinction.** Smith and Varzi ([LIT-1024](../literature.d/LIT-1024.md))
  sort boundaries by whether they track a physical discontinuity. Here the
  test is who made the boundary. A beam's surface would, on the LIT's description, be bona fide in the
  first sense, and it is imposed in the second. The LIT's caution that the two differ is right.

What the counter can cite from this paper is the definition and the
membrane example. It should not cite it for a criterion that would decide
a case.

**For [CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md): value for the organism, and one step past it.** Most of
the paper stays on the side [CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md) permits. Value is value *for* the
organism: "Only that which influences the steady state of the organisms is
real" for it, and teleology is objective "only insofar as an organism is a
center that organizes matter into a living being and its Umwelt". The
Jonas passage the paper quotes on survival also refuses to make
persistence the standard: "Not duration as such, but 'duration of what?' is
the question". That is close to [CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md)'s own point, that viability does
not settle what ought to persist.

But in § 3.1 the paper goes further, and in the direction [CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md)
disclaims. The "Yes!" to continued existence is "the mother-value of all
values". The argument "parallels the central figure of Apel's
'transcendental pragmatics' as well as the ethic of Habermas". And "Our
teleological reality cannot be denied without equally denying our own
status as sentient beings who have a right to the pursuit of an
undisturbed life." This is the inference from self-maintenance to a moral
claim. It is made in one paragraph, by a transcendental argument that
runs from our own case, and its development is left to Hösle (note 14). It
is not argued, so it does not threaten [CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md). But it shows that this
tradition does make the step [CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md) refuses, and a citation of the
paper as a source only of norms for the system would be incomplete.

The paper's teleology is also limited to living things. It has nothing to
say about an institution's viability, which is [CLAIM-075](../claims.d/CLAIM-075.md)'s example.

**Nothing here is a machine-learning instruction.** No anthology topic
holds it.

## Limitations

- An essay of exegesis and convergence. There is no model and no case,
  and the authors' own term for the conclusion is "immodest".
- The step from self-production to purpose is asserted ("Bluntly stated").
  The step from purpose to subjectivity is Jonas's phenomenological
  argument, accepted rather than examined.
- All Kant and Jonas quotations are the authors' translations. The authors
  say published translations "are not always in accordance with the
  author's views".
- The extension to multicellular organisms and to nervous systems is
  deferred to other works.
- The relation of autopoiesis to dissipative structures is settled by
  "concern", not by organisation. The paper does not address the candle
  flame, which [LIT-570](../literature.d/LIT-570.md) and [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md) make central.
- Varela died in 2001, before publication. The paper does not say how the
  writing was divided.

## Open questions

- What observable would tell a produced boundary from an imposed one in a
  system whose biochemistry is not known in advance? A protocell model in
  which the membrane can be made by the network or supplied from outside,
  analysed with and without the supply, would test it.
- Does any combined form of more than one organism meet the second clause
  of the definition? The paper's answer for the multicellular organism is
  no, and it gives no account of what replaces it.
- Can the "point of view" said to come with self-production be found in
  the dynamics, or is it only attributed? Without that, intrinsic
  teleology as "an empirical feature" has no evidence that could fail.
