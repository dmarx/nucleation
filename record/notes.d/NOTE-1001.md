# NOTE-1001: Roseboom & Lush — Serious problems with interpreting rubber hand illusion experiments

## Contribution

The paper turns a disputed correlation into a concrete outcome. The
correlation is between trait phenomenological control, measured by the
subjective scale of the SWASH hypnotisability test, and agreement with the
rubber hand statements (rs = 0.26 in Lush et al. 2020). The outcome is how
often a typical small study would conclude that its participants had the
illusion. The answer depends strongly on who is sampled, and most strongly
for the one statement about ownership. The paper also sets out the
inferential chain by which drift, imaging and animal studies borrow their
link to ownership from report, and says where that chain breaks if report
is confounded.

## Key insight

A correlation of 0.26 sounds weak. But what it does to the conclusions
of small studies depends on how much of the effect the low end of the
trait carries. For the ownership item, the low half of the trait carries
almost none of it. So whether a 20-person study "finds" ownership of a
rubber hand depends largely on how many highly suggestible people it
recruits. Anything later linked to ownership through those reports
inherits that dependence.

## Assumptions

- **The dataset is representative.** All simulations resample the 351
  participants of Lush et al. 2020 with complete data. Unselected samples
  of 20 found significant agreement 83% of the time (lines 249–261). The
  authors take this as showing the sample is "broadly consistent with
  participants from previous studies across the field".
- **Mean above zero is the criterion.** One-sample two-tailed t-tests of
  the mean against 0 (lines 194–216, 238–244). Some studies use cut-offs
  of 1 or 2 (Ehrsson et al. 2007; Lloyd 2007), which are not simulated.
- **Twenty per study.** The authors justify n = 20 as the mean sample
  size of the 30 most-cited RHI publications (median 15; lines 226–229).
  Ten and 50 are in the supplement.
- **A median split on the subjective SWASH.** The notebooks split the
  sample by quartile of subjective SWASH (quartiles 1–2 against 3–4).
  Each half had at least 175 participants (line 236).
- **Only the synchronous condition.** The synchronous–asynchronous
  difference is set aside as "confounded by demand characteristics and
  therefore uninterpretable (Lush, 2020)" (lines 194–196). The
  argument therefore inherits whatever Lush 2020 establishes.
- **Phenomenological control is domain-general.** SWASH covers amnesia,
  musical and visual hallucination and the like. The same trait predicts
  vicarious pain, mirror touch and the visually evoked auditory response
  (lines 375–391). This is what lets the authors read the RHI correlation
  as the RHI being an instance of the general ability.

## Key results

- **The relationship (Fig. 1, lines 149–178).** A scatter of 351 people's
  subjective SWASH score against their mean S1–S3 rating after
  synchronous stroking. The fitted line is y = 0.578x + 0.333, so each
  SWASH point adds 0.578 points of agreement. The rank correlation of
  rs = 0.26, 95% CI [0.16, 0.37] is quoted from Lush et al. 2020. On the
  rendered figure, ratings span the whole −3 to +3 range at every level
  of SWASH.
- **The whole sample (lines 205–216).** Mean S1–S3 agreement was 1.27
  (SEM 0.08), t(350) = 15.42, p < 0.001. By statement, the mean for S1 was
  1.94 and for S2 1.15 (lines 111–112). In the notebook, 12% of
  participants agreed with S1 while disagreeing with S2.
- **Simulated studies of 20, S1–S3 mean (Fig. 2, lines 249–294).**

  | sample | mean positive | significant agreement |
  |---|---|---|
  | unselected | 9996 / 10,000 | 83% |
  | below-median SWASH | 9932 / 10,000 | 51% |
  | above-median SWASH | 10,000 / 10,000 | 98.9% |

- **By statement, studies of 20 (Fig. 4, lines 301–348; notebook outputs).**
  - S1: significant in 95% of below-median and 99.9% of above-median
    studies.
  - S2: 24% against 95%.
  - S3: 4.4% against 74%, and 28% for unselected studies. Of the
    below-median studies, 2485 had a mean in disagreement with S3.
  - The main text gives the S3 figure for the lower half ("only ~4%") and
    not the 74% or 28%; those are from the notebook.
  - Fig. 5: for S3 in the lower half, "the probability of obtaining
    inferential support (p < 0.05) ... is roughly the same as the
    probability of obtaining any other extent of 0.05", that is, p is
    roughly uniform.
- **Sample size changes the picture (supplementary notebooks).**
  - At n = 50, the below-median S1–S3 mean is significant in 94.8% of
    studies and the above-median in 100%. S3 is significant in 6.3% of
    below-median, 99.8% of above-median and 64% of unselected studies.
  - At n = 10, the corresponding S1–S3 figures are 27% and 79%, and for
    S3 4.1% and 42%.
  - So the contrast for the averaged statements shrinks as studies grow,
    while the contrast for the ownership statement does not.
- **Figure 3 (rendered).** The distribution of simulated study means for
  the lower half sits around 0.9–1.0 on the S1–S3 mean and about 0.25 on
  S3. The upper half sits around 1.6 and 1.2.
- **Walsh et al. 2015 (lines 165–172).** It reported no relationship
  between hypnotisability and the RHI, with r = 0.20, p = 0.371, n = 23,
  95% CI [−0.23, 0.57]. The authors argue that this "do[es] not provide
  counterevidence", since the design cannot support a null.
- **Three interpretations (lines 393–457).**
  - (1) The same people control their experience in the RHI. This is the
    most parsimonious reading, and it "does not rule out a multisensory
    contribution".
  - (2) They "happen to be the same participants that can experience the
    RHI, but for distinct reasons (e.g. pure multisensory integration
    account)". This is "not explicitly ruled out by the results presented
    here or previously".
  - (3) They respond as though they do, for other reasons.
  - Faking is acknowledged as a possible hypothesis-awareness effect
    (lines 450–457).
- **Reverse inference (lines 458–501).** "All other findings, such as
  proprioceptive drift or changes in measured blood flow (fMRI) or scalp
  potential (EEG), are linked to changes in subjective ownership only
  through broad correspondence or co-occurrence with these subjective
  reports." For animal paradigms such as the rubber tail in mice, "there
  is, and can be, no direct evidence to support this claim". The authors
  add: "Our claim in no way invalidates behaviourist approaches that seek
  to map body perception to neural function through observed behaviour."
- **Top-down accounts (lines 502–512).** Top-down accounts of the RHI
  (Tsakiris 2010; Ehrsson et al. 2021) treat it as "resistant to
  suggestions". Given uncontrolled demand, "it is premature to consider
  theoretical proposals of top-down effects which are not related to
  demand characteristics".

## Concepts

- **Trait phenomenological control** — "the domain general ability to
  meet expectancies arising from direct or implicit imaginative suggestion
  (including demand characteristics)" (lines 352–355). It is measured here
  by the subjective scale of the Sussex-Waterloo Scale of Hypnotisability
  (SWASH).
- **Hypothesis awareness** — knowing or guessing what the experimenter
  expects. The paper names three ways it can show up: faking, intentional
  imagination, or phenomenological control (lines 53–55, 450–452).
- **Simulated experiment** — a random draw of n distinct participants'
  real reports from the pool, tested against zero, repeated 10,000 times.
  Different draws may overlap (lines 223–238).
- **Reverse inference** — inferring a subjective state, here ownership,
  from a measure that was validated against report of that state, when
  report itself is in doubt (lines 474–495).

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-765](../theory.d/THEORY-765.md) (Proposed), the synchrony effect.**
  - **Does it meet the test?** No. Its `promote_when` allows "a design ...
    in which trait suggestibility is measured and the synchrony effect
    survives it". This paper measures trait suggestibility but analyses
    only the synchronous condition, by design. So it says nothing about
    whether the synchrony effect survives, and it has no drift analysis.
  - **Does it trigger the refutation?** No.
  - **What it bears on.** It bears on the THEORY's middle clause, that the
    rubber hand is "reported as one's own". In the largest RHI dataset,
    agreement with "I felt as if the rubber hand were my hand" after
    synchronous stroking was significant in only 28% of unselected
    20-person studies, and 64% at n = 50. In samples from the lower half
    of hypnotisability it was significant in 4–6% at any size. Referred
    touch (S1) was robust across the trait. As evidence for the THEORY's
    title, then, the referred-touch clause stands better than the
    ownership clause. That is a reading of the authors' notebook outputs,
    not their stated conclusion.
  - **Proposed change (proposal only).** The status stays Proposed. The
    `promote_when` might ask that the suggestion-controlled replication
    report the ownership item separately from the referred-touch items,
    and give trait phenomenological control as a covariate.
- **[THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md) (Proposed), graded common-cause ownership.**
  - **Neither meets nor refutes.** The THEORY predicts graded ownership.
  - **Where the grading comes from.** This paper shows graded ownership
    ratings across people, but the grading follows a trait of
    suggestibility, not any manipulated reliability of input. A
    causal-inference model would have to explain that individual
    variation by the precision of the senses, and nothing here says it
    could.
  - **Proposed change (proposal only).** The held-out test in the
    `promote_when` could control for trait phenomenological control.
- **[THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md) (Proposed), interoceptive signals.**
  - **How it bears.** The paper does not discuss interoception. But the
    THEORY's second leg is an individual-differences correlation: lower
    heartbeat-detection sensitivity goes with greater RHI susceptibility
    (Tsakiris et al. 2011). This paper shows that RHI susceptibility, as
    measured by report, tracks trait phenomenological control.
  - **The third variable, mine.** If heartbeat detection also relates to
    suggestibility, the trait could account for both. Heartbeat-counting
    tasks are themselves open to expectation, which the THEORY's "What
    this does not say" already hints at. That is my inference; the paper
    makes no such claim.
  - **Proposed change (proposal only).** The `promote_when`'s second
    condition could require that the sensitivity–susceptibility relation
    survive trait phenomenological control as a covariate.
- **[THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) (Proposed), minimal selfhood.**
  - **No test.** The paper does not test the part–whole split or the
    agency thesis.
  - **The evidence base.** It names the whole-body illusion (Lenggenhager
    et al. 2007, [LIT-1279](../literature.d/LIT-1279.md)) and body swapping among the paradigms the
    RHI spawned. Its reverse-inference argument applies to them too:
    full-body drift is linked to self-identification only through the
    same kind of questionnaire.
  - **Neither meets nor refutes.**
- **[THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md) (Proposed).** The record has used the RHI as passive
  ownership without agency. This paper finds the ownership statement the
  one most dependent on suggestibility. So the RHI is weaker support for
  the ownership half of that dissociation than the record assumed.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** Lines 458–501 are a compact statement of the
  question's problem for one domain.
  - **The argument.** Every non-report measure of an experience gets its
    meaning from a correlation with report. If report is confounded,
    the non-report measures inherit the confound. Animal versions add a
    further step, assuming that a behaviour in rodents is equivalent to
    one in humans.
  - **The limit.** The paper grants that the reports may reflect genuine
    experience, produced by a different mechanism. So it is a problem
    about which cause an experience has, not about access against
    experience.
- **[NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md), C6, and [THEORY-064](../theory.d/THEORY-064.md).** C6 says ownership is manipulated "by
  multisensory conflict". For the ownership item specifically, this
  reading shows the manipulation working mainly in people high in a trait
  that predicts response to suggestion in general.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **No new data, one dataset.** Every number is a resample of Lush et al.
  2020, from one laboratory, with its own procedure and population. The
  simulations add no information beyond the joint distribution of SWASH
  and the ratings. They are a way of displaying a correlation, and they
  cannot show that suggestion causes the reports.
- **Median split and n = 20 do much of the work.** Dichotomising a
  continuous trait and testing small samples both lower power. The
  "around half the time" figure for the averaged statements is largely a
  matter of power. At n = 50 the lower half finds the averaged
  statements 95% of the time (supplement), and the main text does not say
  so. C4 as worded ("only experiments run with a large proportion of
  participants higher in phenomenological control will provide evidence
  for the RHI") overstates the case for the averaged statements. It holds
  for S3.
- **The low half still agrees on average.** The lower half's simulated
  means for S1–S3 sit near 1 (Fig. 3). Low hypnotisability does not mean
  no reported illusion, and a positive mean in the least suggestible half
  is what interpretation (2) would predict as well.
- **Synchrony is not analysed.** The paper sets aside the
  synchronous–asynchronous contrast on the strength of Lush 2020. So it
  cannot say whether the contrast also shrinks in low-trait participants.
  Lush et al. 2020's pre-registered test of that question is reported in
  the sibling reading as inconclusive.
- **The S1 critique is anecdotal in part.** That some participants read
  S1 as a check on the experimenter's brushing is reported "anecdotally"
  (lines 106–109). The 12% S1-but-not-S2 figure is from the data.
- **Interpretation (1) is preferred by parsimony, not shown.** The
  authors themselves grant that (2) is not ruled out. Their argument from
  domain-generality is that a general process "subsumes domain specific
  demonstrations". That is a theoretical preference, not a result.
- **Authorship.** Both authors belong to the group whose theory of
  phenomenological control the paper defends. The data and analysis are
  open (osf.io/d5hjp), and the supplementary outputs read here match the
  text's figures.

## Open questions

- Does the synchronous–asynchronous difference on S3, with expectancy
  matched, differ between participants low and high in trait
  phenomenological control? If the difference is present and equal in
  low-trait participants, interpretation (2) gains. If it is absent in
  them, interpretation (1) gains. This dataset could partly answer it,
  and the paper does not run it.
- Does proprioceptive drift track phenomenological control in the way S3
  does? The sibling reading of Lush et al. 2020 records rs 0.11 for
  synchronous drift.
- Does the relation between interoceptive sensitivity and RHI
  susceptibility survive trait phenomenological control as a covariate?
- Can an animal paradigm be given any check against the reverse
  inference the paper describes, or is subjective ownership in animals
  out of reach by this route, as the authors say?
