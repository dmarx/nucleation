summary: >-
  Nelkin (2004, rev. 2025). Moral Luck. Stanford Encyclopedia of
  Philosophy. The problem of moral luck, set out from Williams (1976) and
  Nagel (1979) as a conflict between the Control Principle, that we are
  assessable only for what depends on our control, and everyday judgments
  that assess people for what depends on luck. It restates Nagel's four
  kinds (resultant, circumstantial, constitutive, causal) and surveys the
  responses: explaining the appearance away (the epistemic argument;
  Williams's agent-regret and Wolf's virtue of taking responsibility as
  appropriate feeling, not differential blame), Williams's Postscript
  (keep the Control Principle for "morality" and care about ethics
  instead), and accepting moral luck with or without revising practice.
  Read as the stand-in for Williams's paper.

# LIT-tmpnuy19: Moral Luck

## Standing in the record

Filed as a neighbour for Williams (LIT-tmpydurr). It restates the paper's
agent-regret and the lorry driver (quoted, 1976 as reprinted 1993, 43), the
Gauguin case in outline, and the reading of the original paper, which the
1993 Postscript revises. It does not restate the paper's argument in
detail, its other cases (Anna Karenina), or its pages beyond 43.
