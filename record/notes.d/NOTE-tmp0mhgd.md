# NOTE-tmp0mhgd: Chalmers — Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness

## Contribution

The paper names and separates the hard problem from the easy problems, and
gives a reason why the separation is principled rather than a matter of
current ignorance. The easy phenomena are defined by functions, and
experience is not. It then refuses the two common responses, deflation and
mysterianism. In their place it sketches what a nonreductive but naturalistic
theory would look like: fundamental psychophysical laws, constrained by
principles that can be argued for now.

## Key insight

Ask, of any phenomenon, whether "you have explained the function but not
the phenomenon" is a conceptual mistake. For the gene, life, learning and
reportability it is. For experience it is not. Whatever answers the
question "why is this processing accompanied by experience?" must therefore
be something other than more mechanism, because any mechanism is more
structure and dynamics, and "from dynamics, one only gets more dynamics"
(§5).

## Assumptions

- **Functional definability of the easy phenomena (§3).** "All it means for
  reportability to be instantiated in a system is that the system has the
  capacity for verbal reports of internal information." The same is
  asserted for wakefulness, the gene and life. It is asserted as a
  conceptual fact, illustrated, not argued from a theory of concepts.
- **Conceivability (§5).** "Given any such process, it is conceptually
  coherent that it could be instantiated in the absence of experience." So
  experience "is not entailed by the physical". The step from conceptual
  coherence to failure of entailment is stated here and deferred to the
  book ([LIT-tmpwl6af](../literature.d/LIT-tmpwl6af.md)) for defence, including the reply to a posteriori
  necessity (Hill, Levine, Loar), which is a parenthetical "technical note"
  in §6.
- **Causal closure (§6).** "Physical laws already form a closed system";
  psychophysical laws supplement rather than interfere.
- **First-person data (§7).** Regularities between processing and
  experience can be read off one's own case and off verbal report, plus
  simplicity and homogeneity as non-empirical constraints.
- **"Awareness" (§7).** Information "directly available for global
  control"; to a first approximation, directly accessible and potentially
  reportable.

## Key results

All are conceptual arguments, thought experiments or proposals. There are
no data.

- **Easy problems listed (§2).** Discriminating, categorising and reacting
  to stimuli; integration of information; reportability; access to one's
  own states; focus of attention; deliberate control; wake and sleep.
- **Functional explanation (§3).** Explaining a function is specifying a
  mechanism that performs it, and that is what neural and computational
  modelling do. "Function" means any causal role in producing behaviour,
  not a teleological one.
- **Case studies (§4).** Crick and Koch's 35–75 Hz oscillations would
  explain binding and perhaps working memory, not why bound contents are
  experienced. A 1992 *Discover* interview is quoted in which Koch sets
  "subjective feelings" aside. Baars's global workspace "shows promise as
  a theory of awareness, the functional correlate of conscious experience",
  and leaves "why should global accessibility give rise to conscious
  experience?" unanswered. Edelman, Dennett (multiple drafts) and
  Jackendoff are given the same verdict in a sentence each. Five
  strategies: explain something else; deny the phenomenon (Allport,
  Dennett, Wilkes); claim to explain it (Flohr, Humphrey), where the step
  "ends up looking something like magic"; explain the structure of
  experience (Clark, Hardin); isolate the substrate.
- **Extra ingredients (§5).** Nonalgorithmic processing (Penrose), chaos,
  new neurophysiology and quantum mechanics (Hameroff) all explain more
  functions at best. The appeal of quantum theories is put down to a "Law
  of Minimization of Mystery". Consciousness-collapse interpretations
  presuppose consciousness and do not explain it.
- **Vitalism disanalogy (§5).** Vitalists doubted that physical mechanisms
  could perform life's functions, and accepted that explaining the
  functions would explain life. Here the functions are not in doubt, and
  the point is conceptual.
- **Nonreductive explanation (§6).** Take experience as fundamental, like
  charge after Maxwell. The theory is a set of psychophysical laws. The
  position is "naturalistic dualism", "an innocent version of dualism".
- **Structural coherence (§7, principle 1).** There is a direct
  correspondence between consciousness and awareness, and an isomorphism
  between their structures: colour space's three dimensions and the visual
  system's three axes; the visual field's geometry; intensity. It does not
  capture intrinsic character, as the intelligibility of inverted spectra
  shows. It reinterprets work on neural correlates (Crick and Koch, Libet,
  Baars, Farah, Shallice) as hypotheses about the mechanism of awareness.
- **Organizational invariance (§7, principle 2).** "Any two systems with
  the same fine-grained functional organization will have qualitatively
  identical experiences." Argued by reductio: two isomorphs with different
  experiences give a gradual neuron-to-silicon series in which two systems
  A and B, differing in under a tenth of the system, have different
  experiences. Install B's circuit beside A's with a switch, and flipping
  it changes experience with no possible functional trace, so "dancing
  qualia" go unnoticed. That is rejected as implausible. Absent and
  inverted qualia are "logically possible" but "empirically and
  nomologically impossible". The principle is conditional: it says only
  that "if an isomorph is possible, then it will have the same sort of
  conscious experience".
- **Double-aspect theory of information (§7, principle 3).** Information,
  in Shannon's sense and Bateson's "difference that makes a difference",
  has a physical and a phenomenal aspect. Four supports are listed: changes
  in experience track informational changes; invariance needs an
  organisational property to attach to; it might explain structural
  coherence; and it fits the explanation of our judgments about
  experience. If all information has a phenomenal aspect, "perhaps a
  thermostat … might have maximally simple experience". It is offered with
  a Russellian speculation that phenomenal properties are the intrinsic
  nature of the physical, "probably best ignored" for science.

## Concepts

- **Easy problems / hard problem** — problems of explaining functions /
  the problem of explaining experience (§2).
- **Experience** — what it is like to be an organism or in a state; the
  paper's "consciousness" in the strict sense (§2).
- **Awareness** — the functional correlate: information directly available
  for global control (§§2, 7).
- **Explanatory gap** — Levine's term, used for the gap between functions
  and experience (§3).
- **Extra ingredient** — whatever a theory adds to bridge the gap (§5).
- **Naturalistic dualism** — experience as fundamental, linked by natural
  laws, with physics closed (§6).
- **Structural coherence, organizational invariance, double-aspect theory
  of information** — the three principles of §7.
- **Fine-grained functional organization** — the abstract pattern of
  causal interaction between components (§7, defined more exactly in
  [LIT-tmp0swyc](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0swyc.md)).

**Composition and nesting.** The paper says nothing about wholes with
minded parts. Two of its principles bear on nesting anyway. Invariance is
indifferent to what realises the organisation, so it gives no reason to
withhold experience from a system whose components are themselves minded.
The double-aspect principle, unrestricted, puts experience wherever
information is, at every scale. Neither is applied to groups here.

## Limitations

- The decisive premise, that any physical process is conceptually
  compatible with no experience, is asserted and deferred to the book.
- The case studies cover two theories in depth. The rest are dismissed in
  one sentence each.
- The invariance argument is compressed to a page; its loopholes (speed,
  history, mild inversions, unattended qualia) appear only in the full
  paper.
- Structural coherence is stated for "familiar cases", and the paper does
  not say how far beyond them it is meant to reach.
- The double-aspect theory is labelled speculation by its author.

## Open questions

- Does the coherence principle hold for systems unlike us: an animal, a
  machine, a group? If it does, the record's group-awareness concessions
  have consequences for experience.
- What constraint, if any, picks out which information has a phenomenal
  aspect? The paper leaves this as "very much an open question", and it is
  the question an anti-nesting principle would have to answer.
