## Summary

The paper opposes both main theories of human cooperation, which it says are about altruism. The first is the "Big Mistake Hypothesis", which it describes as kin selection plus reciprocity in small groups where reputation is assessed. The second is Cultural Group Selection. Against both, the paper starts from mutualistic collaboration.

Step 1 is obligate collaborative foraging, modelled as a Stag Hunt rather than a Prisoner's Dilemma. Collaborative foraging posed three problems: sharing the spoils, coordination, and free riding. Humans met them with new mechanisms that chimpanzees lack: tolerance and fair sharing after collaboration, joint goals and joint attention with commitment, agent-neutral roles, cooperative communication, and reputation-based partner choice that selects against "hogs" and free riders. Together these make up "joint intentionality". Interdependence makes helping one's future partners pay, through Roberts' Stakeholder model, sB > C.

Step 2 begins when population grows and groups compete. Collaboration is then scaled up to "group-mindedness", or collective intentionality, in four ways:
- behavioural traditions become conventions and cultural practices, with conformity as a group marker;
- social selection becomes social norms, enforced by agents acting as emissaries of the group, and internalized as guilt and shame;
- institutions with status functions follow;
- cultural group selection comes only afterwards, acting on norms that it does not create.

The evidence is mainly comparative experiments on children and great apes, with the ontogenetic order read as a hint of the phylogenetic order. The authors call the account speculative.

## Contribution

The paper is the first compact statement of the two-step, collaboration-first evolutionary story: joint intentionality, then collective intentionality. It joins three things in one account:
- a game model (the Stag Hunt);
- a fitness mechanism (stakeholder interdependence plus partner-choice social selection, offered instead of reciprocity);
- a cognitive story (shared intentionality).

That cognitive story yields norms and institutions as conventionalized social selection.

## Key results and claims (section, printed page, verbatim)

1. **Thesis** (abstract, p. 673): humans' forms of cooperation, cognition and social life "all derive from mutualistic collaboration (with social selection against cheaters)".
2. **Collaboration, not altruism, explains cognition** (introduction, p. 674): "it is collaboration, not altruism, that creates the many coordination problems".
3. **Game model** (First Step, p. 674): "A better model for real-life cooperation is the Stag Hunt" (Skyrms 2004).
4. **Joint goals and commitment** (Coordination, p. 677): "To form a joint goal, we must know together that each of us has the goal of working with the other" (Bratman 1992). Also: "once they have formed a joint goal, humans are committed to it". The evidence cited is from infants and three-year-olds (Warneken et al. 2006; Hamann et al.; Gräfenhain et al. 2009).
5. **Reputation selects good coordinators** (Coordination, p. 678): "social selection based on reputation almost certainly played a role".
6. **Free riders are handled by reputation, not reciprocity** (Temptations to Free Ride, p. 679): "The answer is, of course, social selection by means of reputation." The reputation in question is "not reputation for altruism or anything else external, but simply reputation for being a good collaborator".
7. **Mutualism is a safe nursery for helping** (Interdependence and Altruistic Helping, p. 679): "Mutualistic collaboration thus provides a safe context within which basic tendencies to help others could evolve".
8. **Stakeholder logic** (same section, pp. 679–680):
   - "obligate collaborative foraging produces interdependence among members of a group";
   - the condition is sB > C (Roberts 2005), analogous to Hamilton's rule;
   - it does not solve altruism but "it changes the math because my selfish benefits depend on the well-being of selected others" (p. 680).
9. **Explicitly against reciprocity** (p. 680):
   - "whereas almost all other accounts of the evolution of human altruism rest on one or another form of reciprocity", "reciprocity cannot explain uncontingent acts of altruistic helping";
   - the account "does not depend on reciprocity because I am repaid for my altruistic acts not by reciprocated altruistic acts from others, but rather by their later mutualistic collaboration, which costs them nothing (actually benefits them)";
   - note 4 likens this to "what has been called pseudoreciprocity" (Bshary and Bergmüller 2008);
   - the evidence offered is Sylwester and Roberts 2010, on competitive altruism for partner choice.
10. **The usual suspects are minor at Step 1** (Summary, p. 680): "the usual suspects—kin selection, sexual selection, direct reciprocity, and indirect reciprocity— play only minor roles. The heroes of our story are (1) mutualistic collaboration and the logic of interdependence and (2) social selection based on reputation as a good collaborator".
11. **Table 1** (p. 681): reciprocity appears only in the apes/chimpanzees column. "Harassment and reciprocity" is the apes' way of sharing spoils, and "Reciprocal sharing" is their form of helping. The human entries are cooperative food sharing, partner choice, selection against free riders, third-party punishment and self-reputation.
12. **Step 1's "we" is temporary** (p. 681): "when the foraging trip was over, so was the special “we” it had engendered".
13. **Group identity** (Large-Group Coordination, p. 682): "group identification is a scaled-up version of the “we” intentionality".
14. **Norms are conventionalized social selection** (Large-Group Social Selection, p. 683): "social norms are nothing more than conventionalizations" of Step 1 acts of social selection. This is why norms could not have been directionally arbitrary.
15. **Enforcement and the second-order free-rider problem** (p. 684): the paper notes that a group punishing makes the individual's cost negligible (Boyd, Gintis and Bowles 2010). Non-enforcers are shunned rather than punished: "we simply avoid or shun nonpunishers" (Ellickson 2001; Panchanathan and Boyd 2004, an indirect-reciprocity model).
16. **Guilt as group self-judgment** (p. 684): "I am sanctioning myself or praising myself as an emissary of the group."
17. **Against kinship and reciprocity** (Conclusion, p. 685): "Kinship and reciprocity do not get you culture."
18. **Cultural group selection comes late** (p. 685): the creation of conventions, norms and institutions "is the target of cultural group selection, not its creation".
19. **Partner market** (p. 686): "a kind of market for collaborative partners such that anyone with a poor reputation will be avoided".
20. **Status of the account** (p. 686): "This account is, of course, speculative." The paper ends: "Cooperation is really difficult."

## How the paper treats reciprocity and reputation relative to mutualism and interdependence (asked)

- **Reciprocity is demoted.** Direct and indirect reciprocity are among "the usual suspects" that "play only minor roles" at Step 1 (p. 680). They are shared with "almost all primates" and "do not get you culture" (p. 685). In Table 1 (p. 681) reciprocity is drawn as the ape baseline ("Harassment and reciprocity", "Reciprocal sharing"), not as a human innovation. The paper's argument against reciprocity is that it "cannot explain uncontingent acts of altruistic helping" (p. 680).
- **Reputation is kept and made central, but re-described.**
  - It is "social selection based on reputation as a good collaborator" (p. 680), meaning partner choice in a market for collaborators (p. 686).
  - It is explicitly not reputation for altruism (p. 679).
  - The return to the helper is a later mutualistic collaboration, which costs the partner nothing, not a reciprocated benefit (p. 680).
  - Reputation, self-reputation and punishment make up the "social selection" row of Table 1.
- **Mutualism and interdependence carry the account.**
  - Mutualism is the context ("safe context", p. 679).
  - Interdependence, with stake s, is the fitness logic for helping (p. 679), and it "changes the math" without solving altruism (p. 680).
  - At Step 2, interdependence is scaled to the whole group by intergroup competition.
- **Reader's observation, not the paper's.** Functionally, the Step 1 mechanism is close to indirect reciprocity in Alexander's sense: observers choose later partners by what they saw, and the paper's own evidence (Sylwester and Roberts 2010) is a competitive-altruism study. At Step 2 the paper uses an indirect-reciprocity model, Panchanathan and Boyd 2004, to dispose of second-order free riders (p. 684). The paper separates its view from reciprocity mainly by the currency of the return (mutualistic payoff, not reciprocated aid) and by what is reputed (collaborative competence, not altruism). It does not argue that those differences change the dynamics. The "Big Mistake Hypothesis" that it sets aside is described as kin selection plus reciprocity "especially critical in small groups in which reputational assessment among familiar interactants is constant" (p. 673). This is the closest the paper comes to describing a reciprocity-first account, and it attributes the view to evolutionary psychology (Burnham and Johnson 2005).

## Does it cite Alexander? (asked)

**No.** "Alexander" does not occur in the target article, the comments or the reply, and it is absent from the joint References Cited (pp. 690–692). Trivers and Nowak are absent too. Reciprocity-based accounts are referred to only generically ("almost all other accounts", "the usual suspects", the Big Mistake Hypothesis). The authors' Step 2 intergroup-competition driver ("circle its wagons", p. 685) parallels Alexander's between-group competition, but they set it against Cultural Group Selection (Richerson and Boyd; Henrich and Henrich), not against Alexander. The counter-dominance idea is credited to Boehm 2001 (p. 676).

## Assumptions

- The Stag Hunt is the right model, and early collaboration involved very few partners, so free riding was initially self-limiting.
- Chimpanzees stand in for the last common ancestor.
- The developmental order in WEIRD children is suggestive of the phylogenetic order.
- Partner choice is possible and reputation for collaboration "impossible to fake" with direct observers (p. 679).
- The roles of kinship and cooperative breeding are backgrounded, not denied.
- Demography (group growth and intergroup competition) triggers Step 2.
- Norms inherit their direction from Step 1 social selection.

## Limitations

- By the authors' own statement the account is speculative.
- The ape comparison rests mostly on captive and lab chimpanzees and on WEIRD children.
- The stakeholder model "changes the math" but leaves the free-rider problem in helping open (the Zahavi point, p. 680).
- Separating "reputation as a collaborator" from indirect reciprocity is asserted, not modelled.
- The timing of Step 2 (group size and intergroup conflict) is contested by Sterelny in his comment and conceded in part in the reply. The comments were not read.
- Kin and cooperative breeding are deliberately backgrounded. Hawkes's comment presses this.

## Candidate THEORY

**Sentence:** Tomasello, Melis, Tennie, Wyman and Herrmann (2012) hypothesize that uniquely human cooperation evolved in two steps.
- In the first, obligate collaborative foraging made individuals interdependent, so that helping future partners paid through their stake in one another (sB > C). Partner choice by reputation as a good collaborator selected for joint intentionality: joint goals, commitment, agent-neutral roles and cooperative communication. Reciprocity played only a minor role.
- In the second, growing groups in competition with other groups scaled this up to collective intentionality. Conventions, social norms (conventionalized social selection) and institutions arose, and only then did cultural group selection act on them.

**promote_when:** Evidence on its two load-bearing claims.
- That human partner choice in collaboration, and the helping of future partners, track collaborative competence and stake rather than past reciprocated aid, in tasks that separate the two (including outside WEIRD samples).
- That the comparative and developmental order is collaboration with joint commitment first, then norm enforcement. It is weakened if reciprocity-based partner choice explains the same helping equally well, or if norm-like enforcement is found before joint collaboration in development or in the record of hominin groups.
