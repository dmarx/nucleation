# THEORY-2208: Flow doesn't predict later valence

## Source

Stefan Engeser and Nicola Baumann (2014), *Journal of Happiness Studies*
17(1): 105-124, [LIT-3070](../literature.d/LIT-3070.md), read in [NOTE-2298](../notes.d/NOTE-2298.md).

## What was actually shown

4,476 reports from 100 employees, with the Flow Short Scale and the
PANAVA scales at each signal. Within-person correlations: flow with
positive activation .59, with negative activation -.19, with valence .31
(Table 2). With concurrent flow and prior valence controlled, prior flow
does not predict valence after any transition from work (B = -.12 to .16,
none significant), and concurrent flow does in three of four (Table 5).
Valence is lower and negative activation higher at work than in leisure
(Table 1). Outcome importance: flow B = .09, valence B = -.16 (Table 6).

## The account

The paradox of work is explained by leisure's affective advantages, lower
negative activation and higher valence, not by flow at work paying off
later: "When we controlled for the stability of flow and valence, flow had
no effect on subsequent valence" (§4).

## Where it bears

On [CLAIM-441](../claims.d/CLAIM-441.md)'s third exit ([NOTE-2298](../notes.d/NOTE-2298.md), "Bearing on the record"):
exit 3 predicts the positivity after the episode; these data find it at
the same signal and not at the next. So the exit is not supported here,
and the claim's first defeated_if clause is not met. The qualifications
are the continuous measure, the two-hour lag, the unread contrary finding
of Fullagar and Kelloway ([LIT-3072](../literature.d/LIT-3072.md)), and the shared sample with
Rheinberg et al. ([THEORY-2207](THEORY-2207.md)).
