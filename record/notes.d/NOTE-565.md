# NOTE-565: Responsibility, Moral and Otherwise

## Contribution

It separates two questions that the free-will literature usually runs
together: what makes responsibility deep rather than merely causal, and
what makes it moral. It then argues that the deep kind is mostly a matter
of attributability, which reaches nonmoral traits and works of art, and
that accountability is a separate kind, not a deeper level.

## Key insight

The usual route to metaphysical worry is that accountability needs control
on top of self-disclosure, and control is where determinism bites. If
accountability is independent of self-disclosure and needs only ordinary
avoidability, the depth has to be looked for elsewhere: in what kind of
self is disclosed.

## Assumptions

- The reactive attitudes, after Strawson, mark the participant stance;
  Wolf reads "participation" broadly: the target is one of us "in some
  wider or other sense" (p. 10).
- Ordinary reactions to nonmoral traits and art (love, admiration,
  chilliness) are reactive attitudes in Strawson's sense (pp. 7–9).
- The psychopath is a stipulated figure: lacking empathy and conscience,
  but able to control his behaviour by prudence. Wolf calls this "a
  possibly fictitious and inaccurate conception" (p. 17).

## Key results

- The two distinctions inside the causal vs moral contrast (p. 2).
- Three categories of nonmoral responsibility: executive virtues and vices,
  "aesthetic" traits such as humour or pretension, and art (pp. 3–4).
- Excuses work the same way across moral and nonmoral cases: the ransacked
  home, the accidental painting (pp. 4–5).
- Watson's two-level view stated (p. 13), with control at the second level
  glossed in footnote 5 as "At least, regulative control", citing Fischer
  and Ravizza's *Responsibility and Control* ([LIT-684](../literature.d/LIT-684.md)).
- Its rejection (pp. 15–19). The pie-taker cases show accountability does
  not depend on which self the act discloses. The psychopath is
  accountable for acts whose source is not attributable to him.
- The intelligent-self hunch and its fit with three cases: the psychopath,
  El Greco's rumoured astigmatism, and Strawson's participant stance
  (pp. 21–23).

## Concepts

- **deep responsibility** — responsibility beyond the merely causal, where
  the result "expresses or reflects" the agent's self (p. 5).
- **self-disclosure view** — Watson's name for responsibility as what is
  "governed by and expressive of one's self" (pp. 5–6).
- **attributability / accountability** — entitlement to attribute a trait
  or act to a person's self, vs liability to blame, punishment, and demands
  for compensation or apology (pp. 12–13).
- **intelligent self** — a self with "the same (or better) powers of
  perception, reason, and imagination that we do, so that when she
  responds to the world, it is the same world as ours to which she
  responds" (p. 22).

## Connections

It works from Watson's "Two Faces of Responsibility" (1996) and Strawson's
"Freedom and Resentment"; neither is in the record. Footnote 5 cites Fischer
and Ravizza ([LIT-684](../literature.d/LIT-684.md), unread) for regulative control. The lecture does not
cite *Freedom Within Reason* ([LIT-685](../literature.d/LIT-685.md)) or restate its Reason View.

## Bearing on the record

Source of [THEORY-147](../theory.d/THEORY-147.md).

- **[THEORY-122](../theory.d/THEORY-122.md) (the Reason View, second-hand).** Its `promote_when` says the
  account is refuted as a claim about responsibility by an argument that an
  agent who cannot recognise moral reasons is still fit for accountability
  blame. This lecture, by Wolf, comes close to that: her psychopath, with
  no conscience, may be accountable because he knew the act was forbidden
  and could control it. But it is not the attributionist argument the
  `promote_when` names, and her accountability conditions include being
  "in a position to know that he ought not to so act". Whether that
  knowledge is the normative competence [THEORY-122](../theory.d/THEORY-122.md) means is not settled
  by the text. The record's reading: in 2013 Wolf attaches the
  appreciation requirement to fitness for the reactive attitudes, not to
  accountability. No relation is declared.
- **[THEORY-118](../theory.d/THEORY-118.md) (the asymmetry) and [THEORY-121](../theory.d/THEORY-121.md) (guidance control).** Condition
  (3), that acting so "was up to him", is an avoidability condition for
  accountability, consistent with [THEORY-118](../theory.d/THEORY-118.md)'s blame condition. Wolf
  doubts it needs anything "metaphysically robust", which is nearer to
  [THEORY-121](../theory.d/THEORY-121.md)'s compatibilism than the asymmetry is usually read. The
  lecture argues neither point at length.

For the owner's essay:

- **§8 and §8.1.** The essay uses Wolf's JoJo for normative competence.
  This lecture offers a different handle: the fit target of reactive
  attitudes perceives and understands the world as we do. That is a
  condition on responsiveness to the world's reasons, not only to
  information, which is §8.1's distinction.
- **Organizations (the record's question, not Wolf's).** The lecture says
  nothing about groups. Its split suggests two separate questions for a
  corporation: whether it can be accountable, which on these conditions
  needs knowledge of the rule and control (compare [THEORY-139](../theory.d/THEORY-139.md)), and
  whether it is a fit target of resentment or admiration, which needs an
  intelligent self.

## Limitations

The positive view is explicitly a hunch, offered because it is "also to
early [sic] in my thinking about these questions" to defend an answer
(p. 21). The psychopath is stipulated and makes no
empirical claim. Wolf is "uncertain" whether the intelligent-self reading
recovers depth or bears on determinism (p. 23). The typescript, not the
published text, was read.

## Open questions

What makes features of a person part of her self, and which beings have
selves of the relevant kind (children, animals, psychopaths), which Wolf
raises and leaves open (p. 20). Whether the intelligent self is the same
condition as the earlier sanity condition, which a reading of [LIT-685](../literature.d/LIT-685.md) would
show.
