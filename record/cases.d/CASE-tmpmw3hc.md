# CASE-tmpmw3hc: A proposed study: loot-box economies as a measurable test of whether a minority's spending selects the design of a shared product, tracking the real-money cost of a standardized reward across a game's releases against the economic weight of its high spenders, and the spread of designs across games

## The case

**The owner's move (U14).** The pornography case ([CLAIM-tmpcv1pg](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpcv1pg.md)) cannot be
tested while "extreme" has no measure. An in-game economy has one, and a
single mechanism is enough.

**The variable (A29 §4, A31 §3).** The real-money cost of obtaining a
standardized reward, at a fixed rarity and gameplay tier, across a game's
releases. For a box at price c with independent chance q of the reward, the
number of draws is geometric: expected cost c/q, and P(cost > nc) = (1 −
q)^n. A31's example, c = $2 and q = 2%: median $70, mean $100, 90th
percentile $228. With a guarantee at draw 90 the mean is
c(1 − (1 − q)^90)/q ≈ $83.8 and the tail stops at $180. "Two loot-box
systems with similar expected costs can expose players to substantially
different tail risks", so the distribution, not the mean, is tracked. Free
draws, pity carry-over, bundles, currency rates and direct purchase must be
recorded (Ivern and Carbonell count 136 design features, [LIT-tmpuoy9g](../literature.d/LIT-tmpuoy9g.md)).

**The hypotheses (A29 §7), kept apart:**

- H1, revenue-weighted design drift: within a game, a rise in high
  spenders' commercial weight predicts design changes toward the mechanics
  they are most responsive to, with ordinary-player retention controlled.
- H2, market-level selection: across games, designs with higher returns
  become more prevalent in later releases even when the median player
  prefers others.
- H3, endogenous high-spender demand: exposure to a design changes some
  players' willingness to spend.

"H1 doesn't require H3 to be true", and H2 needs no individual targeting.

**The data (A31 §9).** Player level: purchases, inventories, exposure,
independently elicited budgets and preferences, with groups fixed before
any design change ([CLAIM-tmphgjbz](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmphgjbz.md)). Game level: patch histories, prices,
odds, pity rules and revenue by baseline group. Industry level: launches,
revenue, longevity and imitation. Exogenous changes such as mandated odds
disclosure serve as natural experiments.

**The finding that would decide it (A29 §7).** "the design choices favored
by the median player and the revenue-optimal design systematically
diverge—and that the industry's observed product evolution tracks the
latter more closely". A31 §7's four outcomes, shared drift, personalized
bifurcation, segmentation and audience constraint, are the alternatives;
"a two-tier game economy" is its sharpest prediction.

## What it can show

Whether economic concentration moves the design of a shared product, and
whether by composition (exit) or by preference change. A31 §8 keeps the
normative question apart: "economic influence and wrongful domination are
not synonyms".

## Limits

Not run. The evidence A cites for its premises (Amano and Simonov,
[LIT-tmpftfht](../literature.d/LIT-tmpftfht.md); Close and colleagues, [LIT-tmpk21r4](../literature.d/LIT-tmpk21r4.md); Pape and colleagues,
[LIT-tmpvev3t](../literature.d/LIT-tmpvev3t.md); Cordes and colleagues, [LIT-tmp88875](../literature.d/LIT-tmp88875.md); Han, Ryan and Tong,
[LIT-tmpbl3eg](../literature.d/LIT-tmpbl3eg.md)) is registered unread. A31 rules out inducing harmful spending:
"Experiments involving vulnerable users or harmful spending inducements
would be inappropriate".

## Note of 2026-11-12: Secondary analysis of loot box data: Are high-spending "whales" wealthy gamers or problem gamblers? (Close, Spicer, Nicklin, Uther, J. Lloyd, H. Lloyd 2021), read

From the reading in NOTE-tmps8gyk (LIT-tmpk21r4), the reader's assessment as
checked by the filer: **supports its motivating premise** (the high-spender
group exists and carries economic weight) and characterises that group
(problem-gambling severity, not wealth), which bears on H3 (endogenous
high-spender demand) only as association. It gives no evidence on H1/H2
(design drift tracking high spenders). Its methods note (self-report,
pooled, no company data) is a direct argument for the case's player-level
transaction data.
