# NOTE-tmpin04p: Responsibility to Future Generations: A Moral Reframing Strategy for Combatting Climate Change

## Contribution

It proposes responsibility to future generations (RFG) as a "moral
reframing" of climate change. The claim is that RFG is widely held,
little tied to demographics or politics, and normally not salient, so
appealing to it should move people whom appeals to climate
responsibility leave cold. Thirteen studies test the three parts, and a
large preregistered experiment compares five ways of raising RFG.

## Key insight

Responsibility toward future people is a less partisan version of
responsibility for climate. The two correlate strongly (r = .58 to .76)
but behave differently: Republicans and Independents endorse RFG more
than climate responsibility (RCC), Democrats about equally, and RFG's
link to conservatism is half as strong. One manipulation moved RFG
without moving RCC, which the authors take to show "two separate
mechanisms, despite their high correlation" (p. 32).

## Assumptions

- **RFG is a value people already hold.** The rationale is that "caring
  for future generations is a widely endorsed principle" (p. 4). The
  paper uses caring, duty and responsibility interchangeably.
- **Kinship may carry it.** "Future generations typically include a
  person's descendants", and family receives the most moral
  consideration (p. 6, citing Crimston et al. 2016). Not tested.
- **Self-report stands for behaviour.** Behaviour frequency, intentions
  and policy support are self-reported; the one behavioural measure is a
  lottery-bonus donation.
- **Single items** carry RFG in Studies 1, 2A and 2B.

## Key results

- **Study 1** (pp. 8–12). Four Eurobarometers, 2009–2011, 34 countries,
  N = 109,513. Item: "We need to reform to benefit future generations
  even if that means making some sacrifices now" (1–4).
  - Majority agreement in 33 of 34 countries; Latvia 48%, Lithuania below
    60%, all others above 70% (p. 10).
  - Meta-correlations with RFG: ideology r = .03, subjective status .13,
    age .07, gender (female) −.05. Only status passed the preregistered
    r = .10 threshold (p. 11).
  - Supplementary: binary RFG measures in 1988 and 2008 surveys ranged
    from 25% to 79%; 74.1% of surveyed Ecological Society members called
    RFG a relevant value for a scientist (p. 12).
- **Studies 2A–2B** (pp. 12–19; CloudResearch, N = 457 and 906). Matched
  items: "When deciding how to live, I have a duty to consider the
  impact of my actions on future generations" against "… on climate
  change" (p. 13).
  - RFG above RCC: 2A, M = 5.48 against 5.17, d = 0.26, r = .67; 2B,
    66.51 against 60.08 (0–100), d = 0.26, r = .58 (p. 14).
  - The gap was driven by Republicans (2B: d = 0.66) and Independents
    (d = 0.34). Democrats scored highest on both and slightly higher on
    RCC (d = −0.12) (pp. 15–16).
  - People thought other Americans held RFG more than RCC, and thought
    others found RFG more morally right (2B: d = 0.54), in every party
    (p. 17).
  - Across Studies 2A–3B (N = 3,558), RFG correlated with conservatism
    r = −.21 and RCC r = −.45 (pp. 18–19, Table 3). RFG barely
    correlated with age, income, education, gender, race or religiosity
    (all |r| ≤ .07 meta-analytically).
- **Study 3A** (pp. 20–22; Prolific, N = 395). Five-item scales, RFG
  α = .93, RCC α = .96, correlated r = .76.
  - RFG predicted self-reported proenvironmental behaviour (β = .48;
    .41 with covariates), concern (.52; .37), perceived harm (.50;
    .36), and expecting harm sooner (−.27; −.14). Donations to charity
    only with covariates (β = .12) (Table 4, p. 22).
  - The two responsibilities were never entered in one model, "due to
    the very high correlation" (p. 21).
  - Supplementary Detroit Area Study 2002 (N = 328): RFG related to 15 of
    16 environmental outcomes, βs ≥ 0.20 (p. 23).
- **Study 3B** (pp. 23–28; Prolific, N = 1,800, 600 per party). RFG
  exceeded RCC however climate was framed (global warming, greenhouse
  gas, carbon emissions, climate change), r = .67 to .75. RFG predicted
  five Environmental Attitudes Inventory subscales, β = .47 to .54,
  adjusted R² .22 to .29 (Table 6, p. 27).
  - Supplementary Study S5: RFG predicted less endorsement of protecting
    the environment for anthropocentric reasons, β = −.35 (p. 28).
- **Study 4** (pp. 28–38; preregistered, Prolific, N = 3,175, six
  conditions). Effects against a control (writing about one's daily
  routine), Table 7 (p. 34):
  - **On RFG** (control M = 5.17 on 1–7): letter b = .37 (d = .29);
    sacrifice .29 (.22); legacy .27 (.21); video .19 (.15); longtermism
    .18 (.14). All significant.
  - **On RCC**: all but sacrifice (b = .14, p = .118). The sacrifice
    task did not mention climate (p. 32).
  - **Intentions**: all five; letter b = .54 (d = .54), others d = .18
    to .24.
  - **Policy support**: letter (d = .18) and longtermism (d = .14) only;
    legacy p = .073.
  - **Donations** (of a $10 lottery bonus; control M = $3.31): letter
    only, b = $0.49 (d = .15).
  - The conditions: a letter to a person born in 2022 who will be 28 in
    2050 (after Shrum 2021); reflection on one's legacy (Zaval et al.
    2015); reflection on sacrifices of the past generation (Watkins and
    Goodwin 2020); a 1-minute Grand Canyon video; a 385-word excerpt from
    the introduction of *What We Owe the Future*, section "Future People
    Count" (pp. 30–31).
  - **Mediation** (exploratory, preregistered; pp. 36–37). Every
    condition had a significant indirect effect through RFG on
    intentions, policy support and donations. RCC was not entered as a
    parallel mediator because of collinearity (r = .75); entered after
    RFG, RFG still predicted two of three outcomes (n. 5, p. 36).

## Concepts

- **responsibility to future generations (RFG)**: agreeing that one has a
  duty to consider, or to make sacrifices for, the effects of one's
  actions on future generations.
- **responsibility to reduce climate change (RCC)**: the same duty toward
  one's personal contribution to climate change; in norm activation
  theory a core antecedent of proenvironmental action.
- **moral reframing**: recasting an issue in terms of values an audience
  already holds, to reduce polarisation (Feinberg and Willer).
- **legacy motivation** (as the paper cites it): concern "for being
  remembered by future generations as a good person" (p. 5).

## Bearing on the record

- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** The paper does not
  supply a criterion of the self's extent that comes apart from caring.
  These are inferences from what it measures.
  - No measure of self-extension appears: no overlap with future people,
    no identification with descendants or with humanity, no future-self
    continuity.
  - Responsibility is not separated from caring. The authors justify RFG
    as "caring for future generations" (p. 4) and ask whether it is
    "morally right to care about" the future (p. 13). The 3A harm scale
    averages harm to oneself and one's family with harm to future
    generations and species (pp. 20–21), so self-regarding and
    other-regarding concern were pooled.
  - What it does separate is two responsibilities, RFG and RCC. That is
    not the separation the objection asks for.
  - Its kinship hypothesis, that "future generations" means one's
    descendants (p. 6), is the thesis's own reading, and the paper's
    limitations grant it cannot say whether people think of descendants
    or "future people more broadly" (p. 43).
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** Study 4 compares routes to the
  same felt responsibility. Read as inferences:
  - The weakest route was the impersonal one. The longtermist excerpt
    raised RFG least (d = .14) and did not raise donations. The
    strongest was a letter to one particular person born today (d =
    .29; intentions d = .54).
  - A self-regarding route worked as well as most. Legacy reflection,
    concern for how one will be remembered, raised RFG (d = .21).
  - A route that is neither self nor impersonal good worked too.
    Reflecting on what past generations gave up raised RFG (d = .22) and
    not RCC. That is reciprocity, an obligation from benefit received,
    and it is not concern for an extended self.
  - So the experiment fits the thesis's prediction that concern is
    stronger when the future is made particular and personal, but it
    also gives a non-self route of similar size. The effects are small,
    measured minutes later, and on self-report.
- **[QUESTION-tmpmx9ha](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.md).** A dated note is proposed (section 4). It updates
  the question's "What predicts it" bullet, which cites the 2021 paper.

## Limitations

- **Self-report.** The only behaviour is a share of a lottery bonus, and
  only the letter moved it (p. 42).
- **WEIRD samples** only, by the authors' account (p. 42).
- **"Future generations" is undefined.** No study says how far ahead or
  whose; Study 4's letter targets 2050.
- **No test of RFG against RCC** in one regression; mediation measured
  RFG after the manipulation and with the outcomes.
- **Study 1** merged four waves and so dropped the survey weights
  (p. 9). Its item asks about reform and sacrifice, not duty.
- **Overstatement.**
  - The discussion says "letter, legacy and longtermism interventions
    successfully increased support for policy" (p. 38); legacy's effect
    was p = .073, which the results call "marginal" (p. 33).
  - The general discussion says the results "replicated work on the
    three existing interventions" (p. 41); the results report failures to
    replicate Zaval et al. on policy support and donations (p. 33).
- **Inconsistencies and typos.**
  - Study S2's N is 1,215 in Table 1 (p. 8) and 1,285 in the text (p. 12).
  - The trimmed path model reports χ²(7) = 0.69, p = .686 (p. 36); with 7
    degrees of freedom, 0.69 would give p near 1.
  - The text refers to "Table 11" for the indirect effects, which are
    Table 8 (pp. 36–37). There, legacy → donation has a 95% interval
    printed as 0.29 to 0.14, which excludes its own b = 0.18 (p. 37).
  - Policy-support items are described as capturing "proenvironmental
    intentions for the next three months" (p. 31).
  - "Watkins and Goodwin (2021)" for 2020 (p. 32); "Xiao & McCright,
    2013" in the text, 2015 in the references; "MacAskill, 2022" in the
    text, 2023 in the references; "Stern, 200" (p. 7).
  - "RFT and covariates" (Table 4, p. 22); "batterυ" (p. 23); "t(1543 ="
    (Table 5, p. 25); "Averagte" (Figures 6a–6c, p. 35); "Participants
    were randomly to one of six conditions" (p. 29); "responsibility to
    future generations someone" (p. 29); "Fischer's Z" for Fisher's
    (pp. 18–19).
  - Table 4 prints standardized βs beside confidence intervals that are
    not on the β scale (p. 22).

## Open questions

- Does felt responsibility to future generations vary with a measured
  extension of the self, apart from caring? A study adding overlap with
  one's descendants, or identification with all humanity, and a separate
  caring measure, would test [CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md) across generations. A later
  preprint by Syropoulos, Law and Young, "Caring for Present and Future
  Generations Alike" (PsyArXiv, 2023, 10.31234/osf.io/hzwrt), not held,
  reports by its abstract that identification with all of humanity may
  mediate longtermists' moral regard.
- Does reciprocity toward the past move costly action toward the future,
  and does it work when the future people are strangers?
