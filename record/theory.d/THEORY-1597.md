# THEORY-1597: Volkan argues, from clinical work and facilitated dialogues, that a large group's shared image of an ancestral injury at the hands of an enemy, real or mythologized, changes function across generations into a marker of the group's identity, a chosen trauma, which a threat to that identity brings up, and that a recent trauma still mourned, like the Holocaust, is not one

## The account

Large-group identity is a tent's canvas worn over each person's
individual identity, shared by everyone under the tent. Children absorb it
as parents and other adults "deposit images and ego tasks in children's
developing identities", and "After going through the adolescence passage
however, a person cannot change a core large-group identity that developed
in childhood". Only trauma inflicted by "an identifiable enemy large group
that has deliberately inflicted pain" works on that identity.

A chosen trauma is "The shared mental image of the large group's real,
fantasized or even mythologized historical event, which, as it is
transmitted from one generation to the next, undergoes a 'change of
function' ... and becomes a most significant large-group identity marker."
In dialogues, "after perceiving a threat against his or her large-group
identity", a participant "would start talking, with accompanying emotions,
about a trauma suffered by his or her ancestors at the hand of an enemy
centuries ago". A chosen trauma carries entitlement "to recover what was
lost", and leaders can reactivate it in a "time collapse", as Milošević did
with the Battle of Kosovo of 1389. The Holocaust "is not a chosen trauma":
its stories are alive and it is mourned, not kept for revenge.

## Evidence

The lecture ([NOTE-1250](../notes.d/NOTE-1250.md)), read in place of Volkan 2001. The evidence is
the author's analyses, consultations and facilitated dialogues, told from
memory, with one worked historical case (Kosovo). No method, sample,
coding or comparison is reported, and nothing says how often a threatened
identity did not bring up an ancestral trauma. The account of
transmission, Volkan says, comes mainly from studies of Holocaust
survivors and their offspring.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-297](../questions.d/QUESTION-297.md), the identity route. What joins the descendant's self is a
shared image, which may be mythologized, not the ancestors' injury as it
was. It is called up by a present threat and felt as grievance and
entitlement rather than grief. And because it is deposited by parents and
grandparents, the question's test of distress following identification
"apart from ... family transmission" is one this account would expect to
be hard to pass. Recent, mourned trauma is the case it sets apart.
