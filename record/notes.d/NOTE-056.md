# NOTE-056: Thompson, "Who Goes Nazi?" (Harper's, 1941)

## Contribution

Thompson, a journalist who had reported from Germany, Austria and France, turns a "macabre parlor game" into an essay. She walks around an imagined American society party and predicts which guests would join a Nazi movement in a showdown. She sets aside race and nationality as predictors, and says Nazism appeals to a type of mind and is partly the disease of a post-war generation. Through a series of character sketches she contrasts those with a settled personal code, security or happiness with those driven by resentment, social insecurity, opportunism or vanity. She concludes that "nice people don't go Nazi", and that what decides it is something in the person.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §I: Mr. A, a poor, cultivated editor with his own code, would never go Nazi. Mr. B, a rich banker-sportsman who "fits easily into whatever pattern is successful", would join once the movement looked likely to win. Mr. C, a brilliant, embittered intellectual humiliated by snobbery, is "already a Nazi" in her telling, and a product of a democracy that preached equality and practised snobbery. D, a spoiled son, is "the only born Nazi"; Mrs. E is a masochistic, cowed wife; Mrs. F, a secure and happy former actress, would never go Nazi.
- §II: The staff and other guests. The butler James and the City College student Bill are immune. G, an intellectual rationaliser, would explain Nazism away but never commit. H, a Midwestern historian, would fight, as would a young German émigré who works in an aircraft factory. J, a rich Jewish financier, is drawn to C. L, a labour leader, is "the strongest natural-born Nazi in this room".
- §III: Her conclusion: "Kind, good, happy, gentlemanly, secure people never go Nazi". The frustrated intellectual, the scared speculator, the spoiled son, the labour tyrant and the opportunist would, and the criterion is "something in them", not race, colour, creed or social condition.

## Open questions

- It is a 1941 essay of character typology, not empirical social science. Its claims are the author's impressions, reported here as hers.
- If filed, it should be filed as a primary historical text. A deeper reading would pair it with empirical work on the social bases of fascist support, to see which of her types that literature supports.
- Harper's own archive blurb links the essay to a modern news hook, so its readership is partly contemporary political commentary.
