# CLAIM-431: Corporate remorse cannot be told from prudence

## The objection

[CLAIM-432](CLAIM-432.md) separates two routes by which a corporation's harm to
others reaches its decisions: as one of its own concerns, through
procedures and role duties (functional remorse), or as a threat to its
other concerns, through regulators and markets (functional fear). A
corporation with only the second is functionally psychopathic.

But a corporation's procedures are not given. Each was installed by the
corporation, and a corporation installs a procedure for its own ends:
a harm-weighing rule protects its reputation, a compliance duty limits
its liability, an ethics programme helps it recruit. So a harm that
reaches its decisions through a procedure reaches them, one step back,
as a threat to some concern of its own. The two routes differ in how
far back the threat sits, not in kind. On this view every corporation
has fear and none has remorse, and the clinical word distinguishes no
corporation from another. It adds only connotation (a fixed character,
an object of fear and blame) to a description of incentives, and points
remedy toward blame where the reading itself says the remedy is
redesign.

This is Velasquez's "as if" ([LIT-1432](../literature.d/LIT-1432.md)) in functional form: if the
corporation's intentions are only as if, so is its remorse.

It rests on [CLAIM-418](CLAIM-418.md): an organization's perspective is not thereby an
agent's character.

## What it does not say

- It does not deny that a corporation is an agent, or responsible, or
  that it can bear functional affect. It grants [CLAIM-039](CLAIM-039.md) and denies only
  that remorse is among the affects a corporation can have apart from
  fear.
- It does not deny the structural description of Wells Fargo
  ([CASE-151](../cases.d/CASE-151.md)).

## What would answer it

Three things, any of which would do.

1. **The test the reading names.** Corporations whose procedures make
   others' harms their concern keep weighing those harms when the
   external sanction that might have motivated the procedure is lifted,
   and corporations moved only by sanction do not. If the two differ
   then, the concern is not reducible to the threat, whatever its origin.
2. **Origin is not current function.** A person's remorse was installed
   too, by upbringing, for ends that were in part their carers'. The
   record does not call a person's remorse prudence because of where it
   came from. If the same holds for a corporation, the objection proves
   too much. It would hold only if something about corporations, unlike
   persons, keeps an installed concern answerable to the end it was
   installed for.
3. **The construct's own evidence.** The PCL-R scores lack of remorse
   from "a semistructured interview, case history information, and
   specific scoring criteria" (Hare and Neumann, p. 219; [THEORY-892](../theory.d/THEORY-892.md)),
   so a person's remorse is told from its display by what the record of
   their conduct and statements shows. If a corporation's record can show
   the same difference, the distinction is drawable for it too.

## Note of 2026-10-30: what holds it back

The objection's own second answer is unmet: the record does not call
a person's installed remorse prudence because of its origin, and
nothing in the record says what about a corporation, unlike a person,
keeps an installed concern answerable to the end it was installed
for. Its first answer, whether corporations keep weighing others'
harms once the external sanction is lifted, is a test nobody has run.

## Note of 2026-11-22: Mulgan, read

A **rival framing, not a counterexample** ([LIT-1874](../literature.d/LIT-1874.md), [NOTE-2306](../notes.d/NOTE-2306.md);
[THEORY-2216](../theory.d/THEORY-2216.md)). Mulgan does not try to tell remorse from prudence. He
calls a corporate group psychopathic when its structure lacks any mechanism
for recognising and responding to moral reasons (p. 910), and makes moral
agency depend on a reliable disposition to respond "appropriately" to them
(p. 905), without saying how reliability would be told from
self-interested compliance. That is this objection's difficulty, met by
stipulation. The status is unchanged.

## Note of 2026-11-23: the first answer's test, run on open sources

[LEAD-101](../leads.d/LEAD-101.md) ran this objection's first answer, whether corporations whose
procedures make others' harms their concern keep weighing them once the
sanction lifts, on open sources ([CASE-405](../cases.d/CASE-405.md)). It is **not met either
way**: no case holds reputation and benefit fixed.

- **The test as stated cannot answer the objection through reversion.**
  Worked through ([CLAIM-1169](CLAIM-1169.md)), this objection predicts what [CLAIM-432](CLAIM-432.md)
  predicts of a sanction-only corporation (it reverts) and of a redesigned
  one whose procedure still pays (it persists). They part only for a
  redesigned corporation once keeping the procedure earns it nothing. The
  reversion found in monitored firms ([CLAIM-1136](CLAIM-1136.md)) is evidence for
  neither.
- **What the cases add, as illustration.** Siemens's compliance leaders
  told staff that violations could bring prosecution and "exclusion from
  projects from multilateral development banks, which will have major
  financial consequences for the company" ([NOTE-2410](../notes.d/NOTE-2410.md)): a procedure that
  stopped a bribe at a cost, explained by threat, as this objection says
  installed concerns are. Purdue's response to a 2013 subpoena was to stop
  writing detailed board reports ([NOTE-2392](../notes.d/NOTE-2392.md)), a response to the threat
  of the record, not to the harm.

The status is unchanged.
