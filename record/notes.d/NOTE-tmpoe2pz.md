# NOTE-tmpoe2pz: Engeser and Baumann — Fluctuation of flow and affect in everyday life

## Contribution

It measures flow directly and affect on three dimensions at every
experience-sampling signal, separates active from passive leisure, and
tests whether flow at work is followed by better affect afterwards. It
finds the positivity that goes with flow at the same signal, not at the
next.

## Key insight

Flow travels with feeling good at the moment, moderately, and especially
with feeling energized; it does not leave a residue of better feeling
afterwards that the next signal picks up.

## Assumptions

- **A signal samples the episode under way.** Seven random signals in a
  twelve-hour window, about every two hours (§3.2, §4).
- **Affect by the PANAVA circumplex** (§2.4): positive activation
  (energetic, awake, elated, motivated), negative activation (stressed,
  annoyed, nervous, worried) and valence (unhappy-happy,
  satisfied-unsatisfied), each 1 to 7.
- **Adjacent signals measure a transition.** Overnight transitions and
  missing neighbours are dropped (§3.5); the authors grant that two hours
  is too coarse for sequence effects (§4).

## Key results

- **Sample (§2.1, §3.1).** 101 participants from the University of
  Potsdam's recruitment (newspaper, companies, personal contacts; 30 euros),
  one dropped, 69 women, age 18 to 60; 4,504 of 4,900 signals answered
  (92%), 4,476 analysed.
- **Concurrent correlations (Table 2; within-person standardized; "all
  correlations are significant at 1 % level").** Flow with positive
  activation .59, with negative activation -.19, with valence .31. "Flow
  was weakly and negatively correlated with NA and moderately and
  positively correlated with VA" (§3.3). Raw-score correlations "are very
  similar" (footnote 1).
- **Contexts (Table 1, §3.4).** Flow and positive activation highest at
  work and in active leisure, lowest in passive leisure; negative
  activation higher and valence lower at work than in either kind of
  leisure. Creative active leisure had the highest flow, higher than work,
  with high valence.
- **Lagged test (§3.5, Tables 4 and 5).** Adjacent flow and valence
  correlate weakly to moderately (.11 to .30), but with concurrent flow
  and prior valence in the model, prior flow does not predict valence in
  any transition from work (B = -.12 to .16, none significant), while
  concurrent flow does in three of four (B = .39, .57, .48; .33, not
  significant, into active leisure). "There was no evidence that the flow
  preceding the measures of flow (Flown-1) influenced VA" (sic). The
  reverse, valence predicting later flow, also failed.
- **Outcome importance (§3.6, Table 6).** Three items ("Something
  important to me is at stake here", "I must not make any mistakes here",
  "I am worried about failing"): "the perceived outcome importance showed
  a positive relation to flow and a negative relation to VA" (B = .09 and
  -.16). With it in the model, work no longer differs from active leisure
  in flow. It raised flow at work and not in active leisure; it lowered
  valence in active leisure and not at work.

## Concepts

- **Flow** — "an absorbing state in which the individual feels in control
  of the action even under high demand" (§1.1), measured by the ten-item
  Flow Short Scale, with no enjoyment item.
- **Valence (VA)** — the pleasure-displeasure dimension, assessed
  directly; positive and negative activation mix pleasure with arousal
  (§2.4).
- **Perceived outcome importance** — what is at stake in the activity,
  including worry about failing.

## Connections

It replicates Csikszentmihalyi and LeFevre (1989; [LIT-tmp6jvaq](../literature.d/LIT-tmp6jvaq.md)) with a
direct flow measure, takes up Schallberger and Pfister's (2001;
[LIT-tmps1x50](../literature.d/LIT-tmps1x50.md)) affective explanation, and cites Rheinberg et al. (2007;
[LIT-tmpu5vec](../literature.d/LIT-tmpu5vec.md)) for goals. The two papers' samples match in source, size,
pay, age range and sex ratio, so it appears to report the same data with a
fuller affect measure; the paper does not say so. It cites Fullagar and
Kelloway (2009; [LIT-tmpvw1m3](../literature.d/LIT-tmpvw1m3.md)) for "evidence that flow is associated with
subsequent positive mood", the opposite of its own lagged result, and
Bassi and Delle Fave (2012; [LIT-tmp6uhtn](../literature.d/LIT-tmp6uhtn.md)) and Csikszentmihalyi (1996) for
flow as an ingredient of a happy life "without necessarily being a state
of happiness" (§1.4).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-441](../claims.d/CLAIM-441.md), exit 3** (the positivity comes after the episode). This is
  the most direct test the record holds. Exit 3 predicts valence undefined
  or neutral during flow and positive afterwards. Here valence goes with
  flow at the same signal and is not raised at the next once that is
  controlled. On these data exit 3 is not supported: what positivity flow
  has, it has during the episode. The claim's first defeated_if clause
  ("flow is not reported as positive during the episode, only after it")
  is not met. The qualification is the measure: a moderate correlation of
  a continuous absorption score with valence, not a report that flow
  episodes feel good, and a two-hour lag cannot see a positivity that
  follows within minutes. Fullagar and Kelloway's contrary finding is
  unread.
- **[CLAIM-441](../claims.d/CLAIM-441.md), exit 1 and [CLAIM-433](../claims.d/CLAIM-433.md)** (which valence). Valence here is
  general pleasure-displeasure, so it does not separate intrinsic
  pleasantness from goal-conduciveness. Flow goes more with positive
  activation (.59) than with valence (.31), and outcome importance, the
  nearest thing here to stakes on a goal, raises flow and lowers valence.
  So flow's positivity is not carried by what is at stake. That is
  consistent with exit 1 and does not show it.
- **[CLAIM-440](../claims.d/CLAIM-440.md) and [TERM-076](../terms.d/TERM-076.md).** A parallel: outcome importance includes worry
  about failing, and its lowering valence fits a sign read off threat to a
  course. No edge; nothing here measures progress.
- **[CLAIM-162](../claims.d/CLAIM-162.md).** Background: outcome importance raises flow at work, so in
  flow at work the longer-run stake is present, not absent. Nothing here
  measures whether any interest is overridden.

## Limitations

Correlational and pooled over activities; no isolation of flow episodes.
Flow is a continuous score with no enjoyment item, so "flow" here is
nearer Abuhamdeh's task involvement ([NOTE-1429](NOTE-1429.md)) than his discrete
enjoyable flow. The lag is about two hours. The leisure transitions are
small (n = 45 into passive and 85 into active leisure). Likely the same
sample as Rheinberg et al., so the two are not independent.
