# NOTE-tmpv28va: Calabresi and Melamed — Property rules, liability rules, and inalienability

## Contribution

One framework for property and torts. It separates who holds an
entitlement from how the entitlement is protected, and gives three
protections, property, liability and inalienability rules. The choice
between them is explained by transaction costs and the cost of collective
valuation. Applied to nuisance it finds a fourth rule the literature had
missed; applied to crime it explains criminal sanctions as protecting
property rules against conversion into liability rules.

## Key results

- **Entitlement first** (p. 1090): "the fundamental thing that law does is
  to decide which of the conflicting parties will be entitled to prevail."
  Then "a series of equally difficult second order decisions" about "the
  manner in which entitlements are protected" (p. 1092).
- **Property rule** (p. 1092): an entitlement so protected can be removed
  only if the buyer "must buy it from him in a voluntary transaction in
  which the value of the entitlement is agreed upon by the seller"; the
  state "gives the seller a veto if the buyer does not offer enough."
- **Liability rule** (p. 1092): "Whenever someone may destroy the initial
  entitlement if he is willing to pay an objectively determined value for
  it, an entitlement is protected by a liability rule"; "the holder's
  complaint that he would have demanded more will not avail him once the
  objectively determined value is set."
- **Inalienability** (pp. 1092–93): "An entitlement is inalienable to the
  extent that its transfer is not permitted between a willing buyer and a
  willing seller." Such rules "may also be viewed as limiting or
  regulating the grant of the entitlement itself." They can be partial,
  "prescribing preconditions for a valid sale or forbidding a sale
  altogether" (p. 1111), and do not exclude compensation: inalienability
  does not mean "there will be no compensation to the holder of the
  entitlement if it is taken from him" (n. 44).
- **Mixed protection** (p. 1093): "most entitlements to most goods are
  mixed".
- **Why liability rules** (p. 1110): "a very common reason, perhaps the
  most common one, for employing a liability rule rather than a property
  rule to protect an entitlement is that market valuation of the
  entitlement is deemed inefficient".
- **The fourth rule** (p. 1116): "Missing is a fourth rule representing an
  entitlement in Taney to pollute, but an entitlement which is protected
  only by a liability rule."
- **Unilateral conversion** (§V, p. 1125): "For us to charge the thief
  with a penalty equal to an objectively determined value of the property
  stolen would be to convert all property rule entitlements into liability
  rule entitlements." "Liability rules represent only an approximation of
  the value of the object to its original owner". Hence (p. 1126) "an
  undefinable kicker which represents society's need to keep all property
  rules from being changed at will into liability rules", and "we impose
  criminal sanctions as a means of deterring future attempts to convert
  property rules into liability rules." The nuisance case differs because
  there a liability rule was "appropriate, even though it permitted the
  nonaccidental and unconsented taking of an entitlement"; the thief
  "could have negotiated" (pp. 1126–27).
- **Modesty** (p. 1128): model building "affords only one view of the
  Cathedral."

## Assumptions

- Efficiency (Pareto, the cheapest cost avoider) and distribution carry
  the analysis; a residual "other justice" category is left nearly empty.
- "Legal process" questions, which institution decides and by what
  procedure, are set aside (n. 2).
- Collective valuation is costly and approximate; transaction costs,
  holdouts and freeloaders drive the choice of rule.
- Stylized cases (Taney and Marshall); no data.

## Concepts

Entitlement; second-order decisions; property, liability and
inalienability rules; mixed entitlements; collective valuation; holdout;
freeloader; cheapest cost avoider; moralism, self-paternalism and
paternalism; rule four; the "kicker"; conversion of a property rule into
a liability rule.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpl0sgt.** §V states the claim's mechanism, a thief who takes
  and stands ready to pay converts a property rule into a liability rule,
  and treats the conversion as a harm beyond the single case, which
  criminal sanctions exist to deter. The claim is filed after the
  exchange's A57, which used the 1972 framework but not §V. The paper
  frames the harm in efficiency terms (a liability price is "only an
  approximation"); the claim frames it as control and consent. The two are
  compatible; the consent framing is the claim's, not the paper's.
- **CLAIM-tmp6vck6.** Qualifies. The paper's inalienability limits the
  holder's power to transfer ("limiting or regulating the grant of the
  entitlement itself"), which is near the claim's "the holder lacks the
  power to confer". But it is justified instrumentally, can be partial,
  and leaves compensation in place (n. 44). The claim's grounding in
  standing is not the paper's.
- **CLAIM-tmp3t16f** and **QUESTION-tmpcz1ui.** Left open. The paper does
  not model bargaining after a breach or litigation cost as a strategic
  variable, and sets process questions aside.
- **CLAIM-464.** §V's conversion is an instance of an act changing what
  correction would take: after the taking the holder has only the
  collectively set price.
- The exchange's characterization (A57, A59) is faithful. Its "de facto
  conversion", presented as its own development, is already §V.

## Limitations

A framework with stylized cases and no empirical test. "Other justice"
reasons and process are left out by the authors' choice. The account of
criminal sanctions is one explanation the authors offer, not a theory of
punishment. §II.B was read quickly.
