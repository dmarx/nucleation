# LIT-tmplggfb: 100 Flowers

Jad Abumrad and Robert Krulwich, with producer Lynn Levy and guest Douglas
Hofstadter, "100 Flowers", *Radiolab*, WNYC Studios. The transcript page is
dated 20 October 2014 and opens an episode the hosts introduce as "eight
experiments in translation". The transcript covers the Hofstadter segment
and the closing music. <https://radiolab.org/podcast/1000-flowers/transcript>

## Key takeaways

- **The case.** At sixteen, in 1961, Hofstadter memorized Clément Marot's
  "A une Damoyselle malade", a get-well poem for a young girl at a
  sixteenth-century court. It has 28 lines of three syllables, rhymes in
  couplets, begins and ends on the same line, and puts the poet's name in
  the middle. About twenty years later, the French translation of his
  first book, *Gödel, Escher, Bach* ([LIT-437](LIT-437.md)), sent him back to it.
- **Form first.** Hofstadter's starting position is that "the essence of
  his poem was a form rather than a message", the message being only "get
  well". He rejects a colleague's version for running to 30 lines, though
  it keeps the jam, the pale face and the poet's name.
- **Concepts have halos.** He renders "confitures" as buttered bread,
  arguing that words stand for concepts and a concept has "a kind of a
  halo" of things that go with it.
- **The free version wins.** He sent the poem to about sixty people, and
  the versions became a 700-page book. His mother's version has about 16
  lines and ignores the syllable count, the rhymes and the frame. His first
  reaction was that she had ignored the form, and he now says it "has stood
  the test of time".
- **No rules.** His position became Mao's "let one hundred flowers bloom":
  each angle enriches the original, and "there are no rules". Asked whether
  the facts of a poem are negotiable (jam in one version, ham in another),
  he answers that one fact about the poem is that it was written in French,
  which no translation keeps.
- **One thing from many.** Every translation "show[s] something" about
  Marot, and none gives the whole. His analogy is the single photograph of
  Chopin: Chopin "looked like many things", and knowing him is knowing
  "millions of different things that are united by analogy into something
  that we refer to as one thing".

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-08 at the owner's request and read in full from the
published transcript ([NOTE-tmpjn2wc](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpjn2wc.md)). It is an interview, not an argument. It
is here as Hofstadter's own statement of a view of translation that his
analogy books treat at length: *Surfaces and Essences* ([LIT-420](LIT-420.md)) argues
that categories are held together by analogy, and the Chopin remark applies
that thesis to what makes a person, or a poem, one thing. The 700-page book
the episode describes but does not name is *Le Ton beau de Marot* (1997),
which the record does not hold.
