# LIT-529: Incomplete Nature: How Mind Emerged from Matter

Terrence W. Deacon, *Incomplete Nature: How Mind Emerged from Matter*, New
York: W. W. Norton & Company, 2011 (hardcover, ISBN 978-0-393-04991-6;
Library of Congress 2011030827; xv, 602 pp. per the catalogue record).
Norton reissued it in paperback on 22 April 2013 (ISBN 978-0-393-34390-8,
624 pp. per the publisher). The catalogue record the Internet Archive keeps
dates the hardcover 2012. Wikipedia's infobox gives "November 21, 2011" as
the hardcover's release; `published:` uses that date, which no catalogue
record I reached confirms.

The brief's citation (2011, W. W. Norton) is correct.

## Key takeaways

*Registered from the publisher's description and a library contents note,
not a reading. What follows is what those sources say.*

- **The publisher's description** (Norton, paperback page), verbatim: "As
  scientists study the minutiae of subatomic particles, neural
  connections, and molecular compounds, their attempts at a 'theory of
  everything' harbor a glaring omission: they still cannot explain us, the
  thoughts and perceptions that truly make us what we are. A masterwork
  that brings together science and philosophy, Incomplete Nature offers a
  revolutionary, captivating account of how life and consciousness
  emerged, revealing how our desires, feelings, and intentions can be
  understood in terms of the physical world."
- **The catalogue summary:** "Examines the emergent processes that bridge
  the gap between organisms that think and have consciousness and those
  that do not and discusses the origins of life, information, and free
  will."
- **Contents** (the catalogue's contents note): Absence; (W)holes;
  Homunculi; Golems; Teleonomy; Emergence; Constraint; Homeodynamics;
  Morphodynamics; Teleodynamics; Autogenesis; Work; Information;
  Significance; Evolution; Self; Sentience; Consciousness; Epilogue.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, for the philosophy that tries
to mark living, teleological systems off from merely dissipative ones. No
anthology topic holds it, and it carries no instruction for
machine-learning practice. The Anthology of the SOTA does not hold it: a
grep of its literature.d for "Deacon" and the title found nothing.

`Deferred` because it was not read. This record files a book from its
publisher's description and contents when no lawful text can be read, and
does not rebuild a book from what others say about it.

What the record already says about it, at second hand, from Nahas &
Sachs ([LIT-192](LIT-192.md), read in [NOTE-094](../notes.d/NOTE-094.md)):

- **The demarcation claim.** [NOTE-094](../notes.d/NOTE-094.md) reports Deacon's levels as
  morphodynamics, self-organizing dissipation, and teleodynamics, the
  coupling of mutually limiting morphodynamic processes that preserves a
  system's integrity. The "autogen" model (Deacon, Srivastava & Bacigalupi
  2014) realizes it. This is the book's chapters 9–11 as the contents
  name them. If the account holds, it is the record's candidate for a
  principled line between a candle flame (morphodynamic) and an organism
  (teleodynamic). That is unverified here.
- **Its use in the naturalism debate.** Nahas & Sachs report
  García-Valdecasas's preference for teleodynamics over the organizational
  approach (Moreno & Mossio, [LIT-536](LIT-536.md)), and suggest Deacon for the
  philosophical project of placing mind in nature ([NOTE-094](../notes.d/NOTE-094.md), C5 and C7).
- **The candle-flame case against autopoiesis.** [NOTE-093](../notes.d/NOTE-093.md) and [NOTE-094](../notes.d/NOTE-094.md)
  report, from Nahas & Sachs, that Deacon and Cashman criticize
  autopoiesis (Thompson 2007) for lacking the tools to tell living
  teleology from dissipative self-organization. The source is
  identified here: **Deacon, T. W. & Cashman, T. (2013), "Teleology versus
  mechanism in biology: beyond self-organization", in B. G. Henning & A.
  C. Scarfe (eds.), *Beyond Mechanism: Putting Life Back into Biology*,
  Lanham, MD: Lexington Books (ISBN 978-0-7391-7436-4), ch. 13.**
  Nahas & Sachs's bibliography gives pp. 290–311 and lists the editors as
  "Scarfe & Henning"; the volume's editor page at Gonzaga lists Henning
  first. I did not see the chapter or the volume's pagination, and found
  no lawful copy, so it is not filed. A later paper by the same authors,
  "Steps to a metaphysics of incompleteness" (Theology and Science
  14(4):401–429, 2016, DOI 10.1080/14746700.2016.1231977), is also in
  Nahas & Sachs's bibliography; I did not seek it out.

Where it would bear, once read: on the demarcation question ([LIT-192](LIT-192.md)), as
the strongest claim in this batch that there *is* a line; on Friston's
Markov-blanket account ([LIT-526](LIT-526.md)), which names the candle flame and
excludes it on statistical grounds where Deacon would use constraint; on
Perunov, Marsland & England ([LIT-518](LIT-518.md)), which denies the line; and on
Levin's goal-pursuing Selves ([LIT-439](LIT-439.md)). Its chapters on information,
self and consciousness also bear on the record's consciousness readings,
for instance Seth's biological naturalism ([LIT-135](LIT-135.md)), and on [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)'s
question about minds made of parts.

**Priority for a reading: high** for the demarcation question. Chapters
8–11 are the ones to read first, and the Deacon & Cashman chapter is
probably the shorter way in.
