# LIT-tmp4po64: Collective Guilt

Tracy Isaacs (2011), "Collective Guilt", chapter 3 of *Moral Responsibility in Collective Contexts*, Oxford University Press (ISBN 9780199782963), pp. 71–94 — DOI-10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199782963.003.0003

## Key takeaways

*Not read. The source of each item is marked: the NDPR review is read; the
abstract summary is a search service's paraphrase of the OUP abstract and is
unverified.*

- **Collective guilt is a moral status, not a feeling.** It is "blameworthy
  collective moral responsibility", a feature of collective agents
  (abstract summary).
- **It does not distribute.** That a collective is blameworthy does not
  make each member blameworthy for the collective wrong (King's review,
  citing pp. 81–83, inside this chapter). Each member is responsible only
  for her contribution (review).
- **Collectives may be guilty without guilt feelings.** The chapter
  distinguishes being guilty, believing oneself guilty and feeling guilty,
  and personal, membership and collective guilt, drawing on Jaspers's moral
  and metaphysical guilt. It engages Gilbert's account of collective guilt
  feelings and concludes that collectives may be guilty but "may not" have
  guilt feelings (abstract summary; the exact modal force is unverified).

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-04 at the owner's request, Deferred because it could not be
read lawfully. It is the record's first holding on collective moral
responsibility. No anthology topic holds it.

**What the book is, from the review.** The book argues for a "two-level"
theory, collective and individual responsibility each irreducible. Its
collective agents are organisations (formal decision structures) and
"goal-oriented collectives" that coalesce around a joint goal (a walk, a
study group, a stadium wave), which are agents for the action they perform.
Isaacs holds that collective action is more fundamental than individual
contribution: "individuals' contributions … inherit relevant moral features
from the collective action context in which they occur" (p. 57, quoted by
King). King objects that a goal-oriented collective has no existence apart
from its action, so it is unclear whom collective blame targets once the
goal is abandoned.

**Against Gilbert ([LIT-tmpbdcqn](LIT-tmpbdcqn.md)), no relation declared.** The owner asked
for the debate between Gilbert and Isaacs on collective guilt. By the
abstract summary, Isaacs separates collective guilt from collective guilt
feelings and allows the first without the second. Gilbert, by Huebner's
report, makes collective guilt feelings a plural subject's joint commitment
to feel guilt as a body, and treats their appropriateness as evidence of
collective guilt. So the two agree that collectives can be guilty and
disagree on whether collectives feel it, which is the same question that
divides Gilbert's and Schmid's accounts of collective emotion. Both texts are
unread here, so no relation is declared. A reading of both should check
whether Isaacs rejects Gilbert's account or only declines to need it.

**Neighbours.** Huebner ([LIT-tmpdb036](LIT-tmpdb036.md)) argues that guilt-based defences of
collective emotion establish only holism: members' emotions shaped by their
group. If Isaacs is right that collective guilt is a status that does not
require a feeling, that is consistent with Huebner's point, and it would
leave the question of collective guilt feelings to accounts of collective
emotion. List and Pettit hold group agents responsible (their book Group Agency,
[LIT-391](LIT-391.md), Part III, by its contents). Isaacs's goal-oriented collectives are
looser than their group agents; whether they meet List and Pettit's
rationality conditions is a question for a reading.

**What a reading should check.**

- How Isaacs argues that collective guilt does not need collective guilt
  feelings, and whether "may not" means "need not" or "cannot".
- Which of Gilbert's claims she disputes: the definition by joint
  commitment, or the inference from appropriate feeling to guilt.
- Whether membership guilt, in her sense, is a feeling or a status.
