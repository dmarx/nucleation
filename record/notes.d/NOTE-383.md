# NOTE-383: Against Narrativity

## Contribution

Before it, "the narrative self" was a near-consensus across philosophy,
psychology and psychotherapy, usually stated with the descriptive and the
ethical claims run together. The paper separates them, rejects both, and
supplies the vocabulary later work uses to say what exactly is being
claimed: Diachronic versus Episodic self-experience, and three separable
components of Narrativity. It also makes a case for a temperament the
narrative literature had treated as deficient.

## Key insight

Knowing that you, the human being, have a long past is one thing; feeling
that the self you now are was there for it is another. Narrative theories
assume the second and then add story to it. Strip both away and a life can
still be examined, loyal and good, because the past can shape the present
"without being present or alive as the past".

## Assumptions

- **There is self-experience as distinct from experience of oneself as a
  whole human being** (§2). Taken for granted ("I'm going to take its
  viability for granted"), with Henry James as the example.
- **Introspective reports of temporal temperament are reliable** enough to
  classify people, including the author ([−R] is "the most vulnerable"
  claim, since one cannot notice one's own revision).
- **"I*" may refer to an inner self, but nothing turns on whether it does**
  (n. 8). The paper's claims are about the content of self-experience.
- **The fundamentals of temporal temperament are genetically determined**
  (§3). Asserted ("I take it"), not argued.

## Key results

There are no formal results. The argument, in order:

- **§1.** Four combinations of the descriptive and normative theses.
  Sartre: descriptive true, normative false. Plutarch: the reverse. The
  academy: both true. Strawson: both false.
- **§§2–3.** The Diachronic/Episodic distinction ([D] and [E]); it is a
  matter of degree, may vary with age and health, and is an "individual
  difference variable" found "across all cultures". Diachronics and
  Episodics misjudge each other. Rilke on memories "changed into our very
  blood" shows how the past can inform the present without being recalled.
- **§4.** If being Diachronic were necessary for being Narrative,
  Episodics could not be Narrative; Strawson is Episodic. His
  boat-falling memory is from the inside "visually ... kinaesthetically,
  proprioceptively" but does not present as having happened to me*. The
  inference from emotional from-the-inside character to "happened to me*"
  "is simply not valid".
- **§5.** Statements of the psychological thesis (Sartre's Roquentin,
  Sacks, Bruner, Dennett) and of the ethical one (Schechtman, Taylor,
  Ricoeur, MacIntyre). Those who hold the ethical thesis "are really just
  talking about themselves". Campbell's "what I have made of my life"
  leaves him "bewildered"; Shaftesbury's "twenty successive selfs" is his
  view.
- **§§6–7.** Narrative needs more than Diachronicity: a "form-finding
  tendency" [F]. Diachronicity and form-finding are independent (all four
  combinations are possible). Story-telling [S], fitting one's life to a
  genre without necessarily fabricating, entails form-finding and is
  sufficient for Narrativity.
- **§8.** Revision [R], non-conscious falsification, is common but not
  required, and need not be self-serving or even "charged" by moral
  emotion (John Dean's Watergate recall, flashbulb memories). So the
  ethical thesis survives the revision objection.
- **§9.** The grid applied: Dennett [+D +F +S +R], Sartre [+F +S +R],
  Schechtman [+D +F +S ±R], Strawson [−D −F −S −R]. Against Schechtman's
  claim that unarticulated parts of one's narrative are "only partially
  his": every studied recall alters the memory (citing McCrone 2003 and
  Debiec, LeDoux and Nader 2002), so self-narration drifts from the truth.
  Psychotherapy needs "an assorted basketful of understandings, not a
  narrative".
- **§§10–11.** A "stand off" with those who say he is unconsciously
  Narrative. Heidegger's claim that self-understanding is constitutive is
  false as a universal claim. Montaigne, an Episodic with a bad memory,
  was "better at friendship than at anything else". Diachronicity is not
  necessary for morality or responsibility.

## Concepts

- **psychological Narrativity thesis** — people typically experience their
  lives as a narrative.
- **ethical Narrativity thesis** — doing so is essential to a good life
  and to full personhood.
- **Diachronic [D] / Episodic [E]** — one does / does not figure oneself,
  considered as a self, as having been there in the further past and
  going to be there in the further future.
- **I\*** — "that which I now experience myself to be when I'm
  apprehending myself specifically as an inner mental presence or self".
- **form-finding [F]** — a tendency to seek large-scale unity or pattern
  in one's life.
- **story-telling [S]** — a form of form-finding that fits one's life to a
  recognised narrative genre, not necessarily falsifying.
- **revision [R]** — non-conscious falsification in one's apprehension of
  one's life; "charged" when moved by moral emotions such as pride or
  shame.

## Connections

- **Dennett, "The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity"** ([LIT-442](../literature.d/LIT-442.md),
  [NOTE-364](NOTE-364.md)). Strawson quotes Dennett 1988 (TLS, "Why everyone is a
  novelist", p. 1029) for the "virtuoso novelists" passage, and Dennett
  1991, p. 418 (n. 43) for "concocting and controlling
  the story we tell others – and ourselves – about who we are". The 1988
  piece is, per [LIT-442](../literature.d/LIT-442.md), a later version of the essay held here. Against
  Strawson's grid: [NOTE-364](NOTE-364.md) reads Dennett's self as an abstractum, a
  legitimate posit like a centre of gravity, and his Updike case as making
  a self more determinate, not falsifying it. So Dennett's [+R] is
  Strawson's attribution, resting on the 1991 "concocting". Strawson also
  notes that Dennett's story is "about who we are, rather than about our
  lives" (n. 43), which the essay bears out.
- **Schechtman, *The Constitution of Selves*** ([LIT-468](../literature.d/LIT-468.md), Deferred).
  This paper is the only route by which the record has her words. It
  quotes pp. 93, 96, 117 and 119 and grants that p. 96 is a strong form
  with "usefully weaker forms". It cites the book as 1997; the catalogue
  says 1996.
- **Gallagher** ([LIT-453](../literature.d/LIT-453.md), [NOTE-377](NOTE-377.md)). Gallagher's review
  reports Strawson's earlier minimal self, a "bare locus of
  consciousness" lasting as long as a period of experience. This paper is
  that view's phenomenology and ethics. Gallagher's own extended narrative
  self, "the sum total of its narratives" including "self-deceptions",
  builds revision into the self, which is the [+R] Strawson counts
  against narrative views.
- **Frankfurt and Velleman** ([LIT-293](../literature.d/LIT-293.md), [NOTE-264](NOTE-264.md); [LIT-292](../literature.d/LIT-292.md), [NOTE-268](NOTE-268.md)).
  Strawson's Episodic still cares about "how I am now" and his "opinion"
  (Shaftesbury's word: outlook, character, moral identity). That is
  identification with present motives rather than with a history, the
  shape of Frankfurt's account, not of Schechtman's. The pairing is mine.
- **Parfit and Hume**, filed in parallel. The Episodic's "twenty
  successive selfs" is a lived version of what the SEP map
  ([LIT-454](../literature.d/LIT-454.md)) reports as Parfit's view that identity is not what
  matters. Strawson does not argue from Parfit.

## Bearing on the record

- **The self cluster.** The record held the narrative self ([LIT-442](../literature.d/LIT-442.md)) and
  self-models ([LIT-432](../literature.d/LIT-432.md)) without an objection to either. This paper is the
  objection to the first. It leaves the second untouched: Minsky's
  self-models are tools a mind uses, not a story it must live by.
- **Machines.** Not discussed. A language model that has no memory across
  conversations is, in Strawson's terms, Episodic by construction; whether
  that tells against its being a self depends on which side of this paper
  one takes. The application is mine, and it says nothing about
  consciousness ([THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md) is not engaged).
- **No ML instruction.** Nothing here belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- **The evidence is the author.** The Episodic type is established by
  self-report and by classifying writers from their writings; no
  psychological measure is offered.
- **The metaphysics is elsewhere.** That the remoter past did not happen
  to me* is called a "fact about what I am" and referred to his earlier
  papers.
- **Attributions are by quotation.** The grid placements of Dennett and
  Schechtman are built from short quotations; Strawson himself flags
  weaker forms of Schechtman's view.
- **A missing reference.** Note 43 cites Dennett "1991, p. 418", but the
  reference list has only Dennett 1988. Gallagher's review ([LIT-453](../literature.d/LIT-453.md))
  cites *Consciousness Explained* (1991) p. 418 for a neighbouring
  sentence, so that is probably the work; I did not check.
- **The memory-alteration claim is stretched.** Reconsolidation findings
  about recalled memories are extended to "accurate self-understanding"
  without argument for the extension.

## Open questions

- Is the Episodic/Diachronic difference a difference in self-experience
  or in how people describe it? The paper gives no way to tell.
- If revision is common (§8), does Dennett's fiction-making self describe
  most people better than Schechtman's accurate narrator? The paper's
  grid allows the question but does not ask it.
