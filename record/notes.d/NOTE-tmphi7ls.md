# NOTE-tmphi7ls: Second-Person Engagement, Self-Alienation, and Group-Identification

## Contribution

It recovers an argument of Husserl's that Heidegger overlooked: empathy
matters for the "we" not only because it lets me understand the other but
because it changes how I understand myself. Zahavi makes that argument
the basis of a systematic claim. Second-person engagement is a condition
of a we-identity, because only through it do I come to apprehend myself
as others do, and so as one of us. He defends the claim against two
rivals that take the "we" to be prior to any I–you relation.

## Key insight

To say "we" I must see myself from outside, as one self among others, and
I get that view by taking over another's view of me. Husserl: when I take
over "the apprehension that others have of me", I "fit myself into the
family of man ... It is only now that I am, in the proper sense, an Ego
over against an other and can then say 'we'" (p. 10, quoting Husserl
1989: 254). The resulting "we" keeps the plurality it unites: "A we, a
first-person plural, is not an enlarged I. The social unification and
integration of the involved subjects never amounts to fusion" (p. 10).

## Assumptions

- **Phenomenological empathy.** Empathy is a perceptually based, direct
  experience of another's mind, not inner imitation or projection, and it
  "required a preservation of the self-other difference" rather than
  "leading to fusion" (p. 1). The rest of the argument builds on this.
- **The "we" is experienced from within.** Group identification is
  "phenomenologically real" (Abrams and Hogg, as quoted, p. 11).
  Belonging to a category by birth or by outsiders' classification is
  "not of much relevance" to a we-identity unless one experiences it
  "from within" (pp. 11–12).
- **Reading Husserl's manuscripts.** The argument rests on Husserl's
  texts *Gemeingeist I* (1921) and *Phänomenologie der
  Mitteilungsgemeinschaft* (1932) in Husserliana 14 and 15, and on *Ideen
  II*. It is offered as both exegesis and a systematic position (p. 2).
- **Minimal self-awareness.** Infants have self-awareness from very early,
  on some views from their first experiences (Zahavi 2005). This is used
  against Schmid (p. 15).

## Key results

- **The division in phenomenology (pp. 1–2).** Should the foundations of
  sociality be sought in the face-to-face encounter and the self–other
  difference (Husserl, Scheler, Stein, Walther, Schutz), or in an everyday
  being-with marked by "anonymity and substitutability", among others from
  whom "one mostly does not distinguish oneself" (Heidegger 1996: 111)?
- **Heidegger's objection (§1, pp. 2–4).** Dasein is being-with from the
  start, so there is no gap for empathy to bridge. The I–Thou relation
  presupposes the with-one-another, and "even the self-comprehension of an
  I ... arise[s] only on the basis of the with-one-another" (Heidegger
  2001, as quoted, p. 4).
- **Husserl's ladder of reciprocity (§2, pp. 5–7).**
  - Simultaneous empathy: A experiences B and B experiences A; neither is
    aware of the other's attention.
  - Reflexive empathy: A experiences B attending to A; B is unaware of
    this.
  - Reciprocal empathy: each is aware of being attended to by the other.
  - The I–Thou relation: reciprocal empathy plus communicative address,
    "the intention and will to intimate" (Husserl 1973c: 473, as quoted,
    p. 6). Only this is the primordial social relation and a condition of
    communalization.
- **The we-synthesis (pp. 7–8).** In communicative acts each motivates
  the other, intentions interlock (*Willensverflechtung*), and "a unity of
  willing is established that encompasses both subjects" (p. 8).
- **Second-person engagement defined (§3, pp. 8–9).** Against Pauen, who
  collapses it into simulation, and beyond Schilbach et al., who stress
  interaction: it is "a subject-subject (you-me) relation where I am aware
  of and directed at the other and, at the same time, implicitly aware of
  myself in the accusative, as attended to or addressed by the other"
  (p. 9). It is a form of "interpersonal self-consciousness" and is not
  reducible to first- plus third-person perspectives.
- **Persons are socially constituted (pp. 9–10).** For Husserl,
  personality "is constituted only as the subject enters into social
  relations with others" (Husserl 1973b: 175, as quoted, p. 10). My being
  a person results from "communicative intertwinement".
- **The formal condition of a we (pp. 10–11).** A we must preserve
  plurality, yet the self–other difference cannot stay too salient or no
  unity forms. Husserl's solution: one can group-identify "only ... if the
  difference between self and others is present but somewhat downplayed"
  (p. 10), and this happens when one adopts the other's perspective on
  oneself. This socially mediated self-apprehension is not innate. Husserl
  calls it self-alienation (*Selbstentfremdung*), meaning a self-experience
  "enriched and matured through the incorporation of an external
  perspective" (p. 11).
- **Necessary, not sufficient (p. 11).** Self-alienation does not explain
  why one identifies with philosophers, EU-supporters, Danes or one's
  family. That needs "more substantive elements such as shared norms,
  values, emotions, goals etc.".
- **The parallel with self-categorization theory (pp. 11–12).** Turner's
  self-stereotyping downplays individual differences and accentuates
  in-group similarities "such that social uniformity is generated", which
  Turner calls depersonalization. "The similarity to Husserl's proposal
  should be obvious" (p. 12). The difference is that Husserl attends far
  more to the interpersonal understanding beneath group identification.
- **Schmid's rival (§4, pp. 12–13).** The "sense of us" is a fundamental
  explanans, prior to any I–you relation. Identification merely confirms
  it after the fact. Communication presupposes it. Singular self-awareness
  presupposes plural self-awareness, since small children do not separate
  their goals from others'.
- **Zahavi's replies (pp. 13–15).**
  - Explicit identification is often after the fact, but earlier,
    developmental identifying-with is not (Hobson: the assimilation of the
    other's orientation toward self).
  - Taking the "we" as primitive abandons analysing it, and erases the
    difference between standing in any social relation, "commanding,
    hostile, abusive or dismissive", and being part of a we (p. 14).
  - Joint attention (proto-declaratives) shows infants separate their
    focus from others'. In-group bias and conformity appear at about 3–4
    years; mirror self-recognition at 18–24 months; on other definitions
    self-awareness is present far earlier (p. 15).
- **The concession (§5, pp. 16–17).** The face-to-face "we" is
  "fairly restrictive". There are "sedimented, institutionalized and
  anonymous forms of we", from the bus queue to the nation, and "it is by
  no means obvious" that all can be analysed the same way (Lickel et al.'s
  four group types; Tomasello's second-personal versus collective
  intentionality). An account of the dyadic "we" alone "is insufficient"
  (p. 17).

## Concepts

- **second-person engagement**: a reciprocal you–me relation in which I am
  directed at the other and implicitly aware of myself in the accusative,
  as addressed by them (p. 9).
- **self-alienation** (Husserl's *Selbstentfremdung*): experiencing
  oneself through another's eyes, by taking over their apprehension of
  oneself; for Husserl an enrichment, not a loss (p. 11).
- **simultaneous, reflexive and reciprocal empathy; I–Thou relation**: the
  four steps of Husserl's ladder of reciprocity (p. 7).
- **we-synthesis**: the unity of willing formed when two subjects'
  intentions interlock in communication (pp. 7–8).
- **plural self-awareness** (Schmid): a primitive "sense of us", prior to
  any I–you distinction (p. 13).

## Connections

- **León (NOTE-tmpuba7m; THEORY-tmpsm03n).** León criticizes this paper
  for taking the "we" to require "social uniformity" and for pairing
  depersonalization with self-alienation. The reading confirms both: the
  phrase is Zahavi's gloss on Turner, and he calls the similarity
  obvious. But Zahavi's own condition is weaker than interchangeability:
  difference "present but somewhat downplayed", plurality preserved, no
  fusion. And it is built on the dyadic I–Thou relation, the kind of case
  León uses. Zahavi also flags that intimacy groups and social categories
  may need different analyses (p. 16). So the disagreement is narrower
  than León presents it. That is an inference.
- **Turner et al. 1994 (NOTE-tmpf9mzh).** León's fn. 11 notes that Turner
  et al. have the perceiver appraise self "in relation to others, not from
  the perspective of others". Zahavi's self-alienation is exactly
  appraisal from the perspective of others. So the parallel he draws to
  self-categorization theory (THEORY-tmpo85ke) is looser than "obvious":
  the two accounts reach downplayed difference by different routes.
- **Salice and Miyazono (NOTE-tmpaso81).** Their team reasoner has
  undergone self-transformation. Zahavi's self-alienation is a
  phenomenological account of one such transformation, of the
  self-apprehension rather than of the reasoning.
- **Group entity versus category (TERM-tmpko4rw).** Zahavi adds a third
  line: belonging to a category by birth or by others' classification
  versus a "we" "experienced from within" (p. 12). He also separates
  standing in a social relation from being part of a we (p. 14).
- **Batson (CLAIM-tmpybc4k).** The phenomenologists' empathy preserves
  the self–other difference and does not lead to fusion (p. 1). That is a
  conceptual cousin of Batson's empathy without merging. Zahavi does not
  discuss helping or motivation.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** The thesis's "What it does not
  say" places Zahavi among those who ask whether a "we" is a self at all.
  His answer is no, in one sense. These are inferences; the paper says
  nothing about selflessness, interest or motivation.
  - **The we is not a larger self.** "A we ... is not an enlarged I" and
    never "fusion" (p. 10). If the thesis needs the we to be a self whose
    good the agent pursues, Zahavi denies the premise. The thesis already
    says the extended self is not a group subject but the individual's
    self-identification. Zahavi gives that a precise form: what changes in
    group identification is the individual's self-apprehension.
  - **It is a changed self, not an enlarged one.** The individual comes to
    see herself as others see her, as one self among others. That is a
    "transformation or modulation" of self-experience (p. 11), closer to
    the claim's own worry that depersonalization is a change of self than
    to "larger self". Identity fusion (NOTE-tmp3g9n4) stays the record's
    case for "larger".
  - **Even the personal self is socially made.** If personhood is
    constituted through "communicative intertwinement" (p. 10), the
    contrast between a personal self and an extended one is not the
    contrast between an unsocial and a social self. That weakens any
    version of the thesis that treats the personal self as the baseline
    that then extends.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** Zahavi offers a
  criterion of group identification that is not caring: taking over the
  other's perspective on oneself in second-person engagement. But he calls
  it formal and not sufficient, and particular identifications need
  "shared norms, values, emotions, goals" (p. 11), which brings caring
  back. It is not measurable as stated. It does not supply the
  independent criterion the objection asks for.
- **Batson's objection (CLAIM-tmpybc4k).** Conceptual support only: the
  phenomenological tradition takes empathy to keep self and other
  distinct. It is not evidence about helping.
- **QUESTION-tmprbq0b.** It names this paper for how one comes to see
  oneself as one of us. The answer read here is: through being addressed
  and taking over the addresser's view of oneself, for the face-to-face
  case only.
- **Dated note.** Proposed on CLAIM-tmpjylq8 (Section 3 of the draft).
  No THEORY is filed: the account is exegetical and conceptual, and the

## Limitations

- **No data.** Developmental findings are cited, not analysed.
- **Scope.** The account covers the face-to-face, dyadic "we", by the
  author's own concession (pp. 16–17). Most of what the record's
  social-identity material concerns, nations, fanbases, crowds, is the
  anonymous kind it does not reach.
- **Exegesis and position run together.** It is often unclear whether a
  claim is Husserl's or Zahavi's, for instance the necessity of
  self-alienation.
- **The self-categorization comparison is thin.** It rests on Turner 1981
  and on Abrams and Hogg, and does not engage the 1987 or 1994 statements
  (cf. NOTE-tmpf9mzh).
- **Accepted manuscript.** Not compared with the published text; wording

## Open questions

- Does the account extend to anonymous "we"s, or do they need another
  route, as Zahavi suspects? Brinck, Reddy and Zahavi (2017) and Zahavi
  2017b are cited for this and are not held.
- Can self-alienation be measured? A study relating how far a person
  takes over others' view of herself (meta-perception of the in-group's
  view of her) to self-stereotyping and to identification would test
  whether it and depersonalization are one process or two.
- Is the downplayed difference of the I–Thou "we" the same as León's
  mutual individualization, or its opposite? Zahavi's account seems
  compatible with both, and neither paper decides it.
