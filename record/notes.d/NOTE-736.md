# NOTE-736: Strawson — Intention and Convention in Speech Acts

## Contribution

The paper asks why Austin calls every illocutionary act conventional, and
answers that he is half right. One class of illocutions, the
non-conventional ones, is explained by an amended version of Grice's
analysis of speaker meaning. Another class exists only within rule-governed
procedures. After it, the intention-based account of illocutionary force
has a definite form, with stated limits.

## Key insight

The force of an utterance is what the speaker wholly overtly intends the
audience to understand him to be doing. Conventions come in for two
reasons: as a means of making that intention clear, and, for verdicts and
umpires' calls, as a procedure that gives the intention its effect. The
first is optional. The second is constitutive, but only for one end of a
scale.

## Assumptions

- **Serious use only.** "Etiolated" and "parasitical" uses are set aside.
- **Austin's meaning and locutionary act are taken as given.** Meaning
  "always embodies some limitation on its possible force". Sometimes it
  exhausts the force, as with "I apologize"; often it does not.
- **Standard cases.** For institutional acts, "the participants in these
  procedures know the rules and their roles, and are trying to play the
  game and not wreck it".
- **Necessary, not sufficient.** The amended analysis is offered as
  necessary conditions. Strawson grants that "the way seems open to a
  regressive series of intentions that intentions should be recognized".

## Key results

- **The counterexamples to Austin (pp. 443–445).**
  - Introducing ("This is Mr. Smith") and surrendering ("Kamerad!") are
    conventional, as are the foreman's "guilty" and the umpire's "out".
  - A skater told "The ice over there is very thin" can be warned
    "without its being the case that there is any statable convention at
    all".
  - What makes "Don't go" an entreaty is "something—complex enough, no
    doubt—relating to X's situation, attitude to Y, manner, and current
    intention".
  - An objection in debate: "where is the convention that constitutes it
    an objection?"
  - "Some illocutionary acts are conventional; others are not (except in
    so far as they are locutionary acts)."
- **Grice amended (pp. 446–448).** S means something by x if S intends
  (i1) to produce a response r in A, (i2) that A recognise (i1), and (i3)
  that this recognition be part of A's reason for r. In the watcher case,
  S plants evidence knowing A watches him, and A does not know S knows.
  All three hold, but S is not trying to "let him know". So add (i4):
  that A recognise (i2).
- **Understanding and uptake (pp. 448–449).** For A to understand an
  utterance correctly, A must take S to have the complex intention S has.
  So (i2) and (i4) must be fulfilled, not (i1) or (i3). This is
  identified, tentatively, with Austin's uptake. After Hart's objections
  (a will no one reads, a gift no one knows of), "the aim, if not the
  achievement, of securing uptake is essentially a standard, if not an
  invariable, element in the performance of the illocutionary act".
- **The performative formula explained (pp. 449–451).** The speaker has an
  agent's authority about his intention, and "a motive, inseparable from
  the nature of his act, for making that intention clear". "That was a
  warning" is part of the act of warning. The explicit performative "make[s]
  explicit the type of communication intention with which the speaker
  speaks".
- **Exclusions (pp. 451–454).**
  - Showing off: "recognition of the intention might militate against
    securing the effect".
  - Further effects: informing you that p in order to change your conduct
    does not make the act a different illocution. Warning differs from
    informing because being put on guard is part of the primary response.
  - Insinuating: "The intention one has in insinuating is essentially
    nonavowable."
- **Request, entreaty, order (pp. 454–455).** The same words, "Don't go",
  with the same primary intention.
  - Entreaty is "trying to secure the primary response not merely through
    audience recognition of the intention to secure it, but through
    audience recognition of a complex attitude of which this primary
    intention forms an integral part".
  - An order adds an intended recognition that the utterance is "issued in
    a certain social context such that certain social rules or
    conventions apply", with consequences if it is not obeyed. Here "the
    scheme has to be extended to make room for explicit reference to
    social convention. It can, with some strain, be so extended."
- **Institutional acts (pp. 455–458).** An umpire, a jury, a judge
  pronouncing sentence, a bridge redouble, a marriage. The intended
  response drops out, and "it is not even possible, in other than a
  formal sense, to isolate … a particular audience". "Such acts could have
  no existence outside the rule- or convention-governed practices and
  procedures of which they essentially form parts." A player who lets slip
  "redouble" has redoubled: "Forms can take charge, in the absence of
  appropriate intention; but when they do, the case is essentially deviant
  or nonstandard."
- **The two ends compared (pp. 457–458).** For a non-conventional act,
  "the act of communication is performed if uptake is secured", but its
  overt intention can be frustrated without breach of any rule: "Whether
  it is effective or not is something that rests with his audience." For
  a convention-governed act, once uptake is secured any frustration "must
  be attributable to a breach of rule or convention".
- **The common element (pp. 458–459).** "The illocutionary force of an
  utterance is essentially something that is intended to be understood.
  And the understanding of the force of an utterance in all cases involves
  recognizing what may be called broadly an audience-directed intention and
  recognizing it as wholly overt, as intended to be recognized."
- **Intermediate cases (p. 460).** An official who gives advice "in a
  'take it or leave it' spirit", or speaks as "the mouthpiece, merely, of
  another agency". "We can scarcely expect a general account of linguistic
  communication to yield more than schematic outlines".

## Concepts

- **Illocutionary force.** As Austin uses it: to know it is "to know what
  illocutionary act, if any, was actually performed in issuing" the
  utterance.
- **Uptake.** Austin's "bringing about the understanding of the meaning
  and of the force of the locution", read here as the audience taking the
  speaker to have his overt intention.
- **Wholly overt (avowable) intention.** An intention meant to be
  recognised, and meant to be recognised as meant to be recognised.
  Showing off and insinuating lack it.
- **Essentially conventional act.** One that "could have no existence
  outside the rule- or convention-governed practices" it belongs to.
- **Primary response.** The effect the overt intention aims at. Further
  effects pursued by means of it do not fix the act.

## Connections

The paper reads Austin's *How to Do Things with Words* (1962, [LIT-785](../literature.d/LIT-785.md),
held here unread) and amends Grice's "Meaning" (1957, [LIT-1042](../literature.d/LIT-1042.md)). It
thanks Hart for the objections about wills and gifts and McGuinness for
the case of inviting someone to be impressed. Bach and Harnish's
*Linguistic Communication and Speech Acts* (1979) builds its taxonomy
on the distinction drawn here between communicative and conventional
illocutions. Searle's status functions ([LIT-482](../literature.d/LIT-482.md)) take up the conventional
end of the scale.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md) is confirmed in substance, with one correction.**
  - **Confirmed.** The paper individuates non-conventional illocutions by
    the speaker's overt intention. It does so for request against entreaty
    with the same words, "Don't go". That is the form of [CASE-018](../cases.d/CASE-018.md), where
    the words stay fixed and the act changes. It concedes the
    institutional acts to convention, as [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md) concedes the judge's
    reprimand to Searle ([LIT-482](../literature.d/LIT-482.md)).
  - **Corrected.** On Strawson the speaker's states are not "alone", as
    [CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md)'s defeat condition requires. The act of communication "is
    performed if uptake is secured", so the audience's recognition is a
    condition of performance, not only evidence. And the order's force
    needs an intended recognition of a social context with rules. That is
    the reply [CLAIM-221](../claims.d/CLAIM-221.md) calls missing from the record: the relation to the
    audience enters the act through the content of the intention. It is
    now held, in Strawson's own text.
- **For [CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md).** The paper supplies a three-way division:
  - acts individuated by overt intention alone (warning, entreaty);
  - acts whose overt intention must refer to social rules and standing
    (orders, "with some strain");
  - acts constituted by procedure (verdicts).
  Reproach by a parent sits in the middle class, where standing enters
  through the content of the intention. So whether [CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md) holds for
  reproach turns on whether that content counts as a relation. Strawson
  does not decide that. For teasing he says nothing.
- **For [QUESTION-073](../questions.d/QUESTION-073.md).** It asks for a case in which the intention is held
  fixed and the act changes with standing or uptake. Strawson's scheme
  allows only one such case, the institutional slip ("redouble" said by
  mistake), and calls it "essentially deviant or nonstandard". For
  non-conventional acts the scheme predicts no such case. [CASE-069](../cases.d/CASE-069.md)
  (Labov's sounding) would be a counterexample to that prediction if its
  attributed aim is accepted as the speaker's. The prediction gives the
  question a definite rival to test.
- **Austin's uptake.** [QUESTION-073](../questions.d/QUESTION-073.md) names Austin's uptake as a reply to
  intention-based accounts. Strawson absorbs it into the intention-based
  account, and weakens it after Hart's objections to an aim, not an
  achievement. So uptake does not separate the two sides as cleanly as
  [QUESTION-073](../questions.d/QUESTION-073.md) suggests.
