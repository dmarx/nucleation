# CLAIM-tmpgh3v2: Where a slowly revised governing level (a statute, an objective, a classification) constrains a fast process, the fast process can change the world before the slow level can be revised, so those it harms have no route to the rule as fast as its application reaches them, and the rule dominates them without anyone actively intervening; where the slow level is open to revision on demand, the same separation of timescales keeps the fast process accountable instead

## The claim

A33 §4 of the exchange
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b):
eligibility decisions are made fast; the rules governing them change
slowly, and the statute more slowly still. "From the standpoint of a
caseworker making decisions throughout the day, those higher-level
structures function as constraints." Then:

> The slow level doesn't have to respond quickly to exercise enormous
> power. A statute, algorithmic objective, operating procedure, or
> administrative classification can remain relatively stable while
> governing millions of rapid decisions.

"**A slow variable can govern a fast process, while the fast process
changes the world before the slow variable can be revised.**" "The slow
level possesses enduring authority over the fast level. But affected
people may lack a sufficiently fast route to change the slow level when
its application becomes harmful." Hence "temporal domination without
constant active intervention by a controller": the rule, once set, needs
no one to act for it to keep producing consequences.

**The other side** (A33 §8). "Timescale separation can be
the basis of effective constraint rather than its destruction." An AI
agent's permissions, transaction limits and budget, set beforehand,
constrain fast execution without real-time review: "procedural friction
can be selectively *compiled into enforceable operating constraints*".
The distinction is "between **slow review after action** and **slowly
established rules that constrain action before it happens**". The
condition: "the slower governing process must itself remain open to
legitimate revision. Otherwise, historically contingent decisions become
entrenched as seemingly natural limits on what anyone may do."

## How it sits with the record

- **CLAIM-478**: where some degrees of freedom settle fast relative to
  others, the relation they settle into is a constraint over the window
  between the two timescales. The claim reads a governing rule as such a
  constraint and asks who can revise it within the window. It rests on
  CLAIM-478, which is why it is supposed: the dynamical claim is not shown
  to hold of rules.
- **CLAIM-423** (the owner's): components at different speeds differ in
  which forms of influence they can accumulate and keep, and so in which
  holds authority over others. This is one case: the slow level holds
  authority because those it harms cannot reach it at its speed of
  application.
- **TERM-124** defines domination as a capacity, "used or not"; the claim's
  domination needs no use beyond the rule's standing operation.
- A33 §4 also says automation "accelerated one timescale without
  proportionately increasing another's capacity", so errors are produced
  faster than they are found; that is CLAIM-462's action outrunning
  correction, recorded there as a note. CLAIM-tmph28l6 is the same
  asymmetry stated as cost. The owner's CLAIM-tmp11xvc is what compiled
  constraints preserve: points at which the process stays open.
- CASE-tmpdn372 proposes the study that would separate the two clauses.

## What it does not say

- Not that faster processes defeat slower ones. A33 §8: "It's worth
  resisting the idea that faster processes inevitably defeat slower ones."
- Not that slow rules are illegitimate. Its first clause is a hazard of the
  arrangement, its second the remedy, and which holds depends on whether
  the slow level can be reached.
