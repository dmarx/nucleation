# NOTE-tmpieo7h: Fricker — Epistemic Injustice and a Role for Virtue in the Politics of Knowing

## Contribution

The paper that introduces testimonial injustice. It comes to the concept
from the epistemology of testimony. A hearer's spontaneous critical
reception of testimony is explained by a socially trained sensibility, on
the model of an ethical sensibility. Since that sensibility must use
social stereotypes, prejudice can enter it, and prejudice is the source
of the injustice. It then names the virtue that corrects it, and argues
that the virtue's availability is historically contingent.

## Key insight

Credibility judgements are perceptual and socially trained, not
inferred. That is why prejudice in them is so hard to see and so
ordinary. A hearer can wrong a speaker while sincerely trying to judge
well, and whether he is to blame depends on whether the concepts he would
need to catch himself exist yet.

## Assumptions

- **Testimonial knowledge needs critical reception.** The uncritical
  model is "justificationally lax", and the inferential model is
  "intellectualism in a tight corner" (pp. 2–3).
- **McDowell's "doxastic responsibility"**, a responsiveness to reasons
  that need not be inference, is taken as the target to explain (pp.
  4–6).
- **Zagzebski's definition of virtue**: a motivational component plus
  reliable success (p. 21).
- **No exact credibility ratio.** "There is no such precise probability
  available at all" (n. 11).

## Key results

- **Testimonial sensibility (pp. 7–12).** Ethical sensibility is a
  rational capacity that "comprises virtues; which is inculcated in the
  subject through a process of socialization; and which permits of
  ongoing correction". Its testimonial analogue is "learned and
  internalised through a process of epistemic socialization". It is
  informed by "common cultural stereotypes of intellectual authority",
  which are "positively needed to oil the mechanism" and "fertile ground
  for prejudice". Where stereotypes are "empirically unfounded", the
  sensibility "will be both epistemically and ethically defective".
- **Testimonial injustice (introduction, pp. 12–13).** "A speaker
  receives the wrong degree of credibility from his hearer owing to a
  certain sort of unintended prejudice on the hearer's part." Prejudice
  can produce "credibility excess" or "credibility deficit". More usually
  it "will surreptitiously raise or lower the hearer's degree of belief",
  rather than flip belief.
- **The cases (pp. 14–18).** Tom Robinson's jury "really do find him
  guilty", so their failure is "at once … a gross epistemic failure and an
  appalling ethical failure", and it is culpable. Greenleaf's failure
  with Marge ("there's female intuition, and then there are facts") is
  offered as plausibly non-culpable. Ripley "cynically exploits the
  gender attitudes of the day".
- **Reflexivity (pp. 18–19).** The hearers fail to adjust for the
  difference made "not only that he is black but equally that they are
  white". The virtue is "Reflexive Critical Openness": the hearer "reliably
  succeeds in correcting for the way testimonial performance can be
  prejudiced by the inter-relation of the hearer's social identity and the
  speaker's social identity".
- **Historicism (pp. 20–22).** "A setting in which there is little
  critical awareness of gender is a setting in which no one is in a
  position to possess the virtue." Greenleaf was "always at fault" but
  "not culpably at fault until the requisite critical consciousness of
  gender became available to them". The argument goes through Williams's
  "sound deliberative route". The motive to truth is present, but the
  route is missing because "the critical concepts … are genuinely not yet
  available".
- **Oppression (p. 23).** "Where it is systematic then we should
  recognize it as a face of oppression." The virtue's "anti-oppressive
  power remains hostage to the broader social structures".

## Concepts

- **Testimonial sensibility.** The socially trained capacity that governs
  spontaneous, critical uptake of others' word.
- **Credibility excess and deficit.** More or less credibility than the
  speaker rationally deserves.
- **Testimonial injustice.** The wrong degree of credibility owing to
  the hearer's unintended prejudice. The paper treats deficit.
- **Reflexive Critical Openness.** The virtue that corrects for prejudice
  arising from the relation between the two parties' social identities.
  In the book it becomes "testimonial justice".

## Connections

The paper responds to McDowell's "Knowledge by Hearsay", Coady's
*Testimony* and Elizabeth Fricker's anti-reductionism debate. It draws on
MacIntyre, Zagzebski, Hookway, Karen Jones ("distrust their own
distrust") and Williams. Its two cases are reused in the book and in
the 2017 chapter ([NOTE-tmp3a0fd](NOTE-tmp3a0fd.md)), where Ripley's part is reclassified as
"intentional gaslighting".

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-tmplo3ff](../theory.d/THEORY-tmplo3ff.md).** This paper confirms its testimonial definition,
  "prejudice on the hearer's part", and adds "unintended". It speaks of
  stereotypes of social types and of social identity, but does not use
  "identity prejudice". It also allows credibility *excess* as prejudicial
  dysfunction. The précis account is silent on that, and does not need
  it.
- **[QUESTION-072](../questions.d/QUESTION-072.md), part 1.** The paper's model of credibility as a
  trained sensibility fits the question's case. Whoever files is the
  hearer, and the filing habit is a sensibility shaped by what has been
  filed before. Testimonial injustice then requires two things: that the
  habit rest on an unfounded stereotype about whose moves are worth
  filing, and that it cause a deficit for a speaker. The historicism
  result bears on blame, not on whether the wrong occurs. A filer can do
  the injustice before anyone "is in a position to know better".
  [THEORY-tmpa9fyk](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpa9fyk.md) states the condition.
- **Nothing here is ML practice.**

## Limitations

- The cases are literary, and the culpability verdicts are offered as
  interpretations ("on this (perhaps rather charitable) interpretation").
- Credibility excess is named and set aside.
- The paper is the earliest of the three and predates the book's
  terminology, so its definitions are the first versions.

## Open questions

- Whether a sensibility with no prejudice can still produce systematic
  deficits by statistical stereotype that is empirically "founded" but
  self-fulfilling. The paper counts founded stereotypes as legitimate.
