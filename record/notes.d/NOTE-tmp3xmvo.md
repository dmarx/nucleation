# NOTE-tmp3xmvo: Sheldon, Elliot, Kim & Kasser — What is satisfying about satisfying events?

## Contribution

It tests SDT's three needs against seven rivals drawn from Maslow, Epstein,
terror management, hedonism and the "American dream" (physical thriving,
security, self-esteem, self-actualization–meaning, pleasure–stimulation,
money–luxury, popularity–influence), using two criteria: how salient each
is in people's most satisfying events, and how well it predicts the
event's affect. The three SDT needs pass both, in the USA and South Korea,
over three time frames; so does self-esteem, which SDT does not count
(pp. 325–327, 335–337).

## Key insight

A need should be both what people find in their best experiences and what
makes those experiences feel good. Asking people to recall the single most
satisfying event and rate what it contained gives a natural-language window
on needs, as lexical studies did for traits (p. 326). By that window
self-esteem is as fundamental as autonomy, competence and relatedness, and
more predictive than any of them in two of three US samples.

## Assumptions

- **Needs are "necessary inputs" for thriving, not motives**, and are
  identified by the two criteria (pp. 325–327). The second criterion
  assumes "that satisfied needs should promote well-being and
  psychological thriving in the same way that proper fertilization
  promotes the growth of plants" (p. 327).
- **Each candidate is three self-report items** completing "During this
  event I felt ...", rated 1–5 (Table 1). Self-esteem's items are "That I
  had many positive qualities", "Quite satisfied with who I am", "A strong
  sense of self-respect".
- **Affect is the event's PANAS**, positive minus negative as affect balance
  (p. 328).
- **Need preference** (Study 1) is a pairwise-comparison measure of the
  ten, after Oishi et al. 1998 (pp. 328–329). Its autonomy definition reads
  "Autonomy–independence: Feeling like you are the cause of your own
  actions" (Appendix).
- Retrospective recall of one event; university students.

## Key results

### Study 1: most satisfying event of the last month (N = 315 of 322; pp. 327–330)

- A principal-components analysis of the 30 items gave nine factors, not
  ten: pleasure–stimulation and physical thriving loaded together (Table 1).
- Salience (Table 2): self-esteem 4.08, relatedness 3.99, autonomy 3.98,
  competence 3.74, then pleasure–stimulation 3.53 ... money–luxury 2.37.
- Correlations with affect balance (Table 3): self-esteem .43, autonomy
  .34, competence .26, relatedness .23 ... money–luxury −.12.
- Three SDT needs alone on affect balance: autonomy β = .23, competence
  .21, relatedness .14. All ten simultaneously: autonomy .17, competence
  .12, relatedness .12, **self-esteem .28**, money–luxury −.17 (p. 330).
- Need preference did not moderate: the ten product terms ΔR² = .03,
  p = .21; only self-actualization's was significant (p. 330).

### Study 2: last week, USA (N = 152) and South Korea (N = 200) (pp. 330–333)

- Salience (Table 4): US self-esteem 3.65 first; Korea relatedness 3.65
  first and self-esteem 3.23 second; Koreans higher on relatedness,
  security, pleasure and money, lower on self-esteem.
- All ten on affect balance: US autonomy .29, competence .25, relatedness
  .21, nothing else; Korea autonomy .26, relatedness .20, self-esteem .23,
  security .25, money–luxury −.22, competence .10 (ns) (p. 332).
- In Korea competence correlated *positively* with negative affect (.16,
  Table 5).
- Culture × need interactions: none significant, ΔR² = .02, p = .34.

### Study 3: last semester, satisfying and unsatisfying (N = 233; pp. 333–335)

- Satisfying event, salience (Table 6): self-esteem 3.97, autonomy 3.77,
  competence 3.73, relatedness 3.66 ... money–luxury 2.24. All ten on
  affect balance: **self-esteem .38**, autonomy .17, relatedness .12
  (marginal), competence .00, money–luxury −.14.
- Unsatisfying event, "what was missing": competence 3.02 most absent,
  then autonomy 2.86, security 2.77, relatedness and self-esteem 2.63–2.66.
  Missing security correlated .50 with negative affect, the largest
  (Table 7). All ten on affect balance: competence −.22, security −.26,
  self-esteem −.18 (p. 335).

### The authors' conclusions (pp. 335–337)

- "If one were to pick a single need that is most important to satisfy in
  the United States, the current data suggest it would be self-esteem"
  (p. 336).
- "Self-determination theory does not have a way to account for these
  results, except perhaps to consider self-esteem as a well-being outcome
  rather than a predictor. ... it might be imprudent to consign
  self-esteem to the outcome category rather than considering it as a need
  in its own right." Competence and self-esteem "were empirically
  separable". "There may be four fundamental psychological needs, not
  three" (p. 336).
- A two-tier reading: security a "deficiency" need, autonomy and relatedness
  "enhancement" needs, self-esteem and competence at both levels
  (pp. 336–337).

## Concepts

- **Psychological need (here)** — "particular qualities of experience that
  all people require to thrive" (p. 325); "evolved desires" pulling people
  "toward the same general experiences and incentives" (p. 336).
- **Salience criterion** and **affect criterion** — the two tests (pp.
  326–327).
- **Deficiency vs enhancement needs** — after Maslow: what must be met to
  avoid ill-being against what yields well-being (pp. 333, 336–337).

## Connections

- The nine-item autonomy, competence and relatedness subset became Study
  1's measure in Chen et al. 2015 (LIT-tmpzyunx), which criticizes this
  paper for not testing measurement equivalence.
- Chirkov et al. 2003 (LIT-tmporplv) cites it as evidence that event
  autonomy predicts well-being in Korea as in the USA.
- It answers Iyengar and Lepper 1999 (LIT-tmp0rsz4) and Markus et al. 1996
  on autonomy's universality (p. 333).
- Kasser and Ryan 1993, 1996 for the "dark side" of the American dream.

## Bearing on the record

- **The registration and NOTE-440's reply, corrected.** LIT-tmpd3l9z says
  "self-esteem performs strongly. NOTE-440 holds SDT's reply, that
  self-esteem is derivative." The paper's own authors, Kasser and Sheldon
  among SDT's central researchers, considered that reply in the paper and
  rejected it: treating self-esteem as an outcome would be "imprudent",
  and the "preliminary conclusion" is four needs (p. 336). SDT's later
  position (NOTE-440, pp. 5, 7) is a reply to its own authors' 2001
  conclusion, not to an outside critic.
- **QUESTION-tmpee04a: the paper names the question's problem in its own
  case.** Self-esteem can be a need or "a well-being outcome rather than a
  predictor"; the data cannot decide, because the criterion is affect and
  the self-esteem items ("Quite satisfied with who I am") read like
  well-being. The same holds for the three SDT needs, with less
  vividness. The paper's two criteria are both self-reports of the same
  remembered event, so convergence between them is not independent
  confirmation. It is the clearest worked instance in the record of the
  interdefinition NOTE-439 flags.
- **Its own measure of autonomy preference labels autonomy
  "autonomy–independence"** (Appendix), the conflation Chirkov et al.
  (LIT-tmporplv) argue against two years later.
- **LIT-tmpkzr6r and LIT-tmpk1ssy** test beneficence by the frustration
  criterion; this paper's deficiency analysis (Study 3) is an early form of
  that criterion, and on it autonomy and relatedness "did not as strongly
  influence the affect associated with unsatisfying events" (p. 337).
- **THEORY-052**: Study 3's "missing" ratings are an early, event-level
  measure of something like frustration; competence and security, not all
  three SDT needs, carried the unique variance there.
- Filed from it: THEORY-tmp5tsat (Active).
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- Students only; retrospective recall of one event; self-reported
  outcomes; "rare-but-powerful experiences" probably missed (the authors',
  p. 337).
- Three items per candidate; the ten-factor structure failed (nine
  factors), and competence item 3 cross-loaded .44 on self-esteem (fn. 2).
- Self-esteem items overlap in content with positive affect, which inflates
  its affect criterion; the same risk for the other candidates is smaller
  but not zero.
- Many tests; Study 1's salience comparisons at p ≤ .01, but regressions
  not corrected.
- One collectivist culture.

## Open questions

- Does self-esteem predict outcomes that are not affect reports
  (functioning, health), beyond the three needs?
- Can self-esteem be satisfied independently of competence and
  relatedness, or is it, as SDT later argued, their by-product? A design
  that manipulates one and measures the others would say.
- Does the four-need result survive measurement-equivalence testing across
  cultures (Chen et al.'s objection)?
