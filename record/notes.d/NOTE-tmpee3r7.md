# NOTE-tmpee3r7: H-France Forum 19(5) (2024) — on Prest, Public Theatre and the Enslaved People of Colonial Saint-Domingue

## Contribution

Second-hand access to the book's argument and evidence, with Prest's own restatement.

## Key results

1. Prest (#5): "the story of public theatre in Saint-Domingue is inseparable from that of its enslaved people."
2. Row, quoting the book (p. 187): "we can begin to acknowledge the extent to which the metropolitan-inspired theatre tradition in the colony was reliant on the world of enslaved people of African descent".
3. Sabee: Prest shows that the theatre "could not have existed were it not for the enslaved laborers who contributed to its production and performance."
4. Williard, quoting p. 175: "near invisibility in the theatre records belies what was, in all likelihood, a significant presence and contribution".
5. Sabee: beginning in 1775 "(during a financial crisis, Prest notes, underscoring a profit-related motive), women identified as négresses (free women of color with darker skin) were offered access to the theater", at first "restricted to standing in the corridor at the back of the theater".
6. Row, quoting p. 112: blackface "allowed the white population to control theatrical portrayals of black behaviour and to fashion it in ways that they found entertaining and, more importantly, non-threatening".
7. Row, quoting p. 9: "I have attempted to distinguish clearly between different levels of certainty—between what is certain, probable, or possible—and what is informed speculation".
8. Prest (#5): theatre-makers "publicly performed his (or occasionally her) participation in the local slave economy by advertising for the return of his enslaved “property” in the same edition of the local newspaper".

## Assumptions

The reviewers accept Prest's sources and method; Row questions only how far speculation should go.

## Concepts

Mitigated spectatorship; concealed contributors; critical fabulation (Hartman, via Row); archival silence (Trouillot, via Williard).
