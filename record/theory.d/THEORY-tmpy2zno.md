# THEORY-tmpy2zno: Under uncertainty about which beings are sentient, an appeal to precaution does not by itself fix which error is worse to make: precaution on behalf of a possibly sentient being and precaution on behalf of human liberty point opposite ways, and judging which mistake costs more turns on the very science in doubt

## Source

Schwitzgebel & Sinnott-Armstrong (2026), "Sacrificing Humans for Insects and
AI", [LIT-166](../literature.d/LIT-166.md) (*Ethics* 136(3), 670–696), read in full in [NOTE-116](../notes.d/NOTE-116.md) from the
authors' draft of 3 September 2025. The version of record was not reached,
so page numbers are the draft's.

## What was actually shown

A conceptual argument in §2.1, aimed at Birch's *The Edge of Sentience*.

- **The thesis.** "By itself, an appeal to precaution does not specify the
  type of error most to be avoided" (p. 8). "Err on the side of caution" can
  mean protecting a creature that may be sentient, or protecting human
  liberty and practice.
- **Two precautions in conflict.** Mill's harm principle, read as a
  precaution in favour of liberty, puts the burden of proof on whoever would
  restrict liberty. Birch's framework puts it on whoever risks harming a
  sentience candidate. The reviewers "see no reason always to prioritize"
  one over the other (p. 11).
- **Why the choice is not free.** Birch's "risks are not equal" argument
  (Birch p. 17) assumes that a false negative costs more than a false
  positive. Assessing that "turns on the very science at issue", since the
  cost of wrongly denying sentience depends on how much, and what kind of,
  sentience there is.
- **Worked cases.** Fishing, bathrooms and an ant-colony trolley (§2.1).

What could have come out otherwise: a reading of precaution on which one
error is worse whatever the facts about sentience turn out to be. The
reviewers find none in Birch's text, and give a general reason there is none.

## What this does not say

- **That precaution toward animals or AI is wrong.** The argument is that a
  choice of error preference must be made and defended, not that the
  harm-precaution is the wrong choice. The reviewers take no side.
- **That Birch's books say what the review says they say.** The record holds
  the review, not the books. Its page references to Birch are the
  reviewers' ([NOTE-116](../notes.d/NOTE-116.md), limitations).
- **That the review's other charges stand as firmly.** The charges that
  sentience candidature is policed without principle, that citizens' panels
  cannot be both inclusive and deliberative, and that Sebo's live views
  imply two ants over a stranger are rated moderate in the reading. They are
  not part of this account.
- **That there is no principled way to choose.** [NOTE-116](../notes.d/NOTE-116.md) leaves this open:
  is there a non-question-begging way to choose between harm-precaution and
  liberty-precaution when they conflict?
