# CLAIM-tmp0gs0t: Recruiting an emergency regime, maintaining it and dissolving it are separable capacities, and an organization that can enter exceptional authority more cheaply than it can leave it has an asymmetric transition structure in which the regime can outlast its justification

## The claim

A34 §6, on incident command: "The organization must be capable of
forming an emergency regime, maintaining it while necessary, and
dissolving it afterward." Its table: "Recruitment | Corrective processes
cannot acquire authority quickly enough", "Maintenance | Coordination
breaks down during prolonged incidents", "De-escalation | Emergency
governance continues after its justification has disappeared". "An
organization that can enter a protective regime but cannot appropriately
leave it may become chronically organized around emergency demands."

A36 §15 carries it to emergency powers: "A system that can mobilize
extraordinary authority more easily than it can terminate that authority
possesses an asymmetric transition structure", written \(R(C_{\mathrm
{ordinary}},C_{\mathrm{emergency}}) \neq R(C_{\mathrm{emergency}},C_{\mathrm
{ordinary}})\). "This does not establish that a particular emergency
declaration is unjustified."

A34 adds that the incident commander's authority is not competence: "The
incident commander is not necessarily the most technically knowledgeable
participant", which "illustrates the distinction between epistemic
competence and practical jurisdiction".

## What it adds to the record

CLAIM-731 holds that the cost of imposing a decision and the cost of
questioning, suspending or reversing it are separate. This claim applies
the separation to a regime rather than a decision, and adds the middle
capacity, maintenance, as a third that can fail alone. That a person or
organization can come to rely on emergency mobilization was introduced
earlier in the exchange (A22) and is filed by another group; this claim
is about the transitions, not the reliance.

## Gap filled: the asymmetry in the statute

A36 says only that "The National Emergencies Act establishes mechanisms
for declaration, termination, renewal and congressional consideration of
ongoing national emergencies." Its text (LIT-tmpdpc7y, checked on the LII
page on 2026-11-24) shows the asymmetry the claim names. A national
emergency ends if "there is enacted into law a joint resolution
terminating the emergency" or "the President issues a proclamation
terminating the emergency" (§ 1622(a)). Before 1985 Congress could end
one by concurrent resolution; Pub. L. 99-93 "substituted “there is
enacted into law a joint resolution terminating the emergency” for
“Congress terminates the emergency by concurrent resolution”". A joint
resolution goes to the President, so against a President who wants the
emergency to continue, termination needs two-thirds of each House, while
declaration needs the President alone. The 1985 change followed INS v.
Chadha (1983), which struck down legislative vetoes; that link, and the
2019 instance in which both Houses passed a joint resolution to end a
declared emergency and failed to override the veto, are the assistant's
knowledge, not checked here. § 1622(b) also requires each House, every
six months, to "meet to consider a vote on a joint resolution to
determine whether that emergency shall be terminated"; whether it does is
an empirical question for LEAD-tmps9jkd. The section is the assistant's
(Claude, in this session); adopting it is the owner's call.

## Standing

Proposed, in A's voice. The statute shows that the asymmetry exists in
law; it does not show that it makes regimes outlast their justification,
which is what the defeater asks about.
