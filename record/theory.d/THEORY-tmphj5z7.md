# THEORY-tmphj5z7: Large models are a cultural and social technology rather than agents: lossy, uninvertible summaries of human-produced information that, like prices and bureaucratic categories, let it be reorganized at scale

## Source

Farrell, Gopnik, Shalizi and Evans (2025), [LIT-tmpofhpt](../literature.d/LIT-tmpofhpt.md), read in
[NOTE-tmpusmlj](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpusmlj.md) from the authors' accepted version.

## What was actually shown

Nothing was tested. The paper argues by classification and analogy.

- **The classification.** Like writing, print and search, large models
  give access to what other people produced. Like markets, bureaucracies
  and elections (Simon's "artificial systems of human society"), they make
  "lossy (i.e., incomplete, selective, and uninvertible) but useful"
  representations of it that support new operations at scale.
- **The denial of agency.** Agents perceive and act on a changing world,
  revise models of it from evidence and set novel goals. Large models
  sample from an estimated distribution over sequences and have "no
  conception of truth and falsity"; scaling "won't change this". The
  agent-like interface continues the storyteller's use of fictional agents.
- **The one mechanism.** A model fitted to reproduce sequence
  probabilities on average is most accurate on what is common in its data
  and least on what is rare or novel. The authors say this "might" worsen
  cultural homogenization. That the fit is worst on rare cases follows
  from the training objective; the cultural consequence is not shown.

What could have come out otherwise: nothing in the paper. A demonstrated
capacity for evidence-driven revision of a world model in a large model
would have counted against the denial of agency, but the paper does not
look for one.

## What this does not say

- **Not that large models are unimportant or harmless.** The authors say
  the classification raises their likely impact, as print's and markets'
  was large.
- **Not that no artificial system could be an agent.** Robotics and
  possible future systems are set aside; the claim is about large models
  as now built and deployed.
- **Not that the agent description is useless.** The paper says we
  "tend" to think of large-scale technologies as agents and that this
  distracts, but markets and firms are productively modelled as agents in
  economics; the paper does not show that the description misleads more
  for models than for markets.
- **Not a claim about consciousness or selfhood.** It is silent on them.
