# NOTE-949: Slovic — the construction of preference

## Contribution

The paper adds no new experiment. It collects the preference-reversal line
from Slovic and Lichtenstein (1968) to Tversky, Slovic and Kahneman (1990)
into one argument against procedure invariance, the principle that "the
relation of preference should not depend on the description of the options
(description invariance) or on the method of elicitation (procedure
invariance)" (pp. 364–365). It records the economists' attempts to remove
reversals and their failure except under market discipline. It offers
compatibility and prominence as the mechanisms, and concludes that "beliefs
and preferences are often constructed—not merely revealed—in the
elicitation process" (p. 365).

## Key insight

A preference is not read off a stored order. It is computed when asked, and
the computation depends on the question. Asked for a price, a person weights
the dollar dimension, because it shares the response's scale. Asked to
choose, the person weights the most important dimension, because that gives
a good reason. So two procedures that should agree give opposite orderings,
and the disagreement is in the procedures, not in a cycle of preferences.
Slovic's baseball epigraph, from Tversky and Thaler, puts the third view:
"They ain't nothing till I call them" (p. 364).

## Assumptions

- **Procedure invariance as the normative standard.** Preference reversal
  is defined as H ≻ L with C_L > C_H, where H is the high-probability bet,
  L the high-payoff bet and C their cash equivalents. If invariance holds,
  reversal implies an intransitive cycle (pp. 366–367).
- **Scope.** The constructive conception "applies to judgments and choices
  among options that are important, complex, and perhaps unfamiliar, such
  as gambles, jobs, careers, homes, automobiles, surgical treatments, and
  environments" (p. 369). It is not claimed for familiar, simple options.
- **Evidence is between procedures and between groups.** The studies
  compare responses to different procedures, from the same respondents or
  from separate groups (as in the highway problem, p. 368). None reported
  here follows a respondent's preference across time.

## Key results

The numbers are the cited studies', as the paper reports them.

- **The Las Vegas study (Lichtenstein & Slovic 1973, p. 365).** P bet:
  11/12 to win 12 chips, 1/12 to lose 24; $ bet: 2/12 to win 79, 10/12 to
  lose 5; chips 25 cents. The two were chosen about equally often, but the
  $ bet got the higher selling price about 88% of the time, and 87% of those
  who chose the P bet priced the $ bet higher.
- **Persistence (p. 366).** Reversals survived Grether and Plott's (1979)
  13 controls, raised payoffs (Pommerehne et al. 1982), money on the desk
  (Reilly 1982), experimental markets and arbitrage. "Chu and Chu (1990)
  and Cox and Grether (1994) were finally able to eradicate preference
  reversals in market settings characterized by repetition, feedback, and
  harsh penalties for being inconsistent."
- **Diagnosis (Tversky, Slovic & Kahneman 1990, p. 367).** Reversal rates
  of 40% to 50%; "only 10% of preference reversal patterns were
  intransitive, and the remaining 90% violated procedure invariance".
  Overpricing of the L bet accounted for nearly two thirds of the
  patterns.
- **Scale compatibility (Slovic, Griffin & Tversky 1990, p. 367).** "the
  weight of a stimulus attribute in judgment or in choice is enhanced by
  its compatibility with the response mode." Reversals fell from 41% with
  monetary bets to 24% with non-monetary outcomes.
- **Riskless reversals (p. 368).** L = $2,500 in 5 years against
  S = $1,600 in 1.5 years: S chosen 74% of the time, L priced higher 75% of
  the time, reversals above 50%, again mainly from overpricing L.
- **Process data (Schkade & Johnson 1989, p. 368).** More time on payoffs
  in pricing than in choice among respondents who reversed, little
  difference among those who did not.
- **Prominence (Tversky, Sattath & Slovic 1988, p. 368).** Highway safety
  (Table 1): Program X, 500 casualties at $55M; Program Y, 570 at $12M.
  67% chose X. In matching, "Only 4% of the inferred choices favored
  Program X." "In every case, the primary dimension was given more weight
  in choice than in matching."
- **Environment (Irwin et al. 1993, p. 370).** Air quality preferred in
  choice, the computer upgrade valued higher in price: reversals in 41% of
  respondents.

## Concepts

- **Procedure invariance.** Preference does not depend on the method of
  elicitation (p. 364).
- **Preference reversal.** Choosing one option and pricing the other
  higher; a failure of invariance or of transitivity (pp. 366–367).
- **Scale compatibility.** A stimulus attribute weighs more when it is on
  the response's scale, because mapping across scales costs effort and
  because "a response mode tends to focus attention on the compatible
  features of the stimulus" (p. 367).
- **Prominence; strategy compatibility.** The more important attribute
  weighs more in choice, an ordinal procedure, than in matching, a cardinal
  one (pp. 368–369).
- **Construction strategies.** "anchoring and adjustment, relying on the
  prominent dimension, eliminating common elements, discarding nonessential
  differences, adding new attributes into the problem frame in order to
  bolster one alternative" (p. 369).

## Connections

It summarizes the line Fischhoff, Slovic and Lichtenstein's labile values
(1980) opened, which Feldman and Lynch build on ([LIT-1196](../literature.d/LIT-1196.md), read in
[NOTE-957](NOTE-957.md)). It cites Payne, Bettman and Johnson's adaptive decision
maker for construction and Shafir, Simonson and Tversky for reason-based
choice.

**Measurement context and the interpreter's state ([TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md), [CLAIM-127](../claims.d/CLAIM-127.md)).**
The paper's evidence is of the kind [TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md) calls the measurement context
entering the response. Compatibility and prominence are both effects of the
response mode on how attributes are weighted when the answer is computed;
in A129's model that is C entering f_Y. Nothing in the paper shows C
entering the interpreter's state. The studies compare procedures, and none
asks whether the preference constructed under one procedure is carried into
the next. The one sequential design it reports, Slovic (1975), in which
participants "first matched different pairs of options ... and, in a later
session, chose between the matched options" (p. 368), is analysed for the
discrepancy between the two tasks, not for carry-over. So the paper is a
source for the route A129 draws, direct influence of context on the
response, and leaves [TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md)'s open route open. Its baseball epigraph
("They ain't nothing till I call them", p. 364) reads as the stronger
claim, but the review argues only that the response is computed under the
procedure.

**[CLAIM-341](../claims.d/CLAIM-341.md).** The claim cites the paper for preferences "constructed
— not merely revealed — in the process of elicitation". That wording is the
manuscript's abstract. The published abstract says "constructed in the
process of elicitation" (p. 364), and the phrase "not merely revealed"
appears in the body, of "beliefs and preferences" (p. 365). The substance
holds either way. But construction here is synchronic: each elicitation
computes its own answer. That is the form [CLAIM-341](../claims.d/CLAIM-341.md) sets aside, not the
diachronic form it holds. The paper's support for the claim is therefore
indirect: that a judgement is a construction, which is what would make it
able to change the judge. The mechanisms are classical (anchoring and
adjustment, attention to compatible features, reason-giving). The paper
gives no evidence for or against action being part of the loop.

**[CLAIM-342](../claims.d/CLAIM-342.md) and [QUESTION-121](../questions.d/QUESTION-121.md).** Two points bear on what damps the
effect. First, scope: construction is claimed for options that are
"important, complex, and perhaps unfamiliar" (p. 369), and contingent
valuation is "exactly the kind of complex, unfamiliar task in which one
would not expect to find stable, well-articulated pref[erences]" (pp. 369–370). So
familiarity is the implied moderator, as in Feldman and Lynch, without
studies of it here. Second, the only conditions reported to remove
reversals are "repetition, feedback, and harsh penalties for being
inconsistent" in markets (p. 366). That is outcome feedback scored against
a standard the respondent does not set, which fits [CLAIM-342](../claims.d/CLAIM-342.md)'s
independent scoring. It arrives through the consequences of the
respondent's own choices, which is what [CLAIM-339](../claims.d/CLAIM-339.md) would point to.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-341](../claims.d/CLAIM-341.md)**: weak, indirect support. The paper supports the
  synchronic form (each elicitation computes its answer), which the claim
  does not hold, and says nothing about a constructed preference changing
  later judgements. The claim's quotation should be checked against the
  published wording (p. 364 against the manuscript abstract).
- **[TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md) and [CLAIM-127](../claims.d/CLAIM-127.md)**: evidence for C's direct influence on the
  response, the route in A129's graph; no evidence for an arrow into the
  interpreter's state.
- **[CLAIM-342](../claims.d/CLAIM-342.md)**: the one reported remedy, market feedback with
  penalties, is independent scoring delivered through the consequences of
  choice.
- No bearing on contextuality: the reversals are explained classically and
  are differences between procedures, not order effects within one
  respondent.
- It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice; its practical
  section concerns contingent valuation, informed consent and managing
  one's own preferences.

## Limitations

A review of the author's own line, from an award address. It gives the
cited studies' headline numbers without design detail or variances, and
the market results that eradicate reversals get one sentence. "Constructed"
is not operationalized beyond failure of procedure invariance; the paper
does not distinguish a preference that does not exist before elicitation
from one that exists and is reported through a procedure-dependent mapping
(the paper itself notes that "the labels do not
imply that choice represents one's true preference", p. 367).

## Open questions

- Is a preference constructed under one procedure carried into a later
  elicitation under another, so that the order of procedures matters for
  the same respondent? That is the sequential test [TERM-039](../terms.d/TERM-039.md) asks for.
- Do the market conditions that remove reversals work through feedback
  (evidence the respondent did not select) or through the penalty on
  inconsistency (a scoring rule)?
