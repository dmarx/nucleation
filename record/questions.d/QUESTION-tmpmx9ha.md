# QUESTION-tmpmx9ha: What, if anything, do the living owe people who will live in the far future, and does that obligation rest on the good of those people, on an impersonal good, or on what the living themselves value?

## Why it is a question

The legacy question ([QUESTION-tmpruhu4](QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md)) asks why people care about what
comes after them. This one asks what they owe it. The two meet in the
owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)): if concern for far-future people is owed
and acted on, and those people can never be part of anyone's extended self,
the thesis has its hardest case.

Three grounds of obligation compete.

- **An impersonal good.** Longtermism (Greaves and MacAskill, [LIT-tmp1we1q](../literature.d/LIT-tmp1we1q.md);
  MacAskill, [LIT-tmpk6b4i](../literature.d/LIT-tmpk6b4i.md)) holds that the expected size of the future makes
  its welfare dominate the most important present choices. Bostrom
  ([LIT-tmph7mkx](../literature.d/LIT-tmph7mkx.md), [LIT-tmpr74qy](../literature.d/LIT-tmpr74qy.md)) and Ord ([LIT-tmp0ocqn](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0ocqn.md)) make reducing
  existential risk the priority. Ramsey ([LIT-tmpggdgs](../literature.d/LIT-tmpggdgs.md)) refused to discount
  the future merely for being later. On these views what is owed is owed
  to the good, not to anyone's self. The thesis has nothing to say about
  them as views; it bears only on whether anyone is moved by them.
- **The good of future people.** Person-affecting views (Narveson,
  [LIT-tmpoc3e4](../literature.d/LIT-tmpoc3e4.md)) owe nothing to merely possible people. The non-identity
  problem (Parfit, [LIT-tmpnjafv](../literature.d/LIT-tmpnjafv.md); [LIT-469](../literature.d/LIT-469.md)) makes even harms to actual future
  people hard to state, because different choices bring different people
  into existence. The SEP survey (Meyer, [LIT-tmpll0u1](../literature.d/LIT-tmpll0u1.md)) maps the positions.
- **What the living value.** Scheffler ([LIT-tmpbj0k1](../literature.d/LIT-tmpbj0k1.md); [LIT-tmpl4qml](../literature.d/LIT-tmpl4qml.md)) grounds
  concern for future generations partly in our own valuing, attachments
  and stake in the human story going on. That is the ground nearest the
  thesis: the future matters because of what it means to us.

Two further lines keep it open.

- **Whether longtermism can guide action.** Tarsney ([LIT-tmpklw8s](../literature.d/LIT-tmpklw8s.md)) on how
  fast our predictable influence decays, and Thorstad ([LIT-tmp37kk5](../literature.d/LIT-tmp37kk5.md)) on
  whether high existential risk lowers the value of reducing it.
- **Gardiner's storm** ([LIT-tmp12vk2](../literature.d/LIT-tmp12vk2.md)). Each generation gains from deferring
  costs to the next, which cannot retaliate. Self-interest across
  generations points one way and obligation the other. That is the
  thesis's question in its sharpest practical form.

## What would count as an answer

- **On the normative question:** an argument that one ground is correct, or
  that they combine, and what follows for discounting, for population size
  and for existential risk.
- **On the psychology** (the thesis's part):
  - **How far concern reaches.** Lay judgement does not treat extinction as
    uniquely bad unless the lost future is made salient (Schubert et al.,
    [LIT-tmpk72ci](../literature.d/LIT-tmpk72ci.md)). It counts adding happy people as good, against the
    person-affecting view (Caviola et al., [LIT-tmpka2bf](../literature.d/LIT-tmpka2bf.md)).
  - **What predicts it.** Felt responsibility to future generations
    predicts pro-environmental concern (Syropoulos and Markowitz,
    [LIT-tmpi3189](../literature.d/LIT-tmpi3189.md)), as legacy motivation does (Wade-Benzoni, [LIT-tmpcpdlk](../literature.d/LIT-tmpcpdlk.md);
    Zaval et al., [LIT-tmpetcju](../literature.d/LIT-tmpetcju.md)).
  - **The thesis's test.** Whether such concern varies with a measured
    continuity between the self and the future, apart from caring. That is
    [CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)'s test applied across generations.

## Note of 2026-10-29: eight works read

Eight works were read, three from the authors' working papers. They narrow
the question in four ways.

- **What the impersonal ground needs** (Greaves and MacAskill,
  [NOTE-tmpozxwf](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpozxwf.md)). Expected value taken down to small probabilities, and a
  zero pure rate of time preference, which they call "near-essential".
  Against person-affecting views their case falls back on shaping
  artificial intelligence, where a large future population exists either
  way. They say nothing of motivation: their agent is "a rational
  altruistic actor".
- **Whether it can guide action** (Tarsney, [NOTE-tmp0l9dk](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp0l9dk.md); Thorstad,
  [NOTE-tmpu4i35](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpu4i35.md)). Neither defeats longtermism, but both shift weight to
  the near term.
  - Tarsney calls the epistemic challenge "not fatal". At cautious
    estimates the longtermist option wins only on a very stable far
    future, and the robust win rests on very small probabilities.
  - Thorstad shows that high existential risk lowers the value of reducing
    it unless risk soon falls and stays low. Mitigation "may yet be
    valuable, but perhaps not astronomically so".
- **The living's own stake is a fourth ground** (Bostrom 2003,
  [NOTE-tmpwwa8k](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpwwa8k.md); Bostrom 2013, [NOTE-tmpyret1](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpyret1.md)). On Bostrom's
  person-affecting reading, avoiding catastrophe matters because people
  now alive might share the long future: literal self-interest. In 2013
  he finds "a deficit of motivation", because the living capture only
  their own share of existential safety, and grants that "Unrestricted
  altruism is not so common".
- **The grounds divide the labour** (Meyer, SEP, [NOTE-tmps9poq](../notes.d/NOTE-tmps9poq.md)). A weak
  person-affecting view states harms to actual future people. By its own
  account it gives no reason to prefer a good future to none, and fills
  that gap with Scheffler's grounding in what the living value.
- **Lay concern reaches the far future through a picture of it as good**
  (Schubert et al., [NOTE-tmpbxw6b](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpbxw6b.md); Caviola et al., [NOTE-tmpaz0p7](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpaz0p7.md)).
  - Unprompted, a minority judge extinction uniquely bad. A reminder of
    the lost future brings this to about half; only a long, utopian
    future makes a majority (67–77%).
  - People count merely possible happy people as making a world better,
    but the preference fades with numbers: one billion were preferred to
    ten billion.
  - That is a thin, impersonal valuation, not an extended self. Caviola
    et al.'s account of averagism has people judge a population as one
    life "such as themselves": the self as a template, not enlarged.

## Note of 2026-10-29: the remaining works, read

Gardiner, Parfit and Ramsey were read themselves. Ord, MacAskill, Narveson
and Syropoulos and Markowitz were read through other works, and Scheffler
2018 is still unread. They add six points.

- **Why what is owed goes unpaid** (Gardiner, NOTE-tmp27ib6). Between
  generations the usual remedies for a commons problem are unavailable,
  because the generations "do not coexist". Each generation can seem to
  act while exploiting its temporal position, without admitting it "to
  itself". The question's psychology then has two things to measure: how
  far the self's stake reaches, and how far a concern that reaches
  further is deceived.
- **The good of future people, without anyone in particular** (Parfit,
  NOTE-tmp8x2m0). Parfit's last paper retracts his 1984 appeal to an
  impersonal principle, calling it "a bad mistake". He grounds the
  comparison in a wide person-affecting principle, on which being caused
  to exist with a good life counts as a benefit. What is owed is owed to
  whoever will exist, which our choices decide. He also permits favouring
  present people, and warns that if every generation does so "we and our
  successors might together wreck the Earth".
- **The zero-discount view began as a collective self's prudence**
  (Ramsey, NOTE-tmpgtdq6). Ramsey refuses to discount in one clause, as
  "ethically indefensible" and the product of "the weakness of the
  imagination". He gives no argument, and his planner is one community
  that "goes on for ever", saving in the first person plural. That is the
  thesis's shape, though as a model of what ought to be, not of motive.
  Weakness of imagination is a psychological claim the question can test.
- **The person-affecting ground, in its founder's late form** (Narveson
  2017, NOTE-tmpxt8qz, read in place of the 1973 paper). Happy future lives
  are good and a reason to create them, but no one is owed them. Why they
  matter is said only through the living's likings and interests, so this
  ground collapses into what the living value. That is where lay judgement
  also puts it (NOTE-tmpaz0p7).
- **The impersonal ground's accounting** (MacAskill, Thomas and Vallinder,
  NOTE-tmpo2a6r, read in place of MacAskill's book; Ord, NOTE-tmpxpkld and
  NOTE-tmpbrh86, read in place of Ord's). Value is significance times
  persistence times contingency, and nothing about anyone's self enters.
  Ord's estimates are unchanged since the book. He reports that against
  Covid humanity united "at first", then split within months, and his
  remedy is institutions, not identity. In passing, from one study he
  cites, Ord says people discount benefits to future generations no more
  by timing than benefits to their children. If that holds, concern for
  future people does not fade with distance as concern for one's own
  future does (Hershfield, NOTE-tmp70rn1), which fits concern for a
  category, not for a self extended in time. The record should read that
  study (Frederick 2003) before relying on it.
- **A fourth ground: reciprocity** (MacAskill's notes; Syropoulos et al.,
  NOTE-tmpin04p). MacAskill's notes ground some obligation in paying
  forward what past generations gave. Syropoulos et al. found that
  reflecting on past generations' sacrifices raised felt responsibility to
  future generations about as much as a legacy prompt did. A letter to one
  person born today raised it most, and an impersonal longtermist text
  least. Nothing in the study measures how far the self extends, so it
  does not run the thesis's test.
