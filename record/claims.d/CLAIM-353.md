# CLAIM-353: A will-organization that gives two operators decisive jurisdiction over a pair of options each, respects unanimous preference and admits any profile of operator rankings cannot always choose, and unlike Arrow's impossibility this is not escaped by giving endorsement richer, cardinal or comparable, information, so what distributed governance must give up is jurisdiction, unanimity or the domain, not an informational restriction

## The claim

The owner, at U36, wondered whether "Arrow's Impossibility Theorem might be
useful to us". A36 §2E: "Sen's liberal paradox may be even closer than
Arrow", because it "concerns jurisdiction", and asked: "Can a distributed
will-organization guarantee coherent joint decisions while preserving
specified domains of constituent authority and respecting unanimous
preferences?" A36's answer was "Sometimes the answer may be no."

The record can say more. Jurisdiction is one of the maintained relations by
which [CLAIM-027](CLAIM-027.md) says a will-organization makes a regime effective. Read an
operator's jurisdiction as Sen's minimal liberalism (L*): over some pair of
organizational states, its ranking decides the collective ranking, both
ways. Read respect for unanimity as weak Pareto, and the absence of
restrictions on how operators may rank as an unrestricted domain. Then
[THEORY-027](../theory.d/THEORY-027.md) (Sen (1970), proof checked in this record) applies as it
stands: no rule satisfies all three, even when it need only yield a best
element of each menu and need not be transitive. Two operators with
jurisdiction, unanimity and any profile are enough to leave some menu with
no choosable state.

**What it adds to A36.** A36 §2D, citing [NOTE-263](../notes.d/NOTE-263.md), said "the dual mapping
model should explicitly represent which kinds of information endorsement
procedures may use", because, on Sen's reading, allowing interpersonal
comparisons breaks Arrow's impossibility. Then it cited [THEORY-027](../theory.d/THEORY-027.md) as
"even closer" without saying that the remedy does not carry over.
[THEORY-027](../theory.d/THEORY-027.md): the liberal paradox "cannot be satisfactorily resolved through
the use of interpersonal comparisons", since "neither liberty claims nor
Pareto depend on the intensities of preferences". U, weak Pareto and L*
are stated on rankings alone, and the cycle is forced by the rankings, so
supplying cardinal or comparable information changes no premise. The only
exits are [THEORY-027](../theory.d/THEORY-027.md)'s: weaken unanimity, weaken jurisdiction, or restrict
the domain. For a will-organization that means: some operator's
jurisdiction is conditional, some unanimous preference is overridden, or
some combinations of operator rankings are excluded by the organization's
structure.

So the claim: where distributed governance meets this obstruction, the
fix is a change in how jurisdiction is allocated or bounded, in
[CLAIM-027](CLAIM-027.md)'s terms, and not a richer signal to the endorsement procedure.

## What it does not say

- **That operators have rights.** A36: "Network modules aren't
  automatically moral agents, and their optimization preferences are not
  equivalent to individual rights." The transposition uses the formal
  structure only.
- **That operator jurisdiction is pairwise decisiveness.** That is the
  premise, and the defeater. [THEORY-027](../theory.d/THEORY-027.md) leaves open "whether pairwise
  decisiveness is what liberty is" for persons, and the same question
  arises for operators, whose authority may be over components of a state
  rather than over choices between whole states.
- **That real organizations fail to choose.** Sen's proof needs a handful
  of profiles; an organization whose operators never rank that way, or
  whose jurisdictions never overlap in the forcing pattern, escapes it.
  Which domain restrictions suffice is open in [THEORY-027](../theory.d/THEORY-027.md) too.
- **Which exit is right.** Sen's own suggestion, people's priorities
  between liberty and overall desire fulfilment, is [THEORY-027](../theory.d/THEORY-027.md)'s
  interpretation, not a result, and has no worked analogue for operators
  here.

## Note of 2026-10-31: Massumi's co-patterning meets this limit only where an organization must choose

Massumi's micropolitical aim is "a maximally inclusive co-patterning of
contrasting tendencies, co-habiting the field in a way that facilitates
each fully realizing itself ... in way that they do not oppose and
curtail each other" (LIT-tmpi0u4n, p. 64; THEORY-tmpl13wj). Bencherki,
Brummans and Vézy say organizational becoming "is constituted by the
micropolitics of attempting to reach" it (LIT-tmp3mudg, p. 1358).

Read as a rule for an organization that must act, the aim asks for
something like this claim's conditions. Each tendency would be decisive
over what concerns it, and an outcome that every tendency prefers would
not be passed over. This claim says that two decisive domains, unanimity
and an unrestricted profile can leave a menu with no choosable state.
On that reading, the co-patterning cannot be guaranteed. That gives a
structural reason for what Massumi calls "always-as-yet unknowable".

This is a formal application, and it holds only on that reading. Massumi
refuses the step it needs: the aim is "not to resolve the tension
through a choice" (LIT-tmp8hcfz, p. 12). His co-patterning is not a
social choice function. So the limit applies where the micropolitics
must end in a choice, as the parent's MSF meeting ended in a yes or a no
on training (pp. 1359–1363), and not to Massumi's aim as he states it.
It tells against the parent's descriptive use more than against Massumi.

No status, title or warrant changes.
