# THEORY-tmp8nd9o: Metzinger 2024 calls consciousness without an egoic self or first-person perspective "empirically established" on the strength of retrospective, self-selected reports from meditators, while its own methods chapter grants that a remembered report of a selfless state is "highly dubious" evidence that the state was selfless when it occurred, and leaves that problem open

## Source

Metzinger (2024), [LIT-tmp3uosd](../literature.d/LIT-tmp3uosd.md): introduction, pp. xvi–xxvi; chapter 2,
pp. 12–20; chapter 31, pp. 416–418; chapter 34, pp. 457–467. Read in
[NOTE-tmpy472x](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpy472x.md). Metzinger (2020), [LIT-tmpr0pnp](../literature.d/LIT-tmpr0pnp.md), p. 15, read in full in
[NOTE-tmptylts](../notes.d/NOTE-tmptylts.md).

## What was actually shown

**The data.** An online survey from early 2020 in five languages had 1,403
usable respondents from 57 countries. More than 77% meditated regularly,
and the mean age was 52. Each rated 92 items, and 841 gave usable free-text
reports of past episodes "in which the quality of pure awareness was
particularly salient and/or one which you can remember particularly
clearly" (pp. xvi, 17–18). The instruction defined the target as all
experiences "in which there is an 'awareness of awareness itself'"
(p. xvi). No physiological or behavioural measure was taken.

**What the methods chapter grants** (pp. 12–20):

- Self-selection into the reporting group, and a sample "highly unlikely
  to be representative" (pp. 14, 20).
- "Embodied theory contamination": practitioners' belief systems "act as
  unconscious priors" and "may have both introduced a self-selection bias
  into our sample and biased the questionnaire responses" (pp. 16–17).
- A pull toward impressive episodes, and "response drift" (pp. 17–18).
- Unreliable recall of episodes "possibly even decades ago" (p. 18).
- The grouping of reports is "not a truly systematic qualitative analysis
  at all" and reflects the author's own intuitions (pp. 12–14).
- **The autobiographical-memory problem.** "The respondents' claims that
  they themselves consciously experienced a selfless state at the very time
  when it actually occurred are highly dubious from a methodological
  perspective." A selfless state might be "stored and later retrieved in an
  autobiographical format, as a post hoc mnemonic misrepresentation". "The
  specific philosophical question about MPE and autobiographical memory
  remains open" (p. 19). The 2020 paper goes further: the content of a
  full-absorption episode "cannot be reported", and only entry and exit
  are remembered ([LIT-tmpr0pnp](../literature.d/LIT-tmpr0pnp.md), p. 15).
- Reports that an episode was "not an experience" do not show that it was
  not one, nor that it was (the C-fallacy and its mirror, p. 417).

**What the summary says** (pp. 457–459, 466–467). "Now that full-absorption
episodes, nonegoic self-awareness, and nondual modes of consciousness are
empirically established phenomena, we can see that minimal phenomenal
experience (MPE) is not subjective and having a first-person perspective is
not a necessary condition for consciousness to occur" (p. 457). The strong
reading of "subjective" "is false" (p. 459). "Subpersonal but nonetheless
conscious self-knowledge from the zero-person perspective does exist—this
is what empirical evidence shows" (pp. 458–459). "We now have the first
systematic empirical evidence for a conscious and genuinely nonsubjective
form of self-knowledge" (p. 467).

The summary does restate the memory point. The episodes' quality "may
later become integrated with some long-term self-model", which "via its
subject/object grammar, retrospectively imports a fictitious first-person
perspective" (pp. 466–467). But it reads that point as explaining away the
selfhood in the reports. The methods chapter had read it as undermining the
reports' evidence that the episode was selfless. The same mechanism serves
both readings, and the book gives no way to tell them apart.

**What could have come out otherwise.** The methods chapter could have
answered the memory problem, for instance with reports taken at the end of
a session, which it recommends (p. 18), or with a measure taken during the
episode. It does neither.

## What this does not say

- **That selfless consciousness does not occur.** The reports are
  consistent with it. What the book lacks is evidence that tells it apart
  from a selfless *description*, applied afterwards, of an episode that
  had a self, or of a gap.
- **That the survey is worthless.** Its factor structure and its corpus of
  descriptions are data about how practitioners describe these episodes.
  That is what [THEORY-870](THEORY-870.md) found for the TM study as well: evidence about
  reports.
- **That the problem is unique to this book.** It is the performative worry
  [THEORY-063](THEORY-063.md)'s `promote_when` already names, and the précis named it too.
  This book states it most fully and then sets it aside in its
  conclusions.
