# NOTE-428: How effective psychological treatments work: mechanisms of change in cognitive behavioural therapy and beyond

## Contribution

The paper does three things. It gives a short history of behaviour therapy
and CBT as a cycle of empirically grounded treatment development
(phenomenology → theory of maintaining factors → experiment → treatment →
trial; Clark's six steps). It argues that the field has confused common
factors with mechanisms of change. And it proposes a single mechanism of
change for effective psychological therapy in general. The proposal is the
new part. The rest is an editor's synthesis for an anniversary issue.

## Key insight

Psychological problems are, in the paper's view, "exaggerations of one or
more otherwise normal processes", sustained not by their original causes
but by maintaining factors. The central one is a meaning: the panicking
person reads palpitations as a heart attack; the OCD father reads an
intrusive thought as proof he is dangerous. That meaning drives behaviour
(avoidance, neutralising, safety-seeking), and the behaviour keeps the
meaning from being disconfirmed. Therapy works when it gives the person a
credible alternative account and a way to test it. The paper's slogan is
"don't trust me, find out for yourself". If that is the mechanism, then
exposure, behavioural activation and even insight therapies work to the
extent that they deliver a tested Theory B, explicitly or not.

## Assumptions

- **Maintenance, not cause.** "The past 50 years have seen no significant
  development of our understanding of causal factors in mental health
  problems" (p. 600). Treatment development therefore targets maintaining
  factors (p. 600; Clark's step 2, p. 601).
- **Continuity with normal processes.** Social anxiety continues shyness;
  depression continues ordinary low mood (p. 600).
- **Appraisal drives emotion.** People "experience mood problems because
  they interpret their situation as being more negative or threat-based
  than it really is" (p. 605). This is stated as the conclusion of
  "decades of psychological research", without specific citation at that
  point.
- **Two criteria for a credible alternative.** It must fit the person's past
  experience and survive their future experience, incidental or
  experimental (p. 605).

## Key results

No new data. The results are arguments and a proposal, with cited
evidence.

- **The logic of treatment evidence (p. 599).** "The effectiveness of a
  treatment is not evidence for the validity of the theory on which it is
  based with one important exception": when a treatment demonstrably
  changes a factor crucial to a theory but does not change the target
  problem, that disconfirms the theory. Successful, theory-informed
  treatment is only "consistent with" its theory.
- **Common factors versus mechanisms (p. 603).** "Although mechanism of
  change should necessarily manifest in the occurrence of common factors
  in therapy, common factors do not necessarily reflect mechanisms of
  change." The alliance may follow improvement rather than drive it.
- **The exposure circle (p. 598).** Exposure was adopted as the mechanism
  of anxiety treatment because it was common to successful treatments, and
  inferred from fear reduction. The paper calls this circular, and says
  the confusion of common factors with mechanisms "has in our view held
  the field back".
- **Why mechanisms remain unclear (p. 602).** The paper gives four
  reasons: the emphasis on efficacy studies; a lack of statistical
  procedures linking independent and dependent variables; a near-total
  absence of studies establishing the timeline of change; and failure to
  test CBT-specific interventions against general ones.
- **The case for cognitive change (pp. 603–604).** Against the objections
  from component analyses and from early sudden gains, the paper relays
  Lorenzo-Luaces et al. (2015): only three studies met their four
  questions with temporality controlled, and those authors argue cognitive
  change is the mechanism, whatever procedure produces it.
- **The proposal (pp. 605–608).** The core mechanism is "the explicit
  identification of the problematic negative meaning ('Theory A') and its
  contrast to the shared understanding collaboratively developed as part
  of formulation ('Theory B')" (p. 607). The alliance is "necessary
  insofar as it facilitates the development of a shared understanding …
  but it is not sufficient to engender the necessary psychological
  flexibility in terms of meaning making" (p. 608). Effective
  non-cognitive therapies are said to "implicitly develop alternatives"
  (p. 606), and all psychological therapies are "underpinned by a more or
  less explicit Theory B" (p. 607).
- **Cited support for the proposal (pp. 606–607).** Exposure presented as
  behavioural experiments designed to disconfirm threat beliefs "was very
  markedly superior to exposure alone" in agoraphobia (Salkovskis et al.
  1991, 1999) and social phobia (Wells et al. 1995). Panic attacks undid
  habituation (Matthews et al. 1981).
- **Agenda (pp. 608–609).** Measure mechanisms alongside efficacy; use
  mediation statistics; compare specific with general interventions; and
  establish temporal precedence by Kazdin's (2007) six criteria. Those are
  that the treatment changes the mediator, which then changes the outcome;
  that the mediator–outcome relation is specific; that other mediators are
  ruled out experimentally; consistency; a gradient; and multiple data
  sources. Ecological momentary assessment and sudden-gains methods are
  recommended.

## Concepts

- **mechanism of change (MOC)**: what actually produces therapeutic change.
  It is distinguished from a common factor, which is merely present across
  therapies.
- **common factors / common elements**: features shared by therapies,
  whether relational (the alliance) or topographical (similar techniques).
- **Theory A / Theory B**: the person's threat-laden account of their
  problem, and the collaboratively formulated, testable, less threatening
  alternative.
- **psychological flexibility (as used here)**: being able "to understand
  and weigh different perspectives on what they are experiencing" (p. 605),
  and then to respond more flexibly. It is explicitly not thinking "more
  rationally or logically". The paper cites none of the ACT literature
  that uses the same phrase.
- **descriptive vs mechanistic transdiagnostic processes** (Harvey et al.
  2011): seen in many disorders without a shared functional role (low
  self-esteem), versus maintaining at least two disorders (preoccupation
  with shape and weight).
- **empirically grounded clinical interventions**: the paper's term for CBT
  as a cycle of clinical phenomenology, theory, experiment and outcome
  research (Figure 1).

## Connections

- **Process-based therapy ([LIT-544](../literature.d/LIT-544.md)).** Both papers open with Gordon
  Paul's question. This one uses the 1967 form ("… and under which set of
  circumstances?"). Hofmann and Hayes use the 1969 form, which adds "and
  how does it come about?", and say Paul himself added that clause. Both
  diagnose an efficacy-first field that neglected mechanism, and both cite
  Kazdin (2007) and Nock (2007). They differ on the answer. Hofmann and
  Hayes expect many processes, arranged hierarchically. This paper proposes
  one mechanism across therapies. The word "flexibility" is common to both,
  but the referents differ. Here it is meaning-making flexibility, reached
  by testing beliefs. In ACT, the tradition Hayes leads, "psychological
  flexibility" is a process that the paper under note does not define or
  cite. Whether they are one construct is open.
- **Network theory ([LIT-547](../literature.d/LIT-547.md)).** "Vicious circles which trap them"
  (p. 608) and being "stuck" are the feedback-maintained disorder state of
  Borsboom's theory. The difference is the level of the nodes: appraisals
  and safety behaviours here, manual symptoms there. On Borsboom's
  taxonomy, CBT as described here is a network intervention. It weakens
  the link by which a meaning drives the behaviour that protects it.
- **Emotion regulation ([LIT-554](../literature.d/LIT-554.md)).** The paper asks why mechanism
  research in CBT has not done what "has been done and replicated in
  emotion regulation studies" under strict experimental conditions, and
  cites Gross (1998), Gross and John (2003) and Webb et al. (2012) for it
  (p. 609). Of these, Gross (1998) is the conceptual review the record
  holds, which, by its filing note, has no data of its own. The
  experimental work the paper means must be in the other two. Gross's
  "cognitive change" family of regulation strategies (reappraisal) is
  close kin to the shift from Theory A to Theory B. The paper does not
  draw that link.
- **Paul's question as a shared root.** Both this paper and [LIT-544](../literature.d/LIT-544.md)
  treat Paul (1967/1969) as the founding statement of the field's goal.

## Limitations

- **The mechanism is not tested here**, and by the paper's own agenda
  (Kazdin's criteria) the evidence that would test it is mostly not yet
  collected.
- **"All effective therapies" is argued by reinterpretation.** That
  exposure, behavioural activation or insight therapy work through an
  implicit Theory B is asserted. A rival account (habituation, inhibitory
  learning, reinforcement) is not shown to fail.
- **The appraisal premise is stated as the lesson of "decades of research"**
  without specific citation at that point.
- **Interest.** The first author is the journal's editor, the paper is part
  of an anniversary issue he co-introduced, and all three studies cited
  for the key comparison (C5) have him as an author (Salkovskis et al.
  1991, 1999; Wells, Clark, Salkovskis et al. 1995). The paper discloses the
  editorship and states he was not involved in its review.
- **Unanchored terminology.** The paper takes "psychological flexibility"
  without engaging the measures and literature that already use the term.

## Open questions

- Does a measured shift in belief in Theory A versus Theory B precede, and
  predict, symptom change across therapies, including non-CBT ones?
- Is the paper's meaning-making flexibility the same construct as ACT's
  psychological flexibility, or a narrower one?
- If disorders are network states ([LIT-547](../literature.d/LIT-547.md)), is a change of meaning
  one network intervention among several, or the one that generalises?
