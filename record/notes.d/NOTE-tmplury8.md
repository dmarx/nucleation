# NOTE-tmplury8: Lush & Seth — Reply to: No specific relationship between hypnotic suggestibility and the rubber hand illusion

## Contribution

It names the assumption the comment rests on and gives data against it.
A synchronous-minus-asynchronous difference controls for everything
except timing only if participants expect the same from both conditions,
and in the target sample they did not. The reply adds that the expectancy
difference did not vary with hypnotisability, which turns Ehrsson et
al.'s central null from evidence against the demand account into
something the account predicts. It also separates the ownership item from
the two referred-touch items and shows ownership is the weakest and the
most dependent on hypnotisability.

## Key insight

A control condition protects an inference only against what it holds
constant. The asynchronous condition holds the stimuli, the room and the
questions constant, but not what participants think should happen. If
everyone expects more from synchrony, by the same amount, then everyone
has the same reason to report more under synchrony, and a trait that
governs *how* they meet the demand, by experience or by bias, need not
change *how much* the two conditions differ. On this reading the
synchrony effect is real as a difference in report and silent about its
cause.

## Assumptions

- **Expectancy measures capture demand.** Participants' predicted
  experience for each condition, collected in the target study, is taken
  as the demand the conditions convey (Orne's "totality of cues which
  convey an experimental hypothesis to the subject", p. 1).
- **Two routes to one report.** "Participants can respond to the
  differing demand characteristics by either generating the
  corresponding experiences (if they have high trait capacity for
  phenomenological control, i.e. hypnotisability) or by response bias (if
  they have low capacity for phenomenological control)" (p. 1). The
  second route is proposed, not measured.
- **Raw slopes over standardised correlations.** The authors interpret
  linear models in scale units, and dispute that R² = 0.09 is weak.
- **Instructions that did not move expectancy leave the question open.**
  The target study's suggestion manipulation is treated as a failed
  manipulation, so the comment's null across instruction groups is not
  evidence about suggestion.

## Key results

- **The frame (p. 1).** "All but one [reanalysis] confirm the findings we
  presented in the target paper, and the sole new analysis is insensitive
  and therefore uninformative. The disagreement is therefore not about
  data or analyses, but interpretation." The insensitive one is the
  whole-sample drift analysis. "The asynchronous condition offers no
  protection against demand characteristic effects (including faking,
  imagination and phenomenological control)."
- **Expectancies (p. 1).** "Participant expectancies are greater for the
  synchronous than asynchronous condition", as reported in the target
  article and elsewhere. In the target data (n = 353), hypnotisability
  does not predict the expectancy difference: b = −0.16 Likert units per
  SWASH unit, SE = 0.09, t = 1.78, P = 0.072, BH(0,0.25) = 0.07,
  rs = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.18, 0.03]. A B of 0.07 is evidence for no
  relation. "Participant expectancies arising from demand characteristics
  readily account for our reported null result, since these expectancies
  do not vary with the level of hypnotisability." The size of the
  expectancy difference itself is not given in the reply.
- **Implicit measures (p. 1).** "This applies equally to implicit
  measures of the RHI (e.g., skin conductance response and
  proprioceptive drift) ... people expect the patterns of results that
  are typically obtained in RHI experiments", citing Lush, Seth & Dienes
  2021, not read here.
- **Effect size (pp. 1–2).** Against "weak": Cohen calls R² = 0.09 a
  medium correlation, and Funder & Ozer "a large effect that is
  potentially powerful in both the short and the long run". The target
  article's linear model shows "a 0.6 increase in mean score for the
  three illusion statements (7-point scale) for each 1 point increase in
  SWASH (5-point scale)". This is for the synchronous condition.
- **Ownership against referred touch (p. 2, Fig. 1).** Mean agreement
  (maximum 3): S1 1.9, SE 0.1; S2 1.2, SE 0.1; S3, ownership, 0.7, with
  SE printed as 1.0 in both the PDF and the JATS text, probably a typo
  for 0.1 (my reading). Regressing ownership agreement on SWASH:
  b = 0.76, SE = 0.13, t = 5.79, P < 0.001, 95% CI [0.50, 1.01],
  BH(0,1.4) = 4.2 × 10⁶, rs = 0.27. "Minimum 'ownership' agreement (a
  score of 1) is predicted only for SWASH scores greater than two (the
  top 31% of hypnotisability scores)". The caption does not say which
  condition's ratings are regressed; the text's framing implies the
  synchronous one.
- **Order effects (p. 2).** In a reanalysis reported in a preprint (Lush
  2021, not read), "mean agreement with the ownership statement is seen
  only in the group who underwent the asynchronous 'control' task first".
- **The quartiles (p. 2).** The residual illusion at low hypnotisability
  is already visible in the target article's linear model; it "may
  reflect other effects arising from demand characteristics (e.g., bias
  or compliance effects)". "For the crucial ownership statement, there
  may not be a residual effect (Fig. 1)."
- **The literature review (p. 2).** The commonest method in the 20 most
  influential RHI papers "is to test the difference between synchronous
  and asynchronous conditions, and then interpret only the synchronous
  condition". Even if less cited papers used difference measures more,
  "difference measures are confounded by expectancies".
- **The remaining points (p. 2).** 2AFC methods are no more resistant to
  demand "than the more common methods". Similar correlations for
  synchronous and asynchronous conditions are explained if "both reflect
  suggestion effects"; a lower score for the visual-hallucination item
  may reflect lower expectancy or greater difficulty. The comment's
  analyses are "also exploratory and post-hoc".
- **Conclusion (p. 2).** "Future experiments, with adequate control
  conditions, are needed to establish whether mechanisms beyond demand
  characteristic effects (e.g., multisensory integration) play a
  significant role in RHI measures."

## Concepts

- **Demand characteristics** — Orne's sense: the cues in an experiment
  that convey its hypothesis to the participant.
- **Phenomenological control** — trait capacity to generate experience
  that meets expectation, measured as hypnotisability (Dienes et al.'s
  "cold control", cited).
- **Expectancies** — participants' predicted ratings for each condition,
  the reply's operational measure of demand.
- **Referred touch against ownership** — S1 and S2 against S3, which the
  reply treats separately, following Reader, Trifonova & Ehrsson 2021,
  an Ehrsson paper.

## Bearing on the record

Read against the comment, [NOTE-tmpdtj68](NOTE-tmpdtj68.md). Where the two NOTEs touch the
same THEORY, the verdict is this one's: the reply is the half that
changes it.

- **[THEORY-tmpef8lf](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpef8lf.md) (Proposed), the synchrony effect.** The exchange
  together **partly meets** its promote_when and shows a flaw in it. The
  comment meets the disjunct "trait suggestibility is measured and the
  synchrony effect survives it" on the questionnaire, at n = 353. This
  reply shows that the other disjunct, expectations "measured or
  matched", was measured in the same sample and failed: expectations
  were not matched, and the mismatch is uniform across suggestibility,
  so survival of the effect does not discriminate the multisensory
  reading from the demand reading. The THEORY's own "What this does not
  say" already lists "It does not separate synchrony from expectation";
  after this exchange that is still true, and now shown on a large
  sample. Neither refutation condition is met: no design here matches
  expectations, and drift under asynchrony is not reported. The
  synchrony effect *as a difference in report* is, if anything, better
  supported than before: both sides accept it in 353 participants at
  every level of suggestibility. **Proposed change (proposal only):**
  drop "or in which trait suggestibility is measured and the synchrony
  effect survives it" as a sufficient condition, and require that
  expectations be matched across conditions, or measured and the
  synchrony effect shown to exceed what they predict; and add that a
  first-hand reading of Lush et al. 2020 itself is needed for the
  replication's sample sizes and effect sizes, which neither of these
  papers gives for the synchrony contrast. The status should stay
  Proposed.
- **[THEORY-tmpxdoe8](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpxdoe8.md) (Proposed), graded common-cause ownership.** Bears
  against its readiness, not its content. Its promote_when asks for a
  parametric causal-inference test; the reply's claim that 2AFC and
  other measures are open to the same demand would apply to such a test
  unless expectations are controlled, and the comment's temporal-window
  studies, the closest thing to it, are cited by one side and passed over
  by the other. The ownership result is relevant: the item the THEORY is
  about is the weakest of the three on average and rises steeply with
  hypnotisability in the reply's Figure 1, while its synchrony
  effect is unrelated to hypnotisability (comment, Suppl. Fig. 1C).
  Graded, yes, but graded across people by a trait the THEORY does not
  name. **Proposed change:** add to promote_when that the model-based
  test hold expectations constant across the conditions it varies, or
  measure them as a covariate.
- **[THEORY-tmpom3z2](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpom3z2.md) (Proposed), interoception.** Bears against the
  evidence it rests on, by the reply's logic and not by its words. The
  cardio-visual hand of Suzuki et al. (Seth's group) is a synchronous
  against asynchronous comparison on questionnaire and drift, which is
  the design this reply says "offers no protection against demand
  characteristic effects". The reply does not mention that study; the
  application is mine. The sensitivity–susceptibility leg (Tsakiris et
  al. 2011) is also exposed: if susceptibility partly measures
  phenomenological control, a correlation with heartbeat detection could
  run through it. **Proposed change:** add to promote_when that
  expectancies be matched or measured across the cardio-visual
  conditions, and that the sensitivity–susceptibility relation survive
  controlling for hypnotisability. The non-bodily periodic control the
  THEORY already asks for does not answer this, since participants could
  expect less from it too.
- **[THEORY-tmpuehzc](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpuehzc.md) (Proposed), minimal selfhood.** No direct bearing:
  neither paper measures self-location or whole-body identification. The
  full-body illusion it leans on (Lenggenhager et al. 2007, [LIT-tmpwaa0j](../literature.d/LIT-tmpwaa0j.md))
  uses the same synchronous-against-asynchronous design, so the reply's
  argument would reach it, but the reply does not say so. Both tests in
  the promote_when would need the same expectation control.
- **[THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md) (Proposed).** No bearing.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** The exchange is the question in miniature. The
  reply's position is that report and implicit measures alike can be
  produced by demand, and that the only way through is a control that
  matches expectation; the comment's is that a perceptual illusion
  "should be resistant to suggestions". Both agree the reports occur; the
  disagreement is over what makes them evidence of an experience. The
  reply's own two-routes proposal makes high-suggestibles' reports
  genuine experience and low-suggestibles' bias, which no measure in
  either paper distinguishes.
- **[NOTE-372](NOTE-372.md), claim C6.** The reply leaves "body ownership can be
  manipulated by multisensory conflict" undecided: reports of ownership
  can be manipulated by the rubber-hand procedure, and whether the
  multisensory conflict is what does it is, on this reply, not yet
  shown.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- A reply, post hoc on both sides by its own account. The expectancy and
  ownership regressions are exploratory.
- The size of the expectancy difference, which is the whole argument, is
  not given here; it is referred to the target article.
- The response-bias route for low-suggestibles is asserted. No measure in
  the exchange separates generated experience from bias, so the
  two-routes account fits any pattern of reports.
- Small reporting slips: the ownership SE printed as 1.0; the expectancy
  slope negative with a positive t; Figure 1's caption does not name the
  condition.
- The temporal-window argument is not answered directly.
- Seth's earlier work relies on the design the reply criticises; the
  reply does not discuss its own authors' earlier findings, and need not,
  but a reader of this record should know.

## Open questions

- Does the synchrony effect survive when expectations are equalised, for
  example by telling participants, convincingly, that the asynchronous
  condition is the illusion condition? The target study tried and its
  instructions did not move expectancy; a stronger manipulation would
  settle the main point.
- Do participants expect the illusion to fade at 200–300 ms of
  asynchrony and not before? If not, the temporal window is evidence the
  demand account must explain.
- Is the ownership item's dependence on hypnotisability also present in
  the asynchronous condition, as the comment reports for the mean of the
  three items? The reply's Figure 1 does not say.
