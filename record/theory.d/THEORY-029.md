# THEORY-029: A higher-order attitude cannot make a motive the agent's own by its order alone: any attitude specified without presupposing the agent's participation can itself be disowned, and any specified to include it presupposes what it was meant to explain

## Source

- Velleman (1992), [LIT-295](../literature.d/LIT-295.md), pp. 110–122 of the 2015 reprint, as read in [NOTE-265](../notes.d/NOTE-265.md). This is the primary source for the dilemma.
- Buss & Westlund, SEP *Personal Autonomy* (2018 revision), [LIT-296](../literature.d/LIT-296.md), §3, as read in [NOTE-267](../notes.d/NOTE-267.md).
- McKenna & Coates, SEP *Compatibilism*, [LIT-291](../literature.d/LIT-291.md), §4.2 and supplement §A, as read in [NOTE-270](../notes.d/NOTE-270.md).
- Dryden, IEP *Autonomy* (2010), [LIT-297](../literature.d/LIT-297.md), §2.a.ii, as read in [NOTE-266](../notes.d/NOTE-266.md).
- Frankfurt (2004), [LIT-293](../literature.d/LIT-293.md), the Tanner Lectures, as read in [NOTE-264](../notes.d/NOTE-264.md). This is the only first-hand Frankfurt in the record.

## What was actually shown

Frankfurt's 1971 account is held only **second-hand**, through [LIT-291](../literature.d/LIT-291.md): a person acts of her own free will iff her action issues from "the will she wants". The will is the effective first-order desire, and the second-order volition endorses it. The objection is that nothing about being second-order makes the endorsing attitude the agent's. She can be alienated from it just as from the first-order desire, so the question of authority recurs one level up.

Velleman ([LIT-295](../literature.d/LIT-295.md)) gives this its sharpest form, and the record holds it first-hand. He runs the objection against each stopping point in turn:

- **Second-order desires** fall to the regress. He reports this as Watson's objection, which Frankfurt conceded (IWWCA 65–6). Both halves of that attribution are **second-hand**.
- **Watson's values** fall to the materialism-and-sin case: one can recoil from values that still grip one.
- **Frankfurt's "decisive commitments"** fall to the unwitting decision to end a friendship.

Velleman then generalises to a dilemma (pp. 115–116). A state specified without presupposing the agent's participation is one "from which the agent can in principle dissociate himself". A state specified so as to guarantee participation "beg[s] the question". So it is "a choice between begging the question and not answering it at all".

Buss & Westlund ([LIT-296](../literature.d/LIT-296.md) §3) reach the same place independently, without citing Watson. The regress can stop only at an attitude "no agent can possibly be alienated" from. The one candidate, the desire to be self-governing, cannot be the criterion, because it is at work even when self-government fails.

Frankfurt's late view ([LIT-293](../literature.d/LIT-293.md)) does not answer this. It never mentions the regress. Its stopping point is wholehearted love, whose necessities are "irresistible, but … not coercive" (p. 197). That holds only because lovers are stipulated never to be ambivalent, and any wavering shows the love "had never been truly wholehearted" (p. 198). That is the question-begging horn, reached by definition.

The claim is the record's synthesis across these five readings. No single one of them states it in this form.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say higher-order structure is irrelevant to agency.** Velleman calls Frankfurt's work "the best sustained attempt" (p. 110), keeps his adjudication framing, and targets only the reductive strategy.
- **The dilemma is not proved.** Velleman argues its first horn only by examples, and gives no principle for why every independently specified state must be disownable ([NOTE-265](../notes.d/NOTE-265.md), C5). His own stopping point, the desire to act in accordance with reasons, escapes only "while remaining an agent". Whether that escape is fair is unexamined, and Buss & Westlund's argument would reject it as a criterion.
- **Frankfurt's replies are not assessed.** Satisfaction (1992), active identification (1994) and Bratman's policies are all held second-hand or not at all. [LIT-295](../literature.d/LIT-295.md) expressly excludes "The Faintest Passion".
- **Who first stated the objection is unsettled in the record.** The IEP and SEP *Compatibilism* credit Watson 1975. SEP *Personal Autonomy* treats Watson as a rival account and states the regress as its own. Watson's valuing-versus-desiring distinction is reported in detail by none of them.
