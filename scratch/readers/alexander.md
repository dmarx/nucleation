## Report: Alexander 1985, "A Biological Interpretation of Moral Systems"

**Verdict in brief.** The paper is Alexander's own short statement of the book, and it supports most of what LIT-495 says the book argues. It models indirect reciprocity in words: an "interested audience" watches, and step-by-step sequences show who helps, punishes or cheats whom. Its account of intergroup competition is not group selection; Alexander sides with Fisher's individual-level account. It argues no theory of what is owed, though it says the account "justifies" indiscriminate altruism as an ideal (p. 16). It does not contain the phrase "not easily relatable to universals", so that question stays with the book.

### Summary
- A moral system is "a society with rules". Moral questions arise from conflicts of interest, and humans' interests are ultimately reproductive.
- Conflicts of interest come from genetic individuality. They are reduced by relatedness (nepotism) and by gains from cooperating (reciprocity).
- Using a classification of "effort" (fig. 2), every social investment is phenotypically or genetically selfish:
  - direct and indirect somatic effort, the latter involving reciprocity;
  - reproductive effort, which is nepotism.
- Moral systems are, in essence, patterns of **indirect reciprocity**: returns come from someone other than the person helped, because others are watching.
- Indirect reciprocity spreads altruism, rules and punishment, and also cheating. It therefore selects for:
  - seeming more altruistic than one is;
  - moralizing others into altruism that serves the moralizer;
  - conscience;
  - self-deception and false sincerity.
- In large societies held together by reciprocity, some **indiscriminate altruism** (help given regardless of who is helped) pays as social investment, because observers use it to choose partners.
- Mistaking this investment for self-sacrifice produced the philosophers' ideal of universal, self-sacrificing indiscriminate altruism, and with it justice as fairness and a single just God.
- Practised morality is within-group cohesion, perhaps "cooperation-to-compete" against other groups.
- The arms race is the most serious moral problem. World peace is more likely through models that appeal to self-interest than through "universal brotherhood".

### Contribution
- The paper names and defines indirect reciprocity (Alexander 1977, 1979) and makes it the essence of moral systems.
- It explains, by evolution, three things together:
  - morality as practised;
  - society's sense that morality runs against evolution;
  - idealized universal moralities.
- It is the earliest statement the record could hold, in Alexander's own words, of what Nowak 2006 cites him for.

### Assumptions
- Selection acts mainly at low levels: the individual and the gene.
- Interests are reproductive, whatever people consciously believe.
- Proximate mechanisms follow genealogical links and opportunities for cooperation.
- People assess costs and benefits, quickly and often unconsciously.
- The account is adaptationist throughout. Note 1 rejects the opposition between "biological" and "cultural".

### Concepts
- **Kinds of effort:** somatic, direct and indirect; reproductive (mating, parental, extraparental nepotistic).
- **Phenotypically and genetically selfish or altruistic** (the two-column table of fig. 2).
- **Forms of reciprocity:** direct vs indirect; immediate vs delayed.
- **Indiscriminate altruism or social investment**, and the idealized model of morality built on it.
- **Cooperation-to-compete.**
- **Conscience** as self-serving judgment; **self-deception**.
- **Universal brotherhood.**

### Specific checks
- **How indirect reciprocity is modelled:** in words, with schematic sequences (figs. 3–5) and an informal cost-benefit logic. There is no formal reputation dynamic: no image score and no probability of knowing a reputation. Nowak's q is a later formalization of the "interested audience".
- **Group selection:** no. Selection is "most effective at low levels" (p. 6). Darwin's group selection is called "not unreasonable", but Alexander adopts Fisher's account, which favours individual-level selection ("His theory is, in general, the same", p. 19). Intergroup competition enters as an external threat that makes within-group cooperation pay individuals (p. 15).
- **"Not easily relatable to universals":** absent from the paper. What the paper does deny is two things:
  - that morality as practised is universal indiscriminate altruism;
  - that the idealized universalist morality is realistic.

  This concerns the *scope* of moral concern (everyone versus one's own group). It does not concern whether the same moral rules recur across cultures. So it does not conflict with MAC's seven universal morals (LIT-047), which the paper neither affirms nor denies. Whether the book goes further is unchecked.
- **Normative ethics:** none argued. The paper explains moral systems and the ideals philosophers hold. Two near-normative remarks:
  - the view "justifies indiscriminate altruism as a model worth pursuing" (p. 16);
  - a practical recommendation that world peace go through appeals to self-interest (p. 17; also note 7).

  Neither is an argument about what is owed.
- **Contests, division, possession:**
  - possession and the settling of contests: absent;
  - "division" appears only as division of labor (p. 13), not the division of disputed resources;
  - deference and hierarchy: absent.
- **MAC domains it touches:**
  - kin (nepotism);
  - mutualism (symmetrical joint effort, p. 13, treated as reciprocity);
  - reciprocity, direct and indirect;
  - group (cohesion, cooperation-to-compete);
  - bravery, only in note 5, through Fisher's "prestige of the hero" for kinsmen;
  - fairness, as an origin story for "justice as fairness" (p. 15).

### Candidate THEORY
**Sentence:** Alexander holds that moral systems are, in essence, systems of indirect reciprocity: moral questions arise from conflicts of ultimately reproductive interest, and because acts are watched by others who choose later partners by them, some help given regardless of who is helped pays the helper as social investment. Mistaking that investment for self-sacrifice gave rise to idealized moralities of universal altruism, while the morality actually practised is cohesion within groups that may exclude and oppose other groups.

**source:** the Zygon 1985 paper (to be registered; DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9744.1985.tb00574.x). LIT-495 is the book it previews.

**promote_when:** Promote when another statement by Alexander, the book (LIT-495) or a second reading of the paper's pp. 3–16, confirms that he holds all four of these:
- moral systems are, in essence, systems of indirect reciprocity, arising from conflicts of reproductive interest;
- indiscriminate altruism pays the altruist because observers choose partners by it;
- idealized universal moralities come from misreading that altruism as self-sacrifice;
- practised morality is within-group cohesion that may be directed against other groups.
