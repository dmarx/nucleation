# Article 3. The Contracts We Become

## Relational invocation, stored coordination, and the formation of interdependence

**Abstract.** A contract is usually taken to be an explicit agreement made by parties who exist before it. This article argues that the processes of an organization can come to stand in contract-like relations that no one stated or agreed to; that such relations form from relied-upon regularities; that they are contract-like only if they govern what happens on deviation, conflict or breach, and not merely because something depends on something else; and that, where they are contract-like, they can give a process a standing that does not presuppose moral consideration. It separates regularity, expectation, dependence, obligation and contract, so that no dependence is counted as a contract, and no reliance as a debt, by default. It then prices what such a contract costs to form, keep, recognize, check, revise and leave, using a general cost model of stored work, and shows that leaving can be unaffordable without any preference for staying, and that an obligation bars exit rather than raising its price. Last, it argues that invoking a relationship differs from reusing a stored result, because an invocation can reproduce, form or change the relationship it invokes, which a model that holds the store fixed between uses does not price; and it sorts the ambiguity about which relationship an act invokes into three kinds with different remedies. The account of contracts is a set of conjectures whose defeaters have not been tested. The cost geometry it applies is demonstrated, but in constructions with illustrative values, and none of its models is a measurement of any organization. The article ends on the test that would decide its central open question: whether an invocation that changes its relationship needs new cost terms or only a richer state.

---

## 3.1 Introduction: beyond explicit agreement

Engineers already speak of contracts between things that cannot agree to anything. A service-level agreement binds one service to another; a scheduler's priority rules decide which of two racing processes wins. The idea this article develops starts there: contracts can form between processes, and a contract can operationalize standing, since its prioritization structure can govern how disputes between processes, such as races, are resolved (CLAIM-tmpkvzcn). That adds a kind of party, the process, and a kind of standing, an operational one. Neither presupposes agency or moral consideration.

The same thought keeps the vocabulary apart from moral obligation. Processes need not owe one another moral duties for a system of them to enter a pathological regime of incompatible behaviour, one that takes intervention and a reorganization of their commitments to resolve its systemic frustration (CLAIM-tmpe6yfm). The claim is hedged in two ways. It does not say that no system of processes can bear duties, only that the description need not invoke them; and it does not say that every pathological regime is resolved by reorganizing commitments, since some need resources or new capacities (CLAIM-tmpe6yfm).

What forms, on this account, is an **organizational contract**:

> a maintained relation among the processes of an organization that governs how they interact, explicitly agreed or formed implicitly from relied-upon regularities, and that is contract-like only if it governs deviation, conflict and breach and not only the ordinary course

(TERM-tmplpplx). Two exclusions fix its sense. It is not a legal contract, for "an organizational contract may be enforced by nothing but the dependencies it has created". Nor is it the contractualist's contract, "an idealized agreement about what can be justified to each person" (TERM-tmplpplx). The two share a word and, as far as this article goes, nothing else; contracts in this sense bear on contractualism only as a contrast (QUESTION-483).

The questions are then four. What makes a relation among processes contract-like rather than merely regular (§3.2)? By what route can such a relation form without agreement, and when, if ever, does being relied on make something owed (§§3.3–3.5)? What structure does a contract give the processes party to it, and what standing (§3.6)? And what does a contract cost, to form, keep, use and leave, and what does using it do to it (§§3.7–3.10)? Sections 3.11 and 3.12 state what would test the account, and §3.13 what it does not establish.

The account has neighbours it should eventually answer to: assume–guarantee contracts in system design (Benveniste et al. 2018; LIT-tmpm8d98), relational contracts in the theory of the firm (Baker, Gibbons and Murphy 2002; LIT-tmp1xqzu), psychological and implied contracts in organizations (Rousseau 1989; LIT-tmpwdxhm), convention (Lewis 1969; LIT-473) and priority inheritance in real-time systems (Sha, Rajkumar and Lehoczky 1990; LIT-tmphhs41). Its departure from them is one of direction: it proposes "a contract reached from below, by the history of expectation and dependence, among parties that need not be agents able to agree" (CLAIM-tmp0d1fq). Whether that departure is new against these literatures cannot be said until they are read, and this article does not supply the comparison from memory. On the cost side, incomplete-contract theory is read: "the right to decide what the contract leaves out, the residual control right, belongs to the owner of the relevant non-human assets" (Grossman and Hart 1986; Hart 2016; THEORY-1979), and a relationship fair in every specified term can still leave one party's terms at the other's discretion (CLAIM-728). So are North's institutions, which with technology "fix the costs of transacting" (North 1991; THEORY-429), Arthur's lock-in (Arthur 1989; THEORY-863) and the lock-out of a firm that stops investing (Cohen and Levinthal 1990; THEORY-1518). Transaction-cost economics proper (Coase 1937; Williamson 1979), dynamic optimization and resource-bounded planning are not, and that comparison is open (LEAD-tmple60r).

*A note on method.* The article is a view over the Nucleation record, a maintained body of claims, cases, terms and questions. Each step cites in parentheses the entry that holds it, with its code; where an entry quotes an outside work, the work is cited as in a paper. What is computed below is a check of constructions: a three-process schedule, a dependency model, a norm model and a cost model priced partly from illustrative values. Each shows what its stipulations imply, and none is a finding about any organization. Where a step's firmness matters, the text says so. The standing of each entry, who contributed it, and how the argument was built are in the article's apparatus and the series README.

## 3.2 What makes a relation contract-like

If every stable interaction were a contract, the word would add nothing. "Mere behavioral regularity is insufficient to establish a contract-like relationship. Otherwise every stable causal interaction would qualify." What is needed is "evidence that the arrangement specifically governs counterfactual circumstances of deviation, conflict or breach", and four marks of it (CLAIM-tmp7hqtl):

1. "A maintained relationship establishing reciprocal expectations or requirements."
2. "Differentiated responses to satisfaction and violation."
3. "Governance of circumstances in which requirements conflict."
4. "Some mechanism sustaining or revising that relationship."

**The marks are necessary, not sufficient.** A relation is contract-like *only if* it governs deviation, conflict or breach (CLAIM-tmp7hqtl), and one that meets all four marks is "a strong candidate", not a contract. The term's definition says "deviation, conflict and breach", the claim "or"; as a necessary condition "and" is the stricter, and this article follows the claim (TERM-tmplpplx; CLAIM-tmp7hqtl). The marks serve here as a filter. A relation that fails one is not a contract; a relation that passes all four is a candidate.

Governing breach is not a formality. "A breach can lower the injured party's position if it refuses to settle, so that the breaching party gains leverage over the resolution of its own breach" (CLAIM-716). A relation whose terms say nothing about breach leaves that leverage to whoever breaches.

The filter is not yet a test, and a simple case shows why. Two controllers share a room, one heating and one cooling, with a deadband and a fixed rule that heating yields when both fire. They meet the first three marks, and the fourth if a tuning loop adjusts the deadband. "Whether that is a contract or a regulated regularity is exactly what the test was to decide" (CLAIM-tmp7hqtl). A further condition is proposed for it: "that the requirements be revisable without replacing the processes (the contract, not the participants, is what changes), and that a breach be registered as a breach of the relation and not only as a deviation of one process", with a test by intervention: change the terms, keep the processes, and see whether behaviour follows the terms (CLAIM-tmp7hqtl). The condition is offered "as the missing condition, not as settled". This article adopts the four marks as necessary, treats the fifth as a proposal, and does not use it as a premise. Its test has not been run (§3.12).

A richer schema is available, C = (A, G, J, Π, Σ, M, E, R): assumptions, guarantees, jurisdiction, priority policy, suspension, monitoring, enforcement and revision. It is "a proposed extension for our inquiry, not the standard definition of an assume–guarantee contract" (CLAIM-tmp7hqtl). Assume–guarantee contracts compose and refine on (A, G); nothing yet says how the other six components compose, "or ... that they reduce to (A, G) over a richer history space" (LEAD-tmpbqj9u). Until that is shown, the schema is a list of what a contract governs, not yet a structure.

## 3.3 From regularity to dependence

### The implicit route

Contracts include implicit ones: an unspoken understanding can form as expectations and assumptions built on consistently observed behaviour evolve into dependencies, with no agreement ever made (CLAIM-tmp0d1fq). The route has three steps, observed behaviour, expectation and dependence, and its scope is narrow. It is "not that every regularity, expectation or dependence is a contract": the route ends in a contract "only where the dependence comes to govern how the parties respond to its failure" (CLAIM-tmp0d1fq). That condition is the second mark of §3.2. The claim's defeater keeps it modest: it is not defeated by dependences that never became contracts, since "the claim is that the implicit route exists, not that every dependence takes it" (CLAIM-tmp0d1fq).

### Where an expectation can live

The route needs a sense of expectation that does not require representation. An expectation can be structural rather than represented: a process can be organized so that its working presupposes another's behaviour, and the presupposition can be realized in a capacity it has stopped maintaining, so that "it no longer needs, or no longer knows how, to do the thing for itself" (CLAIM-tmp18v1o). The case is a consumer that drops its buffer against a reliable producer: "Its organization now presupposes reliable delivery". This is more than a loss of capability, because the size of the gap "says which deviations by the producer the consumer can absorb and which it cannot, which is what an implicit contract's tolerances are"; and the claim's defeater asks for exactly that, a prediction the bare loss does not make (CLAIM-tmp18v1o).

A small dependency model shows the route. With service held at 1, reliance rises and independent capacity falls; withdrawn at step 40, "independent capacity at withdrawal is 0.42 and functioning falls to a minimum of 0.34" (CLAIM-tmp18v1o). The model would have failed as an illustration had functioning recovered on withdrawal. It is a model of unilateral dependency formation, "not yet the formation of a reciprocally maintained contract" (CLAIM-tmp18v1o).

There is one documented specimen of the mechanism outside a model. A provider "whose realized reliability exceeds its declared guarantee can lower the resilience of the organization built on it: those who depend on it stop maintaining the fallbacks the guarantee would require" (CLAIM-tmpb1wyc). Its ground is Google's global Chubby lock service: services added dependencies on its global instance on the assumption that it would never go down, so its rare outages reached users, and it is now held to meet but not significantly exceed its service level objective, with a controlled outage in any quarter in which true failures have not brought availability below target (Jones et al. 2016, Alvidrez's sidebar; CASE-tmp3xxqg). One case is an existence result and says nothing of frequency, and the arrangement is "not necessarily a mutually maintained implicit contract in our narrower sense" (ARG-tmptccms). The same mechanism has been proposed for constitutional conventions, where institutions rely on "historically reliable restraint beyond what law guarantees" and stop maintaining alternatives (CLAIM-tmpzas6q). Here the specimen does one thing: it shows a structural expectation in a documented system and not only in a model.

### One-way dependence and interdependence

Whether a dependence formed by loss is dependency or division of labour "is decided by the supply, not the loss": one-way supply set from outside "leaves a dependence that nothing in the dependent's dynamics limits and that withdrawal punishes, while reciprocal loss ... leaves interdependence" (CLAIM-603). The claim is held back by two cautions: its title states the favoured loss too generally, and one thesis it leans on, Moore's on the state that taxes its citizens, is not established (CLAIM-603). In symbioses, dependence, cohesion and host control come apart (SYNTHESIS-053); in one model of an incipient endosymbiosis mutual dependence evolves ahead of cohesion, "but only under the mapping" its authors chose (Athreya, Czuppon and Gokhale 2023; THEORY-1050). Biological dependence and reciprocal maintenance are therefore limiting cases, not examples of contract: a loss to reliable supply is dependency or division of labour according to who supplies it, which says nothing of terms that govern breach (CLAIM-603), and the demonstrated models of support that does the work are of a scalar capacity, not of a relationship (CLAIM-602; CLAIM-675).

No sequence of stages from regularity to contract is established, and this article proposes none. The nearest is a six-step ladder of normalization, from "An exceptional accommodation" to "Enforced: Its withdrawal is treated as a violation" (CLAIM-tmp0i6c0), which ends in enforcement, not contract. What the argument uses are four distinctions, not stages every relation passes through: *regularity*, which is not enough (CLAIM-tmp7hqtl); *expectation*, structural expectation included (CLAIM-tmp18v1o); *dependence*, one-way or reciprocal (CLAIM-603); and *contract-likeness*, the necessary marks (CLAIM-tmp7hqtl). Reciprocity is not a condition of contract. The first mark asks for "reciprocal expectations or requirements", which is not reciprocal loss.

### Relied on is not owed

The ladder's second part keeps the descriptive account apart from the moral one. Repetition and reliance can turn a discretionary contribution into an operative obligation, whose withdrawal is treated as a breach, with no agreement ever made; but being expected, being relied on, being committed and owing are distinct, and "the first three can contribute to the circumstances under which a duty arises. None establishes that duty automatically" (CLAIM-tmp0i6c0). "One cannot necessarily acquire entitlement to another's activity simply by reorganizing oneself around its continued availability" (CLAIM-tmp0i6c0). The distinction rests on a more general one: "a demand's being asserted, maintained or recognized gives it no warrant, and a commitment is neither an obligation nor an authorization" (CLAIM-738). The contract in operation, C^op, and an arrangement that meets the relevant justified standards, C^just, need not coincide, and "there is no reason to presume that these coincide" (CLAIM-tmp0i6c0). Nor is a normalized contribution exploited by being normalized: "it could be a fair division of labor" (CLAIM-tmp0i6c0). Obligation without agreement is familiar in another register: after Mauss, "a gift carries no legal guarantee of return yet obliges one" (Heins, Unrau and Avram 2018; THEORY-1474).

Reliance also opens room for exploitation, in one of two senses of "expose", which the argument keeps as alternatives. On the first, reliance exposes the party who relies: relied-upon expectations "create opportunities for asymmetric relationships, which exploitation takes up" (CLAIM-tmp05a1l). On the second, exploitation exposes, in the sense of reveals, "the relied-upon expectations an implicit contract kept tacit" (CLAIM-tmppx64t). The first is about the opportunity reliance opens, and is "not defeated by reliance that is never exploited"; the second is about what exploitation makes visible. Neither says that a normalized contribution is exploited. What separates exploitation from dependence and from domination, and which declared standard gives the verdict, is open (QUESTION-468).

When reliance does make something owed is also open. Four grounds are candidates: knowing inducement, reasonableness, the relied-upon party's control of what the reliance now needs, and vulnerability (QUESTION-tmprudey; the last as CLAIM-671's supposition). In a case of a volunteer they "give different verdicts" (QUESTION-tmprudey). This article does not choose among them. When it speaks of a contract transforming the participants' alternatives and vulnerabilities, it speaks descriptively.

## 3.4 Operative norms, and how they outlast their reasons

The ladder ends in an operative norm. An expectation is an operative norm rather than a prediction when a discrepancy is met by bringing the behaviour back into line with the expectation instead of revising the expectation to fit the behaviour; and which response a discrepancy gets, adaptation, enforcement, accommodation or revision, "is the work of normative governance" (CLAIM-tmphq6hp). The first half says what makes an expectation a norm; the second, that governing a norm is choosing among four responses, revision among them. It is a direction of fit applied to expectations (CLAIM-tmphq6hp; TERM-151). An operative norm need not be a legitimate one (CLAIM-tmphq6hp). For a discrepancy to change what an organization does at all, "three distinct achievements are needed and any can fail while the others hold": legibility, volitional efficacy and correctability (CLAIM-523). Any response to a violation, corrective or revising, presupposes them.

### A norm stabilized by the reliance it attracts

A norm can be stabilized by the reliance it attracts: compliance makes a practice predictable, others specialize around it, and their stake in its continuation funds its maintenance, which raises compliance; so a practice established past a threshold can persist where a weakly established one would not, and "can outlast a weakening of the reasons that first supported it" (CLAIM-tmpjg5gx). The claim comes with a model: x′ = σ(b + λg) − x, r′ = α(x − r), g′ = β(rx − g), with compliance x, reliance r, maintenance resources g, a baseline tendency to comply b and a feedback strength λ.

In this model, at b = −3 and λ = 8, there are three equilibria: 0.048 and 0.993 are stable and 0.689 is a saddle. Started at x = r = s, g = s², "the system goes to the high state only for s above ≈ 0.689, the saddle", so "the 'sufficiently established practice' has to be established past roughly 69% compliance" (CLAIM-tmpjg5gx); the threshold is traced for starts on that diagonal only. On persistence, an established norm "holds above 93% compliance while unaided compliance falls from 27% to about 1.3%", and "below b ≈ −4.33 only the low state exists" (CLAIM-tmpjg5gx). In the model, then, an established norm outlasts most of its baseline support, though not all of it.

An objection presses further: norms do persist long after their original justification disappears, as a race-based difference in power in the United States outlived the theories of property and rights that first justified it (CLAIM-tmp8l5s9; CASE-tmp3euq0). The answer is a distinction. A norm's original justification and its baseline support come apart: the justification can vanish while the norm keeps support from replacement rationales and from the interests it serves, so a norm that outlasts its justification need not have outlasted its support, and a bistable norm "ends by a push past its threshold rather than by fading" (CLAIM-tmps8ou2). The historical case does not by itself show the reliance loop: "the norm was also maintained by violence, by disfranchisement and by the interests of those it served", and the case "does not separate them" (CASE-tmp3euq0); its dates are recalled and have not been checked against sources (CASE-tmp3euq0).

Two further limits apply. The model's "maintenance rule is supplied by the modeler" (CLAIM-tmpjg5gx), so it shows a candidate stabilizer, not how a standard forms. And it is a model of a norm, not of a contract. The step to contracts is a claim of its own, narrowed to where it holds: an implicit contract's operative obligation, once normalized by the recipient's reliance, can be held by that reliance past the provider's original reasons for the contribution, **where the provider cannot cheaply exit** (CLAIM-tmpu77ve). The condition on exit is what the step adds. Where the provider can leave cheaply, enforcement funded by the recipients' stake drives the provider out instead of holding the obligation. The claim inherits the norm model's limits and has not been tested.

## 3.5 Complementary adaptation

Some relationships form through enactment rather than reliance. An interaction can make available a way of relating that its participants' operative perspectives did not hold, by giving each a role to enact in it, so that taking part can come before, and help produce, the reinterpretation it later seems to express (CLAIM-430). Franklin's request to borrow a rival's book is the case: a favour asked is a perspectival invitation (CLAIM-430; CASE-143). The claim limits itself, "Invitation is not establishment", and neither of its defeat conditions has been tested (CLAIM-430). It is the closest point of contact for implicit contracts: "An interaction can invoke an existing contract, invite a new contract, or ambiguously do both" (CLAIM-430), the last of which is the constitutive indeterminacy of §3.7.

What is tolerated is the other half. A perspective's tolerance for another process's contribution is its criterion for conferring influence on that contribution, set by its own priorities: "tolerances are directed", and meeting one "is necessary for that conferral where the criterion binds, and sufficient only for a grant, under a rule that grants influence on it" (CLAIM-552). A directed tolerance is "very close to a conditional service specification", though "receiving a benefit is not sufficient to establish a contract" (CLAIM-552). Two extensions are proposed and not made: that tolerances and grant conditions "evolve through the history of interaction", giving "endogenously acquired contractual terms", and that an error budget is a tolerance agreed between perspectives (CLAIM-552). The first would supply the formation step this section lacks; it has a design and has not been run (LEAD-tmpobng3). So directed tolerances are established, and their learning and agreement are proposals. That a learned, agreed tolerance is a contract is not established.

Three looser neighbours sit nearby. A recurring conflict that admits a stable accommodation can be settled among the processes more cheaply than by escalation, "though local negotiation and the relations it needs are not free" (CLAIM-358); the marks of §3.2 say what the settled accommodation must do to count as a contract. A heuristic can be a compiled contract, a protocol that settles a recurring conflict among processes without renegotiating it, and some biases can be such a protocol still governing where its assumptions no longer hold (CLAIM-tmpysg95), offered as a candidate mechanism "rather than established explanations of every instance". And an interface that learns to be steered by its user forms a new channel, "a maintained capacity" (CLAIM-788). Only the compiled protocol is said to be a contract, and it is compiled, not enacted. This article does not say that enactment forms contracts.

## 3.6 Contractual structure and operational standing

### The vocabulary

Three terms carry the structure. **Precedence, jurisdiction and suspension**: "among applicable constraints, which one controls? ... which constraints count as applicable to this case? ... when may an otherwise applicable constraint be disabled?", which is "not a ranking of rules" (TERM-017). A **grant** is a conferral that "runs through a maintained relation of granting, delegation, eligibility or support" (TERM-101), and a **conferral** is "any effect one process has on another's share of future influence" (TERM-103; SYNTHESIS-050). And the precedence a motive claims over action is distinct from the authority the rest of the organization grants it (CLAIM-283), a distinction stated for motives and applied here to processes.

### Operational standing

The standing a contract can operationalize is a process's

> maintained eligibility, under an organizational arrangement, to have specified claims upon another process's activity or upon their shared governance become consequential. This need not imply moral consideration, consciousness or personhood.

(TERM-tmpgiy6r). Four quantities "can come apart": asserted claim, operational standing, effective authority and moral standing. "A process might have contractual standing but be systematically starved of resources." And standing is **not reducible to priority**: "A low-priority process could possess a protected guarantee of eventual service, an entitlement to escalate a missed deadline, or immunity from arbitrary cancellation. Standing concerns the structure of recognized claims, not simply one's position in a queue" (TERM-tmpgiy6r).

This narrows the starting point of §3.1, which offered a prioritization structure as the way a contract operationalizes standing. "The prioritization structure is one way a contract secures standing, not what standing is" (TERM-tmpgiy6r), and the claim it narrows never said that a priority structure exhausts the standing it secures; its defeater would be a showing that every entitlement such a contract secures "is fixed by the ordering alone" (CLAIM-tmpkvzcn). Priority is one carrier of standing, not its home (TERM-tmplpplx).

At the scale of whole organizations, a parallel is stated for moral standing (TERM-083): "To have standing toward an agent is to have claims that can constrain what it is entitled to optimize, not only to enter its objective" (CLAIM-tmpa61rf). Entering an objective is to that standing what a place in a queue is to operational standing. The two are stated separately, and this article does not join them. One thing is left open by the term and by this article: "What makes an eligibility 'maintained' and 'recognized' without a representing process is not said" (TERM-tmpgiy6r).

### Priority inversion: reallocation, not relief

Priority inversion is one of nine coordination pathologies proposed as candidate correspondences for organizations, each "with its own mechanism and remedy" (CLAIM-tmpzuzeh). A construction shows it (CASE-tmptpna1). Three processes share one preemptive processor. L holds a lock that H needs; H must finish by time 5; L needs four units of work, M five and H one. Only the arbitration rule changes: fixed priorities in one run, priority inheritance in the other.

Under fixed priorities the completions are "M at 6, L at 9, H at 10"; under inheritance, "L at 4, H at 5, M at 10" (CASE-tmptpna1). Inheritance "takes five units of delay off H, and off L, and puts four on M", and "the total delay summed over the three processes falls by six". No schedule does better for both H and M, since ten units of work on one processor with H done by 5 leave M finishing at 10 at the earliest. So the case shows governance-induced frustration in CLAIM-357's sense "only because M has no deadline"; give M any deadline before 10 and "inheritance only chooses which commitment fails". For M the remedy is "a reallocation of delay, not a costless gain" (CASE-tmptpna1). That is a general point: "dropping a demand is not relief" (CLAIM-556). Inheritance relieves H only in that conditional sense.

How to describe H's position under fixed priorities, recognized and unable to act, is not settled. The construction reads it as CLAIM-283's granted authority without efficacy; the term of operational standing maps CLAIM-283's split onto asserted claim against effective authority, which puts granted authority on the effective side (CASE-tmptpna1; TERM-tmpgiy6r). This article takes neither reading.

### Contracts the ordinary course does not reveal

The two arbitration rules give one schedule whenever L holds no lock H needs, by the definition of inheritance, which acts only on contention. That makes the construction a separating case for a wider claim: an organization's characteristic structure can include the contracts maintained among its processes, which its ordinary behaviour does not reveal, since "two arrangements alike on the ordinary course can part when a contract is breached, an assumption fails or a process invokes its standing" (CLAIM-tmpersgx; ARG-tmp2dpcv). For implicit contracts, six probes part arrangements alike on the ordinary course: interruption of the relied-upon behaviour; a substitute provider; competing demands on the provider; removal of an enforcer; withdrawal of a discretionary contribution; and a request to renegotiate. "Only the responses to these show 'whether a dependence is regulated, by what, and on whose terms'" (CLAIM-tmpersgx). Whether the difference belongs to an organization's character, and not to its circumstances, is Article 1's question.

### What this article does not bridge

Operational standing presupposes no moral standing, and nothing connects a process's operational standing to a party that can be dominated, whose standing is moral (TERM-124; TERM-083). The hedge of §3.1 points one way: processes need not owe one another duties. So does the remark that "a scheduler may systematically starve a process. That can constitute a functional pathology without constituting an injustice to the process itself" (CLAIM-tmpe6yfm).

The far side of the gap is developed: what sorts a dependence "is who controls the terms on which it is met, exit, renegotiation and the conditions of supply", not its magnitude (CLAIM-690), and a dependence is presumptively dominating when an important sphere rests on it, its controllers hold "consequential discretion", and the dependent lacks "effective means of protection, influence, challenge or justified withdrawal" (CLAIM-682; CLAIM-686; SYNTHESIS-092). But these are claims about parties with moral standing. This article makes no claim about domination among processes; the bridge is open, and it is Article 9's.

## 3.7 Ambiguous relational invocation

An act can be ambiguous not about what it requests but about **which relational organization is being enacted through it**. When an act could invoke more than one relationship, the uncertainty is of three kinds needing different remedies (CLAIM-tmp80ny9):

| Kind | What it is | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Epistemic ambiguity | "A determinate arrangement exists, but its applicability is uncertain." | more information |
| Relational disagreement | "Participants embody incompatible specifications of their relationship." | negotiation or arbitration |
| Constitutive indeterminacy | "No established arrangement yet determines what the relationship requires." | forming a contract |

The claim would be defeated by a kind resolved by another kind's remedy alone, or by a showing that the three cannot be told apart "by any observation of the parties' conduct under perturbation" (CLAIM-tmp80ny9).

A second division runs over Γ, the joint specifications compatible with what each party treats as governing: one admissible specification is a determinate relationship, several is contractual ambiguity or under-specification, none is incompatible local specifications. **The two divisions do not coincide** (CLAIM-tmp80ny9). An empty Γ is relational disagreement. "Several admissible specifications" covers both epistemic ambiguity and constitutive indeterminacy, and what tells those two apart "is whether some arrangement is actually maintained, a fact of maintenance provenance" (CLAIM-tmp80ny9; TERM-100), which a deviation probe of §3.6 can show. The article uses the three kinds, treats Γ as identifying only relational disagreement, and treats the probes as what separates the first kind from the third. The perspectival invitation of §3.5, which "ambiguously" invokes or invites, is constitutive indeterminacy resolved by formative invocation (CLAIM-430).

The kinds come from a theory of utterances. The test that separates ambiguity from contextuality for an utterance's readings (CLAIM-044) is moved from readings to the parties' specifications of a relationship, and where an utterance's act depends on its relations to roles, norms and expectations (CLAIM-151), the question becomes which relationship it invokes (CLAIM-tmp80ny9). Whether a relationship an act invokes is itself a communicative kind, "so that invoking a relationship can be misread, translated and transported as a communicative kind can", is open (QUESTION-tmpp8ue3; SYNTHESIS-068).

### Who resolves the ambiguity

A variable other than the degree of ambiguity can matter more: "who controls the resolution of its ambiguity, when resolution becomes necessary, and who pays for it". A party may "treat an employee's recurring accommodation as discretionary when asked to compensate it, but as obligatory when the employee declines to provide it", and so take advantage through the ambiguity without changing any term (CLAIM-tmpro73c). This is "different in kind" from residual control in an incomplete contract (CLAIM-728), because "no contingency is uncovered". It does not reach a verdict of exploitation, which needs a declared standard (TERM-123), and under-specification has productive uses (CLAIM-tmpro73c).

### Productive misunderstanding

One of those uses is a misunderstanding that is productive when identifying and clarifying the ambiguity it exposes is the detection of, and a reorganization around, a novel distinction that has become consequential (CLAIM-tmpupcws). Detection and reorganization are two achievements and can come apart; the novel distinction falls under constitutive indeterminacy, or under relational disagreement "when its resolution forms terms neither party held" (CLAIM-tmpupcws). Two qualifications bound it. A misunderstanding "dates the distinction's relevance rather than its existence", and clarifying can coarsen as well as refine (CLAIM-tmpm76n5). And, as an objection at full strength, "a misunderstanding can be productive because it is left unresolved", where each reading is satisfied on every case the cooperation meets (CLAIM-tmpgx2ej).

The two threads meet. Resolving an ambiguity that no arrangement yet settles forms a contract, and the same act has two outcomes: resolved jointly, it is a productive misunderstanding that reorganizes the parties around a new distinction; resolved by one party who controls when and how, it is exploitation through ambiguity **where the terms it sets fail a declared standard** (CLAIM-tmpisfi1). The final clause keeps exploitation relative to a standard. The claim makes a discriminating prediction, that "the share of benefit each party takes from the new terms tracks how far that party controlled the timing and terms of the resolution, not how ambiguous the relation was beforehand" (CLAIM-tmpisfi1), and the prediction has not been tested.

## 3.8 What a contract costs: the lifecycle as stored work

How a contract's formation, persistence and modification enter a model of stored work is an open question (QUESTION-tmpb7fx7), and half of it has an answer: they are held for any form. To price a contract, then, the article uses a general model of what any stabilized form costs. This section sets out the model and its contractual reading, which covers formation, persistence and modification; §3.10 takes up what the model does not cover, an invocation that changes what it invokes.

### 3.8.1 Stored work

> A stabilized constraint can be stored work: by replacing later work with cheaper operations it changes which activities are realizable within a fixed per-task budget, so amortization becomes capability expansion without any added degrees of freedom or resources; the expansion is conditional on the costs of constructing, keeping, retrieving, verifying and revising the constraint and on the distribution of later demands, and the persistence that buys it can entrench (CLAIM-761).

Its parts were in place before it. Retrieval may be "a kind of amortized computation" (QUESTION-095). "A stabilized specification is past inquiry stored so that later questions become cheaper to ask, and it can make other questions harder" (CLAIM-475). "Persistence is not amortization, which is shown only when earlier work replaces later computation, communication or recruitment" (CLAIM-443). And an accounting: filing an argument "moves work across time ... so it is worth its cost only when expected reuse covers it" (CLAIM-303).

The new step is from totals to a budget. A question costing 12 from scratch and 3 with an acquired framework is out of reach under a per-task budget of 5, and then within it, "whether or not its construction has yet been repaid"; "that is CLAIM-571's point that a threshold is a peak, not a total" (CLAIM-761). Two failures are kept apart: an operational one, which defeats the claim, and an economic one, which "defeats only the stronger reading that the investment was worthwhile, which the claim does not make" (CLAIM-761). The expansion is relative to a budget, and whether such an intervention creates a capability the system lacked "rather than make one it already had cheaper" is open (QUESTION-392). Since an added hard constraint "cannot enlarge the set of admissible trajectories" at fixed dynamics (CLAIM-582), a stabilized constraint that lowers a cost has "changed the mechanism: it supplies cheaper operations ... and not only a restriction" (CLAIM-761).

The claim's scope is narrow. It does not support "that every emergent organization is such an investment", and "some persistent structure saves no work" (CLAIM-761; CLAIM-443; CASE-116). Nor is the expansion an expansion of freedom: whether "newly affordable operations widen substantive freedom, and for whom, is CLAIM-352's question" (CLAIM-761; CLAIM-352). And "stored" does not mean "slow": dynamical and economic separation "come apart both ways" (CLAIM-755).

Two instances bear on contracts. Learned local accommodation (§3.5) is "a fourth kind of stored work", one that "stores neither an answer nor an index" (QUESTION-095). It is an amortization in CLAIM-303's sense, a lower total over repeated encounters, but it names no per-task budget, so it is not stored work in CLAIM-761's sense. And a standing grant "pays it once for a domain before any question in it is formulated" (CLAIM-1016).

### 3.8.2 Three requirements and one cost model

"A form has at least three resource requirements, to construct it, to maintain it, and to move into or out of it, and none fixes another: a capability can be cheap to keep and costly to acquire, so having enough to sustain it is not having enough to acquire it" (CLAIM-569). In the construction, under a surplus of 3 a year, capability X (24 to build, 2 a year to keep) can be sustained and not acquired, and Y (6 to build, 4 to keep) acquired and not sustained: "construction and maintenance rank in opposite orders" (CLAIM-569; SYNTHESIS-112). The claim says "at least three", and that none fixes another, not that they move oppositely.

A cost model makes the requirements operational (CASE-326). Its terms were fixed before any case was priced: construction F at a price of work λ_F; upkeep U; the chance π that the store serves a demand; retrieval ρ and the cost of a miss; verification v with detection chance δ; staleness σ, loss L and revision R; erosion ε of the on-demand route; spill; the on-demand cost c; the price λ and budget B at the moment of demand; and "party", who pays each item. It reports feasibility, lifetime and distribution as three separate verdicts. Its criterion was stated in advance: "A case it cannot price without a new term is a counterexample to the single trade" (LEAD-108). The three cases were the three proposed as a test of whether one trade prices them all (QUESTION-114). Index selection in physical database design is a neighbour without the first term: the tools choose "by searching configurations costed with the query optimizer's own estimates", with "no term for the one-time cost of building" (Piatetsky-Shapiro 1983; Finkelstein et al. 1988; Chaudhuri and Narasayya 1997; THEORY-345). F is exactly that term.

With its terms as fixed, the model priced a cockpit's speed bugs, a filed argument and a LEGO bin "without adding a term for any of them", once work is "priced by the moment it is done and checked against the budget for each demand, not only summed" (CLAIM-891; CASE-326). At its illustrative values, as the approach's workload rises relative to the moment the bugs are set, "the bugs lose at 1 and 1.5 and win from 2"; pricing staleness turns the filed argument's "saving of 11 into a loss of 1 at three uses"; and for one brick among 200 similar ones the bin "pays at its first use", while for a brick that stands out "a bin never pays" (CASE-326). In the model, erosion costs "εc[(1 − π) + π(σδ + φ)]" per demand, so "it weighs little where demand is predictable, the store rarely wrong and checking cheap" (CLAIM-892). That the terms are complete is among what the model "cannot show", and so is any measured cost (CASE-326). One limit matters below: "ε is a fixed fraction, not a function of how long the store has been relied on" (CASE-326). For a capacity learned by doing, erosion that depends on the history of reliance is demonstrated in another model (CLAIM-602), and nothing yet puts the one into the other.

### 3.8.3 The contractual reading

Read as a stabilized relational constraint, a contract has the three requirements: "a construction (formation), a maintenance (persistence) and a transition (modification and exit) requirement, none fixing another" (CLAIM-569). The contractual reading applies the claim and adds nothing to it (CLAIM-569). The same move is available for inquiry: "a contract can be a materialized answer to a historically recurring question about how processes should interact" (CLAIM-475).

The cost model's terms read contractually as follows (ARG-tmpa5ped; CASE-326):

| construction, establishing reliable relational organization | F, λ_F | the first requirement (CLAIM-569) |

These costs are not shown to be independently variable. Independence is demonstrated for two pairs only: construction against maintenance (CLAIM-569), and holding against reaching (CLAIM-571; §3.9). The break-even is n > F/(c − ρ − v − u) (CLAIM-303), or n > 4.8 at F = 24, c = 8, ρ + v = 2, u = 1 (ARG-tmpa5ped). Where invocations leave the arrangement unchanged, as with "an explicit, externally enforced agreement", "the model applies as it stands" (ARG-tmpa5ped).

### 3.8.4 Who pays

"That an organization makes an activity cheaper for those who perform it does not show that work was saved: the work may have been transferred, to an earlier time, to upkeep, or to other parties ... work saved, work transferred and valuable capability expanded are three separate achievements" (CLAIM-760). In its separating case a claims office's cost per claim falls from 3 to 1 while each claimant's rises from 1 to 4, so "counted at the office, the work fell by 2 per claim; counted over office and claimant, it rose from 4 to 5" (CLAIM-760). The model's "party" is that boundary as a term. For contracts, savings that fall unevenly are proposed in three forms: a regulatory surplus "captured asymmetrically", "under the same visible harmony as a relationship that shares it" (CLAIM-tmp8bimm); compensation read as adequacy (CLAIM-700); and a commitment used as a resource (CLAIM-713). None is stated as a transfer in CLAIM-760's sense, and this article does not join them to it.

Work deferred is not among the movements: CLAIM-760 names only "an earlier time". Whether work moved later, possibly at a higher price, is a movement of its own is open (QUESTION-tmpzhnqy). One construction bears on it: when "the status quo irreversibly erodes the capacity to transform", waiting raises a transition cost from 40 to 70 (CLAIM-tmp8mpgs), though not in the cost model's terms and not framed as a movement of work.

The costs an organization's own accounting does not see include the friction "where an opportunity to intervene in a process is exposed" (CLAIM-710); "friction moves from the exercise of power to its contestation" (CLAIM-731); and explanation, contestation and interruption are separate entitlements (CLAIM-740). "The contesting cost a transfer imposes is CLAIM-731's subject" (CLAIM-760). The control over resolution of §3.7 names such a cost. It does not move the work of the accommodation, which the employee did all along; it moves whether that work is paid. So it is not work transferred in CLAIM-760's sense. Whether any of these transfers is exploitation needs a declared standard (CLAIM-760), and is Article 9's question.

One identification stays open, and this article does not close it: that cheap retrieval and amortized computation are the same trade is not established (SYNTHESIS-106). The wider chain from equivalence to recursive capability, in which "persistent organization replaces repeated work" is one step, is "a proposed sequence" and not asserted (SYNTHESIS-107).

## 3.9 Lock-in and exit

### 3.9.1 The geometry

"For a fixed architecture and one resource that must be held at each moment, the resource threshold for reaching a target configuration need not equal the resource requirement of the target: it is the least, over admissible transition paths, of the peak requirement along the path, and it is infinite when no admissible path joins the two" (CLAIM-571). In the example both endpoints cost 0.7 to hold and every path passes a peak of 1.3, so at a budget of 1.0 each is feasible and neither is reachable from the other; the gap of 0.6 is reconfiguration slack (TERM-106). For a cumulative expenditure, "a path costs its integral, not its peak", and the two can differ "by any factor" (CLAIM-646); the six resource types "bound different functions of a path and are not one budget" (TERM-105). Feasible, reachable and reliably reached are "three properties, each implied by the next and none implying it" (CLAIM-567). Connectivity "does not settle reachability among them", and under directed moves "the least budget for going from one configuration to another can differ from that for coming back", in one construction 1 one way and 3 the other (CLAIM-591). The claim says "can differ", not that they usually do. So the affordability of an organizational state does not establish the affordability of reaching it (CLAIM-569; CLAIM-571).

### 3.9.2 Contractual lock-in without preference

Applied to contracts, the threshold explains a familiar trap. Replacing a critical provider can require keeping two providers at once, rebuilding expertise and moving data, so "the new contract would be cheaper to maintain, but the organization cannot afford the transition. This produces contractual lock-in without requiring any preference for the existing arrangement" (CLAIM-571). The two providers held at once are the peak. The same reading suggests that "renegotiation readiness might itself need to be maintained as an organizational capacity" (CLAIM-571); what readiness costs is Article 2's subject, with its executed studies of reserve, redesign and use-following budgets (CASE-315; CASE-343; CASE-412; CASE-397).

"A relationship may be easy to enter, inexpensive to maintain and extremely expensive to leave" (CLAIM-tmpsf9hp). That sentence has more than one reading, and no directed revision cost is defined for it. It can be read as relational profiles, the incoming profile being "the cost of producing it from each other configuration" and the outgoing "the cost of turning it into each", since "a system may be easy to assemble but hard to change once assembled" (TERM-116); the profiles carry no index of who pays. It can be read as a peak threshold (CLAIM-571). But "TERM-116 defines relational profiles, not a revision cost, and no entry defines R(C → C′)" (CLAIM-569). Which cost a contract's exit is, a profile of costs, a cumulative cost under additive costs, or a peak along a path, is open: they can differ by any factor (CLAIM-646), and which applies depends on which resource binds (TERM-105). That entry is cheap and exit dear then follows from "can differ" and adds nothing to it.

The one organizational case of cheap entry and dear exit is an emergency regime: recruiting it, maintaining it and dissolving it are separable capacities, and an organization "that can enter exceptional authority more cheaply than it can leave it" has a structure in which "the regime can outlast its justification" (CLAIM-tmp0gs0t). Its cost of leaving is argued, not priced.

Other routes keep an arrangement with no budget shortfall and no preference. They are neighbours of the threshold, not instances of it. Under irreversibility, waiting to learn has an option value, and "when the status quo irreversibly erodes the capacity to transform, the ranking can reverse" (CLAIM-tmp8mpgs), shown at illustrative values. Increasing returns make adoption "a random walk with absorbing barriers", so that "once it has locked in no small change in returns alters later choices" (Arthur 1989; THEORY-863). Practical compulsion operates "without any rule requiring it" (CLAIM-677). And reliance on another's "historically reliable restraint" leaves no alternatives to fall back on (CLAIM-tmpzas6q; §3.3).

### 3.9.3 An obligation bars exit, and does not price it

Where reliance has made something owed (§3.3), leaving is barred, not priced. "A genuine obligation enters a cost model of exit as a condition on which transitions are permissible, not as a cost: withdrawal can be feasible and affordable yet impermissible until alternatives are arranged or the duty is transferred, so a party who controls the circumstances that keep the restriction in place controls the other's exit without raising its price" (CLAIM-tmpdu9v6). The geometry agrees: a normatively permitted path set gives b*_N ≥ b*, so a normative restriction "can raise this claim's threshold and never lower it, as CLAIM-582 says of any added hard constraint" (CLAIM-571). The last clause has a neighbour: "one party can wrongfully bring about circumstances in which another incurs a genuine obligation, so that the second does right in discharging it and is still wronged" (CLAIM-703). What separates such control "from ordinary reliance on someone's conscientiousness" is open (QUESTION-472; SYNTHESIS-099), and the claim does not say "that every obligation is a bar rather than a weight: defeasible obligations would need rules of priority" (CLAIM-tmpdu9v6). The four predicates this bar is one of, and costed reach indexed by actor and authority, are Article 5's (§5.6).

## 3.10 Stateful invocation

"A cached result normally substitutes for a computation without intentionally changing the underlying problem. Contracts can change the problem" (CLAIM-tmpsf9hp). Invocation is therefore stateful:

  Invoke(q, C_t) → (a_t, C_{t+1}),  C_{t+1} = Φ(C_t, q_t, a_t, f_t).

An organization's response kernel, K_S(q) = Law(a, τ, c, S′ | do(q), S) (CLAIM-1156), extends to a relational kernel, K_C(q) = Law(a, τ, κ, C′, S′ | do(q), C, S), where κ is "the distribution of costs among the participating processes" (CLAIM-tmpsf9hp). The post-state is observed "only through its later responses", not compared directly (CLAIM-1095).

The one operation divides by its effect (CLAIM-tmpsf9hp):

- *reproductive* invocation "exercises and maintains an existing relationship";
- *formative* invocation "participates in establishing a previously unformed relationship";
- *transformative* invocation "modifies the specification or governance of an existing relationship".

"A single interaction can instantiate all three" (CLAIM-tmpsf9hp). Two limits bound the claim. "It does not say what the right update rule Φ is", and nothing defines Φ. And the claim leans toward the null that would deflate it (below).

### What the cost model does not price

The hinge from stored work to contracts is this: invoking a relationship differs from reusing a stored result in that the invocation can change the relationship it invokes, so one operation can reproduce, form or transform a contract, and a stored-work cost model that holds the store fixed between uses prices a contract's lifecycle but not its invocation, which returns an act, a distribution of costs over the parties and the relationship's next state (CLAIM-tmpsf9hp). In the cost model the store "is the same before and after a use, and σ and ε enter as fixed parameters" (ARG-tmpa5ped). For a contract, the erosion of the parties' alternatives and the inadequacy of the arrangement can be "produced by its invocations", and then "the per-demand cost is path-dependent and the fixed-parameter formula misprices it" (ARG-tmpa5ped). This qualifies the model's scope, not its results: "for a store whose use changes it, σ and ε are not fixed" (CASE-326). The two halves of the cost argument fit. The lifecycle adds nothing to the three requirements (§3.8.3); invocation is what the model misprices. Where an agreement is externally enforced and invocation leaves it unchanged, the model applies as it stands. And the cost of resolving *which* relationship an act invokes is not one parameter: only epistemic ambiguity is a retrieval or verification cost (ARG-tmpa5ped), while relational disagreement and constitutive indeterminacy cost a negotiation or a formation (§3.7).

### The test, what is done, and the expectation

The test is designed and not run (LEAD-tmp14n26). It would make the store "a state updated by its own use", with ε and σ "functions of the history of invocations rather than fixed parameters", and then: build two relationships matched in per-use cost and output at the start, one whose invocations leave it unchanged and one whose invocations expand its practical jurisdiction and erode the recipient's alternatives; price each over a horizon, per party, including revision and exit at the end; and ask whether "time-varying ε, σ and R suffice" or a term of a new kind is needed.

Parts of it are done. Erosion that depends on how long a supply has done the work is demonstrated for a capacity learned by doing (CLAIM-602). Two forms of support that give the same capabilities now "can leave it different capabilities later" (CLAIM-675), which is the test's matched pair for a capacity rather than a contract. A state whose funding follows its own use has been run (CASE-343), and carried to rarely invoked guarantees: "the contract might remain formally recognized while its guarantee becomes unrealizable" (CLAIM-921). Budgets that follow use have been run as well (CASE-412; CASE-397), but there the arrangement's terms do not change by being invoked. What is not done is the contract case: an invocation that expands a relationship's jurisdiction, priced per party, with the question whether a new term is needed.

The expectation, stated before any result, favours the deflation: "the critical test is whether these require genuinely new cost terms, or merely richer state variables and transition mechanisms within the existing model. I suspect the latter will cover most of the cases" (CLAIM-tmpsf9hp). The outcomes are fixed in advance (LEAD-tmp14n26). The claim "is defeated" "if the two relationships' later costs never part, or part only by an amount the fixed-parameter formula with a re-estimated ε reproduces"; the richer-state reading is confirmed "if time-varying ε, σ and R suffice"; and a new term is needed "if a per-party cost of resolving or contesting the invocation does not reduce to them". If richer state suffices, an invocation that changes the store is the cost model with state-dependent parameters, and relational invocation names a reparametrization, not a new cost; the claim's own defeat condition anticipates that it may be "only a vocabulary" (CLAIM-tmpsf9hp).

### Where the kernel leaves the account

The kernel's claim is that "two organizations that give the same answers can differ in what answering does to them", observed "through its later responses rather than compared as a state" (CLAIM-1156; CLAIM-1095). The relational kernel extends it, and the update rule Φ that would say how invocation modifies the relational state is not defined. Whether relational invocation adds explanatory value beyond the existing kernel is therefore the same open item as the test's: a richer state within the model, or a new term. This article does not answer it.

## 3.11 Contractual identity

What makes a contract persist as the same contract through renegotiation, a change of the processes party to it and a substitution of how it is implemented is open (QUESTION-tmpp8flf). The proposed fifth condition of §3.2, requirements "revisable without replacing the processes (the contract, not the participants, is what changes)", is about telling a contract from a regularity, not about its persistence. The candidate answer is its converse: "replace the processes and keep the terms, then check whether the relation's responses to deviation survive" (QUESTION-tmpp8flf). The nearest test in hand, the substitute-provider probe of §3.6, "asks whether reliance concerns the function or its provider, not whether the contract survives the provider's replacement" (QUESTION-tmpp8flf). This section states a test and no thesis.

## 3.12 What would test the account

Most of what precedes would be tested together by one study: build systems exhibiting mere coupling, unilateral dependence, coordinated adaptation and maintained conditional commitment, matched on the ordinary course, and probe whether deviation produces different regulatory responses (LEAD-tmpl9349). No such systems have been built.

Its method exists. One executed study runs reciprocally produced, externally reset and feedback-only governing rules "with ordinary trajectories identical", and parts them by interventions on what maintains them (CASE-161); another identifies which of four known mechanisms maintains a rule from diagnostic probes and predicts withheld interventions, "within a known catalog" (CASE-163; CLAIM-tmpyp0ju). Both are constructions in a population model of governing rules; neither is an implicit contract. The extension is that design run on the four systems: the same probe battery, a predictor fitted on one system and tested on the others, and identification accuracy against no probe as the measure (LEAD-tmpl9349).

A comparison of relational structure against control-only models has also been run. A will-organization "whose relations of precedence, jurisdiction, suspension and override decide which regime becomes effective" wins "by 10 to 71 nats beyond its Occam cost" where the case is as stipulated, "alone predicts combined changes of authority never seen together", and "loses heavily where authority is idle or idiosyncratic" (CASE-325). Its relations are those of TERM-017, which the contract schema's J, Π and Σ share (§3.2). It answers whether relational structure adds explanatory value for authority in a stipulated organization, with a stated negative result, and not for contracts or invocation. Its comparison run on the four systems, the contract model against a co-adaptation model fitted on the ordinary course, is the natural extension. The rule-removal and pulse interventions of CASE-161 change a governing rule while keeping the processes, which is the shape of the change-of-terms probe; but they test what restores the rule, not whether behaviour follows changed terms, so that probe has not been run in any form (LEAD-tmpl9349).

Parts of the four systems are in hand: the dependency model of §3.3 (CLAIM-tmp18v1o), the arbitration rules of §3.6 (CASE-tmptpna1), the two controllers of §3.2 (CLAIM-tmp7hqtl), the six probes (CLAIM-tmpersgx), and governance architectures from fixed priority to a "reflexive contract ecology" with executed baselines (LEAD-tmpbqj9u; CASE-325; CASE-396; CASE-397). A shared organizational state (LEAD-tmpfq5cj) and a multi-agent version in which contracts form endogenously (LEAD-tmp5l71z) are further designs.

*Expectations, stated before any run.* Matched on the ordinary course, the four systems should part under the probes as follows (LEAD-tmpl9349):

- under interruption, mere coupling responds by its fixed rule only, and that response does not change when the terms are changed with the processes kept;
- unilateral dependence shows a fall in functioning and no correction;
- coordinated adaptation shows both parties' functioning fall;
- maintained conditional commitment recruits correction toward its terms.

What parts mere coupling from maintained commitment is then the change-of-terms probe, the proposed fifth condition. The account fails if the probes do not part the systems, or part them as well on the ordinary course. It also fails if the two controllers respond to a change of terms with processes kept exactly as a maintained commitment does, since the filter of §3.2 would then have no test behind it. These are expectations, not results.

## 3.13 Discussion: what the argument does not establish

The argument has two layers of different firmness, and they should not be confused. The cost geometry it borrows, the three requirements, the threshold as a least peak, the separations of feasible, reachable and reliably reached, the asymmetry of going and coming back, the cost model's run and its erosion term, and the shrinking of unaided reach under support that does the work, is demonstrated, in constructions. Every claim about contracts is a conjecture with a stated defeater, and no defeater has been tested. A reader who accepts only the demonstrated layer gets the vocabulary in which the argument is stated and the geometry it applies; that reader does not get the argument about contracts.

Within the contract layer, seven things are not established.

- **Sufficiency.** The four marks are necessary. What would make a relation a contract rather than a candidate is a proposed fifth condition with an unrun test (§3.2).
- **Obligation.** Reliance makes an operative obligation, not a debt. When it makes something owed has four candidate grounds that give different verdicts, and the article does not choose (§3.3). Which sense of "expose" fits exploitation is likewise left open.
- **Formation of terms.** That directed tolerances can be learned through interaction, and so supply a contract's terms endogenously, is proposed and not constructed (§3.5).
- **The bridge to domination.** Operational standing is not moral standing, and the well-developed account of domination concerns parties with moral standing. How the two relate is open (§3.6).
- **Exit's cost.** Whether a contract's exit is a profile, a cumulative cost or a peak is open, and no directed revision cost is defined (§3.9.2).
- **The update rule.** Nothing defines Φ, so stateful invocation has a form and no dynamics (§3.10).
- **Identity.** What makes a contract the same contract through change has a candidate test and no answer (§3.11).

Beyond these, the comparison with the literatures named in §3.1 has not been made. Whether contracts "reached from below" are new against relational and psychological contracts, convention, or transaction-cost economics is a question about readings not yet done.

The strongest objection is deflationary, and it comes in two parts that the argument itself sets up. The first: the vocabulary of contracts relabels regulated regularity. The two controllers meet all four marks, and until the change-of-terms probe is run nothing in the argument separates them from a maintained commitment. The second: relational invocation is "only a vocabulary". If time-varying erosion, staleness and revision suffice to price a contract whose use changes it, then the contract is a store with state-dependent parameters, and nothing in the account requires a new term; the expectation stated with the claim is that most cases will go this way (CLAIM-tmpsf9hp). Neither part has been answered. What the argument offers is that both are decidable, by stated studies with outcomes fixed in advance (§§3.10 and 3.12). Whether anything survives the second deflation, if it succeeds, is open. One possibility, which no claim yet states and which has not been tested as one, is that the distinctions of §§3.2–3.6 would still sort what the richer state must carry: whether a dependence governs its failure, whether reliance has become obligation, who controls the resolution of an ambiguity, and whether exit is barred or priced. Those are not cost terms. The first deflation, if the change-of-terms probe fails, would remove the ground for them.

## 3.14 Conclusion

Processes can come to stand in relations that function as contracts without anyone agreeing to them. The route is from consistently observed behaviour, through expectations that can be embodied in what a process no longer does for itself, to dependence; and it ends in a contract only where the dependence comes to govern how its failure is met (§§3.2–3.3). Being relied on is not being owed: reliance can make an operative obligation, but a debt only under a further principle (§3.3). An operative norm can be held in place by the reliance it attracts, in a model past most of its baseline support, and an implicit contract's obligation can be held the same way where the provider cannot cheaply exit (§3.4). Contracts give processes an operational standing that is neither moral standing nor priority, and can lie hidden until a breach, a failed assumption or an invoked standing reveals them (§3.6).

A contract costs what any stabilized form costs: to construct, to keep, and to move into and out of, none fixing another, and, in a model that prices illustrative cases, with who pays as a term (§3.8). Leaving can be unaffordable with no preference for staying, because a threshold is a peak along a path; and an obligation bars exit rather than raising its price (§3.9). The ambiguity about which relationship an act invokes is of three kinds with different remedies, and who controls its resolution can matter more than how ambiguous it is (§3.7). What the model does not price is that using a contract can change it: an invocation can reproduce, form or transform the relationship it invokes (§3.10). Whether that needs a new kind of cost, or only a richer state, is the article's central open question, and §§3.10 and 3.12 say how it would be decided. A companion article says what readiness to reorganize costs; this one says what a relationship between processes is, what it costs, and what using it can do to it.
