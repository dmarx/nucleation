# THEORY-tmpw39mh: Regulatory capture can operate through the evaluative framework a regulator adopts rather than through its decisions: when experts design a standard from an ideologically motivated view of the public interest that consistently marginalizes important public values, outcomes can favour an interest with no lobbying or improper exchange; US antitrust's consumer welfare standard is offered as a strong case

## Source

Short, Hill and Brown (2025), *Perspectives on Politics* 24(2): 680–700,
[LIT-tmpm7glb](../literature.d/LIT-tmpm7glb.md), read in [NOTE-tmpxvj22](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpxvj22.md).

## What was actually shown

**The definition.** Ideological capture occurs "when (1) regulators create
standards that (2) are based on an ideologically motivated (expert) view of
the 'public interest' and (3) consistently and repeatedly marginalize
important public values and goals." It is "not simply about what language
is put into any given standard; it is also about the public goals that are
left out of the standard." The actors "are more likely to be ideologically
motivated individuals, including academics and professionals, who benefit
from their perceived lack of industry ties." Note 4 separates it from
informational lobbying: ideologues act "not by leveraging an informational
asymmetry per se, but by developing ideological perspectives that redefine
the 'public interest'."

**The case.** The consumer welfare standard entered through the Supreme
Court's 1979 adoption of Bork's reading of the Sherman Act and the 1982 and
1984 merger guidelines, which were exempt from notice and comment, not
through legislation; expert support was bipartisan and parties did not
contest it. Merger challenges collapsed after 1981, and the authors know of
no evidence that merging firms lobbied the agencies: "If business interests
get what they want (merger approval) most of the time under the new
guidelines, we would not expect to find pervasive evidence of intense
lobbying." The survey ([THEORY-tmp1kuko](THEORY-tmp1kuko.md)) supplies the marginalized values
and the expert–public cleavage.

**Normative strength.** Capture "varying along a continuum from weak to
strong" by "(1) whether the experts promoting a given framework have any
special claim to scientific legitimacy (a question of truth); and (2)
whether the regulators elicited and considered public views before making a
decision (a question of process)". On both the authors place the standard
at the strong end.

What could have come out otherwise: the survey could have found the public
weighing prices as the standard does, which by the definition's third
condition would have ruled capture out ("so long as that framework does
not marginalize important public values, it does not meet our
definition").

## What this does not say

- **That the counterfactual is shown.** That the standard would have
  differed without expert influence is inferred from the cleavage and the
  history, not observed.
- **That the history is established here.** It rests on cited secondary
  work (Berman 2022, Teles 2008, Short 2022a and b, Kwoka 2014).
- **That disagreement is capture.** The definition excludes that
  expressly, and the drug-approval hypothetical is a framework the public
  might want replaced that would not count.
- **That capture is a loop.** The mechanism is a criterion, set once and
  applied thereafter. Nothing here says it reproduces itself, though the
  paper attributes the lawyers' values to professional training. That a
  framework can sustain its own expert class is the record's
  [CLAIM-tmpjo2zr](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjo2zr.md) and [TERM-tmpe6jtq](../terms.d/TERM-tmpe6jtq.md) territory, not this paper's.
- **That the public's values are right.** "We do not presuppose that any
  specific set of public values or goals are 'right.'"
