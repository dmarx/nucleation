# THEORY-tmp6743v: Across 1,176 premodern archaeological sites, the house-size Gini rises only weakly with the number of levels of political hierarchy and with polity population, and the spread of Gini widens with scale: scale raises the ceiling on domestic wealth inequality without setting its level

## Source

Gary M. Feinman, Gabriela Cervantes Quequezana, Adam Green and six
co-authors (2025), "Assessing grand narratives of economic inequality
across time", *PNAS* 122(16), [LIT-087](../literature.d/LIT-087.md), read in full with its eight-page SI
in [NOTE-049](../notes.d/NOTE-049.md).

## What was actually shown

**The data.** Site-level Gini coefficients of house floor area from the
GINI project's SiteGiniLevel file: 1,176 sites, 47,019 houses, several
world regions, each site-phase dated by the midpoint of its range. Polity
predictors (levels of hierarchy, population, governance) are assigned to
every site in the polity.

**Scale is a weak predictor (Figs. 1–2, S5; values read from the figure
panels).**

| predictor | all sites | apex | H50 | H100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| number of levels | r² 0.158 (n 1,164) | 0.218 (711) | 0.269 (170) | 0.326 (92) |
| log polity population | 0.107 (590) | 0.213 (243) | 0.085 (110) | 0.105 (66) |

All have P < 0.01. The scatters are heteroscedastic fans: the range of
Gini widens at three or more levels while the mean rises little.

**Who reaches the ceiling.** Every apex site in a polity of three or more
levels with Gini above 0.75 is in Southwest Asia (Assur, Babylon,
Zincirli, Nuzi, Tell el-Amarna), South America (Chan Chan, Galindo) or
Europe (Knossos). Collective apex sites have a median Gini of about 0.23
and autocratic ones about 0.41, read from a box plot with no test.

**Time.** No macroregion shows a consistent subregional trend. The largest
upticks postdate the first cities (Southwest Asia after about 3500 BCE,
Mesoamerica after about 500 BCE), so inequality did not rise immediately
with farming or urbanism (Fig. S2, from the text only).

**What could have come out otherwise.** Hierarchy and population could
have predicted Gini strongly, as stage and Malthusian narratives expect.
They explain at most a third of the variance in any subset.

## What this does not say

- **That governance sets the level.** The 0–3 autocracy score does about as
  well as hierarchy (r² 0.10, 0.18, 0.32), and "Hierarchical Clout", levels
  plus inverse governance, reaches 0.30–0.50. But governance was coded by
  the same regional experts at the same time as the house data, and one of
  its three dimensions is architecture, so it may partly read the outcome.
  A rise in r² from summing two predictors is not an interaction, and no
  two-predictor model was fitted.
- **That the P values are right.** Sites share polity-level predictor
  values and are treated as independent in OLS. There is no multilevel
  model and no control for region, period or house-sample size. The
  effective n is nearer the number of polities.
- **That collapse does not level.** Two cases go opposite ways (Roman
  England down, Teotihuacan up), and the authors say the sample cannot
  test it.
- **That house size is wealth.** It is one proxy. A single site can move
  far on method: Tenochtitlan's Gini is .13, .30 or .75 by sample
  adjustment.
- **That all grand narratives are "refuted".** The data show scale is not
  sufficient for high inequality; bivariate correlations cannot carry the
  paper's stronger wording.
