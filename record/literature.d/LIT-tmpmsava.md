# LIT-tmpmsava: What makes biological organisation teleological?

Mossio, M., & Bich, L. (2017). *Synthese*, 194(4), 1089–1114. First
published online 21 November 2014. DOI-10.1007/s11229-014-0594-z

Read in full in NOTE-tmppfo7y, from the accepted manuscript on HAL. Pages
below are the manuscript's.

## Key takeaways

- **Teleology through self-constraint.** Circularity is "necessary but not
  sufficient for intrinsic teleology" (MS p. 2). A system is teleological
  when its activity helps specify the conditions under which its own
  circular relation occurs, which it does by producing the constraints
  that act on it (MS pp. 16, 19).
- **Closure versus cycles.** A cycle is a chain of transformations under
  external constraints, at one level of causation. Closure is a set of
  constraints that maintains itself by canalising processes at another
  level: "by construction an inter-level causal regime" (MS p. 15). The
  water cycle does not generate its own constraints, so it is not
  teleological (MS pp. 16–17).
- **Division of labour is part of closure.** In closure "each component
  makes a specific and complementary contribution", and closure captures
  organisation "by virtue of the interplay between division of labour and
  mutual dependence that it implies" (MS pp. 10–11). The constraints under
  closure are the biological functions (MS p. 15).
- **Dissipative structures.** Whether they self-constrain is unsettled. If
  they do, they are "minimally teleological", generating "a single
  macroscopic constraint (the structure itself)". They "do not realise
  closure", are "not organised", and "their parts have no functions"
  (MS p. 19). No non-biological material system is known to realise
  closure, only abstract models such as autocatalytic sets (MS p. 18).
