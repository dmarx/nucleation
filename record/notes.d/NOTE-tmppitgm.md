# NOTE-tmppitgm: Beuschlein et al. — The ESE and Endocrine Society guideline on glucocorticoid-induced adrenal insufficiency

## Contribution

The clinical account of withdrawing an outside supply that has suppressed
the body's own production. Beside Broersen et al. (2015), which it uses
for prevalence and recovery (its ref. 24), it adds an updated review of
recovery after February 2014 (three cohorts, 202 patients), a review of
taper schemes (five studies), a stated mechanism that includes atrophy,
and the graded taper schedule now recommended.

## Key insight

Recovery needs the suppressed system to be given its work back: the
supply is cut to near the physiologic dose and then kept there only as far
as needed, because "the glucocorticoid dose should provide sufficient
replacement, but also a sufficient stimulus for recovery (meaning avoiding
any over-replacement)" (R 2.7 rationale). Recovery then runs centrally
first and adrenally after, slowly and variably.

## Assumptions

- A consensus guideline. Graded recommendations use GRADE; those "based on
  good clinical practice and experience of the working group members are
  not formally graded" (Methods). The taper schedule (Table 4) and most of
  the taper and testing advice are good clinical practice or very-low
  (⊕○○○) evidence.
- Recovery is defined biochemically (ACTH test, morning cortisol); in the
  recovery cohorts "signs and symptoms of adrenal insufficiency were not
  reported" (Clinical question I).
- The mechanism is cited from the literature, with the atrophy step from
  rodent models.

## Key results

1. **Mechanism** (Introduction, pathophysiology): "Inhibition of CRH and ACTH induced by
   exogenous glucocorticoids is similar to the mechanisms involved in the
   physiologic cortisol negative feedback". Prolonged supraphysiologic
   therapy "often leads to a reduction in the overall responsiveness of the
   anterior pituitary gland"; in rodent models pro-apoptotic effects on the
   pituitary end in "atrophy of the adrenal cortex". After withdrawal
   "there is resurgence of ACTH stimulation of the adrenal cortex", and "In
   most instances, the adrenal cortex will recover and produce adequate
   levels of cortisol", but "the time to full biochemical and clinical
   restitution of the HPA axis is highly variable".
2. **Always suppressed at therapeutic doses** (Introduction, pathophysiology): such doses
   "considerably exceed the equivalent of endogenous cortisol production
   and, therefore, invariably result in HPA axis suppression".
3. **Order of recovery** (R 2.6 rationale): the hypothalamus and pituitary
   recover first, raising ACTH; "Complete recovery of cortisol production
   can remain impaired in a minority of patients".
4. **Recovery time** (Clinical question I). Restates Broersen et al.:
   38.7% to 14.9% at 4 weeks, 56.4% to 25.3% at 6 months. New cohorts: Baek
   et al., recovery in 58.8% "after a median of 16 months"; Leong et al.,
   60.6% with "a median recovery time of 24 months"; Menzies-Gow et al.,
   10% after 3 months. Verdict: adrenal function "can recover in a time
   frame from a few months to up to 4 years in some cases", on low-quality
   evidence "due to heterogeneity and a serious risk of bias".
5. **Predictors** (Clinical sub-question Ia): only biochemical, a higher
   first ACTH-test increment or morning cortisol; no study assessed
   clinical predictors.
6. **Taper evidence** (Clinical question II): five studies, none with
   adrenal function as primary endpoint, none showing symptomatic
   insufficiency; "There were no studies identified comparing different
   tapering schemes." Elsewhere (R 2.6 rationale): "There is no compelling
   evidence to guide optimal tapering." The TOASST trial (abrupt stop
   against gradual taper from prednisone 7.5 mg) is ongoing.
7. **No taper for short courses** (R 2.1, ⊕○○○): courses "of <3-4 weeks,
   irrespective of the dose" can be stopped without testing.
8. **When to taper** (R 2.2, good clinical practice): only once the
   underlying disease is controlled and glucocorticoids are no longer
   required.
9. **The schedule** (Table 4, prednisone equivalent): above 40 mg, 5–10 mg
   weekly; 20–40 mg, 5 mg weekly; 10–20 mg, 2.5 mg every 1–4 weeks; 5–10
   mg, 1 mg every 1–4 weeks; at 5 mg, 1 mg every 4 weeks if symptoms are
   absent or testing negative. "HPA recovery is possible once the
   glucocorticoid therapy has been tapered to a near-physiologic daily
   dose". Graded good clinical practice.
10. **Withdrawal syndrome** (R 2.3 rationale): a separate dependence on
    supraphysiologic doses while the dose falls; severe symptoms call for
    a temporary return to the last tolerated dose.
11. **Testing** (R 2.7, ⊕○○○): morning cortisol after holding the dose at
    least 24 hours, read "as a continuum": above 300 nmol/L recovered,
    150–300 uncertain, below 150 "very likely to have persistent adrenal
    insufficiency". No recovery within a year on the physiologic dose:
    refer (R 2.11).
12. **Consequence at scale** (Introduction): in a Danish self-controlled series of
    286,680 people stopping at least 3 months of oral therapy, hypotension
    (IRR 2.5) and hyponatraemia (IRR 1.5) rose against before treatment.
13. **Exposure and recovery** (R 2.6 rationale): dose, type and duration
    are to be considered in designing a taper; no quantitative relation
    between exposure and time to recovery is given.

## Concepts

- **Therapeutic and endocrine taper.** A taper guided by the disease,
  then one guided by withdrawal and adrenal insufficiency.
- **Physiologic daily dose equivalent.** About 4–6 mg prednisone or
  15–25 mg hydrocortisone, the level of endogenous production; recovery is
  possible only near it.
- **Glucocorticoid withdrawal syndrome.** Symptoms from dependence on
  supraphysiologic doses while the dose falls, distinct from insufficiency.
- **Recovery as a continuum.** Morning cortisol read graded, not by a
  single cut-off.

## Connections

It restates Broersen et al. (2015)'s route, dose, duration and recovery
results (that paper's rows 2, 4–6) and its finding that no withdrawal
scheme is proven, and goes beyond it on recovery time, mechanism and
tapering. **Discrepancy:** Table 6 lists, under "Inhaled glucocorticoids",
duration strata of 1.4%, 11.9% and 27.4% and dose strata of 2.4%, 8.5% and
21.5%. Broersen et al. computed those in asthma patients of all routes;
their inhaled-only figures are 1.3%, 9.0% and 20.3% and 1.5%, 5.4% and
18.5%. The guideline's own introduction gives the figures correctly, "in
patients with asthma". The record's nearest held analogues are the
crowding-out of intrinsic motivation ([LIT-557](../literature.d/LIT-557.md), [THEORY-046](../theory.d/THEORY-046.md)) and legacies
blocking recovery ([THEORY-1468](../theory.d/THEORY-1468.md)).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md).** Supports its conditions on the threat having gone (a
  taper only once the disease is controlled, R 2.2) and on the party doing
  the work (replacement leaves "a sufficient stimulus for recovery"); and
  its point that persistence after withdrawal is what tells, since a test
  after holding the dose is the decision rule (R 2.7). Duration-graded
  risk (R 2.1, R 2.6) goes against the defeater that damage does not grow
  with length, qualitatively only.
- **[CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md).** A strong analogue: outside supply does the gland's work
  and its capacity falls through loss of trophic drive and atrophy; the
  remedy is the claim's, cover only what the party cannot yet do and
  avoid over-replacement. That longer supply means longer recovery is
  asserted for taper design, not quantified.
- **[CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md).** Qualifies: recovery is gradual, ordered and read as a
  continuum, slow reversion rather than a threshold or bistability; the
  persistent minority is the only hint of irreversibility, by atrophy, not
  a flux loop.
- **[CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md).** A contrast case: supplied upkeep can become optional
  again, typically within months to years, because the party makes the
  input itself.
- **[CLAIM-603](../claims.d/CLAIM-603.md).** Supports one-way supply that withdrawal punishes, shown
  at population scale (row 12); nothing on reciprocity.
- **[CLAIM-558](../claims.d/CLAIM-558.md).** Supports in physiological form: a capability falls into
  disuse while supplied and takes months to years to return.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md).** Gives an operational withdrawal test (R 2.7) and a
  graded partial withdrawal as the route to independence, for an existing
  organization rather than a nucleus.
- [CLAIM-601](../claims.d/CLAIM-601.md): no bearing.

## Limitations

- Recovery evidence: three cohorts, unstandardized retest timing,
  biochemical endpoints of unclear clinical relevance.
- Taper evidence: five studies, none with an adrenal primary endpoint and
  none comparing schemes; the schedule is consensus, and the graded taper
  and testing recommendations rest on very-low evidence.
- The atrophy mechanism is partly from rodents.
- Table 6 mislabels Broersen et al.'s asthma strata as inhaled-only.
