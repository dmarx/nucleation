# NOTE-tmpxyktj: Mischel, Ebbesen and Zeiss — Cognitive and attentional mechanisms in delay of gratification

## Contribution

Experimental evidence that how long preschoolers wait depends on where
their attention is during the delay, and that what works is avoidance or
suppression of attention to the rewards, not dwelling on them; with a
reinterpretation of self-control as transforming the aversive waiting
into something bearable.

## Key insight

Waiting is made easier by how the situation is organized, here by what
the experimenter supplies (a toy, an instruction), more than by effort
against temptation.

## Assumptions

- Delay of gratification is a frustration situation: cues that raise
  attention to a wanted but unavailable outcome raise its aversiveness.
- Children trust that the experimenter will return with the preferred
  reward; bell training, in which she returns at once when signalled, is
  part of every procedure.
- Instructions such as "think fun" change what children attend to.

## Key results

- **Abstract**: "children waited much longer for a preferred reward when
  they were distracted from the rewards than when they attended to them
  directly".
- **Experiment I**: with no distractor, under a minute; with a toy or
  "think fun", "the mean delay times in the latter two conditions being
  8.59 and 12 12 minutes, respectively" (OCR; read 12.12). With rewards
  in view and no distractors, "not a single child waited to criterion".
- **Experiment III**: told to think about the obscured rewards, "the mean
  waiting time was only .78 minute. These results utterly contradict the
  belief that ideating about the reward objects in their absence enhances
  voluntary delay".
- **Discussion**: "effective delay of gratification depended on cognitive
  avoidance or suppression of the reward objects during the waiting
  period"; "effective self-control may hinge on transforming the difficult
  into the easy, the aversive into the pleasant, the boring into the
  interesting".
- **Side study**: "the children chose the place to cover quite randomly
  before commencing their voluntary delay".
- **The contingency still governs** (discussion): the mental
  transformations "do not erase or undo the role of the reward
  contingencies"; distractors without a reward contingency kept no one
  waiting.
- **Scope** (discussion): "one obviously cannot generalize from them to the
  role of cognition in forms of self-control other than the
  delay-of-gratification paradigm".

## Concepts

Self-imposed delay; frustrative nonreward; overt and cognitive
distraction; reward salience; transforming the aversive into the
pleasant.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-tmp07hx9](../cases.d/CASE-tmp07hx9.md)** (the Mischel arm). Confirms it, with a correction: the
  attention was redirected by the experimenters, not by the children of
  their own accord, and the children did not know the strategy
  beforehand. That fits the case's point that "waiting depended partly on
  how the situation was organized", and shows waiting bounded by the
  reward contingency.
- **[CLAIM-344](../claims.d/CLAIM-344.md)** (resolve as a maintained organization), which the case
  illustrates. Consistent with the case's use of it, restraint as
  organization rather than force ("effective self-control may hinge on
  transforming the difficult into the easy"), but the paper is about
  attention in one paradigm, not about resolve; not filed as a relation.
- **[CLAIM-tmpt091d](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpt091d.md)**. Holds fixed the variable the claim turns on: bell
  training shows the experimenter returns, so reliability is not tested.
- **[LIT-554](../literature.d/LIT-554.md) / [NOTE-429](NOTE-429.md) / [THEORY-680](../theory.d/THEORY-680.md)** (Gross on emotion regulation). This
  is a classic instance of attentional deployment as a regulation
  strategy.

## Verdict on the exchange's characterization

Partly faithful. A51: "Those who redirected their attention away from the
tempting reward often waited longer than those who focused on it." The
direction is right, but in this paper the experimenters redirected the
attention; spontaneous self-distraction is reported from observation and
from earlier work.

## Limitations

Small groups (about 10 per condition), children of a university nursery
school, instructions from the experimenter (so attention cannot be
separated from demand), a 15-minute criterion, and OCR damage to the
tables.
