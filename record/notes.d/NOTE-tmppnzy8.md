# NOTE-tmppnzy8: Curry 2016, morality as cooperation: a problem-centred approach

## Contribution

The chapter ([LIT-tmpcsgn6](../literature.d/LIT-tmpcsgn6.md)) turns a commonplace into a taxonomy. Its Table 1 collects
fifteen entries, from Aristotle to Greene, who said that morality has
something to do with cooperation. It then uses the game theory of
cooperation to say which problems there are, and so how many moral domains
to expect: seven, one per game. Each domain is laid out the same way: the
game, the animal evidence, the human adaptations, the cultural inventions,
and quotations showing that moralists have praised the solution. Table 2 is
the "periodic table of ethics". Tables 3 and 4 argue that earlier reviews of
cooperation and earlier moral taxonomies (Fiske's relational models,
Shweder's CAD triad, Haidt's moral foundations) each omit or conflate some
of the seven.

## Key insight

If morality is the set of solutions to cooperation problems, then the list
of moral domains is not an empirical inventory to be gathered but a
consequence of the list of games. That gives the theory its reach and its
risk. A moral domain that corresponds to no game is a counterexample, and
the chapter names its candidates in advance (p. 40).

## Assumptions

- **Identity, not cause.** The solutions to cooperation problems "constitute
  human morality" (p. 29). "Morality" is the label philosophers have
  attached to them. This is a stipulation about what the word picks out. It
  leaves Bloom's question in [NOTE-034](NOTE-034.md), why cooperation is moralized rather
  than merely liked, outside the theory's scope.
- **Completeness of the game list.** The seven are the problems game theory
  had identified by 2016. The chapter expects new games to add domains
  ("extending the foundations", p. 40).
- **Two levels at once.** "Biological and cultural solutions" are treated
  together. A domain counts whether it is served by an evolved mechanism or
  by an invented institution, such as land registries for possession.
- **Positive evidence is illustrative.** Every "there is evidence to suggest
  that they are" (pp. 30–37) is followed by quotations from moralists and
  ethnographers, chosen to show that a solution has been praised. No search
  for contrary cases is described.

## Key results

The chapter reports no study. Its results are its taxonomy and its stated
predictions.

- **Seven problems** (pp. 30–37): kin allocation (Hamilton), coordination to
  mutual advantage (Lewis, Schelling, Skyrms), exchange (Trivers, Axelrod),
  and conflict resolution by hawk–dove contests (Maynard Smith and Price),
  division (Nash, Skyrms) and possession (Gintis, Maynard Smith).
- **Seven moral domains** (Table 2): obligations to kin and the incest
  prohibition; friendship, loyalty and conformity; reciprocity with trust,
  gratitude, guilt, apology, forgiveness and patience; hawkish virtues
  (bravery, fortitude, generosity); dovish virtues (humility, respect,
  obedience); fairness and compromise; property rights and the ban on theft.
- **Predictions** (pp. 39–40):
  - the seven are morally good and their failures morally bad;
  - "behaviour that has nothing to do with cooperation … will be regarded as
    morally neutral";
  - the seven are good "in every human culture, at all times and in all
    places", with no culture where morality "is about something other than
    cooperation — say, aesthetics or nutrition";
  - societies weight the domains by the problems they face (family size,
    warfare, inequality);
  - sexual, political, international and religious morality will turn out
    to be cooperative. Religious morality here includes "ancestor worship,
    food taboos, karma, reverence".
- **Against rival taxonomies** (pp. 42–43): Fiske, Shweder and Haidt conflate
  kinship with mutualism and exchange with division, and omit hawkish traits
  and possession. MFT's purity domain has "no apparent connection to
  cooperation" (p. 42).

## Concepts

- **Problem-centred approach**: deriving moral domains from a list of
  cooperation problems rather than from a survey of moral content.
- **Periodic table of ethics**: Table 2, problem × theory × animal examples ×
  human examples × morals.
- **Hawkish and dovish virtues**: the two halves of the display-and-defer
  strategy, after Hume's "heroic" and "monkish" virtues (p. 35).
- **Tools and rules**: cultural inventions that improve on natural
  selection's solutions (p. 29).

## Connections

- **The test.** Curry, Mullins and Whitehouse ([LIT-047](../literature.d/LIT-047.md)) test C3 in sixty
  societies. [NOTE-034](NOTE-034.md) judged that test weakly diagnostic because nothing
  non-cooperative was coded. This chapter shows that the omission matters on
  the theory's own terms: C4 is the theory's stated prediction, and the test
  left it out.
- **The questionnaire.** The MAC-Q ([LIT-tmp0moou](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0moou.md)) operationalizes the seven
  domains and, in its MFQ studies, finds that purity items are rated
  morally relevant, which its authors concede "appear[s] to contradict" the
  prediction that purity will not be.
- **Purity.** Fitouchi, André and Baumard ([LIT-101](../literature.d/LIT-101.md)) argue that puritanical
  norms are cooperative after all, because indulgence is believed to erode
  the self-control cooperation needs. That is a C5-style absorption, and the
  first one in the record that is worked out and tested. This chapter
  already lists patience among the components of reciprocity, citing Curry,
  Price and Price (2008) on cooperative people's lower discount rates
  (p. 33). That gives [LIT-101](../literature.d/LIT-101.md)'s mechanism a foothold inside the exchange
  domain. [LIT-101](../literature.d/LIT-101.md) itself reduces puritanism to harm and fairness concerns,
  and the MAC-Q argues that harm is not a domain. The two accounts therefore
  agree that purity is cooperative and disagree about where it sits.
- **Coordination.** The coordination domain rests on Lewis's *Convention*
  ([LIT-473](../literature.d/LIT-473.md)), which the record holds unread.
- **Obligation.** Tomasello appears in Table 1 only through Tomasello and
  Vaish (2013). His "The moral psychology of obligation" ([LIT-125](../literature.d/LIT-125.md)) gives an
  account of why obligations feel binding, which is the proximate story this
  chapter does not give.
- **Why cooperation pays.** Peters and Adamou ([LIT-035](../literature.d/LIT-035.md)) offer a different,
  variance-reduction account of why cooperation pays. This chapter's games
  are the standard expected-payoff ones, and it does not engage that
  alternative.
- **Not held here.** Haidt and Graham's moral foundations, Fiske's relational
  models, and the evolutionary sources (Hamilton, Trivers, Axelrod, Nowak's
  five rules in Table 3) are being filed in parallel by other contributions.

## Bearing on the record

There is nothing here for ML practice, and the anthology does not hold the
chapter. For the morality-as-cooperation thread it is the source to cite for
what the theory predicts. Two of those predictions go beyond what [LIT-047](../literature.d/LIT-047.md) and
[LIT-tmp0moou](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0moou.md) test: that non-cooperative content is morally neutral, and that
nothing outside cooperation is moralized in any culture. A THEORY document
built on MAC should quote p. 40, and should say that no study in the record
has tested it yet.

## Limitations

- **No data.** Every "there is evidence to suggest" is a set of selected
  quotations.
- **The falsifier is pre-absorbed.** The same page that names "nutrition" as
  a domain no culture's morality will be about predicts that food taboos
  will turn out cooperative (p. 40). Until the cooperative account of a
  given taboo is written and tested, the prediction cannot fail.
- **The count of seven is open-ended.** New games add domains, so finding a
  further cooperative domain does not count against the theory. Only a
  non-cooperative domain would.
- **The definition carries the theory.** Because morality is identified with
  the cooperative solutions, the chapter has no account of what makes a
  solution moral rather than merely preferred or useful. That is Bloom's
  question in [NOTE-034](NOTE-034.md), and the chapter does not address it.

## Open questions

- Coded blind and in the same sample, are non-cooperative domains (purity,
  sexual conduct, treatment of the dead, harm to strangers) moralized as
  often as the seven? This is [NOTE-034](NOTE-034.md)'s open question. This chapter makes it
  the theory's own test.
- For which food taboos, if any, has a cooperative account been written and
  tested?
- Does domain weighting track the ecological variables named on p. 40?

- **The citation.** The owner's citation (in Shackelford and Hansen, eds.,

  resolution is still three of the seven domains.
- **The hawk–dove attribution.** The chapter credits hawk–dove contests to
  Maynard Smith and Price 1973. That paper ([LIT-tmpex4cg](../literature.d/LIT-tmpex4cg.md), [NOTE-tmpjvb5a](NOTE-tmpjvb5a.md)) has
  no Hawk–Dove game: its strategies are Mouse, Hawk, Bully, Retaliator and
  Prober-Retaliator, between identical contestants. It also gives Hamilton
  1964's pages as "1–16, 17–52" with only Part II's DOI.
