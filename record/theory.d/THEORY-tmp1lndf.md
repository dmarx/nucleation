# THEORY-tmp1lndf: Expert texts of Shamatha, Transcendental and Stillness Meditation describe their so-called contentless goal-states as lacking thoughts, perceptions, images and memories but keeping many forms of content, and as differing across the practices in attentional stability and vividness

## Source

Woods, Windt & Carter (2022), [LIT-tmpg6f7h](../literature.d/LIT-tmpg6f7h.md), read in full in [NOTE-tmpz0x5w](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpz0x5w.md)
from the submitted preprint on MindRxiv (osf.io/ve7z2, 2021): method
(§2, pp. 5–8), results (§§3.1–3.18, pp. 8–38), discussion (§§4.1–4.5,
pp. 40–45), limitations (§4.9, p. 47). The version of record was not
read.

## What was actually shown

The authors selected 135 publications by eight teachers: Alan Wallace for
Shamatha, five TM authorities, and Meares and McKinnon for Stillness
Meditation. They extracted every passage about the goal-state, coded each
by feature, and compared the practices feature by feature (pp. 5–7).

- **Absent.** In all three, the goal-state is described as having no
  thoughts, concepts, images, memories, awareness of the meditation
  object, or sense and body perception (only very dull perception in
  Stillness Meditation's shallower states) (§3.2).
- **Present.** 51 of the 65 features appear in all three traditions
  (p. 40). Among them are being conscious and awake with heightened
  wakefulness, stillness, silence, simplicity, naturalness, calm,
  relaxation, bliss or joy, a knowing distinct from knowledge of things,
  freedom, unity and depth (§4.5, p. 44).
- **Different.** Shamatha's goal-state is described as having perfect
  stability and vividness of attention, holding for hours; TM and
  Stillness practitioners cycle in and out of theirs (§3.16). Together
  with attentional balance and Stillness Meditation's very dull
  perception, these are the four differences found (p. 40).

The comparison could have come out otherwise: the texts could have
described the states as lacking everything but being conscious, as one TM
author (Shear) sometimes does (p. 7), or described the three states alike
on every feature they mention. On the majority descriptions, they did
neither.

## What this does not say

- **It is not a finding about experience.** The data are teachers'
  descriptions, some of specific episodes and many generalised, written
  by people who teach or promote the practice. The authors name this as
  the key limitation (p. 47). No practitioner was asked anything.
- **It does not define content.** "Not truly contentless" (p. 44) counts
  qualities such as calm, simplicity and depth as content. A view on which
  only intentional or representational content counts can accept the
  texts and deny the conclusion.
- **It does not show the states are mostly different.** 47 of 65 features
  are unclear, and three of the four differences concern attention. 12 of
  the 14 shared features are absences or the bare facts of being
  conscious and awake.
- **It says nothing about interpretation as a mechanism.** Two passages
  touch it: Wallace's view that "implicit or subliminal
  conceptualization persists" and shapes the state (p. 36), and the texts'
  statement that two meditators "may have the same experience but
  interpret it differently" (p. 26). The paper analyses neither.
- **The coding was done mainly by one author**, a long-time Stillness
  Meditation practitioner, with samples checked by another (p. 47). No
  agreement statistic is given.
