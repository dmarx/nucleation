# NOTE-858: McAllister & McCrea — Long-term consequences of repetitive concussion

## Contribution

A state-of-the-science review for a special issue of the *Journal of
Athletic Training*. It sorts the evidence on repetitive concussion and
repetitive head-impact exposure into short-range effects, neuropsychiatric
effects, cognitive effects and long-range neurodegeneration, and finds
that the short term is well characterized while the long term is not
studied by any design able to answer it. It ends with four research
questions and a cohort design.

## Key insight

The question moved in a decade from "how many concussions?" to "how much
head impact?" and "what happens down the road?", and the evidence did not
move with it. What exists on the long term is retrospective: surveys of
retired players, brain-bank autopsies, case series. None of it can
separate exposure from selection, recall and comorbidity.

## Assumptions

- The scope is sport-related concussion in contact sports, mostly
  American football and ice hockey, high-school to professional.
- The authors take "subconcussive" to be a poor term: it assumes every
  concussion is diagnosed, that the threshold is known, and that such
  impacts injure the brain.
- It is a narrative review. No search strategy, inclusion criteria or
  pooled estimates are given.

## Key results

Figures are as the review reports them from its sources.

- **Recurrence.** Castile et al. (2012): 3.1 recurrent versus 22.2 new
  concussions per 100,000 athlete-exposures in high school, with recurrent
  ones worse acutely (more loss of consciousness, longer recovery).
- **Exposure.** About 520 (Schnebel, 16 players) and 652 (Broglio, 35
  players; maximum 2,235) head impacts per high-school football season.
  Crisco (314 college players, 286,636 impacts): median per season
  257–438 by team, peak linear acceleration 20.5 g at the 50th and
  62.7 g at the 95th percentile, rotational 1,400 and 4,378 rad/s². Ice
  hockey: 287 per season for men, 170 for women.
- **Short range.** "The overwhelming majority of athletes achieve a
  complete recovery in self-reported symptoms and performance-based
  measures of recovery … by 1 to 2 weeks". Season-long exposure studies
  (Broglio, Gysland, Miller, McAllister et al.) mostly find no cognitive
  effect of impact counts. Exception: 24% of contact athletes against
  3.6% of non-contact athletes scored below prediction on the CVLT
  learning trials. Diffusion imaging (McAllister, 80 contact versus 79
  non-contact athletes) found white-matter changes correlated with
  exposure and, for corpus-callosum MD, with verbal learning.
- **Neuropsychiatric.** Depressive symptoms rise after concussion, as they
  do after orthopaedic injury, hence the call for injured controls.
  Longer-term links (3.3-fold depression risk in a child survey; suicide
  rates 3–4 times the population's after hospital-treated concussion in
  Ontario, with a weekend effect that suggests confounding) are
  "neither substantive nor mature enough" for conclusions.
- **Cognitive.** A meta-analysis of 8 studies found no overall effect of
  multiple mild TBIs; other studies disagree in method and result.
- **Long range.** Guskiewicz (2005): retired NFL players with three or
  more reported concussions had 5 times the prevalence of diagnosed MCI
  and 3 times that of memory complaints. CTE is a postmortem diagnosis.
  Bieniek: 21 of 66 contact-sport brains met CTE criteria against 0 of
  198. Ling: 12% of 268 brains, with the same rate in elderly controls.
  Iverson et al. are quoted that the science "does not allow us to
  determine the extent to which repetitive neurotrauma uniquely causes,
  or partially contributes to" specific symptoms.
- **The gap.** "To date, no prospective, longitudinal studies of
  well-defined cohorts over longer periods … have been conducted".

## Concepts

- **Sport-related concussion (SRC).** A diagnosed mild TBI in sport.
- **Head-impact exposure.** Number and magnitude of head impacts,
  measured by helmet accelerometers, with or without diagnosed
  concussion.
- **Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE).** A tauopathy with
  perivascular phosphorylated τ at the depths of cortical sulci,
  diagnosed only at autopsy; traumatic encephalopathy syndrome is the
  proposed clinical counterpart.
- **Trauma controls.** Patients with non-head injuries, used to separate
  the effects of injury in general from those of brain injury.

## Connections

The review writes as part of the CARE consortium's programme (named
through www.careconsortium.net), which is the prospective design it calls
for, and leans on Iverson et al.'s and Belanger et al.'s critical
reviews. Nothing else in the record reads concussion.

## Bearing on the record

- **No THEORY, QUESTION or CLAIM cites this paper**, and none should
  rest on it beyond its epistemic conclusion: as of its writing the
  long-term risk was unmeasured by any design able to measure it.
- **The seed's summary is right**, with one sharpening: its "signals of
  vulnerability in subgroups (e.g., verbal memory)" are one finding (the
  CVLT 24% against 3.6%), not several.
- **Dated by design.** Published in March 2017, it predates the CARE
  consortium's results that it anticipates. A reader wanting the current
  state needs those, not this.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- A narrative review by two authors who also wrote several of the studies
  it reviews (McAllister et al., refs 16 and 17).
- Its figures are second-hand; none was checked against the primary
  studies here.
- It does not weigh studies by quality or pool them.

## Open questions

- Does repeated concussion or head-impact exposure raise late-life risk
  of cognitive decline or neurodegeneration, and for whom? The review's
  answer is a prospective cohort with concussed, exposed-but-unconcussed
  and unexposed athletes, baseline characterization and long follow-up.
