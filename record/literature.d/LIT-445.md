# LIT-445: From the Phenomenology to the Mechanisms of Consciousness: Integrated Information Theory 3.0

Masafumi Oizumi, Larissa Albantakis, Giulio Tononi (2014), *PLoS
Computational Biology 10(5), e1003588* (published 8 May 2014) —
DOI-10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003588, open access (CC BY)

## Key takeaways

- **Axioms to postulates.** Five axioms are "self-evident truths about
  consciousness". Five postulates are "assumptions about the physical
  world", which the authors call "unproven".
- **Exclusion, stated twice.**
  - *Mechanism level:* a mechanism specifies one cause and one effect, the
    maximally irreducible ones. This is defended as "a causal version of
    Occam's razor … causes should not be multiplied beyond necessity".
  - *System level:* "Of all overlapping sets of elements, only one set can
    be conscious – the one whose mechanisms specify a conceptual structure
    that is maximally irreducible." A complex is a local maximum of ΦMax
    "as compared to all overlapping sets of elements". Complexes "cannot
    overlap", and "no subset or superset of ABC can form another complex".
- **Identity.** An experience *is* the maximally irreducible conceptual
  structure (MICS) of a complex. ΦMax is its quantity, and the shape of
  the constellation of concepts is its quality.
- **Many complexes, never nested.** A system "may condense into a major
  complex and non-overlapping minor complexes". Minor complexes are
  "paraconscious", and a split brain is two main complexes. Whether an
  octopus, an ant colony or a computer forms one complex or many "is not
  practically feasible" to determine, but "in principle testable".
- **Function does not fix consciousness.** A feed-forward network can match
  a conscious network's input–output behaviour and have no complex: "true
  'zombies'".
- **Unfeasible beyond toy systems.** The analysis is exhaustive over
  partitions and is "unfeasible for systems of more than a dozen elements
  or so".

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 for the owner's bridge between Minsky's society of mind
and Schwitzgebel's conscious United States. [NOTE-365](../notes.d/NOTE-365.md) is the close
reading, and it placed the work: **Active**. It **is the anti-nesting
principle itself**, in IIT's formal statement.

**Is this the statement Schwitzgebel attacks?** Partly. [NOTE-131](../notes.d/NOTE-131.md), which
read Schwitzgebel's 2014 manuscript of [LIT-159](LIT-159.md), records that its §2 engages
Tononi 2008–2012, this paper and Tononi & Koch 2014. The postulate it
targets, that where integrated systems nest consciousness occurs only at
the most-integrating level, is, in substance, this paper's system-level
exclusion. The strict "local maximum" comparison is also what
makes Schwitzgebel's one-ballot threshold possible. But the *defence* he
attacks is not this paper's. He has exclusion defended by Occam's razor and
the absurdity of a two-person group consciousness. Here Occam's razor
supports exclusion only *within a mechanism*: one cause, not "strong
synapses" and "all synapses" at once. Exclusion *between systems* rests on
the phenomenological axiom alone ("each experience has definite borders").
Group consciousness is never mentioned, and ant colonies are left open.
[NOTE-131](../notes.d/NOTE-131.md) also records that the published version of [LIT-159](LIT-159.md) drops this
paper from its references. The later statements are Tononi & Koch 2015, to
which the book version of the argument ([LIT-216](LIT-216.md), read in [NOTE-188](../notes.d/NOTE-188.md)) replies,
and IIT 4.0 (2023), which [LIT-185](LIT-185.md) (read in [NOTE-122](../notes.d/NOTE-122.md)) takes as the current
theory and which keeps exclusion as an axiom.

On [NOTE-131](../notes.d/NOTE-131.md)'s numbering, this paper **contests C3** for any whole that
overlaps a conscious part with higher Φ. It is **the target of C5**, and
nothing in it answers C5's reductio. It is **neutral on C4**, since Putnam is
not cited, although its system-level exclusion is the formal descendant of
Putnam's stipulation. Two things in it cut the other way and a bridge
should keep them:

- exclusion is to be evaluated "not only over elements, but also over
  spatial and temporal grains", and "the macro may emerge over the micro,
  just like the whole may emerge above the parts". So the theory as
  stated does not privilege the smaller system. A whole at a coarse grain
  can win, which is the opening the book version of Schwitzgebel's
  argument uses (via Hoel, Albantakis & Tononi 2013, this paper's
  reference 20);
- exclusion forbids overlap, not plurality. A brain may host one major and
  several minor conscious complexes side by side.

For the decomposition side, the zombie result is the sharpest thing here.
On IIT, organising mindless parts so that the whole behaves like an agent
does **not** bring experience with it. Whether it does depends on the causal
architecture, recurrent or feed-forward, not on the behaviour. Minsky's
agents ([LIT-429](LIT-429.md)), Brooks's behaviour layers ([LIT-436](LIT-436.md)) and Levin's
goal-pursuing Selves ([LIT-439](LIT-439.md)) all sit under that verdict, and none of
the three engages it.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice. Its remark that
feed-forward classifiers would be unconscious is a claim about ML systems
under IIT, not advice, and the anthology does not hold the paper.
