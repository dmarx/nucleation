# CLAIM-734: Where an entitlement is protected by a requirement of its holder's consent, an irreversible act against it leaves the holder in practice only a claim to compensation, so the actor has changed by its own act the rule by which the entitlement is allocated, from consent to liability, and not only what is paid for it

## The claim

The owner, U26, after A55's account of fait accompli breach: "this feels
like we're starting to enter some sort of intersection of auction theory
(game theory?) with tort theory". A57 of the exchange
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b) answered
"Yes—and there's a remarkably direct connection in legal theory", and
gave it through Calabresi and Melamed's 1972 framework, which separates
who holds an entitlement from how it is protected (A57 §1, its table
abridged):

| Protection | What A must do | What B controls |
|---|---|---|
| Property rule | Obtain B's voluntary agreement | Whether to transfer the entitlement and at what acceptable price |
| Liability rule | Pay compensation assessed under a legally established standard | A claim to compensation, not necessarily a veto |
| Inalienability rule | Refrain from the transfer even if both agree | The entitlement cannot simply be bought or sold |

"**The difference is not just the amount of money exchanged. It's who
controls whether the exchange takes place.**"

**The conversion.** A's example: B values a historic building at
$500,000; A would gain $200,000 by demolishing it. Under a property rule A
cannot buy the right to demolish without B's agreement. If A demolishes
first, "A may have achieved, through wrongful conduct, something
resembling a **de facto conversion of consent-based control into
compensation-based protection**." And:

> **The right to decide whether something happens and the right to compensation after it happens are not equivalent capabilities.**

A57 §3 states the change as one of mechanism, not price: "**A has
potentially changed the procedure by which the entitlement is
allocated.**" "We might call that **endogenous mechanism selection
through unilateral action**." The actor has no legal authority to choose
the mechanism, "But by acting irreversibly, the actor can sometimes make
the practical consequences resemble those of a different mechanism."

**Why it is a wrong and not only a price** (A57 §5): "**A damages award
may address a loss without retroactively establishing that the defendant
had legitimate authority to impose that loss.**" A's question from it:
"**When should an actor be prevented from unilaterally converting another
person's protected authority over an outcome into a claim for
compensation after that outcome has already been foreclosed?**" That is
the owner's question at U20 ([QUESTION-478](../questions.d/QUESTION-478.md)) asked of one capability.

## How it sits with the record

- **[CLAIM-464](CLAIM-464.md)** (Active): an intervention can make a corrective outcome
  unreachable while the standards stay the same. This claim says what
  the holder is left with when the outcome lost is the holder's own
  decision: a claim of another kind, under another rule.
- **[CLAIM-716](CLAIM-716.md)**: the bargaining over that compensation, in which the
  breacher can gain leverage. This claim is the rule changing; that one is
  the terms reached once it has.
- **[CLAIM-723](CLAIM-723.md)**: the owner's wrong of acting before a recognized
  opportunity for intervention can take effect. A59 §6 joins the two: "If
  B is entitled to refuse a transfer, then A's irreversible action can
  effectively replace B's ex-ante control with an ex-post claim for
  compensation." That claim names the wrong in the order of moves; this
  one names what the wrong converts.
- **[CLAIM-722](CLAIM-722.md)** (F4): the claim to what is owed persists. Here the
  entitlement persists in law and what changes is the holder's power over
  it; the two agree.
- **[CASE-128](../cases.d/CASE-128.md)** (Active) is the same restriction consented to and imposed;
  this is the same transfer bargained for and taken.

## What it does not say

- Not that the law permits the conversion. A57 §3: "a wrongdoer cannot
  legally convert one into the other merely by violating it", and A may
  still face "injunctions, punitive consequences, restitutionary remedies,
  or other sanctions". The claim is about effective power.
- Not that bilateral disputes are auctions. A57 §4: "Strictly speaking, a
  bilateral tort dispute is not necessarily an auction"; it is "better
  modeled as a sequential game", and the wider field is mechanism design.
  The owner's question mark on "game theory?" was apt.
- Not that every disruption bears the same weight. A57 §7 pairs
  "**Case A: Innocent disruption**" and "**Case B: Wrongful
  disruption**", with the same post-event state: "Where an actor
  wrongfully creates the conditions that make an entitlement difficult or
  impossible to restore, the resulting constraints should not
  automatically confer additional legitimate bargaining authority on that
  actor."
- A57 §1 calls the conversion "the most important legal-theoretic
  articulation of our entire discussion". That is A's estimate; the work
  it rests on is unread here. Calabresi and Melamed, "Property Rules,
  Liability Rules, and Inalienability: One View of the Cathedral",
  *Harvard Law Review* 85(6), 1972, doi:10.2307/1340059 (the part-4
  ledger).
