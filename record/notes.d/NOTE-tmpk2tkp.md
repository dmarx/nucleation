# NOTE-tmpk2tkp: Mancinelli & Bach — A systematic review of human avoidance learning

## Contribution

Earlier reviews of human avoidance learning were narrative and each covered
one aspect (paradigms, cognitive theories, computational models). This one
searches Web of Science, Scopus and PubMed systematically, codes 116
articles, rates each recurring phenomenon for robustness (A to C, Fig. 4),
and scores five theories against them (Fig. 1). It is the first attempt to
say which empirical regularities any theory of avoidance learning must
reproduce, and which of them actually discriminate between theories.

## Key insight

Whether a theory survives depends on which phenomena it is tested on. The
phenomena that discriminate say that avoidance in these tasks is inferential:
it is learned without terminating the warning signal, it transfers to
stimuli that predict the same harm without having been trained on them, and
it can arise from anticipation alone. "As a whole, these findings support
an inference-based view" (p. 25). One robust phenomenon cuts the other way:
some avoidance persists after the harm is visibly removed, which
inference does not predict. "Yet, the evidence also contradicts a purely
inference-based account" (p. 25).

## Assumptions

- **Inclusion (p. 7).** Published, peer-reviewed, in English; "(1)
  experimental procedures involving healthy humans", individual
  acquisition of an avoidance response, a primary reinforcer (electric
  shock, loud or noxious sound; money and points excluded), no appetitive
  reinforcer during acquisition, and at least 20 participants. Healthy
  control groups from clinical studies were included; patients were not.
- **Search (p. 6).** From August 2023, last updated 12 December 2024. One
  author screened; the other was consulted in uncertain cases (p. 7).
- **Theories compared (pp. 4–6; Fig. 1).** Mowrer's two-factor theory
  (2FT), safety-signal theory, Seligman and Johnston's hybrid model, the
  negative-occasion-setter account, and Lovibond's expectancy theory.
  Computational instantiations exist only for 2FT (Moutoussis et al. 2008;
  Maia 2010), both model-free; "there is no computational counterpart of
  expectancy theory" (p. 6).
- **Robustness ratings (Fig. 4).** By diversity of settings, number of
  studies, confounds and known boundary conditions. A or B marks a
  candidate benchmark for models.

## Key results

- **The sample (pp. 9–10).** 116 articles, 1939–2024, 15 preregistered.
  Median sample 50 (20–189, plus one of 502); mostly undergraduates,
  median age 21.25, 58.8% women. Electric shock in 70 studies, noise in 25.
  59 studies used a single avoidance response, usually instructed. "The
  main concern was ecological validity" (p. 10): the correct avoidance
  response was typically instructed, "which qualitatively changes the
  problem setting".
- **Avoidance without CS termination (p. 12; rated A, 8 studies).** 2FT
  predicts no learning if the response cancels the US but not the CS.
  Eight studies falsified this; "We did not identify any evidence
  confirming this prediction of the original 2FT."
- **Conditioned responses (pp. 10–11; A, 11 studies).** Skin conductance
  falls during avoidance acquisition, but it also falls with unavoidable
  shocks, and the comparisons are mixed. Two experiments (Ng & Lovibond)
  show avoidance intention lowering skin conductance: "The latter findings
  are not predicted by 2FT, but are in line with expectancy theory" (p. 11). Fear responses are
  not needed to maintain avoidance (p. 25).
- **Generalisation and transfer (pp. 12–15).** Perceptual generalisation
  of avoidance (A); latent transfer through learned stimulus equivalence (A,
  5 studies); and US-based transfer, an untrained response used for a new
  CS predicting the same harm (B/C, 3 studies, holding only for those who
  understood the contingencies).
- **Spontaneous avoidance after Pavlovian learning only (p. 16; A).** In
  virtual-reality studies participants kept their distance from a
  conditioned stimulus never trained for avoidance; in one, approach was
  slower even after the electrodes were visibly detached. "This suggests
  that the anticipation of a US is sufficient to elicit an AR" (p. 17).
- **Partial effectiveness (pp. 15–16).** Responses that only sometimes
  work are emitted almost as often as fully effective ones unless nearly
  useless. People also keep responding to a CS whose US is unavoidable,
  declining over trials, which no theory explains "unless they incorporate
  some notion of outcome uncertainty" (p. 16).
- **Protection from extinction (pp. 18–19; A, 9 studies).** With the
  response available during extinction, "42% (Malloy & Levis, 1988) and
  47% (Williams & Levis, 1991) of participants did not extinguish a unary
  and costless AR by the 500th would-be-unpunished trial" (p. 18). In two
  studies, those allowed to avoid "continue to do so and retain higher US
  expectancies in a follow-up test where avoidance is precluded" (p. 18).
  Less effective or more costly responses extinguish faster (A, 6
  studies). Monetary incentives reduce avoidance during extinction, but
  when avoidance is then prevented, US expectancy rises in every group
  regardless of intervention (pp. 18–19).
- **Return after response prevention (pp. 19–20; A/B, 7 studies).**
  When the response is made available again after extinction with
  response prevention, it is used, and removing it again raises threat
  expectancy. Every such study signalled response availability, which may
  itself signal danger, and none extinguished to criterion.
- **Avoidance spreads to the safe stimulus (pp. 19–20, 26; B, 3
  studies).** Signalling a costless response for a stimulus never paired
  with shock (the CS−) leads people to use it, and removing it then raises
  their threat expectancy for that stimulus, though skin conductance and
  startle were unaffected in one study. This "must invoke an inference
  mechanism that deduces CS–US contingencies without direct experience"
  (p. 26), which a model-free actor-critic cannot do, since its critic
  "only updates values for states visited by the actor" (p. 26).
- **Signalled US omission (pp. 21–23; A, 8 studies).** Avoidance persists,
  at a reduced rate, after the US is visibly removed. Response
  distributions are zero-inflated: a few participants carry the group
  effect. No study found the value-by-training interaction that would show
  habits forming with overtraining. Hence "avoidance learning can not be
  fully inferential in the first place, directly challenging expectancy
  theory" (p. 23).
- **Neuroimaging (pp. 23–24).** Thirteen fMRI and six EEG studies, small
  (fMRI median 25). Three findings replicated at least once: caudate
  activity in threat against motor control; periaqueductal gray and
  anterior insula up and vmPFC down as threat comes closer; PAG activity
  consistent with an aversive prediction error.
- **The authors' synthesis (pp. 25–28).** Expectancy theory is best
  supported, tempered by Pavlovian influences. Latent-cause inference
  could explain context effects and generalisation. On persistence:
  "more effective ARs are harder to extinguish when the response is not
  prevented, putatively because effective ARs provide fewer opportunities
  to detect changes in contingency (i.e., the absence of punishment when
  the AR is not performed), causing slower learning" (p. 28). One
  explanation for avoidance raising threat expectancy for safe stimuli is
  that people "might counterfactually infer danger for the safe CS, through
  the artificial belief that if they had not avoided, when they had the
  chance, they would have been punished" (p. 26).
- **Methods (pp. 28–31).** Current paradigms are "structurally less
  complex than real-world settings" (p. 2); the authors want multi-step
  tasks, continuous rather than button-press responses, quantified response
  costs, trial-by-trial individual modelling, and analysis of participants
  who misunderstand the task: "It is indeed possible that in real-world
  scenarios, excessive avoidance might stem from a misinterpretation of
  these contingencies" (p. 31).

## Concepts

- **AR, CS, US, CR**: the avoidance response; the conditioned stimulus
  that warns of the unconditioned (aversive) stimulus; and the involuntary
  conditioned response, often called fear (p. 4).
- **RPE (response prevention extinction)**: extinction in which both the
  US and the avoidance response are removed (p. 18), the laboratory
  analogue of exposure with response prevention.
- **Extinction with AR**: extinction in which the response stays available
  and simply is not punished if omitted (p. 18).
- **Signalled US omission**: removing the outcome visibly (unplugging the
  electrodes) to test whether responding depends on it (p. 21).
- **"Cognitive" against "computational" theory**: a theory naming
  cognitive operations, against an algorithm instantiating them (p. 5).

## Connections

It updates Krypotos et al.'s narrative reviews (2015, 2018, 2020) and
complements Wong et al. (2023) on individual differences. Its framing pairs
two-factor and expectancy theory with model-free and model-based
reinforcement learning. It names Moutoussis et al. (2008) and Maia (2010)
as the only implemented avoidance models, and points to latent-cause
inference (Gershman, Niv) and Bayesian treatments of controllability
(Huys & Dayan 2009; Zamfir & Dayan 2022) as where theory should go. It does
not cite Zorowitz et al. (LIT-tmppvxx9).

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmp8z857 (governance-maintained anxiety).** The review is
  laboratory evidence in healthy people. Within that limit it bears on
  three of the claim's steps, and it sharpens one.
  - **Avoidance destroys identifiability: supported.** The claim's
    central mechanism is that, under avoidance, danger and safety predict
    the same observation. The review finds that available avoidance
    protects threat expectancy from extinction (rated A), and that the
    more effective the response, the longer it lasts, which the authors
    attribute to fewer chances "to detect changes in contingency". That is
    the claim's point in the authors' words, from the human literature,
    with their "putatively".
  - **Precaution as evidence of danger: supported, with a confound.** The
    claim names "if I felt compelled to protect myself, the danger must
    have been serious" as one way the loop closes. The review's phenomenon
    of avoidance offered for a never-punished stimulus raising threat
    expectancy for it when withdrawn (B, three studies) is the nearest
    laboratory case, and the authors' first explanation is a
    counterfactual inference of the same form. The confound they flag is
    that offering a response may itself signal danger.
  - **Belief correction is not behavioural correction: partly
    supported.** Avoidance persists after the outcome is visibly removed
    (A), without habit formation by overtraining. So some avoidance
    outlasts a corrected expectation. But a few participants carry the
    effect, and the review cannot say whether those participants believed
    the removal.
  - **"Avoidance by itself suppresses learning; it does not strengthen the
    belief": complicated.** The claim says this to separate suppression from
    self-maintenance. In the review, return of avoidance after response
    prevention, and its spread to the safe stimulus, raise threat
    expectancy, so in these tasks the availability or withdrawal of
    avoidance does move the belief. The claim should either count that as
    its own closing inference or drop the sentence.
  - **Not anxiety.** No clinical sample was included, and the paradigms
    are by the authors' account simpler than real avoidance. The review
    cannot say whether any of this is how anxiety is maintained.
- **QUESTION-045.** The review's response-prevention studies are the
  closest existing design to the question's test of cutting a maintaining
  link: removing the response during extinction. Their result is mixed:
  threat ratings fall during prevention but return when the response is
  offered again. Monetary incentives to refrain cut avoidance more than
  instructions, but threat expectancy rose in every group when avoidance
  was then prevented (pp. 18–19).
- **Correction to the exchange.** The exchange called it a 2026 review
  giving the strongest support to "expectancy- and inference-based
  accounts". It is a 2025 preprint, in print in 2026. It does support
  expectancy theory by name (the abstract does not), but it also says the
  evidence "contradicts a purely inference-based account". Its studies are
  of healthy people only, with shock or noise.

## Limitations

- **Healthy volunteers only**, mostly undergraduates, with primary
  reinforcers. Nothing here is about anxiety disorders.
- **Ecological validity**, the authors' main risk-of-bias concern:
  instructed, signalled, single-button responses. They argue that such
  tasks may themselves elicit inference.
- **One screener**, with the second author for uncertain cases only.
- **No meta-analysis.** Robustness ratings are judgements; effect sizes
  are not pooled.
- **Supplement and version of record not read.** The study list and
  risk-of-bias ratings are in an OSF data set; the journal version is
  closed.
- **Slips in the preprint.** "needed needed" (p. 4); "Notably, both share
  the feature of signaling AR availability, someting that is unusual"
  (p. 26); "specific to the paradigms we exist in the literature" (p. 28);
  Declercq et al., Trick et al. and Garofalo & Robbins are called "both
  studies" (p. 14). None changes a finding.

## Open questions

- Does avoidance persist longer when it is more effective in people with
  anxiety disorders, and for the same reason (fewer disconfirming
  observations)? A task that varies effectiveness with threat expectancy
  measured trial by trial in patients would tell.
- Is the rise in threat expectancy for safe stimuli an inference from
  one's own avoidance, or a reading of the experimenter's signal? An
  unsignalled, continuously available response would separate them.
- Who are the participants who keep avoiding after signalled omission,
  and what do they believe about the outcome?
