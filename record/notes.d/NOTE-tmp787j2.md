# NOTE-tmp787j2: Swidler — Culture in action

## Contribution

A reorientation of the sociology of culture away from Parsons's values. It
proposes three steps (p. 273): culture as a "tool kit" of symbols, stories,
rituals and world-views; "strategies of action", persistent ways of
ordering action through time, as what culture explains; and culture's
causal role as supplying the components from which strategies are built,
not the ends they serve. It then splits the analysis into two models, for
settled and unsettled periods, with different causal pictures, and lists
research questions that would tell the toolkit model from the values model.

## Key insight

What persists is the way action is organized, not the goal it aims at.
"If culture provides the tools with which persons construct lines of
action, then styles or strategies of action will be more persistent than
the ends people seek to attain. Indeed, people will come to value ends for
which their cultural equipment is well suited" (p. 277). The ascetic
Protestant ethos outlasting the doctrine that produced it is the case: "If
ideas shape ethos, why did the ethos of ascetic Protestantism outlast its
ideas?" (p. 276).

## Assumptions

- **Culture is public symbolic vehicles.** "culture consists of such
  symbolic vehicles of meaning, including beliefs, ritual practices, art
  forms, and ceremonies, as well as informal cultural practices such as
  language, gossip, stories, and rituals of daily life" (p. 273).
- **Action comes in chunks.** "people do not, indeed cannot, build up a
  sequence of actions piece by piece, striving with each act to maximize a
  given outcome. Action is necessarily integrated into larger assemblages"
  (p. 276).
- **Cultures are internally plural.** "all real cultures contain diverse,
  often conflicting symbols, rituals, stories, and guides to action"
  (p. 277).
- **A strategy is not a plan.** "The term 'strategy' is not used here in
  the conventional sense of a plan consciously devised to attain a goal"
  (p. 277).

## Key results

No data. By section:

- **Against values (pp. 274–276).** The culture-of-poverty debate "cannot be
  resolved by evidence that the very poor share the values and aspirations
  of the middle class, as indeed they seem to do" (p. 275). What differs is
  equipment: "One can hardly pursue success in a world where the accepted
  skills, style, and informal know-how are unfamiliar" (p. 275). "Action
  is not determined by one's values. Rather action and values are
  organized to take advantage of cultural competences" (p. 275).
- **The tool kit (p. 277).** Culture "is more like a 'tool kit' or
  repertoire (Hannerz, 1969:186-88) from which actors select differing
  pieces for constructing lines of action". Retooling is costly: the ghetto
  youth pursues group loyalty "not because he disdains what individual
  achievement could bring, but because the cultural meanings and social
  skills necessary for playing that game well would require drastic and
  costly cultural retooling".
- **Weber's functional equivalents (p. 278).** If religious ideas caused
  economic trajectories, culture "must not change more easily than the
  structural and economic patterns it supposedly shapes"; the discovery of
  ascetic ethics everywhere modernization went undermines that.
- **Unsettled lives (pp. 278–280).** Ideologies, "explicit, articulated,
  highly organized meaning systems", "establish new styles or strategies of
  action" and "directly shape action" (p. 278). Ideology, tradition and
  common sense are phases on a continuum (p. 279). But "ideology forms
  around ethos, rather than vice versa": Calvin "'opportunistically'
  revised and reworked his doctrine in order to achieve a particular
  psychological effect" (Walzer; p. 280). And "It is, however, the concrete
  situations in which these cultural models are enacted that determine
  which take root and thrive, and which wither and die" (p. 280).
- **Settled lives (pp. 280–281).** Culture and action are "loosely coupled":
  "People profess ideals they do not follow" (p. 280). Culture's influence
  shows in "cultural lag": "People do not readily take advantage of new
  structural opportunities which would require them to abandon established
  ways of life" (p. 281), not from attachment to values but reluctance "to
  abandon familiar strategies of action for which they have the cultural
  equipment".
- **Figure 1 (p. 282)** tabulates the two models: settled culture has low
  coherence and weak direct control and "Creates continuities in style or
  ethos"; unsettled culture has high coherence and strong control, but its
  "long-term influence depends on structural opportunities for survival of
  competing ideologies".
- **Research questions (pp. 282–284).** Who stays traditional longer after
  immigration (the toolkit model predicts the old and encapsulated; the
  values model the advantaged); how belief systems break down (when they
  stop regulating action, as for Kuhnian paradigms); what capacities
  ideologies confer (pietist Protestants winning whatever their alliances);
  how ideologies become common sense.
- **Against socialization (p. 283).** "Culture does not influence how
  groups organize action via enduring psychological proclivities implanted
  in individuals by their socialization. Instead, publicly available
  meanings facilitate certain patterns of action, making them readily
  available, while discouraging others."

## Concepts

- **Tool kit / repertoire**: the set of symbols, stories, rituals,
  world-views, skills, habits and styles available to actors, "which people
  may use in varying configurations to solve different kinds of problems"
  (p. 273). Taken from Hannerz (1969).
- **Strategy of action**: "a general way of organizing action (depending
  upon a network of kin and friends, for example, or relying on selling
  one's skills in a market) that might allow one to reach several different
  life goals" (p. 277).
- **Settled and unsettled lives**: the contrast is "less between settled and
  unsettled lives, however, than between culture's role in sustaining
  existing strategies of action and its role in constructing new ones"
  (p. 278).
- **Ideology, tradition, common sense**: phases of a cultural system, from
  articulated and contested, to taken for granted but articulated, to
  invisible (p. 279).

## Connections

It builds on Hannerz's repertoire, Geertz, Bourdieu's strategies and
habitus (nn. 5, 9, 11; Swidler's strategies are "the larger ways of trying
to organize a life … for which particular, culturally shaped skills and
habits (what Bourdieu calls 'habitus') are useful", n. 9), Walzer on
Calvin, and Skocpol on ideology and structure. It cites Sewell's work on
French artisans as an example of reappropriated cultural capacities
(p. 283); Sewell's 1992 theory of structure, [LIT-1383](../literature.d/LIT-1383.md), takes up its
transposability in another vocabulary.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-417](../claims.d/CLAIM-417.md) (selves as organizational perspectives).** The claim cites
  this paper with Wacquant ([LIT-1377](../literature.d/LIT-1377.md)) for sense 2, "a perspective's
  dispositional repertoire and the resources coupled to it". Two
  corrections. First, Swidler's repertoire is not dispositional: C6 says
  culture works through "publicly available meanings", not "enduring
  psychological proclivities implanted in individuals". That is the
  opposite of habitus as Wacquant states it, so the two cannot be cited
  together as one account. Second, the repertoire is not the perspective:
  it is a stock of components, and the organized thing is the strategy of
  action built from them. If anything here answers to [TERM-072](../terms.d/TERM-072.md) sense 1,
  a realized organization through time, it is the strategy, not the kit.
  The paper is closer to the claim's lineage and character than to its
  realization: a strategy of action persists across changes of end (C2).
- **[CLAIM-420](../claims.d/CLAIM-420.md) (a figure's legacy: story, perspective, descent).** The
  Protestant ethic case gives a fourth way something persists that the
  claim does not list: a style of organizing action that outlasts the
  doctrine and ends that produced it ("this individualistic way of
  organizing action can be directed to many values", p. 276). That is a
  perspective transmitted without its content, a finer cut than the
  claim's "perspectival" persistence.
- **[CLAIM-416](../claims.d/CLAIM-416.md) (competition over tests).** C4 is the selection half:
  ideologies "in active competition with other cultural frameworks", whose
  survival is decided by the structural situations they are enacted in.
  Swidler does not discuss competitors reshaping those situations, so she
  supports the claim's relativity (success relative to a regime) and says
  nothing of its reflexive part. Her question "What capacities do
  particular cultural patterns give those who hold them?" (p. 283), with
  pietists who "won out politically" whatever their alliances, is one form
  of the claim's question of what success under a regime shows.
- **[CLAIM-287](../claims.d/CLAIM-287.md) and [CLAIM-329](../claims.d/CLAIM-329.md) (persistence).** Cultural lag (C5) is
  persistence through the cost of rebuilding equipment, neither
  continuous running nor transmission of an organization as such. It bears
  on what those claims say is conserved, and does not test either.
- **[TERM-072](../terms.d/TERM-072.md) (perspective).** The tool kit is a set of kinds and
  components, publicly held; the strategy of action is nearer a
  realization; ideology is a representation that, in unsettled periods,
  directly organizes action. Swidler's ordering, ideology formed "around
  ethos, rather than vice versa" (p. 280), is a case where a representation
  follows the organization it describes.
- **THEORY.** The two-model account (C1–C5) is a substantive claim about
  how culture causes action that the record does not hold. Filed Proposed
  as [THEORY-tmptap5g](../theory.d/THEORY-tmptap5g.md), with the paper's own research questions as its
  test.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- A theoretical essay. Every case is illustrative and second-hand
  (Lewis, Liebow, Hannerz, Weber, Walzer, Gerson, Fulbrook), and none was
  chosen to test the model.
- "Structural opportunities" (C4) are not defined, so the unsettled model
  can explain any survivor after the fact.
- The settled/unsettled line is admitted to be "not, of course, absolute"
  (p. 278), and no measure of settledness is given.
- C6 is stated in one passage against socialization accounts, while notes
  9 and 11 borrow Bourdieu's habitus approvingly; the paper does not
  reconcile the two.

## Open questions

- Would the immigration test (p. 283) come out as the toolkit model
  predicts: more traditionalism among the old and encapsulated than among
  the advantaged? The paper names the test and does not run it.
- What marks a period as unsettled independently of the ideological
  activity used to identify it?
- If strategies outlast ends, what transmits a strategy: the public
  symbols, as C6 says, or trained dispositions, as habitus says?
