# NOTE-tmpxpkld: The Precipice Revisited

## Contribution

The author's own update of the risk estimates in The Precipice. It takes
four of the largest risks (climate change, nuclear war, pandemics, AI)
and asks what changed between the book's text being fixed and early 2024.
It is a talk, not a paper: no new estimates, no sources given, and no
moral argument. Its use to the record is as a reliable restatement of the
book's headline numbers and some of its framing, by the book's author.

## Key insight

Risk estimates change in two ways, and they should be kept apart. "Sometimes we have made the risks lower (or
higher), sometimes we've discovered that they always were" (Climate
Change). Lower emissions are a causal change, brought about by human
response. A narrower climate sensitivity is an evidential one: the risk
was always lower than feared.

## Assumptions

- **Existential risk** is human extinction "and other ways that
  humanity's entire potential could be permanently lost" (opening). The
  talk sets aside other harms from the same sources.
- **Much of an existential risk sits in the tail.** For climate, "much of
  the risk comes from the worst case possibilities", so narrowing a range
  lowers existential risk even when the middle stays put.
- **The book's numbers are rounded**, "usually to the nearest factor of
  10" (Conclusions), so moderate changes do not move them.

## Key results

- **Climate change: down.**
  - Emissions: "we are not at all on RCP 8.5"; the path is "headed
    somewhere between the lower two paths". The figure shows the four
    RCPs (2.6, 4.5, 6.0, 8.5) to 2100.
  - Climate sensitivity: when the book was written, the IPCC's likely
    range was 1.5–4.5°C, a two-thirds range, so about a 1 in 6 chance of
    exceeding 4.5°C, "where a lot of the existential risk was coming
    from". The range was first proposed the year Ord was born and did not
    change for 40 years. AR6 narrows it to 2.5–4°C and gives a 95% chance
    the sensitivity is below 5°C.
- **Nuclear: up.**
  - The Russian invasion of Ukraine brought nuclear powers "substantially
    closer to the brink": calls for a no-fly zone, threats of
    retaliation, raised readiness. Not Cuban-missile-crisis level.
  - New START was renewed at the last minute and cannot be renewed again;
    it expires two years from the talk. Ord thinks rising stockpiles "the
    most likely possibility", after a decline since 1986 (figure: totals
    peak above 60,000 in the mid-1980s and fall to about 10,000 by 2020).
  - In the book, one reason nuclear risk was not rated higher was that
    with current arsenals there is no known scientific pathway from an
    all-out war to extinction "(nor a solid story about how it could
    permanently end civilisation)". Larger arsenals weaken that reason.
  - The MacArthur Foundation, about 45% of funding for nuclear peace and
    arms reduction, withdrew; funding for the field halved.
- **Pandemics: mixed.**
  - Covid was "not an existential risk" but had four implications. It
    exposed weaknesses: unity against a common threat was "quite a
    temporary phase — you get a few months of that", then the response
    polarised "along existing national and political lines"; the WHO and
    CDC fell short. It was a warning shot that "punctured" our "air of
    invincibility", yet produced less response than expected: no panic
    stage, and weak biodefence spending in the UK, worse in the US.
    Vaccines arrived within a year, where the fastest before had taken 10
    years (figure: measles, 1953–1963). And gain-of-function research
    continues, though credible inquiries into Covid's origin are "about
    evenly split" on a lab escape that may have killed "more than 25
    million people".
  - Two protective technologies: metagenomic sequencing, which could flag
    a novel pathogen from a patient or wastewater, and clean air
    (filtration, germicidal and far-UVC light). They cover novel and
    engineered pathogens, need little behaviour change, help against
    everyday disease, and are not dual use.
  - AI in biology: AlphaFold took known protein structures from about
    50,000 to 200 million. Language models extend the democratisation of
    biotechnology, so unless the abilities are removed they "will indeed
    increase the risk from bioterrorism".
- **AI: mixed.**
  - From reinforcement-learning agents (AlphaGo, AlphaZero, AlphaStar,
    OpenAI Five) to generative language models. These are not inherently
    agents, so "the classical threat scenario of Yudkowsky & Bostrom (the
    one I focused on in The Precipice) doesn't directly apply". They have
    read about a trillion words, so "the challenge is no longer getting
    them enough information about ethics, but about making them care".
    Trained on human text, their abilities may plateau near the human
    range instead of passing through it quickly (three schematic figures).
    Their compute needs tie labs to corporate partners.
  - Racing: from 2023 a race between "the largest companies on Earth".
    Microsoft's search chatbot threatened users; Google merged Brain and
    DeepMind.
  - Governance: US chip export controls (October 2022); ChatGPT; Hinton
    and Bengio's warnings and the extinction-risk statement (May 2023);
    The Elders; the US executive order; the Bletchley Declaration (28
    countries and the EU); US and UK AI safety institutes.
  - Net: language models mixed, racing bad, governance good.
- **Conclusions.** The book's per-century estimates were 1 in 1,000 for
  climate and for nuclear, 1 in 30 for pandemics, 1 in 10 for unaligned AI.
  None has "moved that far". Climate down, nuclear up, pandemics and AI
  "no clear direction". The book's "existential security" is "a
  hypothetical future state of the world where existential risk is low and
  kept low". The current work is putting out fires; the longer work is
  "establishing the moral seriousness of existential risk", then norms,
  treaties and governance, over decades. The UN and The Elders now treat
  existential risk as a priority; a British prime minister quoted The
  Precipice at the UN.

## Concepts

- **existential risk**: extinction or another permanent loss of
  humanity's entire potential.
- **causal versus evidential change in risk**: risk made lower (or
  higher) by action, versus risk discovered to have been lower (or
  higher).
- **existential security**: a future state in which existential risk is
  low and kept low, with "the mechanisms to ensure fire no longer poses a
  substantive threat".
- **panic–neglect cycle**: the expected pattern after a crisis, too much
  caution then too little. Covid, Ord says, skipped the panic.

## Bearing on the record

- **QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.** Little directly. The talk is about how large the
  risks are, not what is owed or why. It confirms the empirical premise
  of the impersonal ground as Ord holds it: risk this century is high,
  and he sees no reason to lower it. Its programme for existential
  security runs through norms, treaties and institutions, "establishing
  the moral seriousness of existential risk", not through anyone's
  concern for future people as such.
- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** These are inferences; Ord says
  nothing about selves or motivation.
  - Ord's Covid observation bears on a humanity-wide self. A visible
    common threat produced humanity-level unity "at first", then the
    response split along national and political lines within months.
    Self-categorization theory (THEORY-tmpo85ke) would predict that an
    outside threat makes the most inclusive category salient, and that
    salience is fixed by context and does not last when the context
    changes. If so, "humanity" is available as a self only while
    something makes it salient. A standing concern for far-future people
    could not rest on it.
  - The talk confirms that the book's case is not motivational. Its
    remedy for a deficit of concern is institutions that hold when
    concern fades.
  - Ord's line that the AI problem is "no longer getting them enough
    information about ethics, but about making them care" is about
    machines. It is not evidence about people, and is noted only because
    it is the same distinction CLAIM-tmprudek turns on: knowing what is
    good is not caring about it.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** Not addressed.
- **No dated note on CLAIM-tmpjylq8.** The one point (unity against a
  common threat is brief) is one case, observed and not measured. It
  belongs as a line in the dated note on QUESTION-tmpmx9ha (section 4).
- **No THEORY filed.**
- **No ML instruction.** The AI section describes the field; it gives no
  instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **A talk, lightly edited.** No references; figures are unsourced on
  the page (the RCP, stockpile and vaccine charts are recognisable
  standard charts, but the post does not cite them).
- **No new numbers.** Directions only. The overall one in six is not
  restated or revised.
- **Dated to the talk.** "Now" is February 2024, though posted in July.
- **Not the book.** It quotes the book once and reports its estimates;
  the book's moral argument is absent.
- **Typos.** "beleivable"; "issomething"; "and and Yoshua Bengio"; "Le
  Sedol" for Lee Sedol; "MRNA" for mRNA; "the typical time from vaccine
  development" for "for"; "it may be possible for first labs" (missing
  "the"), and that sentence lacks its full stop.

## Open questions

- Does the one-in-six total still stand on Ord's view? The talk implies
  so without saying it.
- Was the brevity of humanity-wide unity in Covid measured? A study of
  superordinate identification ("humanity", "all people") across the
  pandemic would test whether a threat-made human self fades as
  self-categorization theory predicts.
