summary: >-
  A game-theoretic model of a recommender platform that runs an
  online-learning (adversarial linear contextual bandit) algorithm,
  recommending one producer per arriving user, while P non-myopic,
  discounting producers choose a D-dimensional content vector at every step,
  paying cost ||p||^c and earning 1 when recommended. Content quality is
  measured as producer effort ||p|| and as user welfare E[<u, p>]. For the
  standard no-regret algorithms LinHedge and LinEXP3, equilibrium effort
  (and so welfare) is bounded by a quantity that vanishes as t grows under
  typical learning-rate schedules. The cause is slowness: with a small
  learning rate, today's effort moves future recommendation probability
  little, and discounted producers do not find it worth paying for. The
  authors then design "punishment" algorithms, removing a producer for good
  once its content falls below a threshold (PunishHedge,
  PunishLinDirectionHedge), which sustain constant effort, and
  PunishUserUtility, which assigns individualized criteria so producers
  specialize, reaching near-optimal welfare in limiting cases.

# NOTE-1774: Hu, Jagadeesan, Jordan, Steinhardt — Incentivizing High-Quality Content in Online Recommender Systems

## Contribution

A formal result that a platform's learning dynamics, not users' tastes, can by themselves drive equilibrium content quality toward zero, and a design principle that an incentive-aware platform must react "more quickly and directly" to producer actions; technical tools bounding how much an online learner's arm probabilities move when rewards change.

## Key results

- Abstract: "We prove that standard online learning algorithms, such as Hedge and EXP3, unfortunately incentivize producers to create low-quality content, where producers’ effort approaches zero in the long run for typical learning rate schedules."
- §3.1 (Theorem 1, LinHedge; Theorem 2, LinEXP3): upper bound on ||p_j^t|| depending on η_{t+1}, β, γ, c; for η_t = O(1/√t), effort is t^{-Ω(1)}. Intuition, p. 15:7: "a small learning rate reduces the impact of a producer’s action on their future probability of being recommended."
- p. 15:7: the bound "decreases in β, approaching zero as β → 0": more myopic producers invest less.
- §3.3, p. 15:8: "if the learning algorithm reacts too slowly to producer actions, then producers are disincentivized from investing effort in content quality."
- §4.1, p. 15:9: punishment principle, "stop recommending producers if they ever create content with quality below a certain threshold q"; Theorem 4: unique equilibrium with |p^t| = (β/P)^{1/c}(1 − ε) for t < T (zero at T).
- §4.3, p. 15:11: the welfare of PunishLinDirectionHedge is suboptimal in P because "all producers compete along the same direction g".
- §5: PunishUserUtility achieves Ω(||E[u]||) welfare and, per Table 1, approaches 1 as c → ∞ (Corollary 10, not read in proof).

## Assumptions

- Producers commit to their whole content sequence at the start (§2.1: "producers select all content at the beginning of the game"), i.e. oblivious adversaries; Nash, not subgame-perfect, equilibria.
- One user per round, unit-norm user vectors, utility linear in content; platform recommends before seeing current-round content (cold start).
- Users have no activity heterogeneity: every arriving user counts once. Quality is a scalar effort norm; there is no notion of "extreme" content or taste change.
- Stylized algorithms: "While real-world recommendation platforms are unlikely to directly deploy these specific algorithms" (§3.3).
- Negative results in full-information and bandit settings; positive (punishment) results only in full information.

## Concepts

Producer effort; user welfare; intertemporal producer incentives; non-myopic followers in a generalized Stackelberg game with a learning leader; learning rate as incentive lever; punishment-based (moderation-like) algorithms; producer specialization.

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** A's account ("some online-learning recommender algorithms give producers incentives to lower quality over time, and proposes algorithms that sustain it") is accurate. A27 §3's use of it, that the recommender is part of "the production environment, not merely the consumption environment", is fair as to producers responding to the algorithm. But the paper's mechanism is not the heavy-user one: quality falls because the learner is slow and producers discount, with homogeneous one-user-per-round demand. It supports "the algorithm shapes supply", not "the heaviest users shape supply".

- **[CLAIM-653](../claims.d/CLAIM-653.md)** (heaviest users set the content distribution): **qualifies / leaves open**. It supplies a demonstrated (formal) supply-side link: platform algorithms change what producers make, and quality can degrade with no change in anyone's taste, which matches A27 §3's point that escalation is unnecessary. It does not model activity-weighted demand, heavy users or extremity, so it gives nothing to the claim's structural premise (that belongs to [CLAIM-658](../claims.d/CLAIM-658.md)) or to its escalation half. The CLAIM's citation should describe it as a supply-side mechanism of quality decline, not as evidence of minority capture.
- **[CLAIM-658](../claims.d/CLAIM-658.md)** (not named in the LIT): leaves open; no heterogeneous weighting in the model.
