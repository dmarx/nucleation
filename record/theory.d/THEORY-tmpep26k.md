# THEORY-tmpep26k: Cameron finds, in four years of fieldwork with ride-hail drivers, that without managers or coworkers drivers make their work meaningful through two workplace games, a relational game scored by the app's customer ratings, in which the platform appears as a facilitator, and an efficiency game tracked with their own tools and sometimes by manipulating the algorithm, in which it appears as an opponent, and that both games produce engagement

## Source

The new LIT for Cameron (2022), Organization Science 33(1), read in the author's manuscript as a partial stand-in for Cameron (2024), LIT-tmpdkpdp.

## What was actually shown

From participant observation and 136 interviews with 63 drivers in 23 cities: drivers construct a relational game (personalized service, scored by ratings the app tracks; the app is a facilitator, and its suggestions are followed) or an efficiency game (pay per time, depersonalized service, declined rides, self-made trackers, at times manipulation of the algorithm, at risk of deactivation; the app is an opponent). Both produce engagement; they go with amicable and adversarial stances toward the platform.

## What this does not say

- That the games produce consent: "this study does not suggest that workplace games alone can generate consent or long-term commitment".
- That the work is good or bad: the study "has veered away from taking a normative position on on-demand work".
- The later paper's concepts, constant and confined choices and choice-based consent.
