# LIT-406: Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness

David J. Chalmers (1995), *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 2(3), 200–219.
Imprint Academic. The author's copy is at consc.net/papers/facing.pdf. Much
reprinted: in Hameroff, Kaszniak & Scott (eds), *Toward a Science of
Consciousness* (MIT Press, 1996); in Shear (ed.), *Explaining Consciousness:
The Hard Problem* (MIT Press, 1997), with 25 commentaries and the author's
reply; and in Chalmers, *The Character of Consciousness* (Oxford, 2010).

## Key takeaways

- **Easy and hard (§2).** The easy problems are explaining discrimination,
  integration, reportability, internal access, attention, deliberate
  control and the sleep/wake difference. The hard problem is the problem of
  experience, in Nagel's "something it is like" sense. The paper proposes
  keeping "consciousness" for experience and "awareness" for the
  functional phenomena.
- **Why the hard problem is hard (§3).** The easy phenomena are
  functionally definable, so explaining the function explains the
  phenomenon; "you have explained the verbal report function but not
  reportability" is a conceptual mistake. "You have explained
  discrimination, integration and report but not how it is experienced" is
  not. The gene, life and learning have no such further question.
- **Case studies (§4).** Crick and Koch's 40 Hz binding theory and Baars's
  global workspace explain binding and accessibility, and are silent on why
  either is experienced. Five strategies for work of this kind: explain
  something else, deny the phenomenon, claim to explain experience by
  "something like magic", explain the *structure* of experience, or isolate
  its substrate.
- **No extra ingredient helps (§5).** Chaos, nonalgorithmic processing,
  new neurophysiology and quantum mechanics are all more structure and
  dynamics, so all leave the same question. Vitalism is disanalogous: its
  doubt was about functions, and experience is an explanandum, not a posit.
- **Nonreductive explanation (§6).** Take experience as fundamental,
  alongside mass, charge and space-time, connected to the physical by
  psychophysical laws that supplement physics without interfering with it.
  The paper calls this "naturalistic dualism".
- **Outline of a theory (§7).** Two nonbasic principles and one candidate
  basic one:
  - *structural coherence*: the structure of experience is isomorphic to
    the structure of awareness, which is "direct availability for global
    control". So global-workspace and similar proposals are best read as
    theories of the mechanism of awareness, and so of a correlate of
    experience;
  - *organizational invariance*: "any two systems with the same
    fine-grained functional organization will have qualitatively identical
    experiences", defended in one page by the dancing-qualia switch;
  - the *double-aspect theory of information*: information has a physical
    and a phenomenal aspect. If every information state has the second,
    experience is widespread, and "perhaps a thermostat … might have
    maximally simple experience".

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request for David Chalmers in this
record. It is the hard problem's founding statement and the record's first
Chalmers paper on consciousness itself.

**On the hard problem.** The record holds several works that take a side
on it without the source. Frankish's illusionist commentary ([LIT-103](LIT-103.md))
charges Veit with leaving the hard problem "as hard as ever". Seth ([LIT-135](LIT-135.md),
read in [NOTE-169](../notes.d/NOTE-169.md)) proposes to dissolve it by the real-problem method, with
the vitalism analogy that §5 of this paper argues is disanalogous. Minsky's
*Emotion Machine* draft ([LIT-432](LIT-432.md)) calls it a compression of many
problems into one word. Nagel ([LIT-096](LIT-096.md)) supplies the notion of
consciousness the paper starts from.

**On the society-of-mind bridge ([THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)).** The paper bears on
the bridge in two places. My pairings, not the paper's claims:

- *Structural coherence turns List's concession into a question.* List
  ([LIT-401](LIT-401.md), read in [NOTE-340](../notes.d/NOTE-340.md)) takes his awareness/experience
  distinction "following Chalmers 1995" and grants groups awareness in the
  access sense. This paper's coherence principle says that "whenever
  information is available for report and for global control, there is a
  corresponding conscious experience". Stated for "familiar cases", it
  would, if it held without restriction, turn List's grant of group
  awareness into group experience. List blocks that step with an IIT-style
  bridge principle. The paper therefore marks the spot where the THEORY's
  second refusal, the architectural criterion, has to work. A global
  workspace criterion is, on this paper's reading, a criterion for
  awareness, so it cannot by itself separate a modular mind from a nation
  on experience.
- *Organizational invariance* appears here only as a sketch. Whether it
  licenses Schwitzgebel's upward move ([LIT-159](LIT-159.md)) is taken up in the full
  paper, [LIT-392](LIT-392.md), and its answer is no.
- The *double-aspect principle* is the "3a" bridge principle that List
  rejects as too unrestricted ([NOTE-340](../notes.d/NOTE-340.md)). Read with no further
  constraint, it is the opposite of an anti-nesting principle: wherever
  there is information there is experience, at every scale. The paper does
  not discuss nesting, groups or combination.

**On AI consciousness ([THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md)).** The paper does not discuss machines.
Its organizational-invariance principle is the conditional that the
functionalist side of [THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md) assumes. Its hard problem is the reason
[THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md) finds behavioural evidence insufficient: explaining every
function leaves experience open.

**On the extended mind ([LIT-097](LIT-097.md)).** The paper predates it and does not
bear on it directly. The extended-mind paper's own exclusion of experience
from its thesis ([NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md)) fits this paper's split between functionally
defined awareness, which could in principle extend, and experience, which
is not functionally defined. That pairing is mine.

Chalmers's later papers on extended consciousness and on the combination
problem, which bear on the last two paragraphs, are being filed in this
record by other readers at the same time.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice, and it does not
belong in the anthology.

**Replies held in this record.** Dennett's "Facing Backwards on the Problem
of Consciousness" ([LIT-397](LIT-397.md)) is the first deflationary reply to this
paper, and Chalmers's own "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness" ([LIT-425](LIT-425.md))
later asks for an explanation of the conviction Dennett says is all that
remains. Chalmers's reply to Dennett, "Moving Forward on the Problem of
Consciousness" (JCS 4(1), 1997), is not filed.
