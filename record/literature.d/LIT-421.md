# LIT-421: Foreword

David J. Chalmers (2008), foreword to Andy Clark, *Supersizing the Mind:
Embodiment, Action, and Cognitive Extension*, Oxford University Press,
pp. ix–xvi — DOI-10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195333213.002.0007

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, as part of the extended-mind
programme of David Chalmers. It introduces Clark's book ([LIT-412](LIT-412.md)),
which reprints [LIT-097](LIT-097.md) as its appendix. [NOTE-369](../notes.d/NOTE-369.md) is the close reading
of the same day, and it placed the work: **Active**.

**It carries [LIT-097](LIT-097.md) further, and corrects one of its replies.** Clark
invited it as "a retrospective perspective on that article". It extends
the 1998 thesis to new cases: the iPhone as memory, desire, calculator and
planner; and, tentatively, extended desire, perception (the phone's
camera) and mood. It corrects the 1998 reply that Otto's access to the
notebook need not be perceptual: "this is surely too quick: there is no
denying that Otto sees the notebook and reads from it". It also reports
that the "notorious footnote" ordering the authors by degree of belief
does not mean Chalmers rejects the thesis; he is "more confident of the
thesis than I was a decade ago". [NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md) flagged that footnote as
mattering for any attribution to Chalmers, and this settles it.

**What it says the thesis reaches.** Standing beliefs and processes, and
plausibly other non-conscious states. Not consciousness, at least "in our
world": an argument for extended consciousness would need internal
duplicates who differ in experience (an "Olga and Twin Olga" case), and no
Otto-style story yields one. He grants that the physical basis of
consciousness could extend in other possible worlds, for instance if part
of the neural correlate were moved to "a module on one's belt".

**On the hard problem.** The foreword separates the two questions
explicitly. "Functionalism about consciousness is implausible", Chalmers
writes, but that "does not affect the arguments for the extended mind
thesis", which need only the parity principle's "very weak
functionalism". The thesis is "compatible with both physicalism and
dualism". So, on his own account, accepting the extended mind commits
nothing about the hard problem, which his work being filed in this record
in the same batch treats.

**On [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md).** The foreword offers a reason why experience might
stay internal while cognition extends: "the physical basis of
consciousness requires direct access to information on an extremely high
bandwidth". That is the bandwidth idea Andy Clark developed, and it is
the high-bandwidth objection Schwitzgebel answers in [LIT-159](LIT-159.md) §6
([NOTE-131](../notes.d/NOTE-131.md)). Chalmers abandoned it in 2019 ([LIT-415](LIT-415.md)) for directness of
access. So the version of the architectural criterion on which
[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) would have to rely is the 2019 one, not this.

**On AI tools.** The iPhone paragraph is the founding image for the
question [NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md) left open, whether a user's assistant can be part of
the user's mind. The foreword's answer is yes for its stored information
and its functions. But by its own Necker-cube moral, for the question "why
did he reach for the phone?" the user lacked the belief. It carries no
instruction for machine-learning practice.
