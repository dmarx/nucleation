## Reading 1: Précis (JSO 2(1): 59-64), read in full
Summary. Tomasello tells how the theory came about (1999: only humans understand others as intentional agents; ape data on goal and perception understanding broke that; a 2001 Leipzig workshop on Gilbert, "Can chimpanzees take a walk together?", led to shared intentionality, Tomasello & Rakoczy 2003, Tomasello et al. 2005). The book recasts the comparative ape/child data as a hypothetical evolutionary scenario, focused on thinking as three processes: off-line representation, inferential simulation (causal, intentional, logical), and self-monitoring. Three forms of intentionality are distinguished: individual (great apes), joint (early humans before culture; to a degree prelinguistic infants), collective (modern cultural humans). Each form has its own representation, inference and self-monitoring.
Contribution (author's own statement): the distinction of joint from collective intentionality as a "middle step", second-personal (Darwall) dyadic collaboration before culture.
Key results (section = paragraph order; printed page; quote):
- p. 60: the claimed contribution lies "in distinguishing between joint intentionality and collective intentionality" (shared intentionality the superordinate category).
- p. 61: three forms: "individual intentionality, as characteristic of great apes" in general; joint for early humans; collective for modern adults. (Ch. 1 framing.)
- p. 61 (Ch. 2): apes represent situations schematically, "as perceptual abstractions in iconic format", from goal-directed voluntary attention, so no referential indeterminacy; the key representations are situations/states of affairs, since goals are fact-like.
- p. 62 (Ch. 2): if situation representation is primate-wide, "the evolution of propositional thought is a bit less mysterious".
- p. 62 (Ch. 2): apes infer in modus ponens, exclusion (disjunctive syllogism) and proto-modus-tollens forms; "The trick is that the inferences are based on, and only on, causal and" intentional relations. Apes self-monitor.
- p. 62 (Ch. 3): joint goals structured by joint attention, with different roles and perspectives: "This dual-level structure of joint agency" yields "a shared world comprising distinct individual perspectives" and so perspectival representation.
- p. 63 (Ch. 3): cooperative communication by pointing and pantomime (Tomasello 2008), ostensive-inferential, recursive, needing common ground; communicators socially self-monitor from the partner's view. Summary: "(i) perspectival cognitive representations; (ii) recursive inferences; and" (iii) social (dyadically normative) self-monitoring.
- p. 63 (Ch. 4): modern humans became "group-minded creatures" with cultural common ground (conventions, norms, institutions, linguistic constructions); "anyone who would be one of us" led
- p. 64 (Ch. 4): "to an objectification of the group’s social and institutional norms" (curly apostrophe in source); pedagogy's generic voice; result: "objective" representations, "(ii) self-reflective inferences; and (iii) generically normative" self-monitoring.
- p. 64: admits borrowing and modifying philosophers' concepts out of context: "I plead guilty to this charge" (quote only "guilty to this charge", line break before it).
Assumptions: the great-ape starting point is taken as established by experiments "interpreted in certain ways", and its philosophical problems are bracketed; an evolutionary scenario "broadly consistent" with paleoanthropology; infants as a rough model of early humans ("the analogy should not be pushed too far", p. 61).
Concepts: shared/joint/collective intentionality; joint goal; joint attention; dual-level (jointness + individuality) structure; perspectival vs objective representation; recursive inference; social vs normative self-monitoring; common ground (personal vs cultural); second-personal relations; "anyone who would be one of us".
Coverage of the book: Ch. 1 framing and history (well); Ch. 2 two paragraphs (representation, inference, self-monitoring); Ch. 3 two paragraphs (the core); Ch. 4 one paragraph; Ch. 5 (Human Thinking as Cooperation) not summarised as such; Ch. 6 not. No data, no citations of the experiments, no paleoanthropology. A ~5-page abstract of a 154-page argument: the architecture, not the evidence.

## Reading 2: Response to Commentators (JSO 2(1): 117-123), read in full
Summary. Clears terms ("knowledge" = knowledge by acquaintance of what another has seen; "intention" = committed plan toward a goal vs "intentional states" = propositional). Main charge from all four commentators: his account of 1-2-year-olds' joint intentionality is "too generous and too individualistic (neo-Cartesian)" (p. 118) and presupposes the objective content it is to explain. Answer: evolutionary dialectic, not analysis. Apes already collaborate with non-propositional situation content; selection on that practice favoured joint goals (pp. 118-119). Perspective needs only two viewers of a jointly attended object; objectivity comes from the in-principle judgement "anyone who would be one of us", so the objective "perspective is in fact a perspectiveless view from nowhere" (p. 119). Joint intentionality is not propositional, "but it has the seeds of this by joining" individual intentionalities (p. 120); he uses "joint goal" rather than "joint intention", citing Bratman for an idea, not the analysis. Concedes to Schmitz on the "assumption of cooperation": "I had no clear idea of its cognitive status" (p. 120); restates it as joint perception that each is trying to cooperate, reached via a recursive step "he sees me seeing the banana" (p. 120), and calls recursive embedding an outstanding unsolved problem. Rejects enactive alternatives (Satne, Schmitz) as "they are vague at all the key places" (p. 120); rejects Pacherie's team reasoning as metaphorical, Bermúdez as an ape account, and Butterfill's minimal shared goal as chimpanzee group hunting: "this is just way too thin for joint attention" (p. 122). Accepts Satne's point that joint and collective may develop in parallel, but "it is just an empirical fact that young" children are skilled with joint before collective intentionality (p. 122). Agrees with Moll: "shared intentionality is no ordinary adaptation" (p. 122) but a transformative mode, not an additive module, changing representation, inference and self-regulation (pp. 122-123).
Coverage: no chapter summary; clarifies Ch. 3 (joint goals, joint attention, cooperative communication, recursive mindreading), the Ch. 4 account of objectivity, and the book's method and central "changed everything" claim (quoting the book; a Davidson epigraph heads the final chapter). It reports what the commentators read as weak points.
Limitations of the stand-ins together: no evidence is reproduced; Ch. 5 (thinking as cooperation, presumably reasoning and justification) and Ch. 6 essentially unseen; quotations of the book come only via Tomasello's own restatements.
