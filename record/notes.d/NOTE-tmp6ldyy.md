# NOTE-tmp6ldyy: Tomasello et al. — Understanding and sharing intentions: The origins of cultural cognition

## Contribution

A synthesis of comparative and developmental work arguing that the
uniquely human capacity is not reading intentions but sharing them, and
that cultural cognition, language and norms derive from it. It supplies
the model of individual intention (Bratman's planning theory, as a
cybernetic control system) and of shared activity that Tomasello's later
accounts of morality and obligation build on.

## Key results

1. **Shared goals.** Each participant "has the goal that we (in mutual
   knowledge) do X together" (§3, p. 680), "thus creating a “shared
   commitment” (Gilbert 1989)" (p. 680).
2. **Not common knowledge of the same goal.** "But this is not enough; we
   might each want the box open and know that the other does also, but
   still not form a shared goal (perhaps we will compete to see who can
   open it)." (note 3, p. 691).
3. **Apes.** They "are not motivated to create shared goals to which they
   are jointly committed in the same way as humans (such that they would
   be upset if the other reneged; see sect. 4.1.2 on apes)" (§3.2, p. 682).
4. **Morality comes later.** Internalized conventions bring "a new kind of
   social rationality (morality) involving what Searle (1995) calls
   “desire-independent reasons for action.”" (§3.4, p. 684).
5. **Phylogeny.** Humans "are motivationally built for strong reciprocity,
   in which their behavior is governed by social norms of “fairness”"
   (§5.1, p. 688), offered as one possible pressure.
6. **The weakest claim, by the authors' account.** On the 14-month
   collaboration claim: "this claim has the least empirical support of any
   in the article" (response R3, p. 723).

## Assumptions

Intentional action is a control system with goals and plans;
intention-reading is primate-general; a motivation to share psychological
states is the human adaptation; development interweaves the two lines;
universality is asserted with little cross-cultural data.

## Concepts

Shared intentionality; dyadic, triadic and collaborative engagement; joint
attention; role reversal; dialogic cognitive representation; shared goal
and shared commitment; collective intentionality.

## Bearing on the record

- **THEORY-495** (Tomasello 2020, obligation), a precursor with three
  shifts. In 2005 the dyadic commitment carries no obligation, only being
  upset at reneging; morality enters only with internalized conventions,
  borrowed from Searle; by 2020 felt obligation starts inside the dyadic
  joint commitment. In 2005 shared commitment comes with shared goals at
  12–14 months; in 2020 the obligation-specific behaviours appear at about
  three years. Read together the papers put joint intention and committed
  obligation about two years apart, which supports Bratman's point,
  recorded in THEORY-495, that commitment rather than collaboration does
  the work. And in 2005 strong reciprocity is a possible substrate, where
  THEORY-495 argues against reciprocity as a source of obligation.
- **LIT-471 and LIT-488** (Bratman), adopted: the planning theory of
  intention and Bratman's conditions on shared activity, used as
  developmental stages and a species contrast rather than as analysis.
- **LIT-492 and LIT-477** (Gilbert), a borrowed label: "shared
  commitment" here has no plural subject and no rights of rebuke; its mark
  is affective. Note 3 argues in Gilbert's manner against reducing a
  shared goal to common knowledge.
- **THEORY-625 and LIT-485** (Searle), relied on for institutional facts
  and desire-independent reasons; but here we-intentionality is built in
  development, not taken as a primitive.
- **TERM-080**, both senses: participatory (joint intention) and
  institutional (collective intentionality), kept apart by developmental
  stage.
- **LIT-504**, a precursor: the interdependence hypothesis is absent
  here (it is in LIT-tmpsh1xk).
- **CLAIM-096 and QUESTION-007**, background only: no grounding of
  normativity.

## Limitations

A review with no new data; many findings then in press from the same lab;
ape negatives partly from unpublished null results; the 14-month claim
conceded as least supported and about infant–adult, not peer, interaction;
thin cross-cultural and autism evidence; the motivation to share
underspecified; nothing on obligation.
