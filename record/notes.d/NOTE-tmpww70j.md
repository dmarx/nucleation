# NOTE-tmpww70j: Mental Hoop Diaries: Emotional Memories of a College Basketball Game in Rival Fans

## Contribution

A method for studying emotional memory of a real, complex social event
under experimental control. Rival fans watch the same game and are cued
with the same clips. A shot made by Duke is a good memory for a Duke fan
and a bad one for a UNC fan, so positive and negative memories are matched
on every perceptual feature. With it the paper separates the brain
correlates of a memory's emotional intensity from those of its valence.

## Key insight

Allegiance, not the event, fixes whether a remembered play is good or bad.
Across the two sides "the same exact event can be classified as either
positive or negative according to the perspective of the fan"
(introduction). Fans then remember their own side's good moments better,
which reverses the usual finding with simple stimuli, where negative
material is the more intense (Discussion, "Recalling positively- vs
negatively-valent plays").

## Assumptions

- **Fandom is a stable part of the self.** Recruitment rests on Wann's fan
  measures: "Individuals whose fan status is important for their
  self-identity experience intense emotions related to the team's
  performance" (Methods, Participants). Fan identification was used to
  screen, not analysed.
- **A yes/no shot outcome indexes memory for the play.** Accuracy was set
  by pilot work to 70–80%, and only correct trials (hits) enter the fMRI
  analyses.
- **Valence and intensity are what the fan says.** One combined rating
  from −4 to +4 per clip, given in the scanner after the memory decision.
- **Reverse inference.** Midline and temporoparietal activity is read as
  self-referential processing or mentalizing; the authors call these
  contributions "currently speculative" (Discussion).

## Key results

- **Sample and design** (Methods). 12 Duke fans (mean age 20.25) and 11
  UNC fans (22.64), all male, screened for high self-rated fandom and at
  least 75% correct on three basketball-knowledge tests. Women were
  excluded after a pilot in which five female fans recalled shots at 60%
  (chance 50%). The game, played at UNC in 2001, was unknown to all. Each
  watched it three times over a week, mostly in groups of 2–3 fans of the
  same team, the last viewing a day before scanning. 64 clips of 12 s,
  half positive from each side's perspective, balanced for makes and
  misses, shown in game order.
- **Behaviour** (Results, Behavioral Data; Table 1).
  - Overall recall 0.74; Duke 75%, UNC 74%; no group difference in
    accuracy (P = 0.72), confidence (P = 0.065) or intensity (P = 0.42).
    Duke fans answered faster (1383 vs 1898 ms, P = 0.006).
  - More correct memories were positive than negative (P = 0.042).
    Positive shots got higher confidence (P = 0.002) and higher intensity
    (P = 0.001). The authors read the confidence result as confirming
    Kensinger and Schacter's study of Red Sox and Yankees fans.
  - Ratings went the way allegiance predicts for 88.9% of correctly
    recalled shots.
- **Intensity** (Results, Effects of Emotional Intensity; Tables 2, 3).
  Activity rose with rated intensity in lateral and medial PFC, striatum
  (globus pallidus, putamen), red nucleus, left temporoparietal junction,
  posterior cingulate, precuneus and cuneus, temporal cortex, both
  hippocampi, right amygdala, sensorimotor cortex and cerebellum. Nothing
  rose for low over high. In hand-drawn medial temporal ROIs, intensity
  had a main effect (P < .001) and valence did not (P = .096); amygdala
  signal ran from 0.93 (low) to 2.35 (high).
- **Valence** (Results, Effects of Emotional Valence; Table 4). Positive
  over negative: right sensorimotor cortex, left superior PFC (BA 9),
  right parietal lobules, cingulate (BA 24). Nothing for negative over
  positive. Confidence boosted the positive–negative difference in right
  insula, left hippocampus/parahippocampal gyrus, claustrum and left
  dorsolateral PFC. The predicted orbitofrontal effect could not be tested
  because of signal dropout.
- **Across the rivalry** (Conclusions). "Despite strong feelings of
  opposition to the rival team, this study revealed an emotion-induced
  resonance in brain activity across rival fans while reflecting on
  emotionally intense plays."

## Concepts

- **emotional intensity** versus **valence**: how strongly a play is felt,
  versus whether it is good or bad for the fan; rated together on one
  −4 to +4 scale and analysed apart.
- **positivity bias in recall**: better and more confident memory for
  positive events; here for one's own team's successes and the rival's
  misses.
- **fan identification** (Wann and Branscombe): how central being a fan of
  a team is to one's self-identity; here a screening criterion.

## Connections

- **The rivalry case (CASE-098).** The work was filed for "the memory side"
  of how a rivalry is handed on. It studies memory of one game, a day
  after learning it, not transmission.
- **Talarico and Moore (LIT-1125)**, unread. By its LIT summary, fans of the
  winning side of a Lafayette–Lehigh game later recalled it more
  accurately and vividly. Botzung's within-fan positivity bias runs the
  same way; the two cannot be compared further unread.
- **Kensinger and Schacter (2006)**, cited, not held: memory for a Red
  Sox–Yankees game in opposing fans, with overconfidence for positive
  memories.
- **Kilduff, Elfenbein and Staw (NOTE-tmpiffjj).** They measure rivalry in
  the teams and find it a property of the pair; Botzung measures it in
  the fans and finds the same events stored with opposite signs.
- **Berendt and Uhrich (THEORY-tmpk1fow).** They tie rivalry to fans'
  identity benefits; Botzung shows identity reaching into memory, but
  measures no identity variation.

## Bearing on the record

- **The rivalry case (CASE-098).** Three points. They are inferences; the
  paper does not discuss rivalry as a relation or its transmission.
  - Its situation is the case's, by accident. Participants learned a
    rivalry game played years before, which none of them knew, from
    archive footage and among fellow fans. Their side still fixed what
    each play meant to them. A new fan learning the rivalry's old
    incidents would be in the same position.
  - Two sides, one map. Both groups recalled equally well and rated
    intensity alike, and the authors find "resonance" across them. If the
    plays each side found intense were the same plays (the paper does not
    test this), a rivalry would be held in memory as shared salience with
    opposite valence: a two-sided relation in the fans, not only in the
    teams.
  - Each side's history is tilted. Fans remember their own side's
    successes better. Histories carried by fans would then be two partisan
    versions of the same record.
  - It does not touch A5's discriminating variation: nothing interrupts
    transmission, and memory is tested after one day.
  - A dated note is proposed for CASE-098 (section 3).
- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** Weakly. A team's success is
  stored as the fan's own good event, and the authors call the fans
  "highly personally involved with the game despite experiencing it
  vicariously". That fits a self that takes in the team. But fan
  identification was a screening threshold and was not varied, so the
  paper cannot show that valence follows the extent of the self rather
  than caring about the team (CLAIM-tmprudek). No dated note on the claim.
- **No THEORY filed.** One small sample and one game.
- **No ML instruction.**

## Limitations

- **Small and narrow.** 23 men, highly committed fans of two teams; women
  excluded on a five-person pilot.
- **Learned memories, short delay.** An archived game seen three times in
  a lab, tested a day later. Not a lived game, and not long-term memory.
- **Binary recall.** "In or out" measures one detail per play; vividness
  and narrative are not measured.
- **Valence confounded with intensity.** Positive shots were rated more
  intense. Intensity was modelled as a covariate in the confidence
  analysis, and the valence contrast is described as controlling for it.
- **No identity variable.** Fandom was a screen; its strength is not
  related to any result.
- **Figures absent** from this copy; the supplementary questionnaires were
  not seen.
- **Reporting errors and typos.**
  - Every t-test is printed with 23 degrees of freedom. With 23
    participants, between-group tests have 21 and within-subject tests
    22; the printed p-values match those.
  - The confidence reaction-time test repeats the confidence statistic
    exactly ("t23 = −1.95, P = 0.065").
  - The intensity reaction-time test, t = −1.53, gives p ≈ .14, not the
    printed 0.16.
  - The ROI ANOVA prints "df" in place of every denominator degrees of
    freedom.
  - Aggleton et al. (2000) is cited for the amygdala and negative
    stimuli, but the reference listed is on cortical inputs to the rat
    hippocampus.
  - "Maguire et al., 2003" in the introduction; the reference is Maguire
    and Frith 2003. "Buckner and Caroll" for Carroll; "Hearthon" for
    Heatherton in the Kelley et al. reference.
  - "set a P = .05" for "at"; 3.75 × 3.75 × 3.8 mm voxels called
    "isotropic".

## Open questions

- Do rival fans find the *same* plays intense? A per-shot comparison of
  the two groups' intensity ratings, from these data, would say whether a
  rivalry gives both sides one map of what mattered.
- Does the positivity bias survive retelling? A design in which fans who
  learned the game pass it on to new fans, tested weeks later, would bear
  on CASE-098's transmission directly.
