# NOTE-tmpaa0uz: Næss (working paper) — Investigative journalism: Market failures and government intervention through public broadcasters

## Contribution

A public-goods model plus survey experiments. Each person's payoff from investigative journalism is shared; a firm selling access can collect only private willingness to pay (WTP), so when marginal cost lies between one person's marginal benefit and the sum over N people, provision is Pareto-inefficiently low. Surveys measure WTP for three days of investigative journalism and support for funding it through public broadcasters versus subsidies to private media; treatments test exclusive access (a Coasian property-rights fix), widespread dissemination, and a promise of political neutrality.

## Key results

- §1: "the societal value of increased investigative journalism—measured as the sum of all individuals’ WTP—far exceeds the value of payments that private media firms can collect, indicating that markets will underprovide investigative journalism."
- §1: "investigative journalism is often not profitable (Hamilton, 2016)".
- §1: "54% of Norwegian survey respondents expressed a willingness to pay an additional $15 in taxes"; "55% of US respondents, but only 35% of UK respondents".
- §1: given the choice, "70% of US respondents preferred public broadcasters, contrasted by only 35% in the UK and 40% in Norway."
- Abstract: "A Coasian solution is ineffective, as willingness to pay increases with wider output sharing." §7: "In the US and UK versions of the survey, the average WTP is actually higher when the output is shared widely" (Table 1: +0.790, p < 0.05, US/UK).
- §1: the Neutrality treatment raised Norwegian willingness to pay more tax "by 6 percentage points (p = 0.033)".

## Assumptions

Stated WTP measures value (consequentiality was invoked to improve realism); the small-firm assumption; three days of journalism as the unit.

## Concepts

Public good (non-rival, non-excludable); underprovision; Coasian solution via exclusive output rights; public broadcaster; perceived political bias; public-interest vs public-choice theories of public media.

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** The LIT summary (A37 §4) is accurate: investigative journalism as a public good, survey evidence from the US, UK and Norway on underprovision and public broadcasters. It omits the twist that support for public provision is cut by perceived broadcaster bias, and the finding that people value wider sharing.

- **[CLAIM-tmpifp69](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpifp69.md)** (attention-optimizing news works against investigative journalism, "whose value falls largely to people who never pay for them"): **supports the second reason it gives**, the externality: WTP summed over all exceeds what a seller can collect, and exclusivity does not raise WTP. It **leaves open** the attention/clickbait mechanism: the paper does not study attention-based revenue, ranking, or clickbait, and attributes the decline to lost advertising revenue (citing Angelucci and Cagé), which is the rival [CLAIM-tmpifp69](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpifp69.md)'s `defeated_if` names.
- **[CASE-tmpd5eqf](../cases.d/CASE-tmpd5eqf.md)** (newsroom allocating by measured demand cuts investigations): **supports weakly**, by showing value that measured paid demand cannot register.
- **[ARG-tmp7eevo](../arguments.d/ARG-tmp7eevo.md)** (found on recheck; transfer from games to news): consistent with its point that what news "weights" differs (here, external value not captured by any revenue signal).
