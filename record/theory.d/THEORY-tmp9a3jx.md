# THEORY-tmp9a3jx: Akerlof and Kranton propose putting identity, a self-image tied to social categories and their prescriptions, into the utility function, so that one's own and others' departures from the prescriptions cost utility, and use it to account for gender-typed occupations, oppositional identities and the asymmetry in housework

## The account

People "behave in ways that would be considered maladaptive or even
self-destructive by those with other identities", to "bolster a sense of
self or to salve a diminished self-image" (p. 717). Utility falls when
one's own actions break one's category's prescriptions and when others'
actions do; the second is an externality, which one may relieve at a
cost by acting against the violator. Categories are partly ascriptive:
the excluded "could never be a 'true' Green" (p. 748). A two-person game
gives four equilibria that taxes and rhetoric can shift. No group's
welfare enters anyone's utility.

## Evidence

Illustrative (NOTE-1311): a 70-household vignette survey and
ethnographies; aggregate trends in gender-typed jobs; the housework
asymmetry over 29,000 couple-years, where husbands' share rises to no
more than 37% however little they earn. The authors grant the
experiments they rely on "do not show the existence of a 'self' or this
identification per se" (p. 720).

## What it bears on

QUESTION-300 asks whether apparently selfless action is
"self-interest on an enlarged self" and needs "a criterion of the self's
extent that is independent of what the agent cares about". The model
makes identity-driven sacrifice utility-maximizing by construction, with
the self served a self-image rather than a self that includes a group's
good, and its criterion, category assignment fixed partly by others,
could come apart from caring though its weights are never measured.
