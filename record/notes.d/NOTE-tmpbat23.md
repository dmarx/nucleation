# NOTE-tmpbat23: Evolution of the Internal Family Systems Model

## Contribution

A compact statement of the IFS model by its developer. It covers the two
paradigms, the three part roles, the founding "step back" case, the Self and
its eight qualities. Together with the book's chapter 1 ([NOTE-tmpfag1y](NOTE-tmpfag1y.md)), it
gives the record the model's central claims in Schwartz's words.

## Key insight

Inner conflict is a family conflict. Parts have roles, alliances and
polarizations, and the roles are not the parts' nature. A person's healthiest
state is not an achieved balance among the parts. It is a further entity, the
Self, which is already there and becomes available when parts relax. That is
what distinguishes IFS from other multiplicity models.

## Assumptions

- Clients' reports of inner "voices" or "parts" describe real functional
  units that can be addressed, negotiated with and changed.
- Family-systems principles (roles, sequences, polarization, leadership)
  apply unchanged at the intrapsychic level.
- Family-of-origin "values and interaction patterns", more often than
  trauma, are what "create internal polarizations".

## Key results

None formal. The essay's claims:

- Parts "take on common roles and common inner relationships", and these
  "could be changed if one intervened carefully and respectfully".
- The step-back procedure: in the case of "Diane" (the "Cora" of the 2020
  book), asking an angry part to step back produced "a calm, caring voice",
  and clients describe that state as "my Self".
- The Self "wasn't just a passive witness state ... but could also be an
  active healing presence". Self-leadership persists after therapy once
  parts trust it.
- The eight Cs of Self-leadership: "calmness, curiosity, clarity,
  compassion, confidence, creativity, courage, and connectedness".
- Meditation's "Self-less oceanic state" is distinguished from the Self that
  leads daily life: "the Self is a separate wave of that ocean".

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Inner parts take common roles (managers, exiles, firefighters) across people | clinical generalisation from "a large number of clients"; no data | §"Managers, Firefighters, and Exiles" |
| C2 | Everyone has a Self with leadership qualities, even after severe abuse | asserted from "hundreds of clients for more than two decades" | §"The Self" |
| C3 | The Self is an active healing presence, and Self-led people stay centred under emotion | clinical description | §"The Self-Led Person" |

## Concepts

- **Managers, exiles, firefighters**: the three common part roles: control
  and protection, carried pain, and emergency suppression of that pain.
- **Self-leadership**: the Self leading the inner system, with parts
  contributing but deferring.
- **The eight Cs**: the qualities by which Self-presence is recognised.

## Connections

The two companion pages read with it add little to the model. "The Larger
Self" retells the Diane case and argues that the therapist's own
Self-leadership is "the most important variable in how quickly clients can
access their Selves". It identifies the Self with the soul, Buddha nature,
Atman and other names in "the esoteric traditions". The unsigned Outline
lists the model's basic assumptions, among them "it is the nature of the mind
to be subdivided into an indeterminate number of subpersonalities or parts"
and "the Self can and should lead the individual's internal system". It also
lists techniques.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-044](../theory.d/THEORY-044.md) and [THEORY-051](../theory.d/THEORY-051.md).** The record's only account of a
  therapeutic "self" so far is ACT's self as context, a perspective with no
  content. IFS's Self is the opposite kind of construct: an entity with
  qualities, intentions and a leading role. A THEORY that "a non-fused
  observing perspective is a common active ingredient of ACT and IFS" would
  be tempting. Nothing in either source tests it. It would need process
  studies of Self-access in IFS that the record does not hold, so it stays
  a question for now, not a Proposed theory.
- **Multiplicity models in the record.** IFS is a clinical counterpart to
  Ainslie's successive bargaining "interests" ([LIT-tmpne8q4](../literature.d/LIT-tmpne8q4.md)) and to
  Minsky's society of mind ([LIT-432](../literature.d/LIT-432.md)). Unlike both, it posits a leader that
  is not one of the parts.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- Undated and without a venue. Its text dates it to the early 2000s, and
  it may be an excerpt.
- It is clinical testimony. It reports no study, and its "everyone has a
  Self" is a generalisation from the author's practice.
- It does not address dissociative identity disorder or how IFS's parts
  relate to it. The 2020 book's chapter 1 does.

## Open questions

- Whether the eight Cs cohere as one state that can be measured, which is
  the precondition for testing the Self as a mechanism of change.
