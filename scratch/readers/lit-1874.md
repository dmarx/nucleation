## Summary

Mulgan asks what account of corporate agency is "temporally robust": one that
gives the people of a range of credible futures the concepts to recognise and
resist threats from corporate groups. He sorts the present debate into four
positions (individualism; minimal, moderate and extreme collectivism) and
three methods (realist, pragmatist, interpretivist), adds a "moralised"
condition (moral agency only for groups reliably disposed to respond
appropriately to moral reasons), and then imagines two futures. In a broken
future (climate-wrecked, Rawlsian favourable conditions lost), philosophy turns
pragmatist, consequentialist and interpersonal, so individualism loses
credibility and moralised (still moderate) collectivism wins. In a digital
future, recognised digital persons make functionalist criteria standard, so
corporate agency becomes undeniable and the agency/personhood line collapses;
non-moralised extreme collectivism would risk an "inhuman corporate future".
Conclusion: the only temporally robust account is moralised extreme
collectivism.

## Contribution

A methodological argument (choose a theory of corporate agency by its
robustness across credible futures, not only by present truth or fit) and a
taxonomy that separates agency from personhood and adds a reliability-based
moral condition. It is a speculative, scenario-based argument; it reports no
cases.

## Key results, with page and quote

1. **Four positions.** p. 903: individualism, "Corporate groups are not agents
   of any kind"; minimal collectivism, independent agents but not moral
   agents; moderate, "moral agents, but none are fully fledged moral persons";
   extreme, "both moral agents and moral persons". Footnote 4: "I borrow the
   individualism/collectivism distinction from Velasquez (2003), p. 38. The
   minimal/moderate/extreme distinction is my own."
2. **No mainstream extreme collectivist.** p. 904: "I can find no mainstream
   theorist who defends extreme collectivism"; French, List and Pettit,
   Hindriks and Hess "explicitly distance themselves from full moral
   personhood in my sense." Footnote 5 allows French (1984) as a possible
   exception.
3. **Velasquez's slippery slope.** p. 904: "Individualists argue that moderate
   collectivism is an unstable position (e.g. Velasquez 2003; Rovane 2014).
   Once we recognise corporate groups as moral agents, we are on a slippery
   slope to recognising their full moral personhood."
4. **Velasquez bites the bullet on the responsibility gap.** pp. 903–904:
   individualists "must accept that, if no individual is responsible, then no
   one is responsible (Velasquez 2003 explicitly bites this bullet)."
5. **Velasquez's criteria, as Mulgan lists them.** p. 904: being a biological
   organism "(Velasquez 2003; Miller and Makela 2005)"; possessing
   intentionality; being "moral, responsive to moral reasons" (each with
   Velasquez 2003 among others); "individualists often elide agency and
   personhood, defending similar conditions for both (e.g. Velasquez 2003 ...)".
   p. 905: individualists favour "phenomenological or organic accounts where
   intentionality demands either conscious awareness or specifically human
   embodiment (... Velasquez 2003 on the Thomist tradition ...)".
6. **Velasquez as realist.** p. 907: "For the realist, the philosopher's primary
   task is to determine the (metaphysical) facts (e.g. Velasquez 1983, 2003)."
7. **Velasquez rests on Searle.** p. 913: "His defence of individualism
   explicitly cites John Searle's account of consciousness as an emergent
   feature specific to human biological embodiment." Digital-future
   philosophers "will dismiss Searle's position as simple anti-digital
   prejudice."
8. **The moralised condition.** p. 905: recognition vs response, ability vs
   reliability; "The most demanding moral criterion would require reliably
   appropriate response." Groups endorsing it are "moralised collectivists".
   p. 906: List and Pettit 2011 p. 159, Hess 2013 and Tollefsen 2015 ch. 6
   endorse or suggest it.
9. **Psychopathic corporate groups.** p. 906: many for-profit enterprises "are
   closer to psychopaths than to normally functioning human adults (e.g. Bakan
   2004, p. 56ff). They can act, intend, plan, and display instrumental
   rationality. But they feel no empathy ..." p. 910: a group whose
   "management structure lacks any mechanism for recognising and then
   responding appropriately to moral reasons ... is irredeemably
   psychopathic. (And a psychopathic corporate group, unlike its human
   equivalent, has no moral standing at all.)" p. 914: "nonpsychotic
   individuals can together constitute a psychopathic corporate group."
10. **List and Pettit's moral condition is pragmatist.** pp. 906–907: "their
    moral condition is clearly pragmatist. They argue that we should restrict
    moral agency to corporate groups that are morally reliable, not because
    this tracks some underlying metaphysical reality, but rather because it
    best serves the practical purpose of advancing the interests of human
    individuals."
11. **Broken futures favour moralised collectivism.** p. 910: "Broken world
    philosophers will favour collectivism over individualism. They will also
    favour moralised collectivism"; "anyone wishing to create a new corporate
    group ... must first demonstrate that it will be morally reliable."
12. **Digital futures.** p. 913: "In a digital future, collectivists will
    embrace that slippery slope"; p. 914: new corporate groups "must also pass
    their own Moral Turing Test".
13. **Conclusion.** p. 914: "The only temporally robust account is moralised
    extreme collectivism, where suitably sophisticated morally reliable
    corporate groups (and only those corporate groups) are recognised as both
    responsible agents and full moral persons."
14. **Less radical than it looks.** p. 914: it "is consistent with
    maintaining both that no extant digital entity or corporate group is
    (even) a moral agent and also that it is not inevitable that digital or
    corporate persons will ever emerge."

## Assumptions

- A temporally robust ethic must (1) be plausible to future people, (2) let
  them address salient threats, (3) not strike us, after reflection, as
  unacceptable (p. 902).
- Broken and digital futures are credible; the digital future is stipulated to
  recognise digital beings as moral persons (p. 911, fn. 12: epistemic, not
  metaphysical, possibility).
- Future people will "successfully eliminate (or neutralise) morally
  unreliable digital beings" (p. 912), set aside as an assumption.
- Both sides assume corporate groups are not phenomenally conscious (p. 905).

## Verdict on the source's characterization

The LIT's summary (from the abstract) is **accurate**: temporally robust
account, broken or digital futures, domination by corporate groups with no
regard for humans, and "moralised extreme collectivism" for "(only)" reliably
moral groups.

Its expectation, "Expected to report Velasquez's denial of corporate agency",
is **partly met**. Mulgan reports Velasquez's *position* in seven places
(results 1, 3–7), but only by parenthetical citation and paraphrase: no
quotation of Velasquez and no reconstruction of his argument. The record's
gloss in QUESTION-265 ("responsibility needs a mind that forms intentions and a
body it controls, and a corporation has neither") is consistent with what
Mulgan attributes (organic embodiment, intentionality, Thomist tradition,
Searle), but Mulgan adds four attributions the record does not hold:
(a) the individualism/collectivism frame is Velasquez's (2003, p. 38);
(b) he bites the bullet that if no individual is responsible no one is;
(c) he argues moderate collectivism is unstable, a slippery slope to
personhood; (d) his individualism rests on Searle's biological account of
consciousness, and he is a realist about the question.

**How far it stands in for Velasquez (LIT-1429, LIT-1432): in part.** It is a
secondary report by an opponent, enough to say *what* Velasquez holds and
*which premises* he relies on (biological embodiment / Searlean consciousness),
not enough to assess his argument or to count it as answered. Mulgan's reply to
Velasquez is not a refutation on present terms: he concedes the realist "may
not" be persuaded (p. 914) and his answer is that Velasquez's Searlean premise
will not be credible in a digital future.

  reading of French in which "moral person" falls short of Mulgan's
  personhood (rights to life, vote, non-ownership). A filer should check this
  against THEORY-131's title ("a full-fledged moral person"): the two uses of

## Limitations, including gaps in the argument

- **Velasquez citation problems.** Fn. 4 cites "Velasquez (2003), p. 38", but
  the reference list gives Velasquez 2003 as "Business ethics quarterly, 13,
  545–546" (p. 916). The record's LIT-1432 (doi 10.5840/beq200313436) is a
  full article; a page 38 cannot be in BEQ vol. 13's pp. 545–546. Either the
  page or the reference is wrong; a filer should not quote "p. 38" as
  Velasquez's.
- **Predictions stand in for arguments.** The case against individualism is
  that future philosophers "will" find it less credible; for a realist that is
  not a reason, and Mulgan concedes his reply "may not persuade realists"
  (p. 914). His second reply works only for a realist who already grants that
  agency is organisational.
- **The moralised condition is never operationalised.** "Reliably disposed to
  respond appropriately to moral reasons" and the "Moral Turing Test" (p. 914)
  are named, not specified: who certifies reliability, and by what evidence,
  is unstated.
- **Agency and personhood conflated at the conclusion.** The moralised
  condition is argued for moral *agency* (broken futures, p. 910), but the
  conclusion grants *personhood* to the reliable; the step from "moral agency
  only for the reliable" to "full personhood for the sophisticated reliable"
  rests on the digital-persons analogy alone.
- **"Psychopathic groups have no moral standing"** (p. 910) is asserted; it
  does not follow from the reliability condition, which is about agency, not
  standing.
- **Elimination of unreliable digital beings is assumed** (p. 912), though it
  is the hard part of the scenario.
- No empirical evidence; Heede (2014) and Arnold (2016) on climate emissions
  are the only data cited, for the broken-future premise.

"The only account of corporate agency that is temporally robust, giving people
in credible broken and digital futures the concepts to resist domination by
corporate groups, is moralised extreme collectivism: full moral personhood
open in principle to sophisticated corporate groups reliably disposed to

unreliable ones."
