# LIT-tmpkywoj: Levels of valence

Vera Shuman, David Sander & Klaus R. Scherer (2013), *Frontiers in
Psychology* 4:261 — DOI-10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00261

## Key takeaways

- **Multifaceted valence** (pp. 1–3). In the component process model,
  "qualitatively different types of valence are proposed based on
  appraisals of (un)pleasantness, goal obstructiveness/conduciveness, low
  or high power, self-(in)congruence, and moral badness/goodness" (p. 1).
- **Pleasantness is not goal conduciveness** (p. 2). Intrinsic
  pleasantness is "a strong, genetically determined association of
  particular objects with (dis)pleasure". "Goal conduciveness is a second,
  qualitatively different, type of valence." Needs fix goal conduciveness
  directly but only "set thresholds for pleasantness". The split is
  grounded in Berridge: "pleasure is independent of goal conduciveness.
  The sensual enjoyment of an object and the goal striving toward an
  object rely on different brain mechanisms (e.g., Berridge, 2003)."
- **Mixed feelings** (p. 3) arise by three routes: conflict within one
  check, between checks, or between levels of processing of one check.
- **Macro-valence** (pp. 4–7) is one-dimensional, the "integrative
  'common currency' to compare alternatives for choices" (p. 1), always
  present and possibly objectless, like core affect.
- **Integration rules** (pp. 8–9). Micro-valences combine by a weighted
  sum, or by tallying, lexicographic, elimination-by-aspects or
  one-clever-cue rules, for which "no trade-offs between micro-valences
  are required" (p. 8). Some micro-valence may "generally dominate" the
  rest, moral goodness being the candidate (p. 9).
- **Levels interact over time** (pp. 9–12), as a nonlinear dynamic
  system. Macro-valence feeds back on later appraisals. Emotions are
  attractor states. Emotions can themselves be appraised, so their valence
  is not fixed (p. 12).
