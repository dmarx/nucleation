# NOTE-452: The Intimate Contest for Self-Command

## Contribution

An economist's case that the single, consistent consumer of textbook
economics is a convenient fiction for many decisions, and that a person can
be treated, for self-management, the way a family or a firm is: as more than
one decision-maker. The essay's contribution is a vocabulary and a
catalogue: it collects tactics of self-command, sorts habits along
dimensions that matter for those tactics, and sizes the problem. It offers
no mechanism for why the selves differ.

## Key insight

The tactics we use on ourselves are familiar once we see them as the tactics
we use on someone else whom we cannot always control: a child who scratches
in her sleep, a recruit, a parolee. The "manager" is not always in charge,
"especially not when management becomes a problem". So the manager, while
in command, arranges the environment, the incentives and the options that
the other self will face. Mittens at night, or a friend told to ignore your
later plea, are both this.

## Assumptions

- **No theory of the self.** The essay has "no necessary intention of
  questioning what the 'self' is or offering an answer" (p. 100). The "two
  selves" are a manner of speaking about behaviour that alternates.
- **Conscious, strategic behaviour is the focus.** Schelling's interest is
  "at the more conscious strategic end of the spectrum" (p. 101). The
  involuntary cases (sleep, panic, blushing) are used to make the voluntary
  ones look less mysterious.
- **The size estimate assumes** that about 30 million smokers want to quit,
  that they would otherwise smoke for another 15–20 years, and that future
  savings are discounted at a real rate of 4–10 percent (pp. 110–111).

## Key results

The essay argues from cases. Its results are distinctions and estimates.

- **Alternation, not coincidence** (p. 99). "The spirit is willing but the
  flesh is weak" misses that the willing spirit and the weak flesh
  "frequently alternate rather than coincide". The "flesh" seems "endowed
  with as ingenious an intelligence and command system" as the spirit.
- **Against a single discount rate** (pp. 99–100). Modelling the lapser as a
  high discounter would need a rate of "100 percent per hour or per minute".
  It is unclear whether the resolver has a far horizon or "merely has such a
  short memory that he forgets how disagreeable it's going to be".
- **Stark cases** (pp. 103–105).
  - *The pain button*: pain lasts 5 minutes regardless, and 25 more unless
    a button is hit, and the session repeats daily until a full 30 minutes
    is endured. Many could never endure it. A friend would disconnect the
    button and be thanked. The victim, at any moment, would disconnect it
    himself.
  - *The stroke device*: an implant that kills only above a set severity of
    stroke. Schelling conjectures it "would be attractive to many of us",
    and less so if the victim had to activate it.
- **Taxonomy of habits** (pp. 106–109). The dimensions are:
  - time profile (random, cyclical, continuous);
  - "time to indulgence" or onset time;
  - warning time;
  - portability, cost and visibility;
  - the damage function (cumulative or current, threshold or plateau, decay
    rate);
  - whether the habit is "autonomous or self-aggravating";
  - consciousness.
- **Size of the problem** (pp. 109–112). The would-be quitters' cigarette
  spending alone gives "a minimum valuation somewhere around $100 billion".
  With health and other costs, consumer self-management problems "can easily
  be on the order of a trillion dollars (lumpsum, discounted value)", about
  "$100 billion per year". Schelling suggests this matches all other
  consumer-ignorance and budgeting problems combined.
- **Tactics** (pp. 112–117):
  - **New Year's resolutions** raise the stakes and answer "When?".
  - **Lippmann's "plate glass window"**: bright lines that defeat "salami
    tactics", and "zero is a more enforceable limit" than a ration.
  - **Precautionary rules** against precursors and cues.
  - **No self-deceit.** Deceit is "peculiarly unavailable" against oneself.
  - **Finality relieves.** Patients told flatly to stop smoking quit "more
    reliably" and "far more comfortably".
  - **Parole-like monitoring.**
  - **Breaking large tasks into small ones**, and accepting deadlines.
  - **A three-person reward scheme**: "If A lacks the fortitude to get out
    of bed, B has the fortitude to do without baseball because C laid down
    the law at an earlier time."
  - **Mandatory delays** that are almost always withdrawn before they
    expire.
- **The suspense is the worst of it** (p. 117). Failure is often "a
  desperate dash to freedom", not from pain or hunger but "from perpetual
  unfinished decision".

## Concepts

- **egonomics**: the "art or science of 'self-management'", by analogy with
  economics as household management. Its strategic part is "consciously
  coping with one's own behavior" (p. 100).
- **sometime self**: the self that will be in command later and that the
  present self manages as another.
- **time to indulgence**: "the time from the moment of suggestion or
  temptation, or of yielding or decision, to the act" (pp. 106–107).
- **mandatory delay**: a self-imposed waiting period before an indulgence
  becomes permitted (p. 116).

## Connections

- **Ainslie ([LIT-586](../literature.d/LIT-586.md), [NOTE-443](NOTE-443.md)).** Ainslie supplies what Schelling
  declines to give: a reason the selves differ (hyperbolic discounting) and
  a mechanism by which a resolution binds (staking credibility on a
  precedent). Schelling's own argument against a single high discount rate
  (C3) is consistent with Ainslie's, since a hyperbolic curve makes
  impatience depend on nearness rather than being a constant rate. Ainslie
  cites Schelling's *Strategy of Conflict* (1960) for "limited warfare"
  among motivational states, not this essay.
- **Frijda ([LIT-540](../literature.d/LIT-540.md)) and [THEORY-056](../theory.d/THEORY-056.md).** Schelling's examples of using one
  state against another (alcohol "to be braver in an hour", declining a
  drink to keep the cigarette at bay) fit Frijda's picture of control by
  competing states. But most of Schelling's tactics act on the environment
  and the options, not on concurrent motives. They are commitment devices,
  which neither Frijda nor [THEORY-056](../theory.d/THEORY-056.md) discusses.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md).** Schelling poses the ownership question in its starkest
  practical form: when an earlier self asks you to disregard a later plea,
  "which is the authentic 'I'?" (p. 104). He gives no criterion. His
  hardest cases are those in which the earlier self's standard is itself in
  doubt (dieting for vanity, p. 98). That is the record's point that a
  higher-order or earlier attitude cannot settle ownership by its position
  alone. Here the position is in time, not in order. Schelling neither
  argues nor cites this, and the bearing is the record's.
- **Personhood.** The stroke-device and advance-instruction cases are about
  whose wishes govern across a change in the person. They connect to the
  record's personal-identity readings ([LIT-446](../literature.d/LIT-446.md), [LIT-447](../literature.d/LIT-447.md)) only by subject.
  Schelling makes no identity claim.
- **Self-determination theory ([LIT-558](../literature.d/LIT-558.md)).** The three-person reward and
  penalty scheme is self-imposed controlled regulation, and Schelling
  reports that it can work (p. 116). SDT predicts such regulation is less
  stable and less well-being-promoting than autonomous regulation. The
  essay does not compare them.
- **No ML instruction.** Nothing here belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Anecdote and introspection.** No data are presented except the smoking
  prevalence trend and market estimates. The tactics are offered as
  familiar, not tested.
- **The size estimate is avowedly rough**: "it doesn't matter whether you
  come out with one-third of a trillion or three trillion".
- **No mechanism.** The essay explains neither why the selves differ nor
  when a tactic will work.
- **A magazine essay.** It is not the AER lecture the brief named, and it
  does not reach the questions of policy and rational-choice theory that
  the lecture's title announces.

## Open questions

- Does the 1984 lecture ([LIT-573](../literature.d/LIT-573.md)) add a theoretical claim about
  rational choice that this essay lacks? Only a reading can say.
- Which of the habit dimensions (onset time, warning time, self-aggravation)
  predict which tactic succeeds? The essay proposes the dimensions and
  tests none of them.
