# LIT-989: An ability to respond begins with inner alignment: How phase synchronisation effects transitions to higher levels of agency

Tazzio Tissot, Mike Levin, Chris Buckley, Richard Watson (2024), *bioRxiv*.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmp9ypte](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp9ypte.md).*

- **The set-up.** 30 modules of 10 bistable units; couplings 1 inside a
  module and 0.01 across; 2^30 local optima and two global ones. Each
  unit's decision cycle γ_i(t) = (sin(ωt + θ_i) + 1)/2 alternates between
  sensitive and inactive, and acting and insensitive.
- **The timing rule.** θ_i ← θ_i + ν Σ_j (π/4) c_ij sgn(s_i s_j)
  sin(θ_j − θ_i): a unit moves its phase toward partners it currently
  agrees with. It is a gradient on cycle-averaged payoff, "loosely
  analogous" to oscillator entrainment.
- **The transition.** Without the rule 0 of 50 runs reach a global
  optimum. With it, phases synchronise within modules, the modules "wake
  up" suddenly (mostly before t = 20,000), and 50 of 50 runs with the
  learned phases reach one. Blocks switch "like a single macro-scale
  unit".
- **Specific, not global.** Synchrony imposed across all units gives no
  transition. Membership is "who is synchronised with whom".
- **Why it works.** When a module's members are all sensitive at once,
  none acts on the others, so only the weak outside pull remains:
  attention turns "from the ingroup to the outgroup". No signal passes
  within the module.
- **Beyond one game.** The learned timing solves other games over the
  same modules, arises by selection on timing (about 10⁶ against 10⁴
  steps), and iterates to meta-modules, though not scale-invariantly.
