# NOTE-151: Register — The Depth of the Body

## Contribution

A body extends through space, so it has breadth. It also has depth: roughly, the level of abstraction of its parts. For moral purposes the body is characteristically fairly high-level, so individual molecules are not really within the "moral body", the body that figures in bodily rights and interests. Register develops this notion and uses it to resolve puzzles about brain–computer interfaces, some of whose computation runs on external or cloud hardware owned by third parties.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1–2 (BCIs and bodily rights): BCIs can be body parts (Harbisson's antenna, motor and communication BCIs). Offloading computation to external or cloud servers raises a "problem of ownership", where the user's bodily ownership conflicts with a third party's property, and a "problem of transience", where hot-swapped or replaced hardware seems too temporary to be a body part.
- §3 Defining Depth: depth is defined via micro- versus macrophysical commitment. Determinacy and fundamentality are noted as nearby variants that can diverge. It is extended to objects via real definitions: a water molecule is "deeper" than a heart, and real definitions fix persistence conditions. "High-level neutral" events change no high-level properties, and the threshold is context-sensitive.
- §5 Two moral principles: "Bodily interests are high-level" and "Bodily rights are high-level". The examples are that metal detectors and phone radiation are neutral unless they raise cancer risk. The objection from a non-consensual MRI is answered by locating the wrong elsewhere (autonomy, extracted information). The paper leaves open whether brain tissue has a lower threshold.
- §6 Depth and BCIs: the implemented software is a higher-level object than the server. The software is a body part and the server is not. That dissolves the ownership conflict while still forbidding interventions that change the software's high-level properties. It also generates new duties such as backup servers, and it raises an open question about whether moving a BCI process between servers is high-level neutral.
- §7 Conclusion: the distinction applies to organic parts too, is backed by a metaphysics of real definition, and fits existing (subjectivist, interest-based, functionalist, hylomorphist) theories of the body.

## Open questions

- The software-vs-hardware "depth" distinction transfers directly to questions about where an AI system, or a user's extended cognitive tool, is located. It pairs with [LIT-097](../literature.d/LIT-097.md) (extended mind) and [LIT-147](../literature.d/LIT-147.md) (BCIs and mental causation), and with Birch's [LIT-111](../literature.d/LIT-111.md) point that conversations are spread across data centres.
- Check §4, which argues that existing theories of the body already support depth. It was not read in detail, and the ontological backing (real definitions à la Fine and Rosen) carries weight.
- The context-sensitive threshold for "high-level" may make the principles hard to apply. A deeper reading should test them on the brain-tissue case the paper itself flags.
