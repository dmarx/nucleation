# NOTE-tmp4a8iu: Leffler (2024) — Constitutivism's plight: inescapability, normativity, and relativism

## Contribution

A sorting of the inescapability arguments constitutivists use, by whether
each can explain why norms of agency are forceful, against four stated
necessary features of normative force. Its new results are a charitable
reconstruction of Korsgaard's "plight" that passes all four, and the
argument that this reconstruction entails relativism, leaving
constitutivists a choice point.

## Key results

1. **The explanandum is force, not content (p. 2).** "a norm is not
   forceful just because it is a feature of our constitution. Rather, we
   need to show why we are subject to some possible norms rather than
   others." Footnote 2: "I leave the question of which norms apply to the
   side."
2. **Two shmagency questions (§I, pp. 4–5).** The familiar extensional
   one (why be an agent rather than a shmagent) is set apart from the
   explanatory "Arché question", how agency grounds norms at all for those
   who have it. Dialectical and normative inescapability are set aside:
   the first "may not appear to help ground anything positive", the second
   "just pushes the explanatory question a step backwards" (p. 5).
3. **Four necessary features (§II, pp. 6–7).** INVARIANCE: "To have
   normative force, a norm cannot shift merely because agents' desires
   shift." OIC, ought-implies-can. OICF: an agent "has to be able to fail
   to live up to a norm for it to be a norm". CRITICIZABILITY: "An agent
   who fails to live up to a norm is criticizable in virtue of failing to
   live up to the norm." They are necessary, "not necessarily sufficient"
   (p. 7). Reduction worries (open question, is/ought) are "orthogonal to
   the Arché question" (p. 8).
4. **Psychological inescapability (§III, Velleman, Katsafanas).** Fails:
   "while it can capture OIC and OICF, it cannot capture INVARIANCE and
   CRITICIZABILITY" (p. 10). Being a shmagent "sometimes seems valuable"
   (the evil demon, p. 9), and nothing says why one constitutive motive
   rather than another.
5. **Further-factor inescapability (§IV, Walden's social schemata and
   embodiment).** Fails INVARIANCE (enough changed desires change
   schemata), raises OIC worries (the homophobic society), and fails
   CRITICIZABILITY: "Sometimes the schemata are wrong, not the individuals
   facing them" (p. 12).
6. **Standpoint inescapability (§V, Ferrero).** Captures INVARIANCE and
   OIC but fails OICF and CRITICIZABILITY: an agent who fails every
   constitutive norm at once (the failed lie) would be outside agency, so
   neither failing nor criticizable; threshold constitutivism is not
   available to it (pp. 13–14).
7. **Plight inescapability reconstructed (§VI, p. 15).** "I propose that
   we view plight inescapability as an agent's being positioned to
   continuously exercise her agency fully, absent external limits at the
   point in time of the exercise." "Fully" is relative to "the thing to
   do", which is "supported and determined by (all of) the norms that have
   a bearing on that exercise of agency", and context-relative (p. 16).
   Its modality is practical necessity "in the different sense of being a
   precondition for any exercise of my agency" (p. 17).
8. **It captures all four (pp. 17–19).** Invariance, because the
   positioning "is independent of what is desired"; OIC through the
   external-limits clause; OICF because only being positioned is
   inescapable; criticizability because there is a success condition,
   though it "need not be terribly strong" (p. 18). The answer to the
   worry it raised against psychological inescapability: "the standard of
   success in plight inescapability is set by the features and commitments
   of whatever agency one has", so "exercising their shmagency is the
   plight of shmagents" (p. 19).
9. **Relativism (§VII, pp. 19–21).** Categoricity read as applicability,
   invariance or convergence does not escape relativism once constitutions
   vary between and within kinds of agent: "Some corporate agents are
   democratic, others are dictatorial" (p. 19). "plight inescapability can
   only explain how the norms internal to some agent's agency are forceful
   for that agent. Hence, agents with different constitutions have
   different norms applying to them as forceful" (p. 20). Idealized agents
   do not help actual ones (p. 20).
10. **What is missing (p. 21).** "a norm must be binding for agents
    independently of how they are constituted. Call this categoricity as
    constitution independence. But plight inescapability cannot get us
    that."
11. **The choice point (abstract; §VIII).** "accept the plight
    inescapability explanation of normative force and relativism—or give
    up the plight inescapability explanation." The paper is explicitly
    "exploratory": "I do not ultimately defend them as true" (p. 3).

## Assumptions

- The four features are necessary conditions of normative force; the
  argument does not need them to be sufficient.
- Constitutions vary between and within kinds of agent (humans, animals,
  robots, corporations). The relativism conclusion depends on this
  premise, which the paper argues briefly rather than establishes.
- "Agency" is used broadly for whatever features of subjecthood a
  constitutivist builds on: aims, principles, capacities, attitudes.
- The question of which norms apply is set aside; only force is at issue.

## Concepts

Constitutivism; normative force; the Arché question; extensional versus
explanatory shmagency; commitments of agency; dialectical, normative,
psychological, further-factor, standpoint and plight inescapability;
INVARIANCE, OIC, OICF, CRITICIZABILITY; "the thing to do"; external
limits; the problem of bad action; threshold constitutivism; categoricity
as applicability, invariance, convergence and constitution independence.

## Connections

The paper builds on Enoch's shmagency objection ([LIT-117](../literature.d/LIT-117.md), Unavailable,
unread here) and Ferrero's replies, and reinterprets Korsgaard's
*Self-Constitution* (2009). The record's reading of Korsgaard,
[THEORY-075](../theory.d/THEORY-075.md), is of the *Sources of Normativity* argument, whose step to
humanity is what Leffler's §VII says plight inescapability cannot supply
(constitution independence). Leffler's earlier "Agent-Switching, Plight
Inescapability, and Corporate Agency" (Analytic Philosophy, 2025) applies
plight inescapability to corporate agents and is not registered here; this
paper mentions corporate agents only in passing.

## Limitations

- **Exploratory by the author's own statement**: the inescapability
  proposals are "charitably construed" and not defended as true.
- **Variation of constitutions is assumed**, argued in a paragraph; a
  constitutivist who finds a constitution all agents share (Lavin's
  convergence, footnote 14) is answered only by doubt.
- **The four features are necessary, not sufficient**; passing them makes
  plight inescapability "a promising contender", not an explanation.
- **Corporate agents appear only as an example of variation**; nothing
  in the paper is about organizations that are not agents, which is most
  of what the record's organizational account covers.
- **It leaves open whether force can be explained some other way**:
  "Whether another explanation could work is a question for elsewhere"
  (p. 21).
