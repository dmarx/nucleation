# NOTE-tmpdupic: The Ripple Effect: Emotional Contagion and its Influence on Group Behavior

## Contribution

Earlier field studies (Totterdell; Bartel & Saavedra 2000) had shown that
the moods of team members converge, but only concurrently, so causation was
open. This paper manipulates the source of emotion with a trained
confederate and shows that mood spreads to group members. It also shows,
for the first time by the author's account, what contagion does to group
processes: cooperation, conflict and perceived performance. A second study
without a confederate replicates the convergence and the process
correlates in naturally formed groups.

## Key insight

People are "walking mood inductors". A group member's mood changes the
others' mood, and through mood their judgements and conduct, while they
take their resulting feelings and judgements to be their own. The effect
on the group is not merely shared feeling. It is more cooperation and less
conflict, which members attribute to the facts of the task.

## Assumptions

- **Emotion as a broad label.** "Emotion" is used interchangeably with
  affect, following Hatfield et al. and Ashforth & Humphrey (1995). The
  object of study is *mood*, chosen as the most malleable and
  broad-ranging affect; dispositional affect is treated as not contagious.
- **The circumplex model** (Russell 1980) structures the design: valence
  (pleasantness) crossed with energy (activation).
- **Mechanism.** Contagion runs both through automatic mimicry and afferent
  feedback (Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson 1994) and through conscious social
  comparison. The study does not separate them.
- **Change scores as contagion.** Self-reported contagion is Time 2
  pleasant mood (recalled for the second half of the session) minus Time 1
  mood. Time 2 is retrospective, reported after the exercise.
- **Groups.** Ad hoc groups of students in a 30-minute mixed-motive task:
  each member argues for a bonus for "their" employee, while serving the
  company as a whole. If no agreement is reached, nobody gets a bonus.

## Key results

- **Manipulation check (Study 1, Table 1).** Pleasant confederate rated
  6.59 against 3.89 (χ²(1) = 42.67); high energy 7.68 against 3.27
  (χ²(1) = 152.52). There was an unexpected valence × energy interaction on
  perceived energy (χ²(1) = 17.31), controlled for in all tests.
- **H1, contagion (supported).** Individual level: mood change +.41 with
  the pleasant confederate, −.26 with the unpleasant one (χ²(1) = 9.97, p
  < .005). Video-coders, who saw only the members, rated Time 2 mood 2.75
  against 2.33 (χ²(1) = 10.30). Group level (Table 3b): aggregated self-
  and coder ratings, coders' overall group pleasantness (F = 20.68) and
  members' ratings of group pleasantness (F = 10.10) all follow confederate
  valence.
- **H2, unpleasant spreads more (not supported).** The absolute changes did
  not differ (χ² < 1).
- **H3, energy amplifies (suggestive only).** Unpleasant: high energy −.44,
  low energy −.07 (χ²(1) = 2.81, p < .10). Pleasant: high energy +.16, low
  energy +.66 (p < .10), the opposite of the prediction. There was also an
  unexpected main effect of energy on mood change, with low energy more
  positive.
- **H4, cooperativeness (supported).** Individual level (Table 4): mood
  change predicts self-rated cooperativeness (b = .89, p < .001) and
  peer-rated cooperativeness (b = .47, p < .05). Group level (Table 5,
  N = 26): coder-rated contagion correlates with coder-rated cooperativeness
  (r = .44, p < .05) and with a lower SD of funds distributed (r = −.37,
  p < .05). Self-reported group contagion gives .34 (p < .10) and −.19 (n.s.).
- **H5, conflict (supported).** Group contagion correlates with
  coder-rated conflict: self-report −.48, coder −.42 (both p < .05).
- **H6, performance (partly supported).** Self-rated performance b = .44
  (p < .001); peer-rated b = 3.96 (p < .10).
- **Study 2 (113 MBA students, 26 groups).** Mood ICC .00 at Time 1, .24 at
  Time 2. Contagion predicts self-rated cooperation (b = .48, p < .01) and
  self-rated performance (b = .19, p < .05), but not peer-rated
  performance. Group contagion correlates with members' ratings of group
  cooperation (r = .49, p < .01) and conflict (r = −.49, p < .01), but not
  with the SD of funds. Peer cooperativeness was dropped because its ICC was
  .14.
- **Exploratory findings.** High self-monitors showed more contagion.
  Friendship within MBA groups correlated with contagion (r = .44, p < .05).
  In Study 2, contagion was related to winning a larger share of funds
  (printed "β=23", p < .05, presumably .23). No demographic variable mattered.
- **Unawareness.** In both studies members attributed their effectiveness
  to money won and to their own abilities, not to their mood.

## Concepts

- **emotional contagion**: after Schoenewolf (1990), "a process in which a
  person or group influences the emotions, or behavior of another person or
  group through the conscious or unconscious induction of emotion states and
  behavioral attitudes".
- **primitive contagion**: automatic mimicry of facial, vocal and postural
  expression, followed by afferent feedback that produces the feeling
  (Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson).
- **emotional energy**: the intensity with which an emotion is expressed:
  pitch, loudness, tempo, gesture.
- **group contagion (Study 2)**: convergence of members' moods, measured as
  the rise in intraclass correlation from before to after the interaction.

## Bearing on the record

- It is the evidence a THEORY on group emotion would need for its
  mechanism: that convergence of affect in a group is produced in part by
  contagion and has consequences for the group's conduct. A candidate is
  given in the report, not filed.

## Limitations

- **Pre-acceptance manuscript.** Numbers may differ from the published
  article.
- **Small group samples.** 29 and 26 groups. Table 3b gives 7, 7, 8 and 7
  groups per cell, while Table 5 reports N = 26. The manuscript does not
  explain the difference.
- **The process effects are correlational.** H4–H6 regress outcomes on
  measured contagion, not on the manipulated condition, so a third factor
  (for example, a member's own reaction to the task) could drive both mood
  and cooperation. Study 2 has no manipulation at all.
- **Retrospective Time 2 mood**, reported after the exercise and recalled
  for its second half.
- **One male drama-student confederate** played all four conditions, in a
  competitive student task; the author herself suspects context reversed
  the energy effect for pleasant mood.
- **Mechanism untested.** Mimicry-feedback and social comparison are both
  invoked; nothing separates them.

## Open questions

- What happens when members convey different or contradictory emotions?
  The author calls this "fascinating" and untested.
- Do ongoing teams habituate to one another's moods or become more
  sensitive? The friendship correlation in Study 2 hints at sensitivity.
- Does contagion between people who identify as a group differ from
  contagion between strangers, as von Scheve & Ismer's I-mode/We-mode
  distinction would predict?
