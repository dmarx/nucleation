# NOTE-tmpr6jda: Huebner — Genuinely collective emotions

## Contribution

It is the first sustained argument that groups can be in emotional states
in the strong, "collectivist" sense: states of the group that are not its
members' emotions in aggregation. Earlier defences (Gilbert, May,
Tollefsen) rested on how we talk and on practices of collective apology.
This one starts from a computational account of individual emotion and asks
whether a distributed system could implement it. It also names exactly where
such a claim fails: in a well-trained crew, danger is detected without fear.

## Key insight

An emotion is not a feeling but an integration. Fear is what happens when
danger detection, the redeployment of attention, preparatory bodily changes
and higher-level appraisal are coordinated into one representation that
says both "this is dangerous" and "do something now". Nothing in that
description mentions neurons. If a group's routines are integrated so that
the group's state does that work, and no member's state does, the group is
afraid, whether or not it feels anything. The same description also says
what to look for when a group seems emotional: agitation, a whole-system
reorientation that the members' own varied emotions do not explain.

## Assumptions

- **Computational theory of mind and homuncular decomposition (§2, p. 5).**
  Psychological explanation specifies a task, decomposes the system that
  solves it into simpler computational systems, and repeats down to a
  mechanical level (Dennett 1978). Implementation is plastic: any medium
  that maintains the relevant counterfactual invariances will do.
- **Emotions are representational states (p. 6).** They have at least the
  function of carrying information about the world (Dretske, Millikan,
  Prinz). Fear carries information about danger.
- **Formal and particular objects (p. 7).** Emotions represent a formal
  object (danger) and a particular one (this raccoon), following Kenny and
  Prinz. Lazarus's core-relational themes are taken as the formal objects
  (n. 6).
- **Indicative content and imperative force (p. 8).** Emotions are
  "action-oriented representations that reflexively reorient behavior"
  (Griffiths & Scarantino 2005).
- **Collective representation is possible (n. 4).** Defended elsewhere;
  here only sketched (Haugeland's and Clark's criteria; collective
  misrepresentation is possible). Huebner says sceptics may read the paper as
  establishing only that *if* there are collective representations, there
  can be collective emotional representations.
- **Collective includes technology (n. 1).** "Collective mentality" covers
  groups together with the technological structures they are embedded in,
  following distributed cognition.

## Key results

All are arguments; there are no data of the author's own.

- **Holism is not collectivism (pp. 3–4).** Many defences of collective
  emotion, such as Jaspers's and May's "metaphysical guilt" and Tollefsen's
  collective guilt as "the guilt one feels in response to the harms
  committed by one's group", establish only that individuals' emotions are
  socially relational. They do not show a group-level state.
- **Gilbert's plural-subject account is reducible (p. 4).** Gilbert
  distinguishes personal, membership and collective guilt feelings, and
  defines the last by joint commitment to feel guilt "as a body". Huebner:
  it is unclear how this is a collective emotion "as opposed to a
  distribution of individual states that are directly sustained by the
  causal and conceptual connections between the individuals". Following
  Rupert (2005), positing such states adds no explanatory power; a plural
  subject's emotion reduces to individual commitments and aggregation
  rules, and an individual's emotion admits no such reduction.
- **The architecture of fear (p. 10).** At least (1) a capacity to perceive
  things as dangerous (sensory input, salience detectors, conceptual
  interpretation); (2) attentional mechanisms aimed at the threat and at
  planning; (3) systems that produce and monitor system-wide preparatory
  changes; (4) coordination of these outputs into a single state of fear,
  monitored to guide system-level coping. Huebner stresses (n. 12) that this
  is a requirement for representing as emotions do, not a definition of
  emotion.
- **The USS Palau as candidate (§4, pp. 11–13).** After a loss of steam
  pressure (Hutchins 1995), the engineer's report is a low-level danger
  representation, centring the rudders plays the role of an action
  tendency, probability, capacity and urgency of the threat are evaluated,
  and attention shifts to the chart. The resulting representation is a
  state "of the navigation crew as it is situated in the ship", not of any
  member or device. No crew member showed fear.
- **Non-conscious emotion (§5, pp. 13–18).** Huebner concedes the crew
  cannot be conscious: it is "too spatially distributed, too insufficiently
  integrated", with low-bandwidth channels (p. 14). Psychoanalytic cases of
  unconscious fear (the Natalja example) do not help, *pace* Gilbert,
  because they may be phenomenally conscious but inaccessible (Prinz). He
  separates (1) a felt but unreportable emotion from (2) a non-conscious
  emotion in a person who feels only its scattered bodily effects (p. 15).
  Following Sizer on moods, he argues for (2). The feeling of fear needs the
  bodily changes to be integrated into a state introspection can access, and
  the subpersonal routines often run without recruiting introspection
  (Damasio, LeDoux, Tsuchiya & Adolphs). If phenomenal consciousness could
  instead be wholly inaccessible (Block), he says, "all empirical bets are
  off", and collective phenomenal consciousness could not be ruled out
  either (p. 17).
- **Against the homology requirement (§6, pp. 18–22).** Objection, after
  Griffiths: conscious and non-conscious human fear share homologous
  mechanisms, groups do not, so a group's danger representation is not an
  emotion. Reply: requiring homology (i) makes artificial emotion
  uninteresting by fiat, (ii) excludes aliens and convergent evolution, and
  (iii) forces Griffiths's own eliminativism about emotion as one kind even
  within humans. Psychological kinds are fixed at the computational level.
  Emotions are "like vodka" (any sugar source that yields the right product),
  not "like bourbon" (51–79% corn). Against Rey's demand for
  neurotransmitters: "neurotransmitter" is itself a functional role, which
  memos, emails and vocalizations could fill in a group (p. 22).
- **The crew is not afraid after all (§7, pp. 23–25).** Hedahl's objection:
  training that removes individual fear also removes it from the
  distributed system. The Palau case is "collective detection of danger in
  the absence of a fear response"; what is missing is agitation.
- **The McCain–Palin campaign (pp. 23–25).** In autumn 2008 the campaign
  redeployed attention to its opponent, diverted resources, re-evaluated
  threats, and its members were agitated. Yet members' emotions varied
  (hopeful, angry, saddened), so the campaign's fear is not an aggregate of
  members' fear. There is "a real pattern" missed by attending to
  individuals. This is offered as an "intuitively plausible case" until
  "the relevant anthropological data is collected".

## Concepts

- **Holism / collectivism** (after Pettit 1996) — holism: an individual's
  mental states depend on social relations; collectivism: groups themselves
  are in mental states (p. 3).
- **Genuinely collective emotion** — an emotional state of a collectivity
  that cannot be exhaustively characterised as its members' intentional
  states in aggregation (p. 4).
- **Collective mentality** — states of groups, or of groups plus their
  technological environments (n. 1).
- **Emotional representation** — a representation with a formal object
  (e.g. danger), a particular object, indicative content and imperative
  force, produced by integrating subpersonal routines (pp. 7–10).
- **Collective detection of danger** — the Palau state: integrated danger
  representation without the agitation of fear (p. 23).

## Connections

- **Gilbert ([LIT-tmpbdcqn](../literature.d/LIT-tmpbdcqn.md)).** The `rivals` relation is declared on the LIT.
  Huebner and Gilbert agree that collective emotions need not be felt; they
  disagree about what bears them. Gilbert's bearer is a plural subject
  constituted by joint commitment; Huebner's is an integrated computational
  system that may include technology. Huebner's claim that her account
  reduces to individual commitments is the core of the disagreement.
  Gilbert's earlier *On Social Facts* ([LIT-492](../literature.d/LIT-492.md)) and "Walking Together"
  ([LIT-477](../literature.d/LIT-477.md)) are held unread.
- **Schmid ([LIT-tmp1efif](../literature.d/LIT-tmp1efif.md)), unread.** Schmid, by his chapter's abstract,
  gives groups consciousness as plural pre-reflective self-awareness and
  allows them "slow" emotions only. Huebner denies group consciousness
  (p. 14) but finds his own fast case fails and keeps a slow one. On
  consciousness, Gilbert and Huebner stand together against Schmid. On

- **List & Pettit ([LIT-tmphq2d4](../literature.d/LIT-tmphq2d4.md)) and List ([LIT-tmpaxx6q](../literature.d/LIT-tmpaxx6q.md)).** Huebner's test

  need judge the conclusion. In List's taxonomy, Huebner's "holistic"
  emotions are aggregate or common attitudes and his "genuinely collective"
  ones would be corporate attitudes. Neither List paper treats emotion.

  appraisal-like formal objects, are what Barrett's constructionism

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)** (composed and decomposed minds) has a direct new input.
  Huebner's criterion is an architectural one, and his concession that the
  Palau crew is not conscious "because too spatially distributed, too
  insufficiently integrated" is an informal version of List's low-Φ
  reasons in [LIT-401](../literature.d/LIT-401.md). The two agree from opposite starting points.
- **Candidate THEORY (proposed, not filed):** collective emotions, where they
  exist, are corporate attitudes in List's sense, not aggregate or common
  ones. What the social-science literature measures as collective emotion,
  convergence of members' affect (von Scheve & Ismer, [LIT-tmp0fwb0](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0fwb0.md)), is an
  aggregate or common attitude, and so a different thing. Sources:
  [LIT-tmpaxx6q](../literature.d/LIT-tmpaxx6q.md), this paper, [LIT-tmp0fwb0](../literature.d/LIT-tmp0fwb0.md). It would be promoted when a read
  text applies the aggregate/common/corporate split to emotion and finds a
  corporate case with data.

## Limitations

- No instance is established. The only worked case is withdrawn, and its
  replacement is described from news-level knowledge of a campaign, by the
  author's own admission.
- Collective representation is assumed from other work (n. 4). The paper
  is, on its own statement, conditional on it.
- The non-conscious-emotion argument rests on describing a case (a person
  afraid without feeling fear), not on evidence that such states exist with
  no felt emotion at all. The cited neuroscience shows unconscious
  processing of fear stimuli, which is weaker.
- Gilbert's reply to the reduction charge is not considered: a joint
  commitment is meant to be a single commitment of the group, not a sum of
  individual ones. Whether her account reduces is what is in dispute.
- "Agitation", the property the Palau case lacks and the campaign has, is
  never defined in computational terms, though the whole argument is that
  emotions should be.

## Open questions

- What, computationally, is agitation, and how would one detect it in a
  group without relying on members' felt emotions?
- Can a group be in a fast emotion at all, or only slow ones, as Schmid
  holds? The Palau failure suggests the answer depends on training, not on
  speed.
- Does the distributed premise-based procedure of List and Pettit give a
  formal test for Huebner's "not individual states in aggregation"? A group
  emotion would then be one whose occurrence is not a function of members'
  emotions about the same object.
