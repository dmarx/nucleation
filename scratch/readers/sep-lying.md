### Summary
A definitional survey, explicitly confined to "lying to others and other-deception (interpersonal deceiving)"; normative questions excluded. About four fifths is on lying: the traditional definition L1 (statement, untruthfulness, addressee, intention-to-deceive conditions), objections to each, and the split between Deceptionists (simple, complex, moral; L1-L11) and Non-Deceptionists (Carson, Saul, Sorensen, Fallis, Stokke; L12-L17) over whether lying needs an intention to deceive (bald-faced lies). §3 gives the traditional definition of deception, D1, and §3.1 seven successive modifications D2-D7, ending with Chisholm and Feehan's D7, which drops intention.

### Contribution
A taxonomy (Deceptionism vs Non-Deceptionism, Mahon 2014) and a numbered ladder of definitions; Mahon's own D4 (evidence condition) is among them. Little argued beyond that: the author's leanings appear as "not necessarily compelling" remarks (on D2, on negative deception) and as arguments that non-deceptionist definitions fail by their own lights for the student, witness and negotiator cases.

### Key results
1. §3: dictionary definition too broad because "mere appearances can deceive, such as when a white object looks red in a certain light", and because people can "inadvertently" or "mistakenly" deceive.
2. §3: majority view is that deception is intentional: "They hold that deception, like lying, is intentional. They reserve term 'mislead' to cover cases of causing false beliefs either intentionally or unintentionally (Carson 2010, 47)." Minority allowing inadvertent deception: Demos 1960, Fuller 1976, Chisholm and Feehan 1977, Adler 1997, Gert 2005.
3. §3: D1 "To deceive =df to intentionally cause to have a false belief that is known or believed to be false." Deceive is "an achievement or success verb (Ryle 1949, 130)"; D1 "applies to anything that is capable of having beliefs, such as (possibly) chimpanzees, dogs, and infants" (as deceived).
4. §3: no statement, untruthfulness or addressee condition for deception: deceiving by natural signs, icons, symbols, "by omitting to make certain statements, or by remaining silent", paltering, double bluff.
5. §3.1 D4 (Mahon 2007): deception requires "bringing about evidence on the basis of which the person has or continues to have the false belief"; drugging or brain surgery does not deceive.
6. §3.1 D5-D6: negative deception and deception "by omission" (Chisholm and Feehan); Mahon objects negative deception is keeping "in the dark", "The state of being ignorant is not the same as the state of being mistaken."
7. §3.1 D7: "According to Chisholm and Feehan, however, deception can be unintentional": cause or allow another to acquire/keep false belief, lose/fail to gain true belief, no intention.
8. §1.5.1: the one functional definition, of LYING not deception: "any form of behavior the function of which is to provide others with false information or to deprive them of true information" (Smith 2004, 14). Glossed: "Those who make this objection would make lying the same as intentionally deceiving (Ekman 1985, 26)."
9. §1.4: lying is not a success verb: "a lie remains a lie if it is disbelieved."
10. §2.2: in complex deceptionism "strictly speaking, deception is foreseen and not intended" (Fallis 2009, 45).
11. §1.3: one cannot lie to "goldfish, dogs, robots"; one can lie "via intermediaries which are not persons" (an ATM).

### Assumptions
Deception is analysed as a relation between persons (or believers) with beliefs; the deceiver is always an agent (x, "a person"). Success = a false belief (or, D5+, a lost/blocked true belief). Method of cases and intuitions; no empirical material. Non-human, systemic or evolved deceivers are never considered as deceivers.

### Concepts
lying; deception (positive/negative; by commission/omission); misleading (Carson's unintentional term); palter / false implicature; double and triple bluff; bogus disclosure; bald-faced lie, knowledge-lie; warranting context (Carson); assertion as proposal to common ground (Stokke); invocation and breach of trust (Chisholm-Feehan, Fried, Simpson, Faulkner); being deceptive = concealing (Lackey); evidence condition (Mahon D4); achievement verb.

### Q1: does deception require an intending deceiver?
On the entry's majority, yes; unintended false-belief causing is "misleading". Minority (Chisholm and Feehan D7 and others) no. Functional/biological deception: absent (only Smith's functional definition of lying, §1.5.1). Deception by a system: absent as deceiver; non-persons appear only as targets (robots, ATMs) or intermediaries; Morris 1976 "Can computers ever lie?" is in the bibliography and cited only for jokes not being lies (§1.4). Mere appearances (illusion) count as deceiving only on the over-broad dictionary definition (§3). Omission: D6, and lies of omission are not lies (§1.1).

### Q2 (self-deception, for the other reader)
The entry sets it aside: scope is "other-deception"; D1 is "the traditional definition of deception, at least in the case of other-deception (Baron 1988, 444 n. 2)". Only touches: §1.1 inner statements "can be 'internal lies' (Kant 1996, 553-554)"; §1.3 on L1 one cannot lie "to no one whatsoever (i.e., not even myself)"; bibliography: Baron 1988 "What Is Wrong with Self-Deception?", Barnes 1997 "Seeing through self-deception". Nothing on Mele, motivated bias, desire or comfort of belief. Related entry: SEP self-deception (LIT-tmp19b00).
