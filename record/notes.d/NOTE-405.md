# NOTE-405: Rai & Fiske 2011, relationship regulation

## Contribution

- A claim that moral judgment cannot be separated from social-relational
  context: "the moral status of actions cannot be determined independent
  of the social-relational contexts in which they take place" (p. 57).
- Four moral motives derived from relational models theory, each with its
  obligations, violations and constituting cues (pp. 61–64).
- Readings of violence, fairness and purity under the four motives, set
  against Turiel, Hauser, Mikhail and MFT (pp. 65–67).
- Proposals for research on freedom, religion, moral emotions, development
  and neuroscience (pp. 67–68).

## Key insight

The question is not "did the act cause harm, was it unfair, was it
impure?" but "what relationship is this, and did the act honour it?" Each
of four relationship types obliges something different. The same killing
is an atrocity under one model and a duty under another, so moral
disagreement is often real disagreement about which relationship applies,
not error.

## Assumptions

- **Function.** "Our sense of morality functions to facilitate the
  generation and maintenance of long-term social-cooperative relationships
  with others" (p. 59, citing Fiske, Frank and Joyce).
- **Relational models theory.** Four schemas coordinate nearly all social
  interaction; they map onto nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio scales
  (p. 61). The theory's validation is cited, not re-shown (p. 60–61).
- **Scientific, not folk, "moral."** The theory is meant to capture much of
  what is called moral, not to match any culture's folk concept (p. 60).
- **Motives can be tacit.** People need not be able to state the motive or
  endorse the judgment on reflection (p. 59).

## Key results

The four motives (pp. 61–64):

| motive | relational model | requires | characteristic violation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unity | communal sharing | care for and protect the in-group; give by need; shared responsibility; avoid contamination | defilement of the group, failure to aid a member |
| Hierarchy | authority ranking | subordinates defer and obey; superiors lead, guide and protect | disobedience, disrespect, superiors' neglect |
| Equality | equality matching | even balance, in-kind reciprocity, turn-taking, equal shares, eye-for-an-eye | failing to reciprocate, unequal treatment |
| Proportionality | market pricing | rewards and punishments proportional to merit, contribution or guilt; cost–benefit trade-offs | cheating, taking benefits not deserved |

- **Null morality (p. 64).** Where no relationship is constituted, people
  are morally indifferent (Hopi children hurting birds; Ilongot
  headhunting).
- **Violence (p. 65).** Harm can be praiseworthy: against out-group threats
  (Unity), from superiors to subordinates or under orders (Hierarchy), in
  retaliation (Equality), or as a necessary cost (Proportionality).
- **Fairness (pp. 65–66).** Equal treatment is fair only under Equality; it
  is wrong under Hierarchy or Proportionality, and stealing may be
  "nonsensical" under Unity or Hierarchy.
- **Purity (pp. 66–67).** Concerns with purity arise under communal
  sharing; "the foundations of in-group/loyalty and purity/sanctity are
  actually variants of the same social-relational regulation motive" (p.
  66). Incest is a violation of a culturally specific configuration of
  kinship; flag desecration offends those who identify with the nation;
  food taboos constitute clan, caste and religious identity.

## Concepts

- **relationship regulation (RR)** — the theory that the core of moral
  psychology is motives to evaluate and guide behaviour "with reference to
  prescriptive models for social relationships" (p. 59).
- **moral motive** — evaluation plus the motivation to act on it, as
  opposed to a judgment alone (p. 59).
- **constituting** — establishing a relationship model through cues:
  bodily similarity and touch for communal sharing, size, height and
  precedence for authority, concrete matching for equality, symbols such as
  money for market pricing (Fiske 2004, as summarized).
- **null morality** — moral indifference where no relational model is
  active (p. 64).
- **virtues** — "quasi-moral" traits that make someone a good relationship
  partner, a penumbra around the motives (p. 59).

## Connections

- **Morality-as-cooperation ([LIT-047](../literature.d/LIT-047.md), [NOTE-034](NOTE-034.md)).** Shared premise: morality
  sustains cooperative relationships. [LIT-047](../literature.d/LIT-047.md) cites this paper (p. 59) for
  it. The mapping is rough:
  - Unity covers MAC's family and group, and also purity.
  - Hierarchy covers MAC's hawkish and dovish displays (bravery and
    respect), and adds superiors' duty to protect.
  - Equality covers reciprocity and equal division.
  - Proportionality covers merit-based division and utilitarian
    trade-offs.
  - Property has no motive of its own.
  The derivations differ: MAC starts from types of game; RR from the scale
  types of social relations. RR's key move, that context fixes the
  morality of an act, matches [LIT-047](../literature.d/LIT-047.md)'s footnote 3, where a cooperative act
  is bad only when it undermines a larger form of cooperation.
- **Bloom's objections in [NOTE-034](NOTE-034.md).**
  - *Sex.* RR's answer differs from the Reply's. Sex wrongs defile a
    communal relationship (incest, adultery, caste rules), which is
    relational and, in RR's sense, cooperative.
  - *Hitting.* RR denies the premise. Battery is not always moralized as
    wrong: it is often required (honour, revenge, discipline).
- **Moral disciplining ([LIT-101](../literature.d/LIT-101.md), [NOTE-132](NOTE-132.md)).** Both say purity is
  cooperative. [LIT-101](../literature.d/LIT-101.md) says puritanism protects self-control that
  cooperation needs and excludes contamination; RR says contamination is
  exactly the Unity concern. They are rival cooperative accounts.
- **MFT.** RR "adds to MFT ... by grounding the foundations in a theory of
  social relationships" (p. 66). Graham et al. 2013 ([LIT-499](../literature.d/LIT-499.md)) accept
  it as compatible; the MFQ-2 ([LIT-500](../literature.d/LIT-500.md)) takes its Equality and
  Proportionality.
- **Intuitionism.** Haidt 2001 ([LIT-511](../literature.d/LIT-511.md)) used three of the same four
  models for the primate origins of intuitions. RR cites moral dumbfounding
  as people lacking words for the motive in use (p. 69).

## Bearing on the record

- **[NOTE-034](NOTE-034.md), C7.** RR shows a third way of cutting the domain from a
  cooperative premise. It counts against the idea that game theory yields
  a unique list: MAC and RR start from the same function and get different
  units.
- **[NOTE-034](NOTE-034.md), C8 (sexual morality is cooperative).** RR supplies a worked
  version of that claim for incest and honour, which the Reply only
  sketched. It is no better tested.

## Limitations

- No new data. The cases are chosen to illustrate, and none is a test
  that could have come out against the theory.
- Which model is "in force" in a situation is read off after the fact;
  the paper gives no independent way to fix it before observing the
  judgment, so C1 risks being unfalsifiable.
- Purity is assigned to Unity by argument. Food taboos with no group
  boundary (most of the "harmless wrongs" of Haidt, Koller & Dias) fit
  less easily.
- The ethical implications section mixes description and prescription,
  though the authors flag the is–ought question (fn. 4).

## Open questions

- Does manipulating which relational model is salient change purity
  judgments as RR predicts, for example disgust at an in-group member's
  act versus a stranger's?
- Do Equality and Proportionality motives, measured independently, predict
  different judgments of the same violence? The MFQ-2's split
  ([LIT-500](../literature.d/LIT-500.md)) makes this testable.
