# NOTE-tmpvl003: List — Three kinds of collective attitudes

## Contribution

It separates three things that informal talk and the social sciences both
call "what the group thinks": a summary of members' attitudes, an attitude
all members share knowingly, and the attitude of a group agent. It gives
each a definition, a supervenience base, a social role and a rationality
profile, and shows that they come apart in all but one direction. By the
author's description it is a review, not new results; its contribution is
the disambiguation.

## Key insight

How "reducible" a collective attitude is, is fixed by how wide its
supervenience base is. A poll's verdict on p depends only on what people
think about p. A common belief that p depends also on what people think
others think about p. A group agent's view on p can depend on what members
think about everything else, and on what they do, because the group must
stay consistent across its whole agenda. Only the third needs a group agent,
and only the third can come apart from what any member believes.

## Assumptions

- **Functionalism about attitudes (§4.2).** Beliefs and desires are states
  defined by their role in an agent; so a group agent's attitudes are
  "whichever states of the organized collective play the relevant
  functional roles". This answers the charge of circularity in defining
  corporate attitudes by group agents and group agents by their attitudes.
- **A thin definition of agency (§4.2).** Beliefs, desires or goals, and a
  capacity to act on them, with no condition of consciousness and no
  normative content (List & Pettit 2011, ch. 1).
- **Common awareness as an infinite hierarchy (§3.1)**, with the caveat that
  a dispositional or primitive definition would do.
- **Rationality licenses agency ascription (§4.5).** For groups, unlike
  humans, the burden of proof is on whoever ascribes agency; only local
  irrationality is compatible with it.

## Key results

- **Aggregate attitudes (§2).** Defined after Quinton (1975): to ascribe a
  mental predicate to a group "is always an indirect way of ascribing such
  predicates to its members". Formed by direct aggregation (votes, polls)
  or behavioural aggregation (prediction markets: the price of a $1 bet on
  p reflects the market's credence in p, §2.2). They depend on the rule
  (Riker: no "will of the people" independent of procedure). They satisfy
  *propositionwise supervenience* (§2.5). Majority belief can be
  inconsistent (Table 1). Market beliefs tend to coherence only because
  incoherence is an arbitrage opportunity (§2.6).
- **Premise-based rules produce potential corporate attitudes (§2.5).**
  Rules that derive conclusions from voted premises violate propositionwise
  independence; their outputs are better seen as coherent corrections, or
  candidates for corporate attitudes, than as aggregates.
- **Common attitudes (§3).** Every member holds it, believes every other
  member holds it, and so on. Coordination needs them (the coordinated-
  attack problem cannot be solved by finitely many messages, §3.2). Offices,
  currencies and rituals depend on them. They satisfy *enriched
  propositionwise supervenience* (§3.3) and inherit consistency from
  unanimity (§3.5).
- **Non-reductionistic rivals (§3.4).** Searle's we-intentions (a special
  mode) and Gilbert's plural subjects (a new subject) play the role of common
  attitudes "in very different ways". List leaves we-attitudes out until
  shown necessary, judges plural-subject attitudes "more akin" to corporate
  ones, and says invoking a plural subject for every joint action "seems to
  give rise to an unnecessarily rich ontology of subjects".
- **Falsely attributed common attitudes (§3.6).** Clauses (2), (3), … can
  hold without clause (1). This is pluralistic ignorance (Prentice & Miller
  1993; O'Gorman 1975 on white Americans' estimates of support for
  segregation). It can sustain bad equilibria, and public pledges can break
  them (Mackie 1996 on footbinding).
- **Corporate attitudes (§4).** A group agent's, guiding its action. Not
  reducible: a group's attitude on p cannot generally be a function of
  members' attitudes on p, by the List–Pettit impossibility result (§4.4).
  They satisfy *holistic supervenience*, on "individual attitudes across a
  web of interconnected propositions and/or other non-attitudinal
  contributions". The relation is "supervenience without reducibility", as
  in non-reductive physicalism.
- **Three lessons (§5).** (1) An aggregate attitude need involve neither a
  common nor a corporate one. (2) A common attitude need not involve a
  corporate one, and a falsely attributed common attitude need not involve
  an aggregate one. (3) A corporate attitude need involve neither.

## Concepts

- **Aggregate attitude** — a summary of members' attitudes produced by an
  aggregation rule or statistical criterion (§2.1); Gilbert's "summative".
- **Direct / behavioural aggregation** — attitudes elicited and summarised,
  or read off behaviour such as prices (§2.2).
- **Common attitude** — held by all members as a matter of common awareness
  (§3.1).
- **Falsely attributed common attitude** — higher-order clauses hold, the
  first-order clause does not (§3.6).
- **Corporate attitude** — an attitude held by the collective as an
  intentional agent (§4.1).
- **Propositionwise / enriched propositionwise / holistic supervenience** —
  the three supervenience bases of the three kinds (§§2.5, 3.3, 4.4).

## Bearing on the record

- **Candidate THEORY (proposed, not filed):** "collective emotion" names
  three different things, aggregate, common and corporate emotions, and the
  disagreements among Gilbert, Huebner, Schmid and the social psychology of
  convergence are partly about different kinds. Only corporate emotions are
  candidates for being a group's own state. Sources: this paper,

## Limitations

- By its own statement a review. The theorem it relies on is cited, not
  proved.
- Emotions are mentioned once, in a list (§1). Whether affective states,
  which are not obviously propositional or rationally constrained, divide
  the same way is not examined.
- "Not straightforwardly reducible" is supported only by analogy to
  non-reductive physicalism; what more than holistic supervenience it
  amounts to is not said.

## Open questions

- Do corporate emotions exist, and would they need the rationality profile
  that corporate beliefs need? An emotion's coherence conditions are not
  deductive closure.
- Is pluralistic ignorance about emotion (each member thinks others are
  angrier) a falsely attributed common emotion, with the same equilibrium
  effects?
