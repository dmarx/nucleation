# LIT-2665: Moral Crumple Zones: Cautionary Tales in Human-Robot Interaction

Madeleine Clare Elish (2019). Moral Crumple Zones: Cautionary Tales in Human-Robot Interaction. *Engaging Science, Technology, and Society* 5: 40–60. DOI 10.17351/ests2019.260.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading of 2026-11-17; the reading of 2026-11-16 is below.*

- **The concept** (p. 40): responsibility "may be misattributed to a human
  actor who had limited control over the behavior of an automated or
  autonomous system", and "the moral crumple zone protects the integrity
  of the technological system, at the expense of the nearest human
  operator".
- **Control distributed, responsibility not** (p. 49): "While control has
  been effectively distributed, responsibility has not scaled
  accordingly." Certification leaves the human as "the only possible
  malfunction" (p. 50).
- **More than a scapegoat** (p. 51): the operator can be partly
  responsible, as at Three Mile Island (p. 44).
- **Limits** (pp. 50–51): not "a property of every complex and automated
  system"; zones form "in the immediate aftermath of a highly publicized
  event or accident" (Therac-25 is the contrast), and the concept "is not
  a way to explain why these gaps occur".

## Reading of 2026-11-16

Read in full from the open-access version of record. The concept is defined in the abstract and
the introduction: the human "may become simply a component—accidentally or intentionally—that
bears the brunt of the moral and legal responsibilities when the overall system malfunctions"
(p. 40). Two cases are argued (Three Mile Island, Air France 447), one counter-case
(Therac-25), and a forward look at self-driving cars (Uber Tempe, Tesla, Google). The paper
limits itself: the concept "is not a way to explain why these gaps occur" and "moral crumple
zones are not a property of every complex and automated system" (p. 50). It concerns "cultural
perceptions of blame and responsibility", not legal liability (p. 52). The exchange reports it
accurately (A15 §5, A17 §§1, 4), adding "professional" to Elish's "moral and legal". A17's
supervisor and nurse illustrations are A's own. Elish says nothing about credit for failures
that human compensation prevented. The Uber discussion predates the NTSB final report
([LIT-2664](LIT-2664.md)).

receive disproportionate blame for failures in complex technological
