# NOTE-1590: Berglund — Kramers' law: validity, derivations and generalisations

## Contribution

A cross-disciplinary map of the proofs of Kramers' law, written for
mathematicians and physicists, which "endeavours to link concepts and
terminology from both fields" (abstract). Its organizing distinction is
between what large deviations deliver, the exponent of the mean exit time,
and what they do not, the prefactor, which took other methods and holds
only under conditions that bifurcations and irreversibility break.

## Key insight

The exponential order of a metastable escape time is robust and comes from
an action functional: the cheapest way out. The prefactor is fragile. It
depends on the curvature at the well and the saddle, it needs potential
theory to prove, and it fails at degenerate saddles and in non-reversible
dynamics.

## Assumptions

- **Small noise**, ε → 0, in dx = f(x) dt + √(2ε) dW (§2) or, for Kramers,
  dx = −∇V(x) dt + √(2ε) dW (eq. 1.2).
- **For Kramers' law**: gradient drift, so the dynamics is reversible with
  explicit invariant measure; a confining potential; non-degenerate
  (quadratic) critical points.
- **For the large-deviation results**: only a smooth drift f, not
  necessarily gradient.

## Key results

1. **Action functional (§2, eq. 2.3).** "Schilder’s result has been
   extended to general equations of the form (2.1) by Wentzell and
   Freidlin", with I(ϕ) = ½∫‖ϕ̇ − f(ϕ)‖² dt. "One may think of the rate
   function as representing the cost of tracking the function ϕ rather than
   following the deterministic dynamics." It holds only up to logarithmic
   equivalence: "mind that this is only true in the sense of logarithmic
   equivalence (2.5)."
2. **Laplace's method (Remark 2.1).** "The large-deviation principle (2.4)
   can be considered as an infinite-dimensional version of Laplace’s
   method." The book's Introduction describes its method the same way.
3. **Quasi-potential (eq. 2.8).** V(z) = inf over T and ϕ of I(ϕ): "It
   measures the cost of reaching z in arbitrary time."
4. **Exit time (Theorem 2.2, credited to Wentzell and Freidlin 1969–70).**
   lim 2ε log E τ = inf over ∂D of V. The proof sketch treats successive
   time intervals as "viewed as repeated “attempts” of the process to leave
   D". "Wentzell and Freidlin also show that if the quasipotential reaches
   its minimum on ∂D" at a unique point, the exit location concentrates
   there; Day showed τ is asymptotically exponential (Theorem 2.3).
5. **Gradient case (eqs. 2.11–2.13).** The quasi-potential is twice the
   barrier height, so lim ε log E τ = V(z*) − V(x*): Arrhenius' law.
6. **Many attractors (Remark 2.5).** These can "be treated by organising
   these points in a hierarchy of “cycles”", citing Freidlin and Wentzell
   (1998); not developed.
7. **What large deviations cannot do (§1; Remark 2.5).** The approach proves
   Arrhenius' law "for more general than gradient systems, but fails to
   control the prefactor."; "However, to our knowledge a rigorous
   computation of the prefactor by this" route has not been achieved.
8. **The prefactor proved (§1; Theorem 3.3).** The Eyring–Kramers formula
   had "a full proof of (1.9) having been obtained only in" 2004, by
   Bovier, Eckhoff, Gayrard and Klein using capacities.
9. **Metastable ordering (eq. 3.38).** "This means that the minima are
   ordered from deepest to shallowest."; the time from a shallow well to
   the deepest is set by the larger, overall barrier (Example 3.5).
10. **Bifurcations (§4.1).** "Kramers’ law (1.9) cannot be true in the
    presence of" singular Hessians; at a pitchfork the prefactor scales as
    ε^{1/4}.
11. **Irreversible case (§4.3).** Asked whether Kramers' law extends: "In
    general, the answer is negative." The prefactor "need not even converge
    to a limiting value as ε → 0."; the exit location rotates with log ε
    (cycling, Theorem 4.3).

## Concepts

- **Communication height** H(x*, y*): inf over paths of the sup of V; its
  minimizer gives the relevant saddle z* (eq. 1.6).
- **Rate function / action functional** I(ϕ) (eq. 2.3).
- **Quasi-potential** V(z) (eq. 2.8).
- **Equilibrium potential (committor)** h_{A,B}(x) = P^x{τ_A < τ_B} (eq.
  3.1), and **capacity** (eqs. 3.25, 3.33).
- **Metastable ordering** (eqs. 3.37–3.38); **cycling** (§4.3).

## Connections

**Against Freidlin and Wentzell ([LIT-2035](../literature.d/LIT-2035.md)), for which it stands in.**
It restates: the Wentzell–Freidlin action functional as a large-deviation
principle (eqs. 2.3–2.5); the quasi-potential as the cost of reaching a
point in arbitrary time (eq. 2.8); the exit-time theorem, credited to
Wentzell and Freidlin (Theorem 2.2), with the repeated-attempts proof
sketch that the book's Introduction also gives; the concentration of the
exit location; the gradient-case reduction to twice the barrier height;
and, by pointer only, the hierarchy of cycles for many attractors (Remark
2.5). It does not restate: the limit of the stationary distribution among
several stable sets and the graph construction behind it (the book's
Chapter 6); averaging (Chapter 7); Hamiltonian systems and diffusions on
graphs (Chapters 8–9); stability under random perturbations and optimal
stabilization (Chapter 10); perturbations with jumps (Chapter 5). It
stands in for Chapter 4's exit problem, at the level of statements and
sketches, not proofs.

Beyond the book, it draws on Bovier, Eckhoff, Gayrard and Klein (2004) for
the prefactor, Helffer, Klein and Nier for the Witten Laplacian, Day for
the exponential law and cycling, and Berglund and Gentz for the
irreversible case.

## Limitations

- **A review.** Proofs are sketched or cited, not given.
- **Mostly the gradient case.** The irreversible case is covered only in
  §4.3.
- **One route is unjustified.** On the Hamilton–Jacobi route to
  prefactors the author writes "We are not aware, however, of this
  procedure having been justified mathematically" (§2).

## Open questions

- Is there a rigorous Eyring–Kramers formula for non-reversible diffusions
  with a unique saddle, outside the cycling case? The survey leaves it open.
