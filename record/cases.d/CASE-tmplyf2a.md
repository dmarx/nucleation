# CASE-tmplyf2a: One melody from a 1951 gospel record to a 2005 Katrina protest song: "I've Got a Savior", "I Got a Woman", "Gold Digger" and "George Bush Doesn't Care About Black People", each link a different relation, the message turned at every step

## The case

From James Boyle, *The Public Domain*, ch. 6 ([LIT-tmpvmhet](../literature.d/LIT-tmpvmhet.md), read in
[NOTE-tmpjvg8y](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpjvg8y.md)). Page numbers are the Yale edition's.

- **The received origin.** Most sources, and the biography on Charles's
  web site, say Ray Charles made "I Got a Woman" (1955) by rewording the
  1904 hymn "Jesus Is All the World to Me" (p. 127). Boyle hears
  "little resemblance" (p. 133).
- **The actual source.** Claims pointed to a song by the Bailey Gospel
  Singers, "I've Got a Savior", that no catalogue or library could find.
  Boyle's research assistant found a 78 rpm copy in the Rodgers and
  Hammerstein Archives of the New York Public Library. "It sounds like
  the same song": the introduction and backing differ, but "the central
  melody is almost exactly the same", down to the intonation (pp.
  133–134). The record names its composer only as "Ward", perhaps Clara
  Ward. Charles turned another Ward song, "This Little Light of Mine",
  into "This Little Girl of Mine" in the same way (p. 134). "I've Got a
  Savior" itself takes lyrical patterns from a 1940 spiritual, "Ain't
  That Good News" (p. 133).
- **Charles to West.** For "Gold Digger" (2005) Kanye West had Jamie
  Foxx, who had played Charles in *Ray*, imitate Charles's style and the
  melody of "I Got a Woman". An eight-bar loop of Foxx singing it runs
  under the whole song. The fragment was cleared, with payment to the
  Charles estate (pp. 138–140). The words invert the original: "She
  gimme money / when I'm in need" becomes "She takes my money, when I'm
  in need".
- **West to The Legendary K.O.** In September 2005 two Houston volunteers
  recorded "George Bush Doesn't Care About Black People" within days of
  Katrina (pp. 125–126). Its background is "a looped, or infinitely
  repeated, version of the hook that Kanye West and Jamie Foxx had in
  turn taken from Ray Charles" (p. 144).
- **The phrase pattern.** Boyle sets it out: "I've got a savior, Oh what
  a savior" becomes "I got a woman, way over town" becomes "There's a
  Gold Digger, way over town" (p. 142). The Legendary K.O. "found
  themselves sampling Jamie Foxx, who was copying Ray Charles, who was
  copying the Bailey Gospel Singers, who themselves may have borrowed
  their theme from an older spiritual."

Documented: Boyle names his sources and the archive holding the record.
That the Bailey record precedes Charles's song is his dating, "a mere
three or four years earlier". Its copyright status is unknown. The
Copyright Office has no registration for it (pp. 136–137).

## What it can show

**[CLAIM-002](../claims.d/CLAIM-002.md).** The chain is a worked instance of a retelling recognizably
descended from an original it no longer resembles in function. The tune
and the phrase pattern survive four steps, while what the song praises
or condemns changes at each one. The links are not one relation repeated:

- Bailey to Charles is a rewording, with the melody and lyrical pattern
  kept.
- Charles to West is a licensed re-performance by an imitator, with no
  part of Charles's recording used.
- West to The Legendary K.O. is a sample of the recording itself.

So identity of the artifact holds only on the last link, and continuity
of transmission holds on all three. Similarity of function holds on none.

**The relations come apart in the record of the case, too.** The
transmission story that sources repeated, from a 1904 hymn, was wrong,
and it was the melody that located the real source. That bears on
[CLAIM-002](../claims.d/CLAIM-002.md)'s defeater, which asks whether descent judgements are fully
predicted by present similarity. Here similarity corrected a false story
about transmission. That does not defeat the claim: it shows similarity
used as evidence of transmission, not standing in for it.

**[CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md).** The Legendary K.O. step is a rendering in the strict sense,
a transport from a given source (the recording). The Foxx step shares the
melody's kind with Charles's song and was produced from it, so it is a
rendering too, though of the composition, not the recording. The law
tracks the same line. West's use of the composition was cleared (p.
140). The Legendary K.O. took the sound recording itself, which is the
use *Bridgeport* governs. Boyle sums up its rule as "Get a license or do
not sample" (p. 151).

**What it cannot show.** It is one chain, picked out because it is
striking. Boyle's reading of the musical similarity is his own, with no
musicological analysis. Nothing here measures how much of the "message"
a listener hears in the borrowed melody. The record's other cases of
change under serial transmission, such as [CASE-068](CASE-068.md) (risk messages in
diffusion chains), are experiments, and this is not.
