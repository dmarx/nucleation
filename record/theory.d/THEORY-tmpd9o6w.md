# THEORY-tmpd9o6w: Information technology has turned some large executive agencies from street-level into system-level bureaucracies, in which case-level administrative discretion all but vanishes and passes to the designers and legal-policy staff who translate law into algorithms, so that the constitutional problem moves from the legality of individual decisions to political accountability for system design, with a new risk of arbitrariness through rigidity

Bovens & Zouridis (2002), [LIT-2661](../literature.d/LIT-2661.md).

## The evidence

Two Dutch agencies. In student grants, automating a step "demands the
standardization and formalization of the preceding steps" (ms p. 9), until
officers "merely pressed a button" and a living person saw a case only on
formal objection. In traffic fines, a chain of discretionary officials gave
way to cameras and administrative fines, reviewed by a person only on
appeal. The discretion left lies with "the system and process designers"
(ms p. 14), whose work is "mainly a matter of political control and
accountability rather than of legality" (ms p. 17).

## What it does not show

- **Generality.** Two qualitative cases; the authors conjecture the rest
  and exclude professions with goal-oriented mandates.
- **That system-level decision is better.** The "zenith" verdict rests on
  one cited finding, and n. 6 records errors where staff over-relied on
  incomplete systems.
- **That the remedies work.** Hardship clauses, panels and
  informatization reviews are sketched.
