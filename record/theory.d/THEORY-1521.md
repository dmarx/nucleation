# THEORY-1521: Macnamara and colleagues argue that when automation performs a subtask while the operator goes on doing the task, the operator's skill at that subtask decays from disuse, the cognitive part more than the procedural part, and out of the operator's awareness, because the task itself is still done and still succeeds

## Source

Macnamara, Berber, Çavuşoğlu, Krupinski, Nallapareddy, Nelson, Smith,
Wilson-Delfosse and Ray (2024), [LIT-2224](../literature.d/LIT-2224.md); read in [NOTE-1710](../notes.d/NOTE-1710.md).

## What was actually shown

Nothing new: the paper says "We do not report on any studies or original
data." It assembles three premises and argues from them.

- **Partial disuse counts as disuse**: "disuse can also refer to ceasing
  subcomponents of a task, such as when automation is used in place of the
  person applying their skills."
- **Cognitive before procedural**, from Casner et al. (2014) as the paper
  reports it: procedural flying skills "were “rusty” but largely intact",
  while "major declines in cognitive skills emerged", in pilots who "spent
  the majority of their careers flying with high automation". The study
  itself was not read; the paper gives no sample, effect size or interval.
- **Concealment**, argued: decay "may operate outside the performer’s
  awareness because the disuse is only at the level of cognitive skill
  engagement, not with engagement with the task". No measure of awareness is
  cited.

## What this does not say

- How fast the skill decays, or how long it takes to recover. The record's
  rates are Tatel's ([LIT-2079](../literature.d/LIT-2079.md)), for motor procedures with infrequent use.
- That AI assistants cause more decay than automation. The paper predicts
  it and says no research has tested it.
- That Bainbridge said any of this. The paper does not cite her.
- That operators cannot notice the loss. The argument is that doing the task
  gives them no signal of it, not that a test would not show it.
