# THEORY-tmpg3y0n: In fan–celebrity parasocial relationships the celebrity mostly directs fans' identities and the fans mostly interpret the celebrity's, so a mass audience that refuses uptake of a legitimately presented change of persona holds one person's identity fixed, which is a failure of recognition respect that restricts the celebrity's agency

## Source

Archer & Robb (2025), "Ethics of Parasocial Relationships", [LIT-172](../literature.d/LIT-172.md), read in
full in [NOTE-186](../notes.d/NOTE-186.md) from the penultimate draft.

## What was actually shown

An informal argument in three steps.

- **Personal relationships (§1, pp. 2–6).** On LaFollette's criterion, a
  relationship is personal "inasmuch as each person relates to the other as a
  unique individual". Fans relate to celebrities so, with kinship terms and
  grief. Non-reciprocity and illusory intimacy are answered: unrequited love
  is personal, and personae are curated but not detached from the person. A
  weaker fallback is offered, that parasocial relationships share
  distinctive forms of the paradigmatic features (p. 6).
- **The asymmetries (§2, pp. 6–8).** Attention: the fan attends to one
  celebrity, the celebrity to a fanbase. Communication: the celebrity
  broadcasts; a Twitter sample found no replies from celebrities (Kehrberg
  2015). Epistemic: fans know much about the celebrity, the celebrity
  nothing about the fan. Together these split Cocking and Kennett's two
  influences of friendship. Celebrities "do most of the directing", fans
  "most of the interpreting" (p. 8).
- **The wrong (§3, pp. 8–12).** The case is Swift's 2018 break with her "nice
  girl" persona and the backlash. Recognition respect is owed to the self a
  person is "entitled to perform" (p. 10, after Darwall 1977). On
  Lindemann's account, identity is co-constituted by one's stories and
  others' uptake, so refusing uptake restricts agency (pp. 10–11), a form of
  Valentini's public-identity disempowerment. Mass scale and the asymmetry
  of exit (fans can leave, celebrities cannot) aggravate it. Fans should "be
  open to changes in the public persona of their idols" and should "refrain
  from pressuring celebrities" (pp. 11–12). Misleading or ambiguous
  self-presentation mitigates their blame (the Paris Hilton case, p. 12).
  There is no duty to remain a fan (p. 11).

What could have come out otherwise: interpretive influence running from
celebrity to fan as much as the reverse, or refused uptake leaving the
celebrity's self-presentation untouched. Only one case is examined.

## What this does not say

- **What celebrities owe fans.** The abstract promises "ethical
  responsibilities for both fans and celebrities". The body argues only for
  fans' responsibilities, and cites celebrities' duties from earlier work
  ([NOTE-186](../notes.d/NOTE-186.md), corrections).
- **That the identity asymmetry has the effects claimed, in general.** The
  support is celebrity-studies literature and a few documentaries and news
  cases (Swift, Hilton, Spears, the Olsens). There is no systematic evidence.
- **What grounds the entitlement to a presented self.** Recognition respect is
  owed only to a self one is "entitled to perform" (p. 10). What grounds that
  is left open, which matters most for contested personae.
- **That the wrong applies to chatbots or fictional characters.** The
  definition of parasociality admits them (p. 1). The asymmetries plausibly
  transfer to a user and a chatbot persona. The wrong does not: it is done to
  a person whose identity and agency can be restricted. Carrying it over
  needs an argument about AI moral status (compare [LIT-126](../literature.d/LIT-126.md)).
- **That relationships of power share the wrong.** The extension to political
  leaders, managers, religious leaders and teachers (p. 13) is asserted in
  the conclusion without analysis.
- **That the relationship is "personal" in the full sense.** That claim leans
  on how people talk and shifts the burden of proof (p. 6). The fallback does
  most of the work.

## Connections

- **Identity in the practical sense.** The account works with who someone is
  as others track it, not with numerical persistence. [THEORY-tmp8fvw1](THEORY-tmp8fvw1.md)
  (Dufner, [LIT-130](../literature.d/LIT-130.md)) argues that the ethical questions that seem to turn on
  personal identity usually turn on some other relation. This is a case in
  which the relation is narrative and social.
- Velleman ([THEORY-tmpnaq0t](THEORY-tmpnaq0t.md)) separates the self of self-conception from the
  self of persistence. Refused uptake, on this account, presses on the
  first.
- Bunton ([LIT-174](../literature.d/LIT-174.md)) asks the converse question about the same 2016–2018 era
  of celebrity speech: when a celebrity's speaking out is justified.
