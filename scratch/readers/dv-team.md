## Work 1. Bardsley, Mehta, Starmer & Sugden
- Provenance: CeDEx Discussion Paper 2008-17 (5 Dec 2008), https://www.nottingham.ac.uk/cedex/documents/papers/2008-17.pdf, PDF, 54pp, institutional working paper; read in full (text, tables 1 partly, notes). Pages cited are the working paper's, not EJ's.
- Copies: Nottingham PDF (read, 2026-11-20); ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/19354 (search snippet says no full text; not fetched); eprints.ncrm.ac.uk/532 (not fetched).
- Biblio (published): Economic Journal 120(543):40-79, 2010, doi:10.1111/j.1468-0297.2009.02304.x (from search/RePEc; text read is the WP).
- Summary: one-shot coordination games with three treatments: pickers (just pick), guessers (paid to guess a picker), coordinators. CH predicts coordinators = guessers (PC2); team reasoning predicts coordinators at least as concentrated as guessers and choosing the team-optimal rule (PC3; Hi-Lo HL2). Amsterdam (N=161) supports team reasoning; Nottingham (N=134) is consistent with CH. A questionnaire suggests Amsterdam labels primed "stands out", Nottingham labels "favourite".
- Key results:
  - Team reasoning defined, §1.3 p.11: "Roughly, the individual asks ‘What should we do?’, and acts upon the answer in the expectation that other members of the group think and behave analogously."
  - CH's belief structure, §1.2 p.6: "It is a fundamental feature of cognitive hierarchy theory that, at each level, a player believes that his opponent’s level is lower than his own"
  - Higher-order salience collapses, §1.2 p.9: "It seems reasonable to assume that subjects do not have such information, and hence that each subject’s prediction of the modal guess of other subjects is the same as her own guess." (so p1 = p2 = ...; the hierarchy needs only one step of belief about others in practice)
  - Amsterdam text, §3.2 pp.24-25 (NCI pick 1.116, guess 1.259, coord 1.819): "That coordinators’ responses are much more concentrated than guessers’ is contrary to PC2 (and cognitive hierarchy theory) but consistent with PC3 (and team reasoning)."
  - Amsterdam Hi-Lo, §4.2 p.27: "In every case, more than 60 per cent of coordinators chose the team-optimal response"
  - Nottingham, §3.3 p.26 (NCI 1.204/1.982/2.197): "It seems that the Nottingham coordinators are responding as if they were guessers."
  - §7 p.33: "Our experiments seem to have identified two modes of reasoning, each of which is sometimes used by players of coordination and Hi-Lo games."
  - §7 p.34: "In contrast, the theory of team reasoning gives no role to primary salience."
  - §7 p.34, the odd-one-out case: "Here, it seems implausible to appeal to a pre-reflective propensity to pick the odd one out." / "The most obvious answer is that ‘Choose the green cube’ is a better rule for the two players together than ‘Pick a red cube’."
  - §7 p.35: "some essential feature of the human ability to solve coordination problems seems to have escaped formalisation."
- Assumptions: picking elicits level-0 behaviour; labels in number tasks nondescript; near risk-neutrality (n.16).
- Concepts: primary / secondary / Schelling salience; picking, guessing, coordination treatments; normalised coordination index; nondescript Hi-Lo games; rule of selection; team-optimal.
- Bearing: THEORY-1630 / LIT-1698 (Sugden): parallel; same author's empirical test, showing team reasoning operates in some contexts only. Owner's thesis (50-50 as conspicuous solution): supports that an odd-one-out or symmetry-type focal point can be reached by a "best rule for us" rather than by stacked expectations; qualifies it, because in some contexts people do coordinate by guessing what others pick. THEORY-tmpqxwyk (Schelling 1957, branch): background; the authors read Schelling as resisting any single theory.
- Limitations: adults (students) only; says nothing about the cognitive prerequisites in development; team reasoning as modelled still involves "the expectation that other members ... think and behave analogously" (an expectation about others' mode, not a nested belief about their beliefs); the paper does not discuss order of beliefs required in those terms.
- Candidate THEORY: "Bardsley, Mehta, Starmer and Sugden find, in two experiments comparing pickers, guessers and coordinators in one-shot coordination games, that focal points are sometimes reached by team reasoning (choosing the rule that is best for the pair) and sometimes by predicting others' pre-reflective picks, depending on the decision context." promote_when: "the published EJ version is checked to report the same Amsterdam and Nottingham results and the same two-mode conclusion."

## Work 2. Perez-Zapata, McKenzie-Smart, Charest & Apperly
- Provenance: PsyArXiv preprint doi:10.31234/osf.io/96hbp, fetched via https://osf.io/96hbp/download (PDF "ChildrenAdultsMeetingOfMinds.pdf", 44pp, created 2025-01-07); read in full apart from the reference list. Preprint, so wording may differ from the published version.
- Copies: research.birmingham.ac.uk/files/286716279/PerezZapataD2025Meeting.pdf (published version, CC BY per repository page; Cloudflare challenge, stopped); tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/20445911.2025.2452232 (403, stopped).
- Biblio (published, from Birmingham repository page): Journal of Cognitive Psychology 37(3):277-292, 2025, doi:10.1080/20445911.2025.2452232.
- Summary: Study 1: 4-5-year-olds (n=25), 6-7-year-olds (n=29/30) and MTurk adults (n=25) on four pure coordination tasks (coarse visual, fine visual, forced-choice verbal, open verbal); children played against a puppet behind a screen, with no communication. "Alignment" = proportion of same-group others giving the same answer. Study 2 (pre-registered): 42 6-7-year-olds and 24 of their parents, coordinating with a "notional" peer group.
- Key results:
  - Intro p.4, on CH: "this hierarchical reasoning strategy risks a form of regress whereby each person will be trying to anticipate the other’s perspective"; on "we": "it has been proposed that coordination involves reasoning about what “we” (the two people) will find salient or relevant in the context of this problem".
  - Intro p.6: "There is evidence that children as young as five years are capable of group-based reasoning, which does not encounter such problems" (citing Grueneisen et al. 2015a, b; Goldvicht-Bacon & Diesendruck 2016).
  - Age choice p.7: "we tested children aged 4-5 because this is the earliest age at which previous evidence suggested children might be able to succeed on strongly-constrained coordination games."
  - Study 1 RQ1, pp.17-18: above chance in all groups and tasks "except for the Fine Visual task in the case of the four- to five-year-old group."
  - Coarse-visual: 4-5-year-olds did not differ from adults, and both beat 6-7-year-olds (p.19); in Table 1's example item, 79% of 4-5-year-olds picked the same square.
  - Conclusion p.32: "Present findings reveal for the first time that children as young as 4-5 years of age can solve pure coordination problems that have no single obvious answer."; p.32: children "could nevertheless have a good chance of meeting each other at a different location that adults find less intuitively obvious."
- Assumptions: within-group answer convergence measures coordination ability; children understood the goal (check items; pre-test only in Study 2).
- Concepts: intuitive alignment; within- vs between-group alignment; pure coordination game; Schelling salience; mutual knowledge.
- Bearing: owner's thesis: qualifies. Children of about 5 (mean 4.96) converge on salient options without communication, but (a) no 3-year-olds were tested; (b) the partner was a puppet whose answer was "whispered" (p.13), so this is not peer-peer tacit coordination; (c) the paper does not test which reasoning (team, level-k, plain salience) children use. NOTE-185 / THEORY-495 (three-year-olds' equal sharing): background only; the age gap remains. Tomasello (NOTE-tmpe9epc, Bohn et al. 2019 on branch): parallel; it cites Bohn & Köymen 2018 on common ground for "children as young as one year take account of mutual knowledge" (p.6).
- Limitations: the "group-based reasoning" attribution to five-year-olds is the authors' gloss on prior work, not tested here. Their references list "2015b" as the second-order false-belief paper while the text uses 2015b for the four-box salience study (p.6): a citation muddle.
- Candidate THEORY: "Perez-Zapata, McKenzie-Smart, Charest and Apperly find that children aged 4-5 and 6-7, like adults, converge above chance on the same answers in pure coordination games without communication, at lower levels than adults and sometimes on different answers from adults." promote_when: "the published Journal of Cognitive Psychology version is checked to report above-chance alignment for 4-5-year-olds on three of four tasks and child-adult differences in which answers were salient."

## Work 3. Colman, Pulford & Lawrence
- Provenance: accepted manuscript ("In press: Decision. © 2014 American Psychological Association"), University of Leicester institutional repository, figshare.le.ac.uk article 10150418, file https://ndownloader.figshare.com/files/18293054 (repository licence field: "All Rights Reserved"); read in full through the General Discussion; appendices and supplementary material not read. Pages are the manuscript's.
- Copies: core.ac.uk/download/357407304.pdf (Cloudflare, stopped); research.bangor.ac.uk record (not fetched).
- Biblio: Decision 1(1):35-58, 2014, doi:10.1037/dec0000001.
- Summary: reviews explanations of coordination in common-interest games (principle of indifference, social projection, CH, team reasoning) and proposes "strong Stackelberg reasoning". Two experiments (N=68, N=59) using 3x3 and 4x4 asymmetric games where the theories diverge. Level-1 CH predominates; Stackelberg reasoning appears in 3x3 games, team reasoning in both sizes, and more in 4x4. Most players report using several modes.
- Key results:
  - Team reasoning defined, p.9: "Team-reasoning players are assumed to ask, What do we want, and what should I do to play my part in achieving it?"
  - Its belief condition, p.9: "A player has no reason to adopt the team-reasoning mode in the absence of a belief that the other player(s) will do likewise"; in Bacharach's version ω is a parameter, "the value of which is common knowledge in the" game (pp.9-10).
  - CH, p.8: "it relies on an assumption that players never credit their co-players with the same level of strategic sophistication as themselves."
  - Cognitive cost, p.20: "Team reasoning can be accomplished even more easily, especially in larger games, by scanning the payoff matrix for the cell with the greatest payoff sum, and then checking that it is unique." Stackelberg reasoning "requires sequential strategic thinking" and more working memory.
  - On Bardsley et al., p.20: "Team reasoning is easier than cognitive hierarchy reasoning (Level-1 and above) in larger and more complicated games"
  - Results, p.21: Level-1 37-46% of choices (3x3) and 46-50% (4x4); team reasoning 13-20% (3x3), 26-27% (4x4); "team reasoning becomes relatively more influential in the larger, more complex games."
  - Abstract p.1: "Most players considered two or more of these reasoning processes before choosing their strategies."
- Assumptions: payoffs as objective money; social projection excluded because it predicts nothing distinct in asymmetric games.
- Concepts: common-interest game, Hi-Lo, Stag Hunt, payoff dominance, social projection, strong Stackelberg reasoning, Avoid the Worst heuristic.
- Bearing: THEORY-1630: parallel; restates Sugden's/Bacharach's team reasoning and its departure from methodological individualism. Owner's thesis: supports the claim that team reasoning is computationally cheap (find the jointly best cell); qualifies it, because the theories as stated condition team reasoning on a belief, or a common-knowledge probability, that the partner team-reasons. CLAIM-491: background.
- Limitations: adults, matrix games, no labelled focal points; payoff-sum team reasoning, not Schelling salience.
- Candidate THEORY: "Colman, Pulford and Lawrence find, in two experiments with asymmetric 3x3 and 4x4 games, that Level-1 cognitive hierarchy reasoning predominates, strong Stackelberg reasoning is frequent in the smaller games, and team reasoning accounts for a larger share of choices in the larger games, most players considering several modes." promote_when: "the published Decision version reports the same shares of choices by mode."

## Answers to the brief
1. What team reasoning requires, compared with CH. Team reasoning: identify the profile (or rule of selection) that is best for "us" and play your part (BMSS p.11; CPL p.9). It needs no chain of beliefs about beliefs about choices. What it does need, in every version read, is an expectation that the partner reasons the same way: "in the expectation that other members of the group think and behave analogously" (BMSS p.11); "in the absence of a belief that the other player(s) will do likewise" (CPL p.9); in Bacharach's version, a probability of team reasoning that is common knowledge (CPL pp.9-10, reporting). CH: level-k players hold beliefs about lower-level others' choices. BMSS argue that in practice the hierarchy collapses to one step, predicting what a naive picker would choose (p.9). That is first-order: what will they pick, not what do they think I think. Perez-Zapata et al. present CH-style perspective taking as risking "a form of regress" and team ("we") reasoning as avoiding it (pp.4, 6). CPL rate team reasoning as computationally easy (scan for the cell with the largest payoff sum, p.20). None of the three works tests whether team reasoning needs second-order theory of mind. Read strictly, the "belief that others will do likewise" is a first-order belief about the partner's mode of reasoning (my inference, not the authors' claim).
2. Experiments. BMSS: team reasoning in Amsterdam, CH-consistent results in Nottingham; the mode depends on context (stand-out vs favourite labels). CPL: Level-1 CH dominant; team reasoning 13-27% of choices, rising with game size; most players mix modes.
3. Preschoolers. The best evidence read says 5-year-olds, not 3-year-olds. Grueneisen et al. 2015 (abstract only): 3-year-olds did not choose the salient box above chance, while 5- and 8-year-olds did. Perez-Zapata et al.: 4-5-year-olds (mean 4.96) align above chance on three of four tasks, against a puppet partner. Nothing read shows 3-year-olds coordinating on salient options without communication.
