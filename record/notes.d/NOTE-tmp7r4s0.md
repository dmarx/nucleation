# NOTE-tmp7r4s0: Franklin — The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin

## Contribution

The book is the source of the Benjamin Franklin effect, and the record
holds it for that one page (CASE-143). Read whole, it is a manual of
influence written as a life: a self-made printer explains to his son, and
then to the public, the means by which he rose and carried his projects.
What it gives the record beyond the anecdote is a run of first-hand,
named instances of the same technique, of getting people to act before
they have settled what they think of the actor.

## Key insight

Franklin works on the conditions of other people's judgement more than
on the judgement. He hides his authorship, lets an idea appear to arise
in many places at once, leads opponents into concessions by questions,
makes the first move with a request instead of a defence, and keeps up
appearances as carefully as the realities ("I took care not only to be in
reality industrious and frugal, but to avoid all appearances to the
contrary"). He says what this costs him: "If it remains a while uncertain
to whom the merit belongs, some one more vain than yourself will be
encouraged to claim it".

## Assumptions

Not a theory, so there are no formal conditions. What the instances
assume:

- **Reputation is built from what others can see.** The industry
  "visible to our neighbors, began to give us character and credit";
  Dr. Baird's remark at the merchants' club is reported as the turn.
- **Authorship provokes resistance.** "the impropriety of presenting
  one's self as the proposer of any useful project, that might be
  suppos'd to raise one's reputation in the smallest degree above that of
  one's neighbors".
- **Dogmatic speech provokes contradiction.** A "positive, assuming
  manner" "tends to create opposition".

## Key results

The memoir reports; these are its instances, with where they fall.

- **The borrowed book (Part Three, written from August 1788).** Chosen
  clerk of the General Assembly in 1736; the next year "a new member made
  a long speech against me". Franklin "did not, however, aim at gaining
  his favour by paying any servile respect to him, but, after some time,
  took this other method": he asked by note to borrow "a certain very
  scarce and curious book", returned it "in about a week with another
  note, expressing strongly my sense of the favour". At their next
  meeting in the House "he spoke to me (which he had never done before),
  and with great civility; and he ever after manifested a readiness to
  serve me on all occasions, so that we became great friends, and our
  friendship continued to his death." Franklin: "This is another instance
  of the truth of an old maxim I had learned, which says, 'He that has
  once done you a kindness will be more ready to do you another, than he
  whom you yourself have obliged.' And it shows how much more profitable
  it is prudently to remove, than to resent, return, and continue
  inimical proceedings." The member is not named. The motive is stated
  just before: the clerkship "secur'd to me the business of printing the
  votes, laws, paper money".
- **Hiding authorship.** The subscription library was "a scheme of a
  number of friends"; "I ever after practis'd it on such occasions"; the
  academy's proposals likewise.
- **Plurality made to order.** Each Junto member was to form a
  subordinate club "without informing them of the connection with the
  Junto", partly for "our power of doing good by spreading thro' the
  several clubs the sentiments of the Junto". The watch reform was
  "communicated to the other clubs, but as arising in each of them", and
  "by preparing the minds of people for the change, it paved the way for
  the law".
- **Preparing opinion in print.** For the hospital, "I endeavoured to
  prepare the minds of the people by writing on the subject in the
  newspapers, which was my usual custom in such cases"; likewise Plain
  Truth before the defence association and the academy pamphlet before
  its subscription.
- **Matching grant.** The hospital bill made £2,000 of public money
  conditional on an equal private sum, so that opponents had "the credit
  of being charitable without the expence" and donors saw "every man's
  donation would be doubled"; "I more easily excus'd myself for having
  made some use of cunning."
- **Showing the list.** His advice to Gilbert Tennent: apply first to
  those sure to give, then "show them the list of those who have given"
  to the uncertain.
- **Modest diffidence.** From the Socratic method, kept "only the habit
  of expressing myself in terms of modest diffidence"; after the Quaker
  friend's rebuke, "I made it a rule to forbear all direct contradiction
  to the sentiments of others, and all positive assertion of my own", and
  "I had less mortification when I was found to be in the wrong". He
  claims "a good deal" of success "with regard to the appearance" of
  humility and little in its reality.
- **The art of virtue.** Thirteen virtues, one attended to per week, with
  faults marked as spots in a ruled book; "I was surpris'd to find myself
  so much fuller of faults than I had imagined". Order "gave me the most
  trouble"; he never reached the perfection he aimed at, "but was, by the
  endeavour, a better and a happier man".

## Concepts

- **Erratum**: his word for a fault in his conduct, borrowed from
  printing, which a life lived again would "correct".
- **The Junto**: his club of mutual improvement, with queries and essays,
  and "all expressions of positiveness in opinions, or direct
  contradiction" made "contraband".
- **The art of virtue**: a means of acquiring virtue, as against "the
  mere exhortation to be good".

## Connections

Jecker and Landy (LIT-1419, Deferred) tested the anecdote's effect in the
laboratory; the memoir offers it only as a maxim confirmed once. The
Junto's satellite clubs, ideas "arising in each of them", are a
documented case of manufactured attributed plurality: the circulation
the hyperstition interview (LIT-1396, NOTE-tmphc9i5) names, and what
CLAIM-425 says should not stand in for grounds. Soros's two functions
(LIT-1397, NOTE-tmp8x2ha) fit Franklin's printing exactly: his paper and
pamphlets were at once his report of opinion and his means of moving it.

## Bearing on the record

- **CASE-143** tells the anecdote as the text does. One detail could be
  sharpened: Franklin says the opposing member was "a gentleman of
  fortune and education, with talents that were likely to give him, in
  time, great influence in the House", and that his reason for winning
  him over was the profit of the clerkship. The case's standing as the
  anecdote's is right; the text gives no name for the member.
- **CLAIM-275** (an intervention can come before the interpretation it
  makes available). The anecdote fits, but Franklin's own account of why
  it worked is the maxim, not an interpretation following an act, and
  the text says nothing about what the member came to think. Read
  closely, it supports the claim's shape and does not show its
  mechanism.
- **CLAIM-425 and CLAIM-426.** The satellite clubs and the newspaper
  pieces that "prepare the minds of the people" are first-hand cases in which a judgement's circulation was arranged
  so that it would bring about what it said. Franklin arranged the
  appearance of plurality ("as arising in each of them"), which is the
  "attributed plurality" CLAIM-425 discounts and CLAIM-426 keeps as
  evidence about the future. A proposed dated note for CLAIM-426 is in
  the coordinator's report.

## Limitations

- A memoir written decades after the events, for posterity and to
  instruct; Franklin says the book may "gratify my own vanity" and that
  pride "will every now and then peep out".
- Part Three was written "at home, August, 1788" without "the help
  expected from my papers, many of them being lost in the war", and asks
  more than once to be checked against the votes.
- The anecdote's member is unnamed and the effect is reported only by
  the man who sought it.
- **Errors in the edition, not the memoir.** The editor's "Chief events"
  list gives "1737 Elected to the Assembly", but the memoir has him
  clerk from 1736 and elected a burgess only after he left business in
  1748; it puts the Braddock supplies at 1756, where the memoir's own
  advertisement is dated "LANCASTER, April 26, 1755". Its "1746 Publishes
  ... Plain Truth" is also a year early by the usual dating (1747), which
  is from outside the text and was not checked. The transcriber's notes call the
  Harvard Classics a G. P. Putnam & Sons edition, where the title page
  says P. F. Collier & Son.

## Open questions

- Did the favour work on the member, as the maxim says, or on Franklin,
  whose request and note were themselves the first civil acts? The
  memoir cannot separate them, and LIT-1419's experiment is the
  place that might.
