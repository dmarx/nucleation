# NOTE-tmpf00ch: Mulgan (2018) — Corporate Agency and Possible Futures

## Contribution

A methodological argument: choose a theory of corporate agency by its
robustness across credible futures, not only by present truth or fit. And a
taxonomy that separates agency from personhood and adds a reliability-based
moral condition. It is speculative and scenario-based, and reports no cases.

## Key results

1. **Four positions (p. 903).** Individualism: "Corporate groups are not
   agents of any kind"; minimal collectivism, agents but not moral agents;
   moderate, moral agents but not moral persons; extreme, both. Footnote 4:
   "I borrow the individualism/collectivism distinction from Velasquez
   (2003), p. 38. The minimal/moderate/extreme distinction is my own."
2. **No mainstream extreme collectivist (p. 904).** "I can find no
   mainstream theorist who defends extreme collectivism"; defenders of
   corporate "personhood" "explicitly distance themselves from full moral
   personhood in my sense" (French 1979 among them; footnote 5 allows French
   1984 as a possible exception).
3. **Velasquez, as Mulgan reports him.** Individualists "must accept" that if
   no individual is responsible no one is, and "Velasquez 2003 explicitly
   bites this bullet" (pp. 903–904). "Individualists argue that moderate
   collectivism is an unstable position (e.g. Velasquez 2003; Rovane
   2014). Once we recognise corporate groups as moral agents, we are on a
   slippery slope to recognising their full moral personhood" (p. 904).
   "For the realist, the philosopher's primary task is to determine the
   (metaphysical) facts (e.g. Velasquez 1983, 2003)" (p. 907). "His defence
   of individualism explicitly cites John Searle's account of consciousness
   as an emergent feature specific to human biological embodiment"
   (p. 913).
4. **The moralised condition (p. 905).** Recognition versus response,
   ability versus reliability: "The most demanding moral criterion would
   require reliably appropriate response." List and Pettit's version is
   read as "clearly pragmatist", restricting moral agency to the reliable
   because that serves human interests, not because it tracks metaphysics
   (pp. 906–907).
5. **Psychopathic corporate groups.** Many for-profit enterprises "are
   closer to psychopaths than to normally functioning human adults" (p. 906,
   after Bakan). A group whose management lacks any mechanism for
   recognising and responding to moral reasons "is irredeemably
   psychopathic. (And a psychopathic corporate group, unlike its human
   equivalent, has no moral standing at all.)" (p. 910). "nonpsychotic
   individuals can together constitute a psychopathic corporate group"
   (p. 914).
6. **The futures.** "Broken world philosophers will favour collectivism over
   individualism. They will also favour moralised collectivism" (p. 910);
   "In a digital future, collectivists will embrace that slippery slope"
   (p. 913), and digital-future philosophers "will dismiss Searle's position
   as simple anti-digital prejudice" (p. 913).
7. **Conclusion (p. 914).** "The only temporally robust account is moralised
   extreme collectivism, where suitably sophisticated morally reliable
   corporate groups (and only those corporate groups) are recognised as both
   responsible agents and full moral persons." New groups must pass "their
   own Moral Turing Test". It "is consistent with maintaining both that no
   extant digital entity or corporate group is (even) a moral agent and also
   that it is not inevitable that digital or corporate persons will ever
   emerge"; and "my argument may not persuade realists".

## Assumptions

- A temporally robust ethic must be plausible to future people, let them
  address salient threats, and not strike us after reflection as
  unacceptable (p. 902).
- Broken and digital futures are credible; digital persons are stipulated.
- Future people succeed in neutralising morally unreliable digital beings
  (p. 912).
- Corporate groups are not phenomenally conscious, on both sides (p. 905).

## Concepts

Corporate group; agency versus personhood (stipulated); individualism and
minimal, moderate and extreme collectivism; moralised collectivism;
recognition versus response, ability versus reliability; temporal
robustness; realist, pragmatist and interpretivist method; broken future;
digital future; inhuman corporate future; Moral Turing Test.

## Bearing on the record

- **On the LIT's characterization.** The summary, from the abstract, is
  accurate. Its expectation that the paper reports Velasquez's denial of
  corporate agency is **partly met**: Mulgan reports Velasquez's position in
  several places, but by parenthetical citation and paraphrase, with no
  quotation and no reconstruction of the argument.
- **How far it stands in for Velasquez (LIT-1429, LIT-1432): in part.** It
  is a secondary report by an opponent, enough to say what Velasquez holds
  and which premises he relies on (biological embodiment, Searle's
  account of consciousness), not enough to assess the argument or to count
  it answered. Beyond the record's gloss in QUESTION-265, it attributes to
  Velasquez the individualism/collectivism frame, the bitten bullet on
  the responsibility gap, the slippery-slope argument against moderate
  collectivism, the Searlean premise and a realist method.
- **CLAIM-271**: indirect. Mulgan endorses, after List and Pettit, that a
  committee can act on a belief "even though none of its members either do
  X or believe Y" (p. 903); he says nothing of polling.
- **THEORY-131** (French) and **THEORY-139** (Pettit): Mulgan reads both as
  short of "full moral personhood in my sense", and List and Pettit as
  pragmatist moralised collectivists.
- **QUESTION-265**, **CLAIM-431**, **CLAIM-432**: a third position on
  corporate psychopathy, structural absence of any mechanism for moral
  reasons, which on Mulgan's view removes moral agency rather than grounding
  blame.
- **QUESTION-049** and **THEORY-137**: the realist, pragmatist and
  interpretivist methods bear on whether attributions track or shape.
- Dated notes on each, and on LIT-1429, LIT-1432, LEAD-092 and NOTE-1094.
- "temporally robust", "broken future", "Moral Turing Test" and "extreme
  collectivism" are held nowhere else in the record.
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice.**

## Limitations

- **The Velasquez citation does not check out.** Footnote 4 cites
  "Velasquez (2003), p. 38", but the reference list gives Velasquez 2003 as
  "Business ethics quarterly, 13, 545–546" (p. 916), and the record's
  LIT-1432 gives the article as 13(4):531–562. A page 38 cannot be in it.
  The record should not quote "p. 38" as Velasquez's.
- **Predictions stand in for arguments.** The case against individualism is
  that future philosophers "will" find it less credible; Mulgan concedes
  this "may not persuade realists".
- **The moralised condition is never operationalised.** Who certifies
  reliability, and how the "Moral Turing Test" would run, is unstated.
- **Agency and personhood are run together at the conclusion.** The
  moralised condition is argued for moral agency, but the conclusion grants
  personhood to the reliable, on the digital-persons analogy alone.
- **"No moral standing at all"** for psychopathic groups is asserted; it
  does not follow from a condition on agency.
- No empirical evidence beyond the climate figures for the broken-future
  premise.
