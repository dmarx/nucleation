# NOTE-tmpul39i: “First we invented stories, then they changed us”: The Evolution of Narrative Identity

## Contribution

The essay restates narrative identity, the construct McAdams introduced in
1985 and defined with McLean in 2013, and sets it in four frames: human
evolution, individual development, individual differences and culture. The
essay's own addition is the thesis of its title. Borrowing Boyd's phrase about
the species, it argues that in an individual life "we invent stories to make
sense of our own lives. And then these stories change us."

## Key insight

A person's identity, in the psychologist's sense of what unifies a life and
gives it purpose, is a story the person tells. The story is selective,
invented and borrowed from culture. It is still consequential, because once
it exists the person reads their own choices through it. Narrative identity
is the third of three layers of self. Traits (the actor) and goals (the
agent) come first and do not go away. The story (the author) arrives in
adolescence and takes in the other two.

## Assumptions

- **Storytelling is an evolved, social capacity.** "Stories exist to be
  told" and are "nearly always about social life, about intentional human
  agents". Their evolutionary function is to simulate social experience and
  to coordinate groups. This is supported by citation (Boyd, Mar & Oatley,
  Wilson, Henrich, Harari) and by one field study (Smith et al. 2017, the
  Agta). The selection story itself is not tested.
- **Identity in Erikson's sense.** Identity is "a sense of sameness and
  continuity across different social roles and over time". McAdams takes
  the life story to be the form that identity takes.
- **Coding of interview narratives measures something stable.** The
  variation section rests on validated coding systems for life-story
  interviews (cited to McAdams & Manczak 2015). Their validity is assumed
  here, not argued.

## Key results

No new data. The findings it reports, all cited:

- **Development.** Autobiographical memory emerges "in the third year of
  life"; parents scaffold children's self-narration. Retrospection and
  prospection share brain systems, and the default mode network is proposed
  as "part of the infrastructure ... that has evolved to support the
  invention of life stories". Autobiographical reasoning grows from
  adolescence "through late midlife". By the early 20s most people have a
  narrative identity.
- **Variation.** Agentic life stories go with well-being. In psychotherapy,
  change toward agency "predict[s] and precede[s]" symptom decline (Adler
  2012). The redemptive self has five themes: early advantage, suffering of
  others, moral steadfastness, redemption and prosocial goals. It is
  associated with generativity, well-being and "positive emotionality,
  altruism, resilience, and achievement-striving". Narrative incoherence is
  reported in schizophrenia and some personality disorders.
- **Culture.** Master narratives have five features (after McLean & Syed
  2015): utility, ubiquity, invisibility, a compulsory nature and rigidity.
  East–West differences in children's and adults' autobiographical
  narration are reported. Three explanations are offered: Confucian
  tradition, rice versus wheat agriculture (Talhelm et al. 2014) and
  modernity (Greenfield; Taylor; Giddens).

## Concepts

- **Narrative identity**: "a person's internalized and evolving story of
  how he or she has become the person he or she is becoming".
- **Autobiographical reasoning**: deriving themes, causal links, turning
  points and lessons from episodic memories (Habermas & Bluck 2000).
- **Actor, agent, author**: the three layers of the psychological self, with
  traits and roles, goals and values, and the life story.
- **Redemptive self**: a life-story pattern in which suffering leads to a
  positive outcome, framed by early advantage and prosocial goals.
- **Master narrative**: "a cultural script that is readily accessible to
  members of a particular axis of identity" (Hammack 2008).

## Connections

It cites the philosophical narrative view through Ricoeur and Schechtman
([LIT-468](../literature.d/LIT-468.md)). It treats Schechtman's author, character and reader as a
"tripartite operation" also found in reading fiction (Carroll 2018). It does
not engage the philosophical critics of narrative identity. Strawson's
"Against Narrativity" ([LIT-452](../literature.d/LIT-452.md)) is not cited, though the essay's own remark
that some people are more "oriented toward narrative identity" than others
touches his claim that some people are not narrative at all.

Its "motivated agent" layer is the psychology of goals and plans that the
record reads philosophically in Bratman ([LIT-tmp5j4f4](../literature.d/LIT-tmp5j4f4.md)). Its claim that a story
"provides a life with unity and purpose" is the narrative candidate for the
cross-temporal unity that Bratman's chapter explains instead through plans.
Bratman, in that chapter, calls narrative unity a tempting idea that applies
to "many cases" but is not the "more basic cross-temporal unity".

- **[THEORY-044](../theory.d/THEORY-044.md) and [THEORY-051](../theory.d/THEORY-051.md).** The RFT account places "the narrative self"
  as the conceptualized self, the content of verbal relations about oneself.
  McAdams's narrative identity is a developed version of that content. ACT
  treats attachment to it as a source of inflexibility. McAdams treats its
  construction as a developmental achievement. The two are compatible only
  if holding a story and being fused with it are distinguished. Neither
  source draws that distinction.

## Limitations

- A review essay for a humanities-facing journal. Every empirical claim is
  cited, and the strength of the cited studies cannot be judged from it.
- The evolutionary section is explicitly speculative ("must have", "may
  have"). The essay gives no evidence that narrative identity, as opposed to
  storytelling, is an adaptation.
- The well-being findings are correlational except Adler (2012). The essay
  does not discuss whether the story causes well-being or reports it.
- It concedes that the idea of "having" a life story may be "a cultural
  construction that resonates well with the sensibilities of the modern
  world". That qualifies the universality of C1–C2 and the essay does not
  resolve it.

## Open questions

- Whether people low on narrative orientation (the ANIQ's low scorers, or
  Strawson's Episodics) differ in well-being or self-continuity, which would
  test the rivalry with [LIT-452](../literature.d/LIT-452.md).
- Whether narrative change precedes symptom change in replications of Adler
  (2012), as C3 needs.
