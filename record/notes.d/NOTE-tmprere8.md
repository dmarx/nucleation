# NOTE-tmprere8: Rule Consequentialism (SEP)

## Contribution

The entry defines full rule-consequentialism as a theory that chooses rules solely by the goodness of their consequences and lets those rules determine which acts are wrong, which decision procedure to use, and when blame is apt; Berkeley is cited as its first proponent. It surveys the consequentialist family (utilitarianism, theories of welfare, non-welfare goods, global consequentialism) and the choices in formulating rule-consequentialism: actual vs. expected good, compliance vs. acceptance, complete vs. incomplete acceptance. It gives three ways of arguing for it and defends it against the long-standing collapse-or-incoherence dilemma and the charge of rule-worship before reviewing newer objections.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §4 separates three components — criterion of wrongness, decision procedure, conditions for sanctions — and "full" rule-consequentialism is rule-based on all three.
- §5 contrasts global consequentialism (Pettit and Smith 2000; Kagan's "multi-dimensional direct consequentialism"), which assesses everything directly.
- §6.2–6.3 argue for formulating in terms of acceptance/internalisation rather than compliance, counting the cost of teaching the code and assuming imperfect uptake (D. Miller 2021).
- §7 gives the arguments: from reflective equilibrium with intuitive rules, and from consequentialism itself (indirect assessment yields better outcomes; Brandt, Harsanyi).
- §8 answers collapse into act-consequentialism and incoherence, and rebuts rule-worship via a "prevent disaster" rule (Brandt 1992).
- §9 lists remaining objections: excessive demandingness in the real world (Carson 1991; Mulgan 2001), the problem of teaching new generations, and conflicts among rules (Eggleston 2007).

## Open questions

- The acceptance-vs-compliance and imperfect-internalisation framing (§6) parallels designing rule sets for agents that will only partly absorb them; check the exact formulations.
- §8's collapse argument is the canonical case of a rule-based policy degenerating into case-by-case optimisation — a useful analogue for rule-based vs. outcome-based specification of AI behaviour.
