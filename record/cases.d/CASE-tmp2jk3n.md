# CASE-tmp2jk3n: United 173 and Crew Resource Management: a crew that knew the fuel was running out and did not make the captain act, and the training that followed, which opened routes for challenge without abolishing the captain's command

## The case

On 28 December 1978 United Airlines Flight 173, a DC-8, circled near
Portland, Oregon, for about an hour while its crew worked a landing-gear
problem, ran out of fuel and crashed; ten people died. The NTSB
([LIT-tmp6l0tg](../literature.d/LIT-tmp6l0tg.md)) found the probable cause in the captain's failure to
monitor the fuel state and to respond to the crew's advisories about it,
and a contributing cause in the other crew members' failure either to
grasp its criticality or to communicate their concern to the captain
successfully.

The accident became a standard starting point for Crew Resource
Management. Beginning with a NASA workshop in 1979, airlines trained crews
in assertion, challenge and coordination. Helmreich, Merritt and Wilhelm
([LIT-tmplaidn](../literature.d/LIT-tmplaidn.md)) trace CRM through five generations to error management,
avoiding, trapping and mitigating error, with the captain's command kept.
Separately, after TWA 514 struck a mountain on 1 December 1974, it emerged
that a United crew had nearly made the same error weeks earlier and the
warning had stayed within United. The Aviation Safety Reporting System
followed in 1976. Both papers and the report are registered unread; the
1974 and 1976 history is the exchange's, unchecked.

## What it shows

- **Access without authority.** The crew had the fuel state; it did not
  govern the captain's action. That is [CLAIM-349](../claims.d/CLAIM-349.md)'s separation of access to
  a signal from its authority over action, in a cockpit.
- **Authority without abolishing command.** A25: CRM was "authority
  without abolishing command"; it sought to "maintain meaningful avenues
  of challenge while preserving the capacity for decisive action". The
  reform changed how authority is organized, not who holds it. This is
  [CLAIM-tmpqktfo](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpqktfo.md)'s governance of the conditions of authority, and A25's
  answer to its own counterexample of an over-active safety officer:
  "Non-domination doesn't imply unconditional veto power."
- **Information partitioned between collectives.** ASRS answered a warning
  that one airline had and the others did not. A25 kept this apart from
  the cockpit case: the information was partitioned "between collectives",
  not within one.

## What it does not show

- That CRM caused the later decline in accident rates. The case is the
  reform's history, not its evaluation.
- That a crew is an agent. It is an organization of authority whose
  holder was a person throughout.
