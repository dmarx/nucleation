# NOTE-1269: Shaping Humanity's Longterm Trajectory

## Contribution

A formal tool for comparing longtermist interventions. Ord says its aim
is "not to argue for longtermism, but to develop a technique for
comparing different ways of shaping the longterm future" (p. 7). He
represents the whole future as one curve of value over time and derives,
for four idealised changes and for existential risk reduction, how each
one's value scales with the future's height and its length. The results
are mostly qualitative: which interventions grow with the future's
duration and which do not.

## Key insight

Shifting the future earlier and multiplying it are different in kind. An
advancement replaces a sliver of the present with a sliver of the end, so
its value is (v_τ − v_0)δt however long the future lasts. Existential risk
reduction and enhancements multiply the whole area, v̄τ. So, the longer
and better the future, the less advancing progress matters relative to
securing it, unless the advance also changes the shape of the curve,
for instance by cutting risk or locking in better values (pp. 26–29).

## Assumptions

- **Value is roughly quantitative and separable across time** (pp.
  3–4), a ratio-scale flow that can be integrated. This admits the total
  view, the critical-level view, an average view integrated over time,
  "some person-affecting views", and views with diminishing value of
  population at a time. It excludes views that are not time-separable,
  such as the average wellbeing of everyone who ever lives.
- **Only value.** "If there are important parts of morality that are
  unrelated to value (such as personal freedoms or unbreakable rules) the
  framework will remain silent about those" (p. 4).
- **No pure time preference** (p. 9). Discounting money for growth is
  set aside because the curve is value itself. Discounting for risk is
  set aside because outcomes are evaluated ex post; risk would enter
  through uncertainty over trajectories, not a fixed hazard rate (p. 10).
- **Finite duration** (p. 10). Infinite sums are avoided by treating τ
  as a parameter and asking how values scale with it.
- **Marginal, idealised, permanent changes** from t = 0 (pp. 11–12),
  with diagrams deliberately not to scale (pp. 7–8).

## Key results

- **The size of the future** (pp. 5–7). The species has lasted about
  300,000 years; a typical species lasts about a million; Earth stays
  habitable for about a billion more years, and other stars could extend
  that "at least a thousand times". The future's value relative to this
  century is (v̄/v_0) · (τ/100): how much better the average century is,
  times how many centuries there are. "Each of these factors might be
  truly vast — quite possibly a billion or more" (p. 7). Duration alone
  "is more than enough to get longtermism off the ground" and is "the most
  widely accepted part" (p. 7).
- **Advancements** (pp. 12–17): the curve shifted left by δt.
  - Exogenous end time: δV ≈ (v_τ − v_0)δt. It does not depend on τ or on
    the curve's shape (p. 15). If value changes by under 5% a year the
    approximation is within 5% for advancements up to a year; a year's
    advancement "may require something comparable to the entire effort of
    all currently existing humans working for a year" (p. 15).
  - Endogenous end time (the end comes δt sooner too): δV ≈ −v_0δt. Unless
    the present has negative value, the advancement makes the future
    worse (p. 16).
  - A temporary advancement that lasts until time t_e is worth about
    (v_e − v_0)δt, best if it lasts to the peak and no further (p. 25).
    Ord's footnote: Bostrom 2003 "was focused on an advancement of this
    kind" (fn. 11).
- **Speed-ups** (pp. 17–20): the curve compressed by γ_t. Exogenous end:
  δV ≈ (v_τ − v̄)(1 − 1/γ_t)τ, positive only if the end is better than the
  average. Endogenous end: δV = −v̄(1 − 1/γ_t)τ, positive only if the
  future's total value is negative. A possible speed-up is likely
  overdetermined, and then it is only an advancement (p. 20). The
  agricultural revolution arose independently in at least five places.
- **Gains** (pp. 20–22): a fixed rise δv; δV = δv τ. Examples: a fixed
  rise in everyone's wellbeing in a stable population; saving an
  ecosystem, species or work of art, where its value lasts as long as it
  does. A gain must be contingent; saving "something irreplaceable from
  permanent destruction" resists the objection (p. 22).
- **Enhancements** (pp. 22–23): a proportional rise γ_v;
  δV = (γ_v − 1)v̄τ. Examples: better health or prosperity for all,
  proportionally more people (on some views), better moral understanding
  or motivation. Contingency is again the challenge; the best case is
  values improved before a lock-in (p. 23).
- **Comparisons** (pp. 26–29).
  - Advancements scale with v_τ − v_0; speed-ups with (v_τ − v̄)τ; gains
    with τ; enhancements with v̄τ (p. 26).
  - An advancement beats a gain if δt/τ > δv/(v_τ − v_0). "Advancing
    progress by a millionth of humanity's lifespan is ten times as good as
    permanently gaining a ten-millionth of the gap" (p. 27).
  - An enhancement beats a gain if (γ_v − 1) > δv/v̄; τ cancels (p. 27).
  - **Existential risk.** Model near-term risk as occurring at t = 0 and
    a catastrophe as leaving about zero value. Raising survival by a factor
    γ_p gives δV = (γ_p − 1)v̄τ. Example: 20 points of risk, survival raised
    one point, γ_p = 81/80 = 1.0125 (p. 28). This is "almost identical"
    to an enhancement, and irreversibility, built into existential risk,
    makes contingency easier to meet.
  - An advancement beats risk reduction only if
    δt/τ > (γ_p − 1) · v̄/(v_τ − v_0) (p. 28). "On a million-year lifespan
    (that of a typical species) a one-year advancement would be roughly as
    important as a one-in-a-million improvement in nearterm survival
    probability — but the latter seems much more achievable" (p. 29).
- **Conclusion** (pp. 29–31). For advancing progress to beat risk
  reduction, it must do something more, such as advance defensive
  technology, wisdom or moral progress, effects that can also scale with
  τ. Proposed extensions: uncertainty over trajectories or hazard curves,
  non-marginal changes, and idealised shapes (a plateau, exponential or
  cubic growth; fn. 14 suggests quadratic if new locations are fixed
  resources).

## Concepts

- **longterm trajectory**: humanity's instantaneous value v(t) over time,
  from t = 0 to the end τ.
- **instantaneous value of humanity**: value per unit time, under
  whatever theory of value; on classical utilitarianism, happiness minus
  suffering at that time (p. 3).
- **advancement / delay**: the future curve shifted earlier / later.
- **speed-up / slow-down**: the curve compressed / stretched in time.
- **gain / loss**: a fixed rise / fall in value at all times.
- **enhancement / diminution**: a proportional rise / fall.
- **exogenous versus endogenous end time**: an end fixed by nature (the
  Sun) versus one that moves with our development (a dangerous
  technology).
- **contingency**: whether a change would have happened anyway; an
  overdetermined speed-up is only an advancement.
- **lasting change**: one that persists for at least a tenth of the
  future (p. 25).

## Connections

- **The Precipice ([LIT-1678](../literature.d/LIT-1678.md))**, unread. See section 3.
- **Bostrom 2003 ([NOTE-1314](NOTE-1314.md)).** Bostrom compared the cost of delay
  with existential risk reduction and found a percentage point of risk
  worth over 10 million years of delay. Ord's framework generalises that
  comparison and says Bostrom's case was a temporary advancement (fn.
  11). It adds what Bostrom's does not: if the end time moves with
  progress, advancing it is a loss.
- **Greaves and MacAskill ([NOTE-1302](NOTE-1302.md))** call a zero pure rate of time
  preference "near-essential". Ord adopts it with the same lineage
  (Pigou, Ramsey, Harrod; Ramsey is [LIT-1733](../literature.d/LIT-1733.md)) and adds the empirical
  claim that people do not discount benefits to others.
- **Tarsney ([NOTE-1247](NOTE-1247.md)) and Thorstad ([NOTE-1308](NOTE-1308.md)).** Ord sets
  risk aside by evaluating outcomes ex post and putting all risk at t = 0.
  Their objections live exactly where he leaves the framework for later:
  a hazard that persists after this century. Inference: once risk after
  t = 0 is added, the τ in (γ_p − 1)v̄τ becomes an expected duration, and
  Thorstad's point is that this expectation may be short.
- **Hershfield ([NOTE-1259](NOTE-1259.md)).** Ord notes that apparent pure time
  preference for oneself is hard to tell apart from weakness of will
  (p. 9). Hershfield ties it to felt discontinuity with one's future
  self. See Bearing on the record.
- **MacAskill ([LIT-1747](../literature.d/LIT-1747.md))**, unavailable, is cited on value lock-in
  (2022: 78–9).

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-299](../questions.d/QUESTION-299.md).** It sharpens the impersonal ground without
  defending it. Ord's argument needs an evaluative premise, value summed
  over time with no pure time preference, and no claim about anyone's
  self. Two results matter for the question.
  - Duration alone carries the case (p. 7). What the living owe, on this
    view, grows with how long humanity lasts even if life never gets
    better.
  - Besides survival, the only other route to the whole future is to
    change what will be valued before it locks in (p. 23). Inference: this
    makes the present generation's values a long-run lever, which brings
    the legacy question ([QUESTION-301](../questions.d/QUESTION-301.md)) into the normative one. To
    shape the future's values is to leave a legacy of the living's
    values, whatever the impersonal justification.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).** Ord says nothing about
  motivation; his agent is an "altruist" (p. 20). Two points bear on it.
  Both are inferences.
  - **Concern for others' futures is not discounted, on Ord's report.**
    People "don't have" pure time preference "regarding benefits to other
    people, such as one's children or future generations"; they are
    "roughly indifferent to the timing" (Frederick 2003, p. 9). Hershfield
    ([NOTE-1259](NOTE-1259.md)) finds people discount their own future more the less
    continuous they feel with it. If concern for future generations were
    concern for a self extended through time, it should fade with
    distance the same way, since felt continuity with people further off
    must be weaker. Indifference to timing does not fit that. It fits
    concern for future people as interchangeable members of a category
    ("humanity", "future generations"), not as parts of a self continuous
    over time ([TERM-096](../terms.d/TERM-096.md)). The thesis can absorb this only with a
    category-level self whose extent is not graded by time. That is the
    self-categorization reading ([THEORY-1114](../theory.d/THEORY-1114.md)), and [CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md)
    asks what fixes its extent apart from caring.
  - **Caveats.** The result is reported in one sentence, from one
    study, and Frederick 2003 is not in the chapter's bibliography. It is
    about judgements of when benefits should come, not felt concern or
    action. And Schubert et al. ([NOTE-1270](NOTE-1270.md)) find that lay judgement
    reaches the far future only when prompted. Timing-neutral judgement
    and weak unprompted concern can both be true.
  - **"Humanity" as one life.** The framework treats humanity as one
    subject with a "lifespan" (p. 27) and compares its survival risk with
    an individual's at eighty or ninety (p. 10). The analogy is
    rhetorical; the value summed is impersonal. It is not evidence that
    anyone identifies with humanity.
- **A dated note is proposed for [QUESTION-299](../questions.d/QUESTION-299.md) (section 4), not for
  [CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md).** The discounting point bears on the thesis's test, but
  it rests on a secondhand report the record should read first (see Open
  questions).
- **No THEORY filed.** A valuation framework, not an account of what is
  true.
- **No ML instruction.**

## Limitations

- **Author's version, April 2023**, not compared with the 2025 book
  text; pages differ.
- **Idealised and marginal.** Ord grants real interventions are "much
  messier" (p. 12). No uncertainty, so no hazard after t = 0.
- **Silent on what is not value**, by its own statement (p. 4), and so on
  rights, special obligations and what the past is owed.
- **The empirical aside on time preference** (C5) is thin; see above.
- **Typos and slips.**
  - "We can all such a change a gain" for "call" (p. 20); "had in not
    started" for "it" (p. 20); "speedup" beside "speed-up" (p. 20);
    "achieved later the default trajectory", missing "on" (p. 22);
    "preferrable" (p. 26); a missing full stop after "who will ever
    live" (p. 4).
  - "Ord 2022: 153–8" (p. 23) for Ord 2020: 153–8, as cited on p. 2.
  - Bibliography: "Collision, P." for Collison (p. 32); "That Is Not
    Dead Which Eternal Lie" for "Which Can Eternal Lie" (p. 32); "Long-
    Term" (p. 31). Cited but not listed: Frederick 2003, MacAskill 2022,
    Pigou 1920, Ramsey 1928, Harrod 1948, Koopmans 1960, Ng 1991.

## Open questions

- Does Frederick 2003 show what Ord says? If people weigh lives saved
  later the same as lives saved now, but discount their own future with
  felt discontinuity, then concern for others' futures and for one's own
  are structured differently. That is a direct test of whether concern
  for future generations is an extended self's. The record does not hold
  the paper. Crossref's likely match, given the chapter does not list it:
  Frederick, S. (2003), "Measuring Intergenerational Time Preference: Are
  Future Lives Valued Less?", Journal of Risk and Uncertainty 26,
  doi:10.1023/A:1022298223127.
- What happens to the comparison of advancement and risk reduction when
  risk persists after this century, as Thorstad argues? Ord names the
  extension but does not make it.
