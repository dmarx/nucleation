## Offered, not filed (read-and-file step 12)

Ranked by how far the assistant would stand behind each.

1. **The resident community as one competitor** (demonstrated, cheap to
   check). In [CLAIM-369](../../record/claims.d/CLAIM-369.md)'s class, with an interior resident set S at
   equilibrium and f_S = U_S n_S* its total impact vector, a rare
   component's growth is ‖u_i‖(κ_i − cos(u_i, f_S) ‖f_S‖). For a lone
   resident ‖f_S‖ = κ_j, which is [CLAIM-1181](../../record/claims.d/CLAIM-1181.md). So Chesson's pairwise
   criterion extends with the resident community standing as one species
   of fitness ‖f_S‖ and niche f_S. Its defeater would be that it only
   restates [CLAIM-885](../../record/claims.d/CLAIM-885.md)'s n*_i d_i² bound; it does not, since it is an
   identity rather than a bound, but the two should be compared.
2. **Predators keep the potential** (hypothesis). Chesson's §2.3 says
   predation enters ρ and κ as resources do. If the same coefficients set
   a consumer's effect on a predator and the predator's effect on it, the
   interaction matrix stays a Gram matrix, and [CLAIM-369](../../record/claims.d/CLAIM-369.md) would extend to
   consumer–resource–predator models. A reading lead for Chesson and
   Kuang (2008), the chapter's [16], would test it.
