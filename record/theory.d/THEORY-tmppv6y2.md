# THEORY-tmppv6y2: Covert influence that exploits a person's decision-making vulnerabilities harms her autonomy in the process of deciding, by making her opaque to herself, even when it benefits her; so it carries a harm in every case without being wrong in every case

## Source

Susser, Roessler and Nissenbaum (2019), [LIT-1002](../literature.d/LIT-1002.md), *Internet Policy Review*
8(2), read in full in [NOTE-tmpijdk1](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpijdk1.md): the definition (p. 4), attempted
covertness (p. 5), the autonomy harm (pp. 9–10), beneficent manipulation
and harm against wrong (pp. 10–11). Noggle (2018, rev. 2026), [LIT-1007](../literature.d/LIT-1007.md),
§2.1 and §§3.2–3.3, read in full in [NOTE-tmphtua8](../notes.d/NOTE-tmphtua8.md), for the objections
that set this claim's scope.

## What was actually shown

An argument from the structure of the influence, not a demonstration.

**The influence.** "[M]anipulating someone means intentionally and
covertly influencing their decision-making, by targeting and exploiting
their decision-making vulnerabilities." Covert means "in a way they
aren't consciously aware of, and in a way they couldn't easily become
aware of were they to try" (p. 4). Persuasion and coercion are
"forthright"; this influence works only while hidden, and "when we learn
we have been manipulated we feel played", because "at the time of
decision we didn't understand our own motivations".

**The harm is to autonomy.** Such influence "can lead them to act toward
ends they haven't chosen" and "for reasons not authentically their own",
challenging both the competency and the authenticity conditions of
autonomy (pp. 9–10). The economic harm "is derivative of the deeper harm
to autonomy" (p. 10).

**It is carried even when the influence benefits the target.** "[T]he
fundamental harm of manipulation is to the process of decision-making,
not its outcome." The paternalist harms his target "by rendering him
opaque to himself"; someone whose lifelong bad habit suddenly vanished
"might also be deeply confused and suspicious about the source of the
change" (pp. 10–11).

**Harm, not wrong.** "[W]e have not demonstrated that manipulation is
necessarily wrong in every case—only that it always carries a harm"
(p. 11). A life-in-danger case is allowed as one where the harm is
outweighed.

What could have come out otherwise: the vanished-habit case is offered as
one where intuition finds a harm with no bad outcome. A reader who finds
none there has a counterexample.

## What this does not say

- **It is not an analysis of manipulation.** The paper stipulates that
  manipulation is covert influence, and Noggle ([LIT-1007](../literature.d/LIT-1007.md), §2.1) sets
  against it peer pressure, nagging and guilt trips, which seem
  manipulative and are open. The paper answers only by calling overt
  tactics failed attempts (p. 5). So the claim is filed of covert,
  vulnerability-exploiting influence. Whether open manipulation carries
  the same harm is not addressed, and on this argument, which runs through
  the target's not knowing, it would not.
- **It does not say that such influence is always wrong.** The paper is
  explicit. Noggle's survey finds the field leaning, with a "perhaps", to
  manipulation being prima facie and pro tanto wrong (§3.1).
- **It does not meet the objections to autonomy accounts.** Noggle
  (§3.3) cites manipulation that enhances autonomy (steering someone away
  from returning to an abuser) and S. Buss's argument that the duty not to
  manipulate "has relatively little to do with the value of autonomy".
  The paper's reply is available only on its process reading: the harm is
  the self-opacity, whatever the outcome for autonomy. It does not engage
  those authors.
- **It says nothing about intent.** The definition requires intent three
  times (pp. 4, 5, 9) and never examines the requirement. The harm
  argument does not use it: self-opacity would follow from covert,
  vulnerability-exploiting influence chosen by an adaptive system with no
  one's intent. The paper does not draw that conclusion, and this account
  does not claim it.
- **It is about single decisions.** Manipulation of institutions and
  systems is set aside (p. 3), and global manipulation of a whole
  character is not discussed.
- **The policy proposals are not filed.** They are advice to regulators.

## Connections

No relations are declared. The account bears on both questions it was
filed for and answers neither.

- **[QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md).** The harm argument runs through the target's access to
  the influence, not through the influencer's mind. That makes it one
  route on which a whole with no manipulative intent in any member could
  still do the harm manipulation does. Whether that is "the same wrong"
  is what the question asks, and the paper, which claims a harm and not
  a wrong, does not say.
- **[QUESTION-071](../questions.d/QUESTION-071.md).** The criterion is historical and epistemic: whether
  the agent was, and could easily have become, aware of what shaped her
  decision. It is one answer to what in an agent's history would have to
  be recorded. It is argued for single decisions, not for the installed
  character of the four-case argument.
- **[THEORY-040](THEORY-040.md).** The paper's counterfactual-reflection condition on
  autonomy is a present-structure condition of the kind [THEORY-040](THEORY-040.md) says a
  manipulator can install. Its criterion of manipulation, though, is not
  present structure but the agent's relation to the history of her state,
  which is the kind of historical condition [THEORY-040](THEORY-040.md) asks for, unargued
  for the global case.
- **[CLAIM-016](../claims.d/CLAIM-016.md).** The harm is placed on the authenticity condition, the
  family of the set-aside claim.
- **[THEORY-047](THEORY-047.md).** Its controlling conditions of acquisition and this
  paper's covert influence both make how a motive was acquired bear on
  how autonomous acting on it is. The paper does not cite it.
