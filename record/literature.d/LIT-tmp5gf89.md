# LIT-tmp5gf89: Folding DNA to create nanoscale shapes and patterns

Rothemund, P. W. K. (2006). Folding DNA to create nanoscale shapes and patterns. *Nature* 440(7082): 297–302. doi:10.1038/nature04586.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmp8kyp2](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp8kyp2.md). Page numbers are the journal's.*

- **Compatible by construction.** Crossovers fall where "the scaffold
  can form a crossover only at those locations where the DNA twist places
  it at a" tangent point between helices (p. 297); every staple binds the
  scaffold only, and "the intramolecular nature of scaffold folding
  enforces a kind of equimolarity" (S9).
- **Feasible is not reliable.** Yields of well-formed structures run
  from about 1% (an equiangular triangle of rectangles) to 90% (a bridged
  rectangle); 11% for a star on a linear scaffold and 63% on a circular
  one (pp. 300–301).
- **Staple excess is a construction resource.** At 100:1 and 9:1 the
  holes were indistinguishable, at 1:1 about 1% of structures were
  rectangles (p. 301); "excess staple strands might be recycled" once
  folding is done (S10, untested).
- **Observing acts.** AFM resolved single missing staples only with sharp
  tips and hard tapping, and "repeated scanning created holes difficult to
  distinguish from holes due to missing strands" (p. 301).
- **Why it works is proposed.** Strand invasion, staple excess,
  cooperativity, and design "that intentionally does not rely on binding
  between staples" (p. 302); none tested.
