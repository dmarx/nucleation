# NOTE-tmpbdqc5: Social Influence and Group Identity

## Contribution

A review that puts group identity at the centre of social influence. It
sets the self-categorization account against three rivals: normative
influence (complying for approval), interdependence and network
approaches, and persuasion research that treats the group as a source of
bias. It rereads a run of classic findings in identity terms. It adds a
section relating group norms to injunctive norms, morality and intrinsic
motivation, which the earlier statements of the account lacked. It ends
with the SIDE model's online findings.

## Key insight

If the group is part of the self, being influenced by it is not giving
way to an outside pressure. "The fundamental insight of the
self-categorization approach to group influence is that the group is not
only an external entity but (for high identifiers, at least) becomes
internalized as a level of self. Thus, social influence is partly
self-influence, saved from circularity by the fact that other in-group
members form its source" (p. 373). The same idea explains the SIDE
paradox. Because the group is "a dimension of the self", isolation and
anonymity need not weaken it (p. 382).

## Assumptions

- **Self-categorization theory's levels of self.** Personal and group
  identities are both selves, up to the human level (p. 369). Salience
  depends on accessibility and fit, comparative and normative (p. 373).
- **Identification as the test of process.** Group identification is
  "the litmus test of whether group identity is the driving process"
  (p. 380). An effect it does not moderate is read as normative
  influence instead.
- **Categorization over similarity.** Categorization is taken to underlie
  inferences of similarity, after Murphy & Medin 1985 (p. 372).

## Key results

The review reports results and presents none. Each is cited to the
literature, much of it the author's own.

- **Three realms of influence** (Figure 1, p. 370). Two axes: level of
  self (personal or group) and attentional focus (self or other).
  - The *personal* realm: self-interest, personal goals, the need to fit
    in. Normative influence, interdependence and networks belong here.
  - The *group* realm: group identity, in-group norms, group interests.
  - The *other* realm: focus on the task or the information, with "few
    concerns about creating or protecting the self" (p. 370). This is
    informational influence.
- **Normative influence is instrumental** (p. 371). Deutsch and Gerard's
  normative influence is "going along to get along", closer to power than
  influence. "A theoretical shift was needed to see the group as part of
  the self" before group influence could count as private acceptance.
- **Referent informational influence (RII)** (pp. 372–373). Turner's name
  for group influence that is informational: members trust in-group
  members to know the answer. Physical and social reality testing are
  blurred. Asch's participants can be genuinely perplexed, enough to
  affect private acceptance.
- **Fit decides which identity carries influence** (p. 373). Lea et al.
  (2007): four-person groups, two British and two Dutch, two men and two
  women, one of each sex in each nationality. On topics pre-tested to
  divide the nationalities ("British food is bad") the nationalities
  bipolarized toward their norms. On topics dividing the sexes ("there
  should be more sport on TV") the sexes did. That is comparative and
  normative fit at once.
- **Classic findings reread** (pp. 374–376).
  - Shared identity strengthens convergence in Sherif's autokinetic
    paradigm (Abrams et al. 1990) and conformity in Asch's.
  - Milgram's participants identified with the scientific project, on
    Haslam and Reicher's reappraisal.
  - In emergencies people "often conform to prosocial norms even if it
    involves self-sacrificing behavior" (Drury and colleagues; p. 375).
  - Bystanders help others in peril "primarily when they share a social
    identity with them" (Levine & Crowther 2008; p. 375).
  - Emotional contagion and mimicry stop or reverse at out-group
    boundaries (van der Schalk et al. 2011; Spears et al. 2004).
  - Moderate feminists were influenced by radical feminists cast as part
    of "the feminist sisterhood", not when cast as an out-group (David &
    Turner 1999; p. 376).
- **Crowds** (p. 375). Deindividuation theory, after Le Bon, held that
  crowd members lose individual rationality and normal regulation.
  Reicher's analysis says that "rather than losing one's self or identity
  in the mass, crowd members actually switch to a group identity". The
  meta-analysis of Postmes & Spears (1998) "showed that the classic
  conditions associated with deindividuation (i.e., group immersion and
  anonymity in the group) actually enhanced depersonalization and thus
  conformity to these local group norms". Local group norms must be kept
  apart from the general societal norms that make crowd behaviour look
  antinormative.
- **Norms and morality** (pp. 376–378).
  - Cialdini et al.'s (1990) descriptive/injunctive distinction mentions
    "group" twice and identity not at all.
  - Group norms predicted recycling among high identifiers; social
    injunctive norms did not (White et al. 2009).
  - "Group identities can become so internalized (for high identifiers)
    that they become self-perceived as personal" (p. 377).
  - Raz's social self-authorship and Amiot and Thomas's work are cited
    for group-motivated behaviour being as intrinsically motivated as
    individual behaviour (p. 377).
  - In-group bias and fairness are injunctive norms at two levels of
    self-categorization. Right-wingers showed more in-group bias and
    endorsed the superordinate fairness norm less (Iacoviello & Spears
    2018; p. 378).
- **Moderators** (pp. 379–381).
  - The prototype is the position that maximizes similarity within and
    difference without (meta-contrast). Group polarization is explained
    by an implicit out-group at the other pole: "a group effectively
    becomes salient and operative only in contrast to a (relevant)
    out-group" (p. 379).
  - A meta-analysis of 20 studies found more prototypical leaders more
    influential (Barreto & Hogg 2017; p. 379).
  - Peripheral high identifiers may overconform to prove their worth.
  - High identifiers may reject norms they see as harming the group
    (Packer's normative conflict model), especially when secure in the
    group ("loyal deviance"; Masson & Fritsche 2019). "Interest flows from
    a focus on group identity as well as on the individual, especially for
    high identifiers" (p. 380).
  - Threat to personal control raised conformity to aggressive or
    nonviolent norms, whichever the group held, but identification did not
    moderate it, so it is read as normative influence (Leander et al.
    2020; p. 380).
  - Group-level threat raises self-stereotyping, perceived homogeneity and
    loyalty (pp. 380–381).
  - Reactance follows the dominant realm of self. People with independent
    self-construals resisted in-group sources more; people with
    interdependent ones resisted out-group sources more (Graupmann et al.
    2012; p. 381).
- **Online influence and SIDE** (pp. 382–384).
  - Cognitive SIDE effects concern the salience of identity; strategic
    ones concern accountability to in-group and out-group audiences.
  - Depersonalization is "not to be confused with the loss of self
    implied by deindividuation theories" (p. 382).
  - Spears et al. (1990): anonymous groups polarized toward the group
    norm when group identity was salient and away from it when personal
    identity was (p. 382).
  - When identifiable, people using text chat were swayed by argument
    strength. When anonymous, the group norm mattered more than argument
    quality (Lee 2008; p. 382).
  - Group anonymity raises salience, identification, cohesion, trust,
    knowledge sharing and "the tendency to perceive the collaborative
    group or dyad as an entity" (p. 383).
  - Visually cued categories reverse the pattern. Gender-based influence
    rose under webcam visibility while nationality-based influence rose
    under anonymity (Lea et al. 2007; p. 383).
- **Conclusion** (p. 384). Group identity is "not some product of its
  individual building blocks, but the primary foundation for much of our
  being and behavior". "It is when we strip the individuals away that we
  get to commune with our group nature, perhaps in its purest form."

## Concepts

- **referent informational influence**: influence from in-group members
  accepted as valid information because they share one's self-category.
- **normative influence**: conformity to others' expectations to gain
  approval or avoid rejection; instrumental compliance.
- **three realms**: personal self, group self and other/outward focus, as
  bases of influence (Figure 1).
- **descriptive and injunctive norms**: what others do, and what they
  approve or prescribe (Cialdini et al. 1990).
- **loyal deviance**: a secure, highly identified member departing from a
  group norm for the group's sake.
- **cognitive and strategic SIDE effects**: effects of anonymity on which
  identity is salient, and on what one can express given who can see and
  sanction it.
- **depersonalization**: seeing self and others as similar and
  interchangeable group members; explicitly not loss of self (p. 382).

## Connections

- **Turner et al. 1994 (NOTE-tmpf9mzh).** The 1994 paper gives influence
  one paragraph: "private category change" (p. 461). This review is the
  full development of that paragraph, with the evidence named. It adds the
  three realms, the norms-and-morality section and the online work.
- **Hornsey 2008 (NOTE-tmp3dav9).** Hornsey gives SIDE and Postmes &
  Spears 1998 one sentence each. This review gives the 1998 meta-analysis
  a paragraph and SIDE a section, and adds the emergency, helping and
  contagion findings that Hornsey does not mention.
- **Skorich (NOTE-tmplcvy8, THEORY-tmpdftev).** Two points bear on it.
  The polarization account posits an implicit out-group at the other end
  of the scale (p. 379). And anonymity is reported to increase perceiving
  the group or dyad "as an entity" (p. 383).
- **Spears & Postmes 2015 (read alongside).** The review points there for
  "a more complete review" of SIDE (p. 382). The two overlap on SIDE; this
  one covers far more of influence.
- **Identity fusion (NOTE-tmp3g9n4).** The review does not mention
  fusion. Its remark that internalized group identities become
  "self-perceived as personal" (p. 377) describes something close to it.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** The review bears on it in four
  ways. These are inferences; Spears does not discuss selflessness.
  - It applies the thesis's form to belief. Being influenced by the group
    is "partly self-influence" (p. 373). The thesis says that acting for
    the group is acting for a self. The review says that being persuaded
    by the group is being persuaded by a self.
  - It names a realm the thesis has to place: the "realm of the other",
    with no self-focus and "few concerns about creating or protecting the
    self" (p. 370). It concerns attending to information, not concern for
    another's good, and Spears says Turner would have denied that there is
    any "pure information without a social (or group) dimension" (p. 384). Still, it is a self-categorization theorist's own category
    for activity with no self in play. The thesis should say whether it
    covers motive at all.
  - It separates a group's interest from its norm. High identifiers can
    reject a norm that harms the group, and "interest flows from a focus
    on group identity" (p. 380). That supports reading the thesis as
    pursuit of a larger self's interest rather than as conformity to it.
  - It leaves "larger or changed?" open. On one side, internalized group
    identities can come to feel personal (p. 377), which is close to
    fusion. On the other, the review calls the group self "the primary
    foundation" and says stripping the individuals away gets us to "our
    group nature" (p. 384). That is a different self, not an extended
    one. Either way the crowd member is not selfless, so the thesis's
    denial of selflessness holds while its word "larger" is not
    supported.
- **Batson's objection (CLAIM-tmpybc4k).** Levine's bystanders help
  "primarily" when they share an identity with the victim (p. 375). Drury's
  emergency survivors sacrifice under a shared identity. Both fit the
  thesis. "Primarily" also leaves helping without shared identity, which
  is where Batson's case lives. Neither study is read here.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** The field's test for an
  identity process is moderation by identification (p. 380).
  Identification scales include caring about the group, so this test does
  not give the independent criterion the objection asks for. The salience
  manipulations reported in Spears & Postmes 2015 come closer.
- **Self-categorization theory (THEORY-tmpo85ke).**
  - Lea et al. (2007) fits the promote_when's shape. It holds the people
    fixed, varies the topic, and measures which category carries
    influence. But it comes from the author's group and is reported
    without data, so it does not promote the account. It is worth filing
    as a LIT.
  - The implicit out-group device bears on the THEORY's test against
    Skorich: "an experiment that removes the comparison group". If the
    theory can always posit a contrast category at the other end of a
    scale, removing the visible out-group does not remove comparison. The
    test needs a design that rules out an implicit contrast as well.
- **Group entity versus category (TERM-tmpko4rw).** Anonymity is reported
  to make people see the group or dyad as an entity (p. 383). On the
  term's reading, accentuating likeness yields interchangeability, not
  unity. If the report holds, depersonalization can yield perceived unity.
  This comes at second hand, and "entity" may have been measured as
  entitativity, which the term sets aside.
- **CASE-104 and QUESTION-119.** Prototypical members carry more
  influence, and anonymity strengthens local norms. Together they suggest
  that an online fanbase's norm can be set by its most prototypical
  voices without any procedure. That is a derivation, not a finding about
  fans.
- **A dated note is added to CLAIM-tmpjylq8.** Proposed text, to be
  combined with the note proposed from Spears & Postmes 2015:

  > Spears (2021) says the self-categorization account treats group
  > influence as "partly self-influence" (p. 373), and treats high
  > identifiers who defy a harmful group norm as serving the group's
  > interest (p. 380). Both fit the thesis read as a group self's
  > interest, not as conformity. But the review also names a "realm of
  > the other" in which no self is in play (p. 370), and it treats the
  > group self as foundational rather than as an extension of the
  > individual (p. 384). So the thesis should say whether it is a claim
  > about motives only, and whether its larger self is enlarged or
  > replaced.

- **No THEORY filed.** The influence account would be a separate THEORY;
  the record has read none of its primary studies.

## Limitations

- **A review by an insider.** Spears is an author of the SIDE model and of
  several of the studies cited. The balance of evidence is his.
- **No data.** One count (20 studies in Barreto & Hogg's meta-analysis)
  and no effect sizes. Postmes & Spears 1998 is described without its
  number of studies or effect sizes.
- **The primary fit and frame studies are absent.** Oakes, Turner &
  Haslam 1991 and Haslam et al. 1992 are not cited. Fit is illustrated
  only with Lea et al. 2007.
- **No word on replication.** None of the cited effects is checked
  against the replication debates.
- **Typos.**
  - "This is not to deny the absence of motivation" (p. 370) stands for
    its presence.
  - References: "Tavsitock" (Cartwright & Zander); "identifcaion" and
    "three process" (Kelman 1958); "ougroups" (Noel et al. 1995);
    "Jannssen" (Van Baaren et al. 2009); "Baumeister RB" for RF.
  - Mackie et al. 1990 is given as volume 57 of JPSP, which is the 1989
    volume.
  - Van Zomeren et al. 2008 is given as *Psychol. Bull.* volume 34; the
    1998 meta-analysis is in volume 123, so 2008 cannot be 34.
  - Spears & Postmes 2015 is listed in "The Handbook of Psychology of
    Communication"; the book is *The Handbook of the Psychology of
    Communication Technology*.

## Open questions

- Is there motivated action in the "realm of the other", with no self
  in play? If so, it is the case CLAIM-tmpybc4k needs. If the realm covers
  only information processing, the thesis is untouched.
- Does referent informational influence survive preregistered tests? A
  replication of Abrams et al. 1990 or of the in-group Asch effect would
  answer it for the record.
