# THEORY-tmp6qwmt: In the Social Sciences Replication Project's pre-registered replication of Study 1 of Shah, Mullainathan and Shafir (2012), 619 online players given fewer guesses in a Wheel of Fortune word game did no worse than players given more on a later attention task (pooled p = 0.71, both stages' estimates in the opposite direction); the project tested no other study of the paper, and the original authors, whose own replications reproduced its over-borrowing result, call Study 1 a false positive

## Source

Camerer et al. (2018), LIT-tmpess4c, its supplement, the SSRP replication
report on Shah et al. and the original authors' comment; read in
NOTE-tmppfiub. The original is LIT-tmp6ahq0 (unread).

## What was actually shown

- **Original** (as the report restates it): 45.12 poor against 52.93 rich
  on the Dots-Mixed task, F(1, 54) = 4.16, p = 0.046, n = 56.
- **Stage 1**, n = 278, 90% power for 75% of the original effect: 46.94
  against 44.51, p = 0.15, r = −0.087.
- **Pooled**, n = 619, 90% power for 50%: 45.07 against 44.63, p = 0.71,
  r = −0.015; relative effect size −0.056. Same website code as the
  original.
- **The original authors**: their own replication, n = 997, also ran the
  other way (THEORY-tmplj3t9), and "Having now twice failed to replicate
  Study 1, we are convinced that the original finding was a
  false-positive."

## What this does not say

- That scarcity does not lead to over-borrowing or focus: those studies
  were not tested by the project.
- That scarcity never taxes later cognition: Mani et al. (2013), a
  different paper and design, was not tested here.
- Why the original result appeared: the authors suggest the rich did more
  work in the game, which the replication did not measure.
- Anything about the other 20 studies beyond the project's averages.
