# NOTE-025: Joint Publication 3-12, Cyberspace Operations

## Contribution

JP 3-12 gives authoritative joint doctrine for planning, executing and assessing cyberspace operations (CO) by US armed forces. It describes cyberspace as part of the information environment and dependent on the physical domains. It sets out the challenges of attribution, geography, technology exposure and reliance on private infrastructure and foreign supply chains. It covers authorities, roles and responsibilities and the command-and-control arrangements. This 2018 revision replaces the classified 2013 edition with an unclassified publication plus a classified appendix.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- Summary of Changes (p. iii): the publication moves from classified to unclassified with a classified Appendix A. It reflects USCYBERCOM's status as a functional combatant command, adds the Cyber Mission Force, and adds "information" as a joint function.
- Executive Summary (pp. vii-viii): CO is defined as using cyberspace capabilities where the primary purpose is to achieve objectives in or through cyberspace. The summary sets out the three-layer model of cyberspace and notes that access "is not guaranteed" because of legal, policy and operational limits.
- Executive Summary (pp. ix-x): USCYBERCOM has three lines of operation: secure, operate and defend the DODIN; defend the nation; and support combatant commanders. The summary names attribution as crucial before any action beyond authorized self-defense, and says "there is no stateless maneuver space" in cyberspace.
- Executive Summary (pp. x-xii): it defines the three missions. OCO projects power in and through foreign cyberspace. The summary maps CO onto the joint functions: cyberspace attack as "fires" with possible cascading physical effects, and maneuver in gray and red cyberspace as exploitation.
- Table of contents (pp. v-vi): Chapter III covers authorities, including a US Code figure (Title 10 and related), and Chapter IV covers planning and C2 for routine and crisis operations.

## Open questions

- It is a primary source for official US definitions (OCO, DCO, DODIN, cyber-persona) that later scholarship and policy use. Definitions should be quoted from here, not from secondary summaries.
- Doctrine may have been revised since 2018. A deeper reading should check for a later edition of JP 3-12 before citing this one as current.
- Chapter III on legal authorities, including the Title 10/Title 50 boundary, is the most analytically load-bearing part and was not read.
