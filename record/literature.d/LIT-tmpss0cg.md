# LIT-tmpss0cg: The bounds of cognition

Fred Adams, Ken Aizawa (2001), *Philosophical Psychology* 14(1), 43–64 —
DOI-10.1080/09515080120033571

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-02 at the owner's request, as the first of the critics of
Chalmers's extended-mind programme. [NOTE-tmpyudcv](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpyudcv.md) is the close reading of
the same day, and it placed the work: **Active**.

**How it bears on [LIT-097](LIT-097.md).** It is a direct reply, and it attacks the
step [NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md) found unargued: from causal coupling to parthood. Its §3.2
argues that "the mere causal coupling of some process with a broader
environment does not, in general, thereby, extend that process", using the
bimetallic strip in a thermostat and filtration in the kidney. It does not
call this a fallacy. The phrase "coupling-constitution fallacy", which
[NOTE-180](../notes.d/NOTE-180.md) attributes to these authors, is from their later work; Chalmers's
2019 chapter ([LIT-tmph59ls](LIT-tmph59ls.md)) cites it to their 2008 book. Unusually for a
critic, they accept [LIT-097](LIT-097.md)'s parity sentence ("true and obvious") and
place the dispute in what counts as cognitive.

**What the critics show and do not.** The paper shows that the 1998
argument needs a premise about what makes something cognitive, and that
[LIT-097](LIT-097.md) offers none ("they have suggested none"). It does not show that
its own two marks are correct. Both are assumed, and Clark's reply
([LIT-tmp92hsa](LIT-tmp92hsa.md)) presses exactly there: the authors themselves concede it
is "unclear to what extent each cognitive state ... must involve
non-derived content". Rupert ([LIT-tmplqv7e](LIT-tmplqv7e.md)) distances himself from the
derived-content argument as question-begging, and says his own argument,
developed independently, dovetails with the other half: that internal
processes have cognitively relevant properties external ones lack.

**On consciousness and [THEORY-tmp31lxe](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp31lxe.md).** The paper assumes "without
argument" that qualia and phenomenal consciousness are not essential to
cognitive processes. So it says nothing about the hard problem or
group consciousness. Its last move, though, is an argument about
natural kinds: there will be no science of human-plus-tool systems,
because they are "a motley collection". If sound, that cuts against
counting a nation as one cognitive system, which [LIT-159](LIT-159.md) needs. But it is
a speculation ("Slim to none, we speculate") and is not applied to groups.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice. Its §2.2 uses a
look-up table that passes the Turing test and chess programs' search as
cases of behaviour without cognition; those are illustrations, not claims
about ML.
