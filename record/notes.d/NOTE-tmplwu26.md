# NOTE-tmplwu26: Durkheim — Qu'est-ce qu'un fait social ?

## Contribution

The chapter gives sociology an object of its own. Before it, "social" was used
for nearly anything that happens in a society with some generality; on that use,
Durkheim says, eating and sleeping are social and sociology dissolves into
biology and psychology (p. 5). The chapter replaces generality with two marks,
exteriority and constraint, and argues that the facts that carry them form a
distinct order whose substrate is the group. It closes with the formula that
later work quotes (p. 19).

## Key insight

What makes a way of acting social is not that many people do it but that it
exists outside any one of them and pushes back. A rule of law, a currency or a
language "fonctionnent indépendamment des usages que j'en fais" (p. 6). Its
force is invisible to the person who conforms and shows itself to the person
who resists, as a sanction, as ridicule, or as plain failure (p. 7). Generality
is the consequence, not the cause: a social fact is in each individual "parce
qu'il est dans le tout" (p. 15).

## Assumptions

- **Three exhaustive orders of fact.** Organic phenomena, psychic phenomena
  ("qui n'ont d'existence que dans la conscience individuelle et par elle") and
  social phenomena (p. 8). The argument that social facts are "une espèce
  nouvelle" needs the first two to exhaust the non-social, and this is not
  argued.
- **Constraint can be present without being felt.** "La preuve, c'est qu'elle
  s'affirme dès que je tente de résister" (p. 7), and it persists
  "quoique inconsciente" in the compliant case (p. 9). The inference from
  "shows itself under resistance" to "is there all along" is the chapter's
  main unargued step.
- **Education as a window on history.** What education does to a child shows
  "comme en raccourci" how the social being was formed historically (p. 11).
  Asserted.
- **Spencer's non-coercive pedagogy is set aside** because "no known people"
  has practised it (p. 11): a fact about practice, used against a proposal.
- **Statistical rates measure collective states.** Dividing annual totals by
  the population at risk lets individual circumstances "se neutraliser
  mutuellement", so the rate expresses the collective current (pp. 13–14). It
  assumes the individual factors are independent of one another and of the
  current.

## Key results

All are definitions with illustrations. There is no data beyond the mention of
rates.

- **Exteriority (pp. 5–6).** Duties of brother, spouse and citizen are "définis,
  en dehors de moi et de mes actes, dans le droit et dans les mœurs"; beliefs
  and practices of a religion were found "toutes faites"; sign systems, money,
  credit instruments and professional practices work independently of use.
  Hence "des manières d'agir, de penser et de sentir" that "existent en dehors
  des consciences individuelles".
- **Constraint, graded (pp. 6–8).** Law reacts by preventing, annulling or
  punishing; moral maxims by public censure; conventions of dress by ridicule
  and distance; language, legal tender and industrial technique by making
  deviation fail ("je me ruinerai à coup sûr"). Even a successful innovator
  meets resistance.
- **A new species, with society as substrate (p. 8).** Social facts consist
  "en représentations et en actions", so they are not organic; they are not in
  any individual consciousness, so they are not psychic; "n'ayant pas
  l'individu pour substrat", their substrate is the political society or one of
  its partial groups (churches, schools, professional bodies). Durkheim
  defends the word "contrainte" against "un individualisme absolu" (p. 8).
- **Social currents (pp. 9–10).** Unorganised facts have the same objectivity:
  enthusiasm or indignation in an assembly comes to each "du dehors". Evidence
  offered: alone afterwards, the feelings seem foreign; crowds of harmless
  individuals can be carried into atrocities. Lasting currents of opinion are
  "identiquement" the same.
- **Education confirms it (pp. 10–11).** Upbringing is "un effort continu pour
  imposer à l'enfant des manières de voir, de sentir et d'agir"; the constraint
  stops being felt because it produces habits that "ne la remplacent que parce
  qu'elles en dérivent". Parents and teachers are intermediaries of "la
  pression même du milieu social".
- **Not generality (pp. 11–14).** A thought found in every consciousness is not
  thereby social. Collective habits "se précipitent" into formulas (legal and
  moral rules, proverbs, creeds, codes of taste) that can exist "sans être
  actuellement appliquées" (pp. 12–13). Where no formula exists, statistics
  dissociates the current from its cases (pp. 13–14). Individual manifestations
  are "socio-psychiques" (p. 14), of interest to sociology but not its matter.
- **General because collective (pp. 14–15).** "S'il est général, c'est parce
  qu'il est collectif (c'est-à-dire plus ou moins obligatoire), bien loin qu'il
  soit collectif parce qu'il est général." A collective sentiment is "une
  résultante de la vie commune, un produit des actions et des réactions qui
  s'engagent entre les consciences individuelles". "Chacun est entraîné par
  tous."
- **Two criteria, one fact (pp. 15–16).** A social fact is recognised by
  external coercive power, itself recognised by a sanction or by resistance to
  individual violation; or by diffusion within the group plus existence
  independent of its individual forms. The second is easier where constraint is
  indirect (an economic organisation), and is "une autre forme de la première",
  since a way of conduct external to individuals can spread only "en
  s'imposant".
- **Against Tarde (n. 3).** Imitation is a consequence of a fact's social
  character, not its cause or definition; an individual state that spreads
  "ne laisse pas pour cela d'être individuel".
- **Morphology is consolidated physiology (pp. 17–19).** Population
  distribution, roads, housing types and political divisions also constrain;
  political divisions are "morales" and studied through public law. But they
  are "des manières de faire consolidées": a road is the bed the current of
  exchange has dug for itself. Between structure and free currents there is
  "toute une gamme de nuances", differences only "dans le degré de
  consolidation". Note 4 extends this, speculatively, to organisms.
- **The formula (p. 19).** "Est fait social toute manière de faire, fixée ou
  non, susceptible d'exercer sur l'individu une contrainte extérieure ; ou bien
  encore, qui est générale dans l'étendue d'une société donnée tout en ayant
  une existence propre, indépendante de ses manifestations individuelles."
  It is identical in the 1894 text.

## Concepts

- **Fait social** — a way of doing, fixed or not, capable of external constraint
  on the individual; or general in a society and independent of its individual
  manifestations (p. 19).
- **Contrainte extérieure** — a pressure from outside the individual, recognised
  by a determinate sanction or by the resistance the fact opposes to individual
  violation (p. 15).
- **Courants sociaux** — social facts without crystallised form (enthusiasm in
  an assembly, movements of opinion) (p. 9).
- **Socio-psychique** — the individual manifestation of a social fact, mixed
  with the individual's organic-psychic make-up (p. 14).
- **Manières d'être / manières de faire** — morphological facts (substrate,
  structure) against physiological ones (practice), the first being the second
  consolidated (pp. 17–19).
- **Âme collective** — used once (p. 14), for what a statistical rate
  expresses. Not in the 1894 text.

## Connections

- **Tomasello ([LIT-125](../literature.d/LIT-125.md), [NOTE-185](NOTE-185.md)).** [NOTE-185](NOTE-185.md) records Tomasello's definition of
  the sense of obligation as "the internalized social/rational pressure from a
  joint or collective self-regulating agent 'we'". Durkheim's pressure is also
  internalised, through education into habit, but it comes from a "milieu
  social" the individual did not author, and he says the immense majority of
  social facts reach us that way (p. 15). Tomasello's is pressure from a "we"
  one helped make. Neither cites the other; the contrast is this record's.
- **Schwitzgebel, List and [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md) ([LIT-159](../literature.d/LIT-159.md), [LIT-401](../literature.d/LIT-401.md)).** Durkheim's
  collective sentiment as "une résultante" of interactions among individual
  consciousnesses is a composition claim, and "l'âme collective" sounds like a
  group mind. The chapter does not say the group is conscious or an agent; it
  says sociology has facts of its own. [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)'s dispute, whether

## Limitations

- The chapter is a definition chapter. Its later chapters (rules of
  observation, of the normal and pathological, of explanation) were not read.
- No evidence beyond examples and the mention of rates; the suicide study it
  points to (n. 2) is not part of it.
- The elimination in C3 and the hidden-constraint inference in C2 carry the
  weight and are asserted.
- "Constraint" is stretched across law, ridicule and market failure; whether
  these are one thing is not argued.

## Open questions

- Is "external constraint" one property or several (sanction, social pressure,
  coordination necessity)? Later accounts of convention would split the third
  off.
- What is the relation between the social fact's "substrate" (the group) and
  the individuals in it? The chapter gives the slogan (in each part because in
  the whole) and no account.

## Corrections

- **Date and place of first appearance.** The request cited the 1895 book. The
  chapter first appeared in the *Revue philosophique* 37 (January–June 1894),
  in the instalment at pp. 465–498, chapter I at pp. 466–475.
- **The text changed between 1894 and the book.** In 1894 the generality
  paragraph reads "C'est si peu la répétition qui les constitue qu'ils existent
  en dehors des cas particuliers où ils se réalisent. Chaque fait social
  consiste soit dans une croyance, soit dans une tendance, soit dans une
  pratique qui est celle du groupe pris collectivement"; the book recasts it.
  The statistics passage in 1894 ends at "ne contribuent pas à le
  déterminer", without the book's "Ce qu'il exprime, c'est un certain état de
  l'âme collective" and the sentence after it. Only these passages were
  collated.
- **Text used.** The UQAC edition named in the request (PUF, 16th edition,
  1967) was not reachable from its own site. The Alcan 1919 printing read here
  is the book text.
