# THEORY-1472: Michaels, Nigmatulina, Rauch, Regan, Baruah and Dahlstrand find that greenfield neighbourhoods given formal plots, roads and water mains under Tanzania's 1970s–80s Sites and Services projects, on which residents built their own houses, had by the 2010s larger, more regular and better-connected housing and better-preserved roads and water mains than adjacent land unbuilt at the start, while squatter areas given similar infrastructure spending per square metre were no better than comparison areas and their infrastructure deteriorated; they attribute the difference to a feedback in which enough owners able to invest keep up the infrastructure that draws their investment

## Source

Michaels, Nigmatulina, Rauch, Regan, Baruah & Dahlstrand (2021),
[LIT-2168](../literature.d/LIT-2168.md); read in [NOTE-1651](../notes.d/NOTE-1651.md). Page numbers are the accepted
manuscript's (LSE Research Online, dated 14 November 2020).

## What was actually shown

- **The support** (§2, pp. 6–9): 12 de novo areas in seven cities were
  given "formal plots, roads, and water mains" (p. 7) in 1975–84, and
  residents built; cost recovery largely failed.
- **Housing, de novo against adjacent land unbuilt at the start**
  (Tables 1–2, p. 17): footprints "roughly 12 percent larger" across seven
  cities from 2016 imagery; in the three surveyed cities, footprints "about
  50 percent (or 0.41 log points) larger", buildings "about 23 percentage
  points (or 48 percent) more likely to be connected to electricity", log
  hedonic value +0.446. A third of the quality gap is owner sorting
  (p. 19).
- **Infrastructure** (Table 4, p. 20): roads and water-mains connections
  higher in de novo areas, "showing that de novo investments translated
  into better infrastructure in the long run".
- **Upgrading** (Tables A8–A11, pp. 20–21): squatter areas with similar
  spending per square metre are no better than nearby or non-upgraded
  areas, and on survey measures worse; the authors explain this by
  infrastructure that "deteriorated more than that of de novo areas"
  (p. 20). Descriptive only, since those areas were built before treatment.
- **The attribution** (p. 3; §4): "infrastructure investment crowds in
  private investment in housing quality, which in turn preserves
  infrastructure quality". In the model, high-quality infrastructure decays
  "unless the fraction of high quality housing is larger than a constant φ"
  (p. 22), and sunk low-quality houses are rebuilt only when destroyed.

What could have come out otherwise: the discontinuity at the boundary could
have been null, and the roads and mains could have decayed in de novo areas
as in upgrading ones.

## What this does not say

- That the threshold exists: φ is assumed in the model, not estimated, and
  no bistability is shown.
- That upgrading caused worse outcomes: that comparison is descriptive.
- That the advantage grew, held or faded: there is one cross-section and no
  time path.
- That the result would scale: the authors warn that "poor and credit
  constrained residents may be unable to make full use of them" (p. 27) if
  de novo investments were widened.
- That value was created rather than drawn from elsewhere (p. 15), or that
  the land-value gains are measured rather than model-derived.
