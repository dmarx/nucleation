# LIT-tmpt6rok: Psychological Flexibility as a Fundamental Aspect of Health

Todd B. Kashdan and Jonathan Rottenberg (2010), *Clinical Psychology Review*
30(7):865–878 (first printed in 30(4):467–480) — DOI-10.1016/j.cpr.2010.03.001

## Key takeaways

- **Flexibility over strategy.** No regulation strategy is good everywhere.
  Students in New York after 11 September 2001 who could both express and
  suppress emotion on instruction adjusted better over two years than those
  good at either alone (Bonanno et al. 2004). Variable copers did better
  than consistent ones (Cheng 2001). Anger helps in some negotiations and
  hurts in others.
- **Inflexibility runs through psychopathology.** Depression shows
  rumination, rigid attributions, flat emotional reactivity, and lost
  physiological variability, which Rottenberg calls "emotion context
  insensitivity". Anxiety shows experiential avoidance and stereotyped
  behaviour. Whether inflexibility causes disorder or follows it is open.
- **What it rests on.** Executive functioning, default mental states
  (habits, heuristics, change blindness) and personality (neuroticism,
  positive affect, openness, self-control) make flexibility more or less
  likely. Measuring it needs repeated, in-situation assessment, not single
  self-reports.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, as the review that generalizes
psychological flexibility beyond ACT. The `psychopathology-and-treatment`
blurb names the construct. No anthology topic holds it, and it carries no
instruction for machine-learning practice.

Its place in the line that starts with experiential avoidance:

- **The 1996 paper ([LIT-tmpsjur3](LIT-tmpsjur3.md))** is cited for the inevitability of
  negative emotion and for the interpersonal costs of poor executive
  function. The review keeps experiential avoidance, defined after Hayes,
  Strosahl and Wilson (1999), as the mark of inflexibility in the anxiety
  disorders.
- **The ACT model ([LIT-tmpxkxq0](LIT-tmpxkxq0.md))** is presented as "extends the concept of
  ego-resilience", with its meta-analysis cited as 32 studies and a mean
  correlation of .42. The review also criticises the AAQ, ACT's global
  self-report, as the kind of static measure a dynamic construct cannot be
  captured by.

No `extends` relation is declared. The review draws on ACT as one of five
literatures (emotion regulation, mindfulness and acceptance, social and
personality psychology, neuropsychology, ego-resiliency). It could stand
without the 2006 paper, so the relation is stated here in prose instead.

It also bears on two works another agent filed in parallel. It argues
against fixed rankings of regulation strategies, "the conventional wisdom
in psychology that some regulatory strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal)
are always better than others (e.g., suppression)". It cites Gross and John
(2003) for that wisdom, not Gross's 1998 review ([LIT-tmptr6ik](LIT-tmptr6ik.md)). As its
filing describes it, that review's definition already makes no assumption
that regulation is good or bad, so the target here is the later
reappraisal-beats-suppression findings. Its open
question about causal direction is the same one Borsboom's network theory
([LIT-tmpmi1g3](LIT-tmpmi1g3.md)) answers differently, by making disorder a self-sustaining
state of the symptoms themselves.
