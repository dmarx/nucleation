# NOTE-tmpswm39: Episteme Symposium on Group Agency: Replies to Gaus, Cariani, Sylvan, and Briggs

## Contribution

The authors' own clarifications of *Group Agency* ([LIT-391](../literature.d/LIT-391.md)): which of the
book's claims are modest possibility claims, which conditions can be
relaxed, and what the book holds about the responsibility and rights of
group agents. For this record it is the nearest lawful view of the book's
Parts II and III.

## Key insight

A group's beliefs need its members' acceptance, but they cannot be read
off it. Knowing which members' acceptance counts, and that the structure
lets the putative belief play the role of belief, already requires the
group-level view, so "it is the group belief that explains the role-based
member acceptance, not the role-based member acceptance that explains the
group belief" (p. 13).

## Assumptions

- Functionalism about agency and attitudes, including group emotions if
  there are any (p. 5).
- An acceptable account of joint intention is presupposed, not supplied
  (p. 13).
- Normative individualism, adopted in the book "as just a plausible
  assumption" (p. 17).
- The existence of group agents: the book's argument (GA 75, quoted p. 14)
  omits the empirical premise that collections do form and act on robustly
  rational bodies of attitude, which it "mostly take[s] for granted".

## Key results

- **Gaus** (pp. 1–5). The premise-based procedure is "merely a particularly
  simple – but also rather inflexible – instance" (p. 1). Ecological
  rationality is classical rationality relative to environment (p. 2).
  Robust group rationality may be weakened: domain restrictions where
  members' views are cohesive, incompleteness where tolerable (pp. 3–4).
  Proposition-wise ("independence") rules remain "extremely limited" even
  then (p. 4). Only a shared logic, not a shared structure of premises, is
  assumed (pp. 4–5). Group emotions need not be simple functions of
  members' emotions; some corporations "may exhibit certain behavioural
  patterns usually associated with psychopathy" (p. 5, citing Bakan).
- **Cariani** (pp. 5–10). Democratization, decomposition and
  decentralization are ideal types; chapter 4 gives possibility results
  (quoting GA 102–103). Units of epistemic comparison are whole group
  agents with a given member reliability profile. Reliability is reported
  as positive and negative pairs because an always-safe medical panel
  could score high on an average (p. 8). An expert panel may do best with
  a supermajority rule that leaves many propositions undecided (p. 8).
- **Sylvan** (pp. 10–14). Non-redundancy is a matter of degree, and the
  theory lies towards the non-redundant end (citing GA 77–78). Workable
  group agents "allow any proposition, however long endorsed, to be
  rejected on grounds of inconsistency" and assign subgroups to issues
  (pp. 11–12). Executive members must act on the group's propositions but
  "need not personally assent to it" (p. 12).
- **Briggs** (pp. 14–17). The book's verdict, quoted from GA 182: hold
  group agents "responsible in their own right", with "restricted rights
  as compared with individuals". The book "do[es] not pay much attention
  to the range of responsibilities" (p. 15). Its answer to the objection
  that members, not groups, have control relies on Jackson and Pettit's
  programme explanation; Briggs proposes List and Menzies'
  difference-making instead, which the authors say they were aware of and
  "didn't develop" (p. 15). Personhood is performative: a group agent can
  be "party to a system of accepted convention, such as a system of law"
  (GA 173, quoted p. 16). They are agnostic about hedonic group states
  (p. 16) and defend normative individualism tentatively: "individual
  agents are the producers of social arrangements, corporate agents the
  precipitates" (p. 17).

## Concepts

- **robust group rationality** — universal domain plus collective
  rationality (consistency, completeness); here its strength may be
  "adjusted from case to case" (p. 4).
- **ecological rationality** — rationality of a procedure in the
  environments to which it is adapted (p. 2).
- **non-redundant realism** — group attitudes are real, and no accessible
  recipe from individual facts makes the group-level posit dispensable
  (pp. 10–14).
- **role-based acceptance** — members treating a proposition as true for
  group purposes, by assenting in the group's name or acting on it in an
  executive role (p. 12).
- **normative individualism** — "something is good only if it is good for
  individual human or, more generally, sentient beings" (GA 182, quoted
  p. 16).

## Bearing on the record

It is the source of no account here; it adds to two.

- **[THEORY-127](../theory.d/THEORY-127.md).** The authors say that weakening universal domain or
  completeness complicates the formal arguments but that rules satisfying
  proposition-wise supervenience remain "extremely limited" and exclude
  "some of the most natural methods of aggregation" (p. 4). That supports
  the claim beyond the strict conditions its title states, but on the
  authors' word, not by proof. The account's `promote_when` is unchanged.
  The replies also confirm that the premise-based procedure is one
  illustration, so [THEORY-127](../theory.d/THEORY-127.md) should not be read as recommending it.
- **[THEORY-tmpe1ql0](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpe1ql0.md).** These replies give the book's own verdict, quoted
  from GA 182: group agents are fit to be held responsible "in their own
  right". They add three things the account does not yet say. Fitness is
  assigned "only in the domain where it is equipped to form and act on
  attitudes" (p. 16). The book does not address the range of a group's
  responsibilities (p. 15). And the control objection has two answers in
  the literature, programme explanation in the book and List and Menzies'
  realization-insensitivity in Briggs's proposal ([THEORY-tmp3fc4z](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp3fc4z.md)). The
  concession that existence is taken for granted, and the call for
  empirical study of "the actual dynamics of group reasoning" (p. 15,
  quoting Briggs), match that account's note that its empirical premise
  is asserted, not shown.

For the owner's essay:

- **§5.3.** The quoted claim that the agential perspective lets us "see
  higher-level regularities" and respond "in abstraction from much of what
  happens at the lower level" (GA 6, quoted p. 10) is the authors' version
  of what an agentive explanation adds.
- **§7.1.** Executive members may act on a group belief they do not hold
  (p. 12), so corporate endorsement is not member endorsement.
- **§8.3.** Workable group agents can reject "any proposition, however long
  endorsed" on grounds of inconsistency (p. 12), a structural condition for
  recursive revision.
- **§8, on psychopathy (the record's link).** The authors grant that some
  corporations show patterns "usually associated with psychopathy" (p. 5).
  Wolf's lecture ([THEORY-tmpq6s8b](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpq6s8b.md)) separates accountability from fitness
  for reactive attitudes in exactly the psychopath's case. Neither text
  mentions the other.

## Limitations

Replies, not a treatise: arguments are compressed and often point to the
book. The commentaries are known only through the replies. The book's
pages are quoted, not read. Several claims rest on the aggregation
literature, cited and not reproduced.

## Open questions

What range of responsibilities a group agent should bear, which the
authors say the book leaves open. Whether the "extremely limited"
possibilities under relaxed conditions include any rule a real organisation
uses.
