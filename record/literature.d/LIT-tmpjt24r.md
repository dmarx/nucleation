# LIT-tmpjt24r: Reply to Professor Stich

Daniel C. Dennett (1980), "Reply to Professor Stich", *Philosophical
Books* 21(2), April 1980, 73–76, answering Stephen P. Stich, "Headaches"
(a critical notice of *Brainstorms*), same issue, 65–73. Read from
Dennett's final draft in the Tufts Digital Library.

## Key takeaways

- **The intentional stance protects personhood, with one concession.**
  Dennett agrees with Stich's account of its strategic role: grounding
  belief in predictability by a stance, not in "machine architecture",
  makes our status as believers and moral agents "well-nigh
  invulnerable" to scientific disconfirmation. The concession is that the
  intentional system is "an unrealizable idealization", and so is the
  concept of a person built on it.
- **Evolution is a minimal ground.** He grants malignant beliefs and
  desires, "suicidal rationality" and patterns remote from biological
  need. He holds only that such growths can be understood as belief and
  desire structures on the assumption that they extend control systems
  selected because they are benign in the long run.
- **Irrationality has no stable description.** Following Quine, evidence
  for ascribing the belief that p is the best evidence against ascribing
  a contradictory belief that q. Psychologists of reasoning who ascribe
  contradictory beliefs uncautiously will be confused, "not 'simply', but
  subtly"; in ordinary life we tolerate the slack ("he sort of believes
  that p").
- **Pain: three options.** (A) Bite the bullet: there is no pain. (B)
  Reform the concept, with counter-intuitive results (anaesthetics
  gratuitous for fish; people in pain despite sincere denials). (C) Side
  with common sense: pain is like voices, holes and haircuts, which no
  one can say in a mature science's terms either. No option is entirely
  satisfactory; the choice is "in some measure a matter of policy".
- **The resolution is to stay put.** He ends "claiming that there are no
  such things as pains, although of course people do feel pain, and
  leaving it at that".

**On [LIT-393](LIT-393.md) ("Artificial Intelligence as Philosophy and as
Psychology").** [LIT-393](LIT-393.md) is filed unread, and its account of the
homunculus argument comes entirely from this source: Stich's notice,
which quotes the essay with page numbers, and this reply's opening, "I
have no quarrel with Stich's summary of my views." This reading
**confirms** that account and changes nothing material in it.

- Every quotation [LIT-393](LIT-393.md) gives from Stich is in Stich's text as given,
  with the same page numbers (123, 124): "homunculus talk is ubiquitous
  in AI", the "team or committee of relatively ignorant, narrow-minded,
  blind homunculi", "nesting of boxes within boxes", "armies of such
  idiots", and "duplicate entire the talents they are rung in to
  explain". The opening of the reply is quoted correctly.
- Two refinements, neither a correction of substance:
  - Stich says AI theorists organise "armies of such idiots" to
    *simulate* intelligent behaviour. [LIT-393](LIT-393.md)'s takeaways paraphrase this
    as "to produce" it.
  - Dennett's acceptance is of the summary as a whole. The reply never
    returns to homunculi or to the AI essay; it discusses only
    intentional systems and pain. So the acceptance is a blanket one,
    which [LIT-393](LIT-393.md)'s wording ("which Dennett accepted") does not
    misstate, but which is weaker than a specific endorsement.
- Stich adds one gloss [LIT-393](LIT-393.md) does not record: the lesson of AI is that
  homunculus psychology is legitimate so long as there is a reasonable
  expectation of reaching homunculi so stupid that what they do "could be
  done (not by a machine but) by a nerve" (p. 67). That is Stich's
  wording, not a quotation from Dennett.
- The full sentence around "armies of such idiots" is not in this text
  either: Stich quotes only the fragment.
- Pagination: [LIT-393](LIT-393.md) gives the reply as pp. 73–76, which is right.
  Stich's notice runs pp. 65–73.

**Neighbours.** It continues "Intentional Systems" ([LIT-440](LIT-440.md), read in
[NOTE-350](../notes.d/NOTE-350.md)): the 1971 paper's rationality assumption is the norm Stich
attacks here, and the reply concedes in so many words that the norm is
an unrealisable idealisation. "True Believers" ([LIT-398](LIT-398.md), [NOTE-353](../notes.d/NOTE-353.md)) is
the later defence of the same strategy. The reply names "Three Kinds of
Intentional Psychology" as forthcoming; it was later collected in *The
Intentional Stance* ([LIT-430](LIT-430.md), unread). On pain, it is a forerunner of the
illusionist thread: "Quining Qualia" ([LIT-433](LIT-433.md), [NOTE-357](../notes.d/NOTE-357.md)) attacks the
concept of qualia as "Why You Can't Make a Computer that Feels Pain"
attacked the concept of pain, and Frankish's "Illusionism as a Theory of
Consciousness" ([LIT-tmpl6if3](LIT-tmpl6if3.md), [NOTE-tmprtlbn](../notes.d/NOTE-tmprtlbn.md)) later separates the
experiences people have from the phenomenal properties they are said to
have, as this reply separates feeling pain from there being pains.
Taylor's reconstruction of Dennett ([LIT-tmpoxm1b](LIT-tmpoxm1b.md), [NOTE-tmpj7u7y](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpj7u7y.md)) answers
Block's charge of self-contradiction with real patterns; Stich's is the
same charge eleven years earlier, and the reply's option (C) is the
"haircuts" comparison Taylor's key quotation from *Consciousness
Explained* repeats.

**On the society-of-mind bridge ([THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)).** The reply does not touch
composition. Its one bearing is on [THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md)'s point that agency is
cheap to agree on: the reply makes belief, and with it agency, depend on
an idealised rationality that no system fully meets, which is why it
transfers so easily to any system whose stance pays.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice, and it does not
belong in the anthology.
