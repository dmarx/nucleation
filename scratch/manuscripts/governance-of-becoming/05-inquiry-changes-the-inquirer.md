# Article 5. Inquiry Changes the Inquirer

## Costed epistemic frontiers, stored specification, and adaptive readiness

**Abstract.** What an organization can find out depends on more than what it can answer once someone hands it a question. This article argues that inquiry is a form of specification that can revise what would count as its own answer, and that formulating a question is itself a costed realization. Two things follow. The questions an inquirer can later ask, and what asking them costs, are a product of what its earlier inquiry stored, which cheapens some questions and hardens others. And what an organization can find out is a property of asking, answering and the arrangement that couples them, not of either capacity alone. The formal centre is a constructive result: at one budget, the questions an agent can formulate and the answers it can reach once a question is supplied do not fix which question–answer pairs it can reach together; across all budgets they do, under a declared rule for composing the two costs, except where the way a question is posed changes what answering it costs. On that ground the article treats authorization and legibility as costs on the same budget as asking and answering, and, separately, as conditions that bar a transition without pricing it. It reads executed models in which governance changes whether, and how much, an organization goes on to inquire, and in which organizations with the same output differ in their readiness for a contingency. It sets out overconsolidation, a proposed generalization of overfitting, against the machine-learning literature that qualifies it, and the conditions an organization's readiness for epistemic demands would have to meet. The title holds in two precise senses and not in a third: inquiry changes which questions the inquirer can ask and at what cost, and it can take its own terms as its object; that inquiry is thereby a way the inquirer learns is not shown.

## 5.1 Introduction

An organization that cannot answer a question may be short of the means of answering. It may also be unable to ask it: the concepts are missing, the time to make the question determinate is spent elsewhere, or the question has to be justified to someone before anyone may pursue it, and the justification costs more than the window allows. The second failure leaves less trace than the first: "a question nobody formulates leaves no recorded failure to answer it" (CLAIM-1003).

This article asks what makes the second kind of capacity, the capacity to inquire, and how an organization's arrangements shape it. It argues in four steps, each resting on the one before.

The first is an account of inquiry. An inquiry is a specification that can revise what an answer could be, and formulating one has a cost, paid from the same resources as realizing anything else. A finding that stabilizes is stored inquiry: it makes some later questions cheaper and others harder (§§5.2–5.3). Set beside an account of learning in which a learner's present capability, its capacity to acquire, and its capacity to inquire are three frontiers that need not move together (§5.4), this fixes the senses in which inquiry changes the inquirer (§5.5).

The second is a result about asking and answering together. The pairs of questions and answers an organization can reach jointly are not fixed by what it can ask and what it can answer taken at one budget. What fixes them, given the two capacities, is the arrangement: the rule by which their costs compose, the schedule, and what posing a question does to the cost of answering it (§5.6). A related result shows that organizations giving the same answers can differ in what answering does to them, and says how such a difference can be measured (§5.7).

The third carries the result into governance. Permission to inquire is a further query on the same budget, and its cost can be placed so as to steer which questions are pursued; it is also a condition that bars a transition, and the two readings have not been joined (§5.8). What one party can reach can shrink while what the parties reach together grows, and an obligation can bar a party's exit without pricing it (§5.9). In executed models, governance that penalizes what inquiry reveals teaches an institution to stop inquiring (§5.10).

The fourth turns to the long run. A learner can become better at its present task while becoming costlier to teach, a pattern proposed as a generalization of overfitting and not yet measured in any learning system (§5.11); and an organization's readiness for demands it cannot yet name would have to be maintained, at a cost, whether or not it is used (§5.12).

§5.13 sets out what the argument does not establish and answers the strongest objection to it, that what is here attributed to governance is resource allocation under another name. §5.14 concludes.

**A note on method.** Each step cites in parentheses the entry of the Nucleation record that holds it; the companion apparatus gives each entry's standing and history, and the record's review of this draft. The text says where a step's firmness matters: a result shown by construction, a model with stipulated parameters, a proposal not adopted, a question open. The formal results are constructions with stipulated costs, and the executed studies are simulations; none measures an institution. The argument was developed with AI assistants, which also maintain the record that holds it, and for that reason the record is not offered as evidence for its own account (§5.13.3).

## 5.2 Inquiry as specification

Begin with what an inquiry is. An inquiry can be written as six components: the language it is posed in, Σ; the answer space A, which says which differences would count as an answer; the interventions 𝓘 and observations 𝓞 that make those differences accessible; the criteria 𝓚 that rank candidate answers; and the resources 𝓑 it runs on. An inquiry "is reflexive when any of these becomes what it is pursuing" (TERM-095). The tuple is a construct of the argument, not a standard definition, and a reader who rejects the thesis below can still use it.

The thesis is that inquiry is a form of specification. "A question fixes which differences would count as an answer, an inquiry adds the operations that make those differences accessible, and what it finds can stabilize into a specification that constrains later inquiry; so inquiry stands to its findings as a specification to its realizations, with one difference, that an inquiry can revise what an answer could be" (CLAIM-470). The difference is the line between refining within a framework and revising it: a procedure that splits states ever more finely under a property it never questions refines without revising. The thesis is interpretive, and it is narrower than it may sound. It "does not say that inquiry makes what it investigates" (CLAIM-470), only that the specification through which a thing is investigated can be historically constituted.

Two cautions keep the account from proving too much. First, the achievements it distinguishes, formulating a question, pursuing it, answering it, "form a partial order, not a nested sequence" (CLAIM-474). Inquiry, which measures and revises models and apparatuses under resource bounds, can occur without organizational autonomy, and nothing below assumes that formulating, pursuing and answering come in one order. Second, an inquiry that can revise its own criteria does not thereby govern itself: an apparatus that revises its comparison rules by consistency alone converges to agreement, not knowledge (CLAIM-474). What would make a system's revision of its own inquiry its own governance, rather than a reconfiguration someone else directs, is an open question (QUESTION-388), and §5.13 returns to it.

## 5.3 The cost of asking, and what stored specification does

### 5.3.1 Formulating is a costed realization

The second thesis is that asking costs. "Specifying is itself a resource-bounded realization: formulating a question takes time, concepts and computation as realizing a configuration takes energy and matter, so the questions an agent can ask are a product of what it has already realized; a stabilized specification is past inquiry stored so that later questions become cheaper to ask, and it can make other questions harder" (CLAIM-475). In its plainest form: one cannot interrogate a question that takes more time than one has even to ask it. The symmetry between specifying and realizing is one of kind, not of measure.

The object of this thesis is the inquiry frontier: the questions an agent or organization can formulate, "making them determinate enough to pursue, with the concepts, records, tools, time and computation it actually has; distinct from the questions its language can express, from those it can pursue to a stated standard, and from those that have answers" (TERM-092). A question can be expressible and still off the frontier. The frontier is the first of the article's objects to be indexed by a budget, and the argument of §5.6 depends on that index.

### 5.3.2 Stored specification cheapens

The last clause of CLAIM-475 is two-sided, and both sides matter.

On one side, storage cheapens. Carried from specifications to constraints in general: "A stabilized constraint can be stored work: by replacing later work with cheaper operations it changes which activities are realizable within a fixed per-task budget, so amortization becomes capability expansion without any added degrees of freedom or resources" (CLAIM-761). The gain is conditional on the costs of constructing, keeping, retrieving, verifying and revising the constraint and on the distribution of later demands, and persistence alone is not amortization (CLAIM-761). A proposed governance reading extends this: "slow organizational modes arise partly as ways of amortizing inquiry and coordination costs" (CLAIM-1060), a step from amortization to slow modes that is itself proposed (ARG-238) and is narrowed to the economic sense of "slow" (CLAIM-755).

Three limits keep the cheapening honest. A fall in one party's cost "may be work transferred, not saved" (SYNTHESIS-106). Storing is not free: filing an argument "moves work across time", and it is "worth its cost only when expected reuse covers it" (CLAIM-303). And using what is stored has a cost of its own, on an objection not yet adopted: "certifying that stored answers cover a new question is NP-complete even for conjunctive queries", "so each filing raises the cost of later reuse" (CLAIM-314). So storage cheapens net of a cost of recognizing that what is stored applies, and the size of that cost is contested.

### 5.3.3 Stored specification hardens

On the other side, storage hardens. A contract can be read as "a materialized answer to a historically recurring question" that "can make unfamiliar questions more expensive" (a dated note on CLAIM-475, recorded as an instance, with the claim unchanged). Several earlier results and proposals are instances of hardening. A sufficient state "can stop being sufficient when a source is discredited, the model is revised or the decisions change" (CLAIM-300). Question drift is "a provisional answer becoming part of the question an inquiry is pursuing" (TERM-056). Anchors entrench, on a proposed objection (CLAIM-184). And refining a category changes what is noticed: "refining a category makes misfits in the refined region more noticeable rather than fewer" (CLAIM-794), a proposed empirical claim about a record's grammar, whose test would be a recoding of the census in §5.13.3.

The two sides are one mechanism. The same storage that makes a recurring inquiry cheap raises the cost of revising what it stored, and that is the cost a reflexive inquiry, one that takes its own answer space or criteria as its object (TERM-095), has to pay. Stored specification "cheapens some questions and hardens others" (SYNTHESIS-114).

## 5.4 A learner's three frontiers

The account of inquiry sits beside an account of learning. Learning, on a definition that is proposed and not adopted, is "an experience-dependent change in the system's retained organization that alters what it can later do, at what cost and with what reliability, judged against a counterfactual in which the experience did not occur" (TERM-131). On that definition, a proposed claim holds that a learner's acquired organization "fixes three resource-indexed frontiers that move separately": an operational frontier, "what it can reliably do now within an operating budget"; a developmental frontier, "what it can come to do reliably within a further acquisition budget, given a declared interface for what it may learn from"; and the inquiry frontier of §5.3.1, "what it can formulate and pursue". So "no scalar measure of competence or plasticity ranks learners or learning processes independently of context, though one can once a task distribution, resource costs, horizon and evaluative preferences are declared" (CLAIM-754). It would be defeated by "A showing that one frontier fixes another: that within a declared class of learners and tasks, equal operational frontiers imply equal developmental frontiers, or that the inquiry frontier is a function of the operational one" (CLAIM-754).

The separation is proposed, and this article does not rest a later step on it as a whole. It rests on three parts of it that stand more firmly.

The operational frontier is resource-conditional. A disposition "is resource-conditional": repeated sampling raises a frozen model's chance that some attempt succeeds "from p to 1 − (1 − p)^N with no change to its parameters", and "coverage is not delivery, which needs a selector that recognizes success" (CLAIM-697, a proposed result shown by derivation). What a learned system retains is relative to its readers, cues, interventions and budgets (SYNTHESIS-096). On the empirical side, reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards "can make a disposition the base model already had cheaper to realize … without enlarging what the base reaches at large budgets", which is "an empirical question per run" (CLAIM-695).

Equal present capability can leave unequal later capability. This is shown by construction, though for support from another party rather than for a learner's own training: "Two forms of support that give a party the same capabilities now can leave it different capabilities later … support that does the work shrinks what the party can reach unaided while support that covers only its shortfall does not" (CLAIM-675).

What sustains a capability is not what acquires it: "a capability can be cheap to keep and costly to acquire, so having enough to sustain it is not having enough to acquire it" (CLAIM-569). That separation of maintenance from acquisition is what a developmental frontier distinct from an operational one presupposes, and it is shown in general (§5.11.2).

## 5.5 Three senses of the title

The title, "inquiry changes the inquirer", can be read three ways, and they stand differently.

**First, inquiry changes which questions the inquirer can later ask, and at what cost.** This is CLAIM-475: the askable questions "are a product of what it has already realized", and stored specification moves their costs (§5.3). In this sense the title restates an existing thesis. A broader formulation, inquiry as "both product and mechanism of reflexive individuation", adds no claim to it; it generalizes existing claims and is an instance of CLAIM-475 (SYNTHESIS-tmp4tvbr).

**Second, inquiry can take its own language, answer space, criteria or resources as its object.** That is reflexive inquiry as defined (TERM-095), with the thesis that "an inquiry can revise what an answer could be" (CLAIM-470). Here too the title adds nothing to a definition and a thesis.

**Third, inquiry is a way the inquirer learns**: the change that stored inquiry makes would be a learning in the sense of TERM-131. This is not shown, and the reason is instructive. The "experience" in TERM-131 is mostly secured, since an inquiry includes interventions and observations (𝓘, 𝓞), and those are experience on any reading. But TERM-131 also requires a claim to say which system and horizon it means. If the stored specification lies inside the declared inquirer, the third sense follows at once and is the first sense relabelled. If it lies outside, in a shared record or a notation, then what changed is the record's holders, not the inquirer. Either way no further claim is made; the system boundary is a free parameter until it is fixed. What remains open is narrower and definitional: whether formulating a question, with no observation at all, counts as an experience that can change the learner (TERM-131). Nor does any existing statement tie learning and inquiry together as one process: that is among the links the line on learning and inquiry leaves unstated (SYNTHESIS-118).

The rest of the article uses the title in the first two senses. The nearest statement in the third concerns answering rather than inquiry, and it is a proposal (§5.7).

## 5.6 The joint question–answer frontier

### 5.6.1 The joint object

The inquiry frontier of §5.3.1 is the askable set. An inquiry also has an answer side: for each question, the answers an organization can attain once the question is supplied, which may be called the answer frontier (CLAIM-1009). The object this section concerns is neither, but the pairs an organization can bring about together. The joint inquiry frontier is "the question–answer pairs it can bring about together … its question side is the inquiry frontier's investigable set, and its answer side, indexed by question, is the answer frontier" (TERM-160). The term is kept beside the inquiry frontier rather than replacing it, so TERM-092 keeps its sense as the askable set. An alternative, revising the inquiry frontier itself to be "the boundary of the question–answer processes an agent or organization can jointly realize within a declared budget, horizon and inquiry regime", was deferred (TERM-159).

A prior question is whether asking and answering compete at all. That each is resource-bounded does not make them compete: "a common budget, a common bottleneck or a sequential dependence supplies the coupling, and a shared deadline is not a shared pool of worker-hours, since independent processes can spend the same elapsed time at once" (CLAIM-1157). This is shown by construction, and reproduced independently, but it rests on a further result, that where a response's stages do not compete for a shared resource its time "is a property of the route" (CLAIM-1114), which has not been independently rechecked; it is therefore stated here as shown on that premise. Whether asking and answering compete in a given organization is open (QUESTION-520).

### 5.6.2 The result

The central result is this. "At one budget, the questions an agent can formulate and the answers it can attain once a question is supplied do not fix which question–answer pairs it can attain together, but their budget-indexed families do when the costs of formulating and answering compose by a declared rule; what is left over is the rule, and where the way a question is formulated changes what answering it costs, not even the least costs fix it" (CLAIM-1115). It is shown by four constructions, each reproduced in a kept script.

1. **One budget does not fix the pairs.** Two systems both cost 10 to answer a question. One costs 10 to formulate it and the other 2. At a budget of 12 both have the same askable question and the same conditional answer. Spending in sequence, the first reaches no pair (20 > 12) and the second reaches the pair (12 ≤ 12).
2. **The families over budgets fix the pairs, under a declared rule.** From the two frontiers at every budget the least costs of formulating and of answering can be recovered, and with an additive rule the joint set follows. Checked over budgets 0 to 39, the recovered set equals the true one at every budget.
3. **The rule matters.** Formulating and answering each cost 6, against a horizon of 10. Run in sequence they take 12, so the pair is out of reach; run in parallel they take 6, so it is in reach.
4. **Formulation can change the cost of answering.** One way of formulating a question costs 2 and leaves an answer costing 9; another costs 6 and leaves 3. The least costs sum to 5, but the cheapest joint route costs 9. At budgets of 5 and 8 the least costs say the pair is reachable when it is not.

A negative control has two routes whose answering cost does not depend on the route of formulation. There the least costs give the joint set at every budget, as construction 2 says they should.

What carries the result is a simple point, made precise. An organization may be able to formulate a question within ten hours and, given the question for free, answer it within ten hours, and still be unable to do both within the same ten hours. Joint realizability is a site at which inquiry can fail, distinct from forming queries and producing answers, and at one budget it is not reducible to them (CLAIM-1190). Its irreducibility is relative to one budget, and it should not be read as irreducibility outright. Over the budget-indexed families, under a declared composition rule, the two frontiers do determine the joint set, except in the case of construction 4 (CLAIM-1115; CLAIM-1190). What the frontiers leave out is the coupling: the rule, the schedule, and what formulating does to answering. That coupling is in neither frontier (CLAIM-1115).

It would therefore overstate the result to say that the feasibility of question formation and the conditional feasibility of answering do not determine their joint realizability under a shared budget. They do not at one budget. Over all budgets, given the rule, they do, unless how a question is posed changes what answering it costs.

### 5.6.3 Bounds, deadlines and depth

The separate frontiers do bound the joint set. The pairs reachable together lie between an inner bound, posing and answering run one after the other, and an outer bound, each fitting alone, "and where between them depends on precedence and sharing that neither frontier records" (CLAIM-1025). In its construction three organizations face a horizon of 4, with posing and answering each taking three days. They finish at 4, 6 and 6: one meets the horizon and two do not, though their separate frontiers are the same. These are bounds, not a characterization, and the inner bound is qualified by construction 4, where the sequential sum of least costs is not attainable.

Under a deadline, how deeply to inquire has an interior optimum: "the chance of success p(k)F(H − t(k)) is greatest at an interior depth where the relative gain in finding a suitable response equals the time cost times the relative loss in execution readiness" (CLAIM-1008). And "when execution takes either a fast or a slow route a longer deadline can make a much shallower inquiry best, because the time it saves buys the slow route" (CLAIM-1203). A longer deadline, in that case, does not buy more inquiry; it buys a different route for what follows it.

All three results are shown by construction and reproduced independently from their statements. They share one dependency: each rests, directly or through the others, on the coupling result of §5.6.1 and so on its unrechecked premise (CLAIM-1114). They are stated here as shown on that premise.

### 5.6.4 What the arrangement contributes

It is natural to say that the organizational arrangement determines which questions and answers become jointly attainable. The result supports a weaker and more exact statement. The arrangement is part of what fixes the joint set, together with the frontiers at every budget; it does not fix the set alone. What the result does show is that the same competence can be differently usable under different arrangements. In construction 3 the same costs give different joint sets under sequential and parallel schedules (CLAIM-1115). A simple case makes the point for authorization: formulating takes 3, per-question authorization 4 and investigation 5, against a deadline of 10. Authorized question by question, the route takes 12 and misses the deadline; under a standing grant it takes 8 and meets it (a dated note on CLAIM-1016).

The case generalizes in principle to any term of a contract: "changing the contractual ecology can change the inquiry frontier without changing the intrinsic capabilities of any constituent process" (a dated note on CLAIM-1016). No relation indexed by the contractual ecology has been defined, however, and what that ecology would range over is not fixed. Whether such a relation can be stated is an open question (QUESTION-tmplkq92).

## 5.7 Same answers, different development

The joint frontier concerns what an organization can do at once. A related result concerns what answering does to it. A proposed claim holds that "Two organizations that give the same answers can differ in what answering does to them, so a response kernel that includes the organization's state after answering, observed through its later responses rather than compared as a state, separates what an answer benchmark treats as equivalent, such as one that learns from disagreement and one that suppresses it" (CLAIM-1156). This is the nearest the argument comes to saying that answering changes an organization. It is a proposal, and it does not use TERM-131's definition of learning.

Its last clause is there because of an objection, shown by construction, which the claim was revised to concede. A kernel that includes the post-answer state "is not an observational quantity: compared directly, it separates any two organizations whose post-states differ, even when no test could tell them apart". So the comparison "should observe the post-state only through its later responses, as a bisimulation pseudometric does" (CLAIM-1095; Calo et al. 2024; THEORY-247). A two-query construction gives these distances:

| pair | answers only | post-state compared directly | bisimulation |
|---|---|---|---|
| s, t: identical behaviour, distinct states | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| u, v: same answers now; u learns, v suppresses | 0 | 1 | 5.4 |

Only the bisimulation metric gets both pairs right: it does not separate the organizations no test could tell apart, and it does separate the one that learns from the one that suppresses. The construction is stipulated. It shows what a measure must do, not that any organization learns or suppresses.

For inquiry in general there is a shown result of the same form, about histories of belief rather than organizations' answering: "Two inquiry histories can agree on every current conclusion and still call for different revisions of it, so a current marginal belief is not a sufficient state for updating on evidence about what it depended on" (CLAIM-294). Present agreement, whether in answers or in conclusions, does not fix what comes next.

## 5.8 Authorization and legibility

### 5.8.1 Permission is costed

The joint frontier prices asking and answering. A third cost often sits between them. An employee who must justify an inquiry's time to a manager spends some of the window on the justification. Asking permission is resource-bounded, and that boundedness connects an account of inquiry under deadlines to the question of what makes an organization's members legible to its authorities (CLAIM-1065, a proposal). A thought experiment gives the shape: an opportunity is open for ten days; the idea is formulated in one day, justified in two, approved in two and implemented in six. Under that workflow the opportunity is lost, 11 days against a window of 10, and it is "kept by preauthorized discretion that lets justification run beside a first experiment" (CASE-386).

Everything in this section's next three subsections is proposed and interpretive, except the model of §5.8.2, which is shown by construction. The steps rest on one another: the circle of §5.8.2 and the steering of §5.8.3 rest on the first claim below, and the remedy of §5.8.4 rests on both.

The first claim generalizes the case. "Permission to pursue an inquiry is a second-order query, formulated by the proponent and evaluated by the authority within their own budgets, so the questions an organization authorizes are a subset of those its members can ask, and an authorization can take so long that the opportunity it authorizes has closed, whatever the verdict" (CLAIM-996).

### 5.8.2 The circle between evidence and permission

Authorization can be circular. "Where the evidence that would justify authorizing an inquiry can only be produced with resources that need the authorization, the dependency is circular, so a proposal's failure to persuade does not show that the possibility behind it is worthless" (CLAIM-1140). This blocks an inference. It does not show that unpersuasive proposals are worthwhile.

A model of the circle makes its structure exact. In a two-state model of one proposal, where permission is granted only on the proposal's own evidence and evidence is produced only under permission, "the state with neither is absorbing and is reached with certainty from every state whenever review or investigation can fail; making review more lenient, even certain, never leaves it, and only permission not conditioned on the question's evidence, as a standing grant gives, or evidence produced without permission, as seed evidence is, breaks it" (CLAIM-tmp5ld2o). The model is shown by construction and has been checked only by its author. Its lesson is that leniency is not a remedy. A review that approves everything it sees still sees nothing, because nothing reaches it.

### 5.8.3 Legibility as a cost the institution places

What an authority sees depends on what it costs to make a proposal visible to it. "The cost of making a proposal legible to the authority that controls its resources is not a property of the idea but of how an institution distributes interpretive labour, so an institution can steer which questions are pursued by deciding who must pay to make them legible, with no rule against any idea" (CLAIM-1117). An institution can thus shape its inquiry frontier without forbidding anything. An integration of these claims puts it in one sentence: "Legibility is itself a resource-bounded organizational accomplishment, and permission structures govern who must pay the cost of achieving it", so "adaptive governance needs maintained pathways by which locally intelligible inquiry acquires evidence, resources and authority" (CLAIM-1132). It is proposed with all its premises.

### 5.8.4 Changing the unit of authorization

Why do the remedies for the circle work? "Authorization granted question by question puts its cost between formulating a question and investigating it, on the same budget, while a standing grant pays it once for a domain before any question in it is formulated, so the remedies for the circle between evidence and permission work by changing the unit of authorization, and authorization's cost belongs in the joint inquiry relation as a third cost on the shared budget" (CLAIM-1016). The model of §5.8.2 bears this out, and adds a route the claim does not name: evidence produced outside the circle, as seed evidence is (CLAIM-tmp5ld2o). The standing grant is not free either. Its "initial establishment and continuing maintenance have costs not included in this per-inquiry calculation" (a dated note on CLAIM-1016).

A study that would compare four arrangements of review has been designed and not run (CASE-388); its design now includes a fifth arm, standing budgets under centralized review, and tasks in which novel inquiry is genuinely unhelpful, so that the comparison can fail (LEAD-tmptvvja).

### 5.8.5 Separate achievements, none sufficient

Formulating a question is not yet pursuing it. A proposed claim separates four achievements within an organization: "being able to formulate a question, having standing to pursue it, having the resources to pursue it and being able to make its findings consequential are four separate achievements, and the entitlements that secure the last three make up an operational epistemic constitution" (CLAIM-tmp2kfwd). Its new step is that standing and resources come apart, as operational standing comes apart from effective authority (TERM-tmpgiy6r). It parallels a separation of legibility, volitional efficacy and correctability as "three distinct achievements" (CLAIM-523), and a separation of a contribution's uptake: "admission … recognition … credibility … uptake … and its contributor's part in revising the standards of uptake are separable" (CLAIM-403).

A separation says that each achievement can be had without the next. It does not say that having all of them secures a consequential inquiry. Each is necessary, and none is shown sufficient. For one case the conjunction is bounded further. A protected objection "is effective only if six achievements hold together", from its becoming formulable to its being maintained, so "its chance of success is no greater than that of its weakest stage, and a reform that raises one stage's success can lower the whole" (CLAIM-732, a proposal). It carries the separation of CLAIM-523 upstream, to whether the objection can be formulated at all (TERM-092).

The capacities that make one member's competence usable by others, which might be called transactive capacities, are not the subject of any claim. What such a capacity is, and whether the same competences can be differently usable under different governance arrangements while the members' own capabilities are unchanged, is open (QUESTION-tmpg48pb). The joint-frontier result of §5.6.4 answers the second half in miniature, for schedules and per-question authorization; it does not define the capacity.

### 5.8.6 A cost and a condition

CLAIM-1016 prices authorization as a cost on the shared budget. It can also be read as a condition. The resource-bounded constraint system on which the transitions of this series are written (TERM-114) can be extended by an authority component Θ, recording "whose changes of architecture are authorized, as distinct from which are feasible and at what cost" (a note on TERM-114; the extension has not been adopted). Both readings are sound for their purposes. A grant one lacks is a bar; the process of seeking one takes time. A lead that would index reach by authority combines them, treating a transition as authorized when the party holds, or is able to obtain, the grant it requires (LEAD-tmpeo8wq). Whether obtaining a grant is itself a costed transition, with its own path, peak and failure modes, is the question that would join the two readings, and it is open (QUESTION-tmpant34).

## 5.9 Reach by actor and by authority

The joint frontier is an organization's. This section asks what one party can reach as distinct from what the parties reach together, and under whose authority.

### 5.9.1 Four predicates on a transition

A transformation can be physically feasible, affordable, authorized and normatively permissible, and these "need not coincide" (CLAIM-tmpdu9v6). They are treated in different places, and no single object holds the four together (LEAD-tmpeo8wq).

| Predicate | Where it is written |
|---|---|
| feasible | the admissible processes P_z(q, q′) of the constraint system (TERM-114) |
| affordable | "q ⇝_b q′ ⟺ ∃p ∈ P_z(q, q′) : κ(p) ⪯ b" (TERM-114) |
| authorized | Θ, a proposed extension of the constraint system (a note on TERM-114) |
| permissible | Perm_N(p), "its permissibility under a declared standard" (CLAIM-tmpdu9v6, a proposal) |

The existing account of how a transition fails covers the first two only: "a feasible path exceeds a resource ceiling, no path exists in the fixed configuration space, a path exists that no available input can execute, or a path exists that cannot be reliably followed" (CLAIM-568). It names no failure of authorization or of permissibility.

### 5.9.2 An obligation bars a transition without pricing it

The fourth predicate behaves unlike the second. On a proposed claim, "A genuine obligation enters a cost model of exit as a condition on which transitions are permissible, not as a cost: withdrawal can be feasible and affordable yet impermissible until alternatives are arranged or the duty is transferred, so a party who controls the circumstances that keep the restriction in place controls the other's exit without raising its price" (CLAIM-tmpdu9v6). Read beside §5.8.6, the argument has a cost reading of authorization and a bar reading of obligation. How the two compose on one transition has not been stated.

### 5.9.3 Together and alone

The mechanism by which reach shrinks is stated first in prose. "A capability lost because another reliably supplies it is the Black Queen's loss", and whether it is dependency or division of labour "is decided by the supply, not the loss" (CLAIM-603, a proposal). Resting on it, an arrangement "can produce the dependence through which it exploits": by reliably supplying what a party then ceases to supply for itself, "it creates a reliance on the provider that the party did not have before" (CLAIM-670, a proposal). The institutional reading behind support that does the work is the Samaritan's dilemma: "predictable aid weakens the recipient's own upkeep" (Ostrom et al. 2002; THEORY-1480).

Three results, each shown by construction, make the mechanism exact for what one party can reach unaided.

- Support that does the work "shrinks what the party can reach unaided while support that covers only its shortfall does not" (CLAIM-675).
- Where capacity is learned by doing and decays with disuse, "what decides dependence is the order in which supply and capacity are used, not the amount of supply" (CLAIM-602). Its model is dC/dt = λP(1 − C) − μC.
- Where growth also needs capacity already present, dC/dt = λPC(1 − C) − μC has an unstable threshold below which capacity does not regrow, so "support that does the work has a deadline after which withdrawal no longer restores capacity, and a slow taper of such support is worse than an abrupt cut" (CLAIM-605). It is the form of the capability trap in which aid that ignores the learning by which a state's cost of taxing falls drives a state with weak initial institutions to lose its tax capacity (Azam, Devarajan and O'Connell 1999; THEORY-1483).

A fourth result, shown by construction and run as an executed study but not adopted, applies these dynamics to a restriction. In capacity dynamics learned by doing, "making reinstatement a threshold on capacity turns a restriction justified by a lack of competence into a self-perpetuating trap", with "a recoverability deadline" (CLAIM-950; CASE-364). A restriction imposed because a party lacks competence can, under these dynamics, ensure that it never acquires it.

All four results concern a scalar capacity, not a set of reachable states in the constraint system of TERM-114. Together they are the construction for one party's reach under supply, with its negative control, support that covers only the shortfall (LEAD-tmpeo8wq).

The same shape appears in values in a bargaining model. Specialization raises joint production while it lowers one party's independent capacity. At full specialization with no protection of one party's fallback, "the split is 14.75 to 11.25 in A's favour; with full protection, 12.25 to 13.75" (CLAIM-tmp2jhc1, a model with stipulated values). Under the equal-weight Nash solution, u_B = (V − o_A + o_B)/2, so B's fallback moves the split "whether or not B ever takes the outside option". The model does not price the maintenance of the fallback (CLAIM-tmp2jhc1).

### 5.9.4 What remains open

That collective reach can grow while one party's unaided reach shrinks is therefore not open: for one party's unaided capacity it is shown (CLAIM-675; CLAIM-602; CLAIM-605). What is open is narrower: an index of reach by authority, the four predicates written per transition in one object, and whether the shrinkage of one party's reach, rather than any price, moves the terms the other can offer (LEAD-tmpeo8wq).

The lead's null is that "If R_i cannot shrink without some transition's price changing, the dependence is already priced by TERM-116's profiles, and the index adds notation only" (LEAD-tmpeo8wq). For that null to be able to fail, the lead must first say whether the decline in a party's own capacity, as in CLAIM-675, counts as a change in some transition's price. Until it does, the remaining question is not yet posed sharply enough to test.

## 5.10 Governance that changes whether an organization inquires

That authority transforms inquiry has its strongest support in two executed models. In both, what changes the inquirer is the governance of its inquiry, not inquiry itself.

**Suppression by score.** The criterion was set before the run. Where governance penalizes newly recognized violations without rewarding their discovery, "an organization may acquire an incentive not to improve its identification of its obligations" (CLAIM-662); less inquiry under the penalty would support that claim, and none would defeat it (LEAD-181). In the model, a simulated institution faces 10 visible and 20 hidden requirements under five regimes. "Penalized for every recognized violation and unrewarded for discovery, it learns to make no inquiry in every setting while its internal record shows almost no violations, against half its budget spent on inquiry when an independent evaluator scores it" (CASE-396). The pathwise result behind it is shown: in an institution whose recognized violations are all penalized and whose discoveries earn nothing, "inquiry that only reveals requirements never raises its score when the hidden requirements do not otherwise come to light" (CLAIM-1064). The study has a negative result of its own: "removing the penalty on newly recognized violations does not by itself bring inquiry back, because inquiry still earns the institution nothing" (CASE-396). What restores some inquiry is scoring by an independent count, a reward for discovery, or an outside cost of being found out large enough to outweigh the penalty. By its own criterion the run supports CLAIM-662.

What the institution learns, in this model, is whether and how much to inquire: a share of its budget. It is told only its score, and it does not learn which questions to ask (CASE-396). The model is evidence that governance can change how much an inquirer inquires, not that it shapes the content of its questions.

**Self-certification against a narrow specification.** In the second model, a demand that the organization's specification does not name is introduced mid-run, and the question is whether the regime revises its specification or excises the demand (LEAD-226). An organization that never enters the demand "scores zero violations against the original specification and its own while the harm runs for all 120 periods" (CASE-397). Its record is clean because its record cannot see.

Two proposals sit on these models. An organization can lose adaptability by two routes, and "the second is harder to detect, since a question nobody formulates leaves no recorded failure to answer it" (CLAIM-1003); the self-certification model is a construction of that undetected route. And of two ecosystems equivalent under routine queries, the one "that optimized away capacities only an unasked query needs is rewarded, its success confirms the questioner's belief that the routine family suffices, and the two strategies co-adapt" (CLAIM-1054). This query-induced capability trap is asserted without a model; one is specified and not built (LEAD-218), and the variables it would use are named but not the model.

A further design would test a contract-governed agent under centralized inquiry ("Every novel question requires approval") against local inquiry with standing exploratory allocations (LEAD-tmpbqj9u). The models above, with an earlier one, already supply its baselines (CASE-325; CASE-396; CASE-397). What stays open is its reflexive arm, in which the agent may revise its own contract, and the composition of the contract schema.

## 5.11 Overconsolidation

### 5.11.1 A generalization of overfitting

A learner might learn itself into a trap. The proposal is that, relative to a declared family of later acquisitions outside the training task distribution, "continued stabilization of a learner can keep improving its present objective while raising the budget it needs to acquire that family to a declared reliability with its held capabilities kept above a declared floor … it is a failure only relative to that family and horizon" (CLAIM-758). It is offered as a generalization of overfitting, one that captures the informal notion of "over-cooking" a learning process. Its scope conditions do work: the later family is declared, it lies outside the training distribution, and acquisition is priced with held capabilities kept.

Two of those conditions were forced by the machine-learning literature. The first separates the claim from task overfitting. Meta-learners can improve their training loss while losing adaptation to new tasks from the same distribution, which Yin and colleagues call "a subtle form of task overfitting" (Yin et al. 2019; THEORY-2056; Rajendran, Irpan and Jang 2020; THEORY-2053). Task-level overfitting is one of the failures the claim must be kept apart from, and declaring the later family outside the training distribution excludes it. The second prices collateral forgetting into the cost of acquiring. Past an inflection point in pre-training length, a longer-pretrained language model ends worse after fine-tuning, mainly because fine-tuning degrades more of what it held (Springer et al. 2025; THEORY-2006). That work is the claim's prime case, and it qualifies the claim: what grows is not the cost of learning the new task alone but the cost of learning it while keeping what was held.

Other work is neighbouring rather than confirming. Han and colleagues compare weight-decay regimes in pre-training and do not follow a time course (Han et al. 2026; THEORY-2015). Dohare and colleagues show plasticity lost over long sequences of tasks, with the present objective falling, not rising (Dohare et al. 2024; THEORY-2027). Raghu and colleagues find that "feature reuse is the dominant factor" in fast adaptation, and track neither quantity over training (Raghu et al. 2019; THEORY-2025).

No reading shows the full signature: present competence rising, a declared later acquisition costing more, held capabilities kept (CLAIM-758). That any learner is overconsolidated has not been measured (SYNTHESIS-105). A neighbouring proposal asks whether relief has a deadline: it "depends on how the capacity to acquire is lost", and "the evidence read so far fits" the case where training uses that capacity up, which renewed practice does not restore (CLAIM-757).

### 5.11.2 What is shown, and what is not

It is tempting to distinguish the demonstrated possibility of operational improvement with rising developmental cost from the open empirical question of how often it occurs. The line falls more tightly than that.

- **Shown for support.** Where the work is done by another party, equal present capability can leave unequal later capability (CLAIM-675; CLAIM-602).
- **Shown for matched output against readiness.** In an executed model of an organization's transitions under six budget regimes, "every regime produces the same output until a contingency arrives, while readiness at a deadline of six periods ranges from 0.147 to 0.894" (CASE-412). The range is a model result, not an estimate of real institutions. The model concerns budgets for an organization's transitions; it does not model a learner's training.
- **Shown in general, for the cost structure.** "A capability can be cheap to keep and costly to acquire" (CLAIM-569). That is the general form of a developmental cost distinct from an operational one, and overconsolidation rests on it beside CLAIM-675. It says nothing about what training does to either cost.
- **For a learner's own training: not shown, not even as a possibility.** Overconsolidation in training rests on the result for support, with the further step that "training can be such a process for the learner itself" (CLAIM-758). That step is proposed and interpretive. The literature read so far qualifies the claim and supplies no instance of it.

Carried to an institution, overconsolidation would be an epistemic overconsolidation: an organization that becomes better at its routine questions while becoming costlier to turn to new ones. No claim states it, and as a claim it would be CLAIM-758 with an institution declared as the learner. Whether it holds of any institution is open.

### 5.11.3 The study that would measure it

What is missing is the measurement in one run: present performance rising, the constrained cost of a declared out-of-distribution family rising with training time, and forgetting measured. A study is designed for it (CASE-294). Across checkpoints of one pretraining run of the Pythia models, it would measure the budget each checkpoint needs to acquire held-out task families, "to see whether later checkpoints are better now but cost more to teach new tasks". A calibration on two task families and four checkpoints comes first (LEAD-010), then the study proper (LEAD-012). Neither has been run.

*Expectation, stated before any result.* Later checkpoints are better on the pretraining objective and costlier to teach. The prior result the design draws on is plasticity loss over Pythia training (Dong 2026; THEORY-1353). *Falsifier:* adaptation cost flat or falling across checkpoints, with forgetting held to the floor. A null on the chosen families would not refute CLAIM-758, which is relative to a declared family, but it would leave the claim without the instance it lacks.

## 5.12 Adaptive inquiry readiness

### 5.12.1 Readiness for demands not yet named

If inquiry can be suppressed and capacity to acquire can be used up, an organization that wants to keep the capacity to inquire must maintain it. A proposed term names the condition. Adaptive inquiry readiness is "the condition of meeting its current epistemic obligations while keeping admissible, affordable and sufficiently reliable pathways to investigate the relevant demands within their horizons; kept up through the institution's own activity, it is a viability condition" (TERM-163). It extends a notion of readiness, "the probability that an organization operating under a specified policy can deliver an effective response to a specified contingency within its deadline" (TERM-115), with epistemic demands as the contingencies.

As written the term names a condition and does not yet measure one. Its probability is not defined, since nothing says what it is over: the states it would be taken across need a declared law (TERM-163). Its definition is unsettled.

### 5.12.2 What would be maintained

A conjecture says what an organization would maintain: persisted perspectives "may serve as conceptual nucleation sites for pursuing inquiry in depth in parallel" (CLAIM-1004). On a proposed reading of that conjecture, what is maintained is "an ecology of partially independent inquiry-and-response capacities" (CLAIM-1177). A maintained nucleus differs "from an archived alternative, which keeps only a description, and from active parallel inquiry, which keeps receiving resources" (CLAIM-1148). In a linear model, holding one ready "costs its decay rate times that level over the efficiency of investment whether or not it is used" (CLAIM-1195). The model is shown and reproduced independently in its qualitative conclusions; it rests on the distinction of CLAIM-1148, which is proposed. A capacity of this kind "decays with disuse and is shown only by a retention test or the contingency it serves" (SYNTHESIS-113). Readiness, that is, cannot be read off the organization's ordinary output; §5.11.2's matched-output model is the general form of that point.

A caution applies to reading such models. A model's verbal conclusion "often reads a parameter as the concept it was introduced to stand for" (CLAIM-tmpyxemn, a proposal). A decay rate in a linear model is not, without further argument, the rate at which an organization forgets how to inquire.

### 5.12.3 Diversity, narrowed

It might seem to follow that diversity is a good measure of an ecosystem's richness (CLAIM-1034, a proposal). What the models support is narrower. The coverage model supports it only under one-at-a-time selection with no shared budget, and what should be maintained is capacity, not pursuit (SYNTHESIS-122). Three proposals narrow it the same way. What tracks richness is "diversity of consequential capabilities, not the number of constituents" (CLAIM-1038). Parallel perspectives help "only insofar as their failures are independent" (CLAIM-1002). And maintained perspectives "are alternative legibility regimes", so keeping one means "keeping its practices, expertise, channels and capacity for work, not only an archive of its ideas" (CLAIM-1119), which ties this section back to §5.8.3.

### 5.12.4 A matched-organization study, and what it must separate

The natural test is a matched comparison: organizations "matched in initial expertise, technical resources and performance on familiar inquiries but differing in how inquiry is authorized and perspectives maintained", with "a difference in success on the novel ones" as the discriminating result (CASE-395). It has not been run; it is specified for simulation first, with an arm on slack that holds authorization fixed (LEAD-tmp8ogv0).

The design faces an obstacle it names itself. The matched-performance model "already shows the predicted pattern from a different mechanism, which the design must separate" (CASE-395): in the readiness and self-certification models the pattern is "produced by budget rules and reserves, not by authorization or perspective maintenance". A result matched on familiar inquiries and different on novel ones would not, by itself, discriminate authorization from budget.

*Expectation, stated before any result.* If the study ran as first designed, the gap between familiar and novel performance should track slack, a held reserve, at least as strongly as it tracks the authorization regime. *Falsifier:* with slack held fixed, "an organization that keeps a reserve and one that does not, with authorization held fixed" (CASE-395), the gap persists across authorization regimes. Only that arm would bear on the remedies of §5.8.4 and the ecology of §5.12.2 rather than on the budget mechanism of the readiness model.

The slack arm rests on earlier results. Reserve and redesign "change different components of readiness" and, under a budget that follows peak use, "are complementary requirements rather than independently sufficient alternatives" (CLAIM-1093). With one capacity, a redesign that lets the reserve fall "keeps the redesigned transition and loses every other transition the larger reserve kept whose threshold lies above θ′ and whose least-peak path does not use the redesign" (CLAIM-1180). And records kept per capacity bound a reserve's readiness "between the sum of their probabilities less one less their number and the weakest of them" (CLAIM-632).

## 5.13 Discussion

### 5.13.1 What the argument does not establish

The firm part of the argument is small and formal. It is shown by construction that the joint question–answer frontier is not fixed by its two sides at one budget, that over all budgets it is fixed given a composition rule except where formulation changes answering's cost (§5.6.2), and that an observational measure of what answering does to an organization must observe the post-state through later responses (§5.7). Executed models show inquiry suppressed by a score that penalizes what it reveals, self-certification against a narrow specification, and matched output with unmatched readiness (§§5.10, 5.11.2). The theses that connect these to inquiry, that inquiry is specification and that formulating is costed (§§5.2–5.3), are interpretive.

Several things are not established. The separation of three frontiers is proposed (§5.4). The bounds, deadline and depth results of §5.6.3 are shown on a premise not yet independently rechecked. Every step from the joint frontier to authorization and legibility (§5.8) is proposed and interpretive, except the model of the circle, which has been checked only by its author. The query-induced trap has no model (§5.10). Overconsolidation in a learner's own training is not shown even as a possibility, and nothing has been measured (§5.11). Adaptive inquiry readiness names a condition without defining its probability (§5.12). The matched-organization and review-arrangement studies are designed and not run. That inquiry is a way the inquirer learns, the third sense of the title, is not shown (§5.5). And no model here measures an institution.

Four joins the argument would like are open. Whether obtaining a grant is a costed transition, which would join the cost and condition readings of authorization (QUESTION-tmpant34). How an obligation's bar and authorization's price compose on one transition (§5.9.2). Whether a relation indexed by the contractual ecology can be defined (QUESTION-tmplkq92). And whether revising an inquiry's criteria, in the sense of TERM-095, is revising governing standards in the sense that would make a system self-governing (QUESTION-388).

### 5.13.2 The strongest objection: this is resource allocation

The strongest objection is that everything this article attributes to governance is resource allocation under another name. The joint frontier is a budget constraint. Authorization, on CLAIM-1016, is a third cost on the same budget. The suppression model's institution reallocates its budget away from inquiry. The matched-output model's differences in readiness come from budget rules and reserves. On this view the governance of inquiry adds nothing that a careful accounting of costs, with a schedule, would not already contain.

The objection is partly right, and the argument concedes that part. The cost reading of authorization is deliberately a budget reading: its point is that authorization belongs in the joint relation as a cost, so that a standing grant works by changing where the cost falls (§5.8.4). And the matched-organization study is designed around exactly the confound the objection names, with a slack arm that holds authorization fixed (§5.12.4). If the gap between familiar and novel performance tracks slack and vanishes when slack is held fixed, the objection wins for that design.

Three things the objection does not reach. First, the joint result is not a budget constraint on either side. At one budget the two sides are fixed and the joint set still varies with the composition rule, the schedule and the dependence of answering's cost on how a question was posed (§5.6.2). Those are features of the arrangement, not of how much is spent. Second, the circle between evidence and permission is not relieved by more budget or by more lenient review: in its model the state with neither evidence nor permission is absorbing however lenient review is, and only a change in what permission is conditioned on breaks it (§5.8.2). In its model, that is a structural fact about the arrangement, not a quantity. Third, on the proposal of §5.9.2, a condition that bars a transition is not a price. An obligation can make exit impermissible while leaving it feasible and affordable (§5.9.2), and an authority component distinguishes which changes are authorized from which are feasible and at what cost (§5.8.6). Whether the condition reading reduces to the cost reading once obtaining a grant is itself costed is the open question of §5.8.6. Until it is answered, the objection is not met in full, and the article says so.

### 5.13.3 The record as a case

It is natural to offer the record that holds this argument as a case of it: a resource-bounded, reflexive epistemic organization that stores specification, authorizes its own inquiries and certifies its own work. The argument does not use it that way. The record's grammar, its reviews of its own standings and its screening of joins were designed, run and described by the same assistants that developed the argument; as evidence for the argument it would be circular.

What the record has measured about itself can still be reported as an illustration, with its guard. One census found that "of its 114 decisions to 2026-11-20, 88 changed the grammar, 62 by refinement, 25 by revision and 1 by restraint … the noticing moved from filing agents to review procedures while judgment about revisions stayed with the owner" (CASE-300). What such a record secures is "traceable change over its accountability structure, which is neither sufficiency for revision nor correctness" (SYNTHESIS-017). Shown results back that guard. Decision sufficiency, historical accountability and correctness "can hold while the other two fail" (CLAIM-299); "the perfectly accountable false theory" is a community whose record traces every commitment while its vocabulary leaves out the right explanation (CASE-084). And a family of probes that supplies a system's training signal "cannot also certify what the system preserves" (CLAIM-266); that this applies to a record whose own checks shape what it files is this article's reading, not the claim's. The models of §5.10 describe the danger in the record's own terms: promotion by use would be the self-certification CLAIM-1064 and CASE-397 model. How a collective that keeps its inquiry in a governed record can keep "the speed of incorporation from outrunning its capacity to evaluate what it incorporates" (QUESTION-471), and whether it can improve its own procedures "without losing the capacity to detect failures in that apparatus" (QUESTION-457), are open.

## 5.14 Conclusion

Inquiry changes the inquirer in two precise senses. Formulating a question is a costed realization, so the questions an inquirer can ask are a product of what it has already realized, and the specifications its inquiries store make some later questions cheaper and others harder (CLAIM-475; CLAIM-761). And an inquiry can take its own language, answer space, criteria or resources as its object, revising what an answer could be (CLAIM-470; TERM-095). That inquiry is thereby a way the inquirer learns is not shown: once the learner is declared, it is either the first sense relabelled or a change in someone else.

What an organization can find out is a property of asking, answering and the arrangement that couples them. At one budget the two capacities do not fix the pairs an organization can reach together; across budgets they do, given the rule by which their costs compose, unless posing a question changes what answering it costs (CLAIM-1115; CLAIM-1190). That is why the same competence can be differently usable under different arrangements, and why authorization belongs in the account of inquiry: as a cost on the same budget, whose placement can steer which questions are pursued without forbidding any (CLAIM-1016; CLAIM-1117), and as a condition that bars a transition without pricing it (CLAIM-tmpdu9v6), the two readings not yet joined. In executed models, governance that penalizes what inquiry reveals teaches an institution to stop inquiring while its record stays clean (CASE-396; CLAIM-1064; CASE-397), and organizations with the same output can differ widely in readiness for what they have not yet met (CASE-412). Whether learning systems become better now at the cost of becoming harder to teach is a proposal the literature qualifies and no study has measured (CLAIM-758). The account says what readiness for the unasked would require, and what the study that tested it would have to separate; it does not yet say how much of it any organization has.
