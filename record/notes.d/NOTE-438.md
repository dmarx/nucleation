# NOTE-438: A meta-analytic review of experiments examining the effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation

## Contribution

The most extensive meta-analysis of the reward literature to its date, and
the first to include unpublished dissertations (20 studies), to separate
engagement- from completion-contingent rewards, to test age as a moderator,
and to test whether undermining lasts. It is also an adversarial
reanalysis: it sets out, study by study, where its coding departs from
Cameron and Pierce's (1994) and why.

## Key insight

Whether a reward undermines interest depends on what the reward is likely
to mean to the person: a controller of their behaviour or an affirmation of
their competence (CET's "functional significance", p. 628). Tangible rewards
given for doing a task are mostly controllers; positive feedback is mostly
information. So the reward literature is not "do rewards work?" but a set of
predicted interactions, and averaging across them hides the effect. The
paper's methodological claim follows: meta-analysis "is inherently
conservative" in a theory-driven field, because published studies were
designed to find the moderators that a meta-analysis then averages over
(p. 631).

## Assumptions

- **Only interesting tasks are in scope.** Tasks below the scale midpoint of
  rated interest, or defined as uninteresting, are excluded from the primary
  analyses (p. 635). For dull tasks "the critical theoretical issue is how to
  facilitate internalization" (p. 635). Cameron and Pierce collapsed
  interesting and dull tasks (p. 651), and their 2001 reply ([LIT-562](../literature.d/LIT-562.md),
  unread; by its abstract) reports reward effects on low-interest tasks as
  well, so the scope is part of the dispute.
- **Measures after the reward stops.** Intrinsic motivation is free-choice
  persistence or self-reported interest measured once the contingency is
  over; measures taken during the reward phase mix motives (p. 635).
- **Missing effect sizes imputed as zero**: 8 of 150 free-choice and 26 of
  121 self-report effects (p. 637).
- **Hedges–Olkin weighting**, homogeneity by Q, moderators before outliers;
  fewer than 4% of effects dropped as outliers (p. 634).
- **Free choice is the more valid measure** (pp. 655–656): unobtrusive, less
  open to demand, and its main bias would shrink, not inflate, undermining.

## Key results

- **All rewards:** free choice d = −0.24 (k = 101); self-report 0.04, n.s.
  (k = 84) (p. 638). CET makes no prediction at this level.
- **Verbal rewards:** free choice 0.33 (k = 21); college 0.43, children 0.11
  n.s. Self-report 0.31 after three outliers (pp. 638–639).
- **Tangible rewards:** free choice −0.34 (k = 92); self-report −0.07
  (k = 70) (p. 639). Children worse than college students on both measures
  (pp. 646, 656).
- **Unexpected tangible:** 0.01 and 0.05, n.s. (p. 640).
- **Expected tangible:** −0.36 and −0.07 (p. 640).
- **Task-noncontingent:** −0.14 and 0.21, n.s. (p. 640).
- **Engagement-contingent:** −0.40 (children −0.43 after outliers, college
  −0.21) and −0.15 (p. 641).
- **Completion-contingent:** −0.44 after one outlier, and −0.17 after two
  (pp. 641–643).
- **Performance-contingent:** free choice −0.28, self-report −0.01 n.s.
  Split by control group and feedback: maximum reward vs no-feedback control
  −0.15; less-than-maximum vs no-feedback control −0.88; reward signalling
  good performance vs positive-feedback control −0.20; vs negative-feedback
  control −0.03 n.s. (pp. 643–644).
- **Published vs dissertations:** no difference for verbal or tangible
  rewards (p. 649).
- **Persistence over time (children):** −0.40, −0.49, −0.53 (p. 650).
- **Interesting vs dull tasks (13 studies):** free choice −0.68 vs 0.18 n.s.;
  self-report −0.37 vs 0.10 n.s. (p. 651). Adding the dull-task conditions
  back weakens composites slightly and removes significance only for
  completion-contingent self-reports (pp. 651–652).
- **Informational vs controlling feedback (4 studies):** controlling
  positive feedback lowers intrinsic motivation relative to informational,
  d = −0.78; informational vs neutral 0.66, controlling vs neutral −0.44
  (p. 652).
- **The behavioural account fails** (pp. 654–655). Eisenberger and Cameron
  predict undermining only for performance-independent rewards, through
  helplessness. But engagement-contingent rewards, which the person controls
  by doing the task, undermine, while task-noncontingent rewards, the only
  plausible helplessness case, do not. Their own analysis shows the same.
  Two studies with luck-based rewards (Weiner 1980; Kruglanski et al. 1975)
  went the opposite way to their prediction. Their 1998 claim that
  performance-contingent rewards with specific standards enhance intrinsic
  motivation is not borne out (free choice −0.21, self-report −0.02, both
  n.s., pp. 655).

## Concepts

- **engagement-contingent reward**: for working on the task, regardless of
  finishing or quality (p. 628).
- **completion-contingent reward**: for finishing the task (p. 628).
- **performance-contingent reward**: for doing well, meeting a standard or
  surpassing a criterion (p. 628).
- **task-noncontingent reward**: for something other than the task, such as
  turning up (p. 628).
- **functional significance**: what a reward means to the recipient,
  controlling or informational (p. 628).
- **free-choice measure**: time spent on (or trials of) the target activity
  in an unobserved free period after the reward phase (p. 635).

## Connections

- **The earlier meta-analyses (pp. 631–634).** Rummel and Feinberg (1988),
  Wiersma (1992) and Tang and Hall (1995) found undermining; Cameron and
  Pierce (1994, [LIT-556](../literature.d/LIT-556.md)) and Eisenberger and Cameron (1996) did not, and
  called for "abandoning cognitive evaluation theory" (p. 632, quoting
  Cameron & Pierce p. 396). The paper treats the last two as "essentially the
  same meta-analysis" reported in pieces (p. 632).
- **Cameron, Banko & Pierce (2001), [LIT-562](../literature.d/LIT-562.md), Deferred.** The reply,
  known here only from its abstract: negative effects only for tangible,
  expected rewards "loosely tied to level of performance", on high-interest
  tasks, and no pervasive harm.
- **SDT summaries ([LIT-559](../literature.d/LIT-559.md), [LIT-558](../literature.d/LIT-558.md), [LIT-560](../literature.d/LIT-560.md))** cite this paper
  as having settled the matter. The 2006 paper says the earlier analysis was
  wrong by errors "numerous enough to generate the 'null' results"
  ([LIT-560](../literature.d/LIT-560.md), p. 1568). This paper's own claim is more measured: it
  explains the discrepancy by several factors and finds that one of them,
  the dull tasks, accounts for little of it (p. 652).

## Bearing on the record

- **Reward undermining.** The narrowest claim this paper supports, that
  expected tangible rewards for merely engaging in an interesting task lower
  later free-choice engagement (d = −0.40), is also the one Cameron and
  Pierce found by this paper's account (task-contingent −0.23, p. 649;
  performance-independent −0.29, p. 641), and the one the 2001 abstract
  keeps ("loosely tied to level of performance").
- **The methodological point** (meta-analysis averages over the moderators a
  theory-driven literature was built to find, p. 631) bears on how the record
  reads any meta-analysis of a contested effect.
- **No ML instruction.** "Reward" here is a psychological manipulation, not
  a training signal. Nothing here belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- **CET's categories are both the coding scheme and the hypothesis tested.**
  The performance-contingent category mixes per-unit pay, rewards for "doing
  well" and rewards for beating a norm (Appendix A). If those behave
  differently, as the 2001 abstract implies for rewards "linked to level of
  performance" ([LIT-562](../literature.d/LIT-562.md), unread), the category hides it.
- **Most effects are small.** Self-report effects of tangible rewards are
  around −0.07 to −0.17.
- **The age finding is post hoc.** "This set of findings has never been
  predicted before" (p. 656); the explanations offered are speculation.
- **Imputed zeros** are numerous for self-report (21%), which the paper itself
  takes as a sign that self-report is the weaker measure (p. 656).
- **Mediation was not tested.** "A similar analysis has not been done with
  respect to reward effects" (pp. 653–654) for perceived self-determination.
- **Long-term, multi-session rewarding** is nearly unstudied with controls:
  two such studies, nine rewarded participants in all (p. 650).

## Open questions

- Do repeated rewards over weeks, with no-reward controls and adequate
  samples, undermine? The paper calls for such studies (p. 650).
- Does perceived locus of causality mediate tangible-reward effects?
- Why does positive feedback fail to raise children's free choice?
