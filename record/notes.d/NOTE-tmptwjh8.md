# NOTE-tmptwjh8: Jokes and the Logic of the Cognitive Unconscious

## Contribution

The memo extends Freud's censor theory of jokes from emotional taboos to
intellectual failures. It puts nonsense, paradox and wordplay under one
mechanism with sexual and aggressive humour. That mechanism is a learned
store of "negative meta-knowledge", censors that suppress
known-unproductive states of mind. The memo grounds the store in an
argument from the frame memo: commonsense logic cannot be made safe, and
general methods cannot be patched for particular failures.

## Key insight

Minds get smarter as much by learning what not to think as by learning what
to think. A general method that fails in a particular case cannot be fixed
without breaking it elsewhere. All one can do is remember to suppress it
there. Humour is the social and internal channel for these subtractive
lessons. It explains why jokes are about the prohibited and the absurd,
and why they wear out.

## Assumptions

- **Two brains.** An "A-brain" faces the world. A "B-brain" takes its inputs
  from A's interior and can affect A, recognising "metapsychological"
  conditions such as meandering, circling or confusion without knowing what
  A's states mean.
- **Frames.** Perception and understanding use frames with terminals and
  weak defaults, after the frame memo. Commonsense inference is shifting
  between frames that share terminals (§III).
- **Society of Mind.** The mind is "composed of many smaller minds" (Note 1).
  Partial mental states follow K-lines (Note 2).
- **Censors learn, at different rates.** Censors for different subjects have
  different time-constants (§V, "Time constants").
- **Method.** The author would "experiment, not on people … but on computers
  to see if a proposed theory-fragment can be made part of a system that
  shows mind-like activity" (Note 16). No such experiment is reported.

## Key results

These are conjectures, argued by example.

- **Five-part thesis (pp. 2–3).**
  1. Commonsense logic is unreliable and unrepairable.
  2. Errors must be anticipated and prevented by censors, hence humour's
     concern with the prohibited.
  3. Analogy and metaphor must be policed, hence its concern with nonsense.
  4. Faulty reasoning is as "naughty" to the cognitive unconscious as
     Freudian wishes.
  5. Humour evolved socially, as a disarming way to correct others.
- **Problems of commonsense reasoning (§I).**
  - The liar and the barber show that no logic barring self-reference is
    adequate, since "without the power to think about Thinking", no mind is
    powerful enough.
  - The response is to find "islands of power" and mark their unsafe
    boundaries. Paradigms like the Barber, the Tortoise and the Liar are
    road-signs to "stop -- and laugh".
- **Censorship (§II).**
  - A type (i) censor stops a thought once it has started. A type (ii)
    censor prevents it, by learning its precursors.
  - Precursor-sensitive censors act quickly enough to leave no
    phenomenology.
  - "This 'negative meta-knowledge' … may be a large portion of all we
    know."
- **Meaning and metaphor (§III).**
  - The frame theory is restated, including the invisible chair under a
    sitting person.
  - Logic is cast as frame-shifting, and the "game" problem as a system of
    prototype frames.
  - Particular analogies are recorded additively, as difference-network
    links after Winston. General analogy methods are corrected
    subtractively, by censors: "positive general principles need always to
    be supplemented by negative, anecdotal censors."
  - "Unexpected frame-substitution" is the element common to humour.
- **Traumatic cognitive experiences (§IV).**
  - The nearness paradox ("Ten is almost a Hundred"), Zeno, and
    chicken-and-egg act as intellectual traumas that build censors and
    cognitive phobias.
  - "Mystical experience" is a last-resort short-circuit that creates the
    illusion a problem is settled.
- **Humour and evolution (§V).**
  - Laughter disrupts reasoning and focuses attention on the absurd thought,
    so that censors can learn it, "much as 'pleasure' is often supposed to
    mediate ordinary learning".
  - A social origin is offered for its mixed conciliatory and aggressive
    display, later internalised.
  - "Humor" names a web, not a thing: "less a Thing-Part of the mind, and
    more a Thing-Theory in the mind".
  - The persistence of sexual humour is put down to slow censors, with
    attachment and mourning given as examples of long time-constants.
- **Notes.**
  - Note 8: ethnic humour as a side-effect of the need for stereotyped
    person-frames.
  - Note 12: pleasure as a "constructed illusion" of coherence, with a
    conjectured central brain unit.
  - Note 17: an "acquisition envelope" hypothesis, in which puberty shuts
    off phonetic learning so that children learn the parents' language,
    not the reverse.

## Concepts

- **Censor.** A learned suppressor of a mental activity. Type (i) stops it;
  type (ii) recognises its precursors and prevents it.
- **A-brain and B-brain.** A two-level mind in which B perceives and acts on
  A's internal states, as A does on the world.
- **Negative meta-knowledge.** Knowledge about patterns of thought and
  inference that have been found defective or harmful.
- **Islands of power.** Domains within which naive reasoning stays safe.
- **Frame-substitution.** Re-viewing all the elements of a scene through a
  different frame.
- **Cognitive unconscious.** The unconscious working of censors on
  intellectual, not emotional, matters.
- **Acquisition envelope.** A genetically controlled window on how something
  is learned (Note 17).

## Bearing on the record

- **Somatic markers.** The somatic-marker hypothesis ([LIT-384](../literature.d/LIT-384.md)) and the
  gambling-task study ([LIT-383](../literature.d/LIT-383.md)) describe learned, nonconscious signals that
  prune options before reasons are available. Censors are a cognitive
  analogue that works on lines of thought, not on options for action. The
  two together make the record's case against a clean affect–intellect
  split from two directions, neural and computational. The pairing is mine.
  Neither work cites the other, and the memo offers no neural mechanism.
- **Logic.** The memo treats paradox as a developmental event and argues
  that no logic forbidding self-reference is adequate for psychology. That
  is a claim about minds, not about formal systems, and it does not bear on
  the logic papers held here.
- **THEORY.** No THEORY in the record should cite it as support.
- **Boundary.** No machine-learning practice instruction; no anthology
  topic holds it.

## Limitations

- **No evidence.** There are no experiments on humour, laughter or learning,
  and no program.
- **The unifying claim (C4) bends.** Note 16 concedes that adult "good
  humour" uses good frame-shifts as well as bad ones, so the
  positive/negative distinction "seems to fall apart". The memo saves it by
  appeal to adult development, not by evidence.
- **Ad hoc time-constants.** The "slow censor" premise is introduced to
  absorb the counterexample of sexual humour (C7).
- **Untested side theories.** The notes add hypotheses (ethnic humour,
  pleasure, phonetic acquisition) that are independent of the main argument
  and equally untested.

## Open questions

- Can a system with a B-brain that learns precursor censors be built, and
  does it show anything like humour's wear-out with repetition? This is the
  test Note 16 itself proposes.
- Does the funniness of a joke track whether its frame-shift exposes a
  "bug", as C4 and C5 would predict, more than it tracks surprise in
  general?
