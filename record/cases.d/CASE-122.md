# CASE-122: Warhol's Orange Prince, licensed to Condé Nast in 2016 from a 1981 Goldsmith photograph: the Supreme Court judged the use by its purpose and the dissent by its meaning, and the same cover came out a substitute on one test and a new work on the other

## The case

From the slip opinion of *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts,
Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023) ([LIT-1302](../literature.d/LIT-1302.md), read in
[NOTE-1019](../notes.d/NOTE-1019.md)). Page numbers restart for each opinion.

- **The source and the rendering.** Lynn Goldsmith photographed Prince
  in 1981. In 1984 her agency licensed one portrait to Vanity Fair as an
  "artist reference for an illustration", for one use (maj. p. 3).
  Warhol made the Vanity Fair image and fifteen more works from it. The
  Court describes Orange Prince as one that "crops, flattens, traces, and
  colors the photo but otherwise does not alter it" (maj. p. 8).
- **The use at issue.** In 2016 Condé Nast paid AWF $10,000 to use Orange
  Prince on the cover of a tribute issue after Prince's death. Goldsmith
  got neither fee nor credit. People paid Goldsmith $1,000 to use another
  of her photographs for its own tribute issue, and Rolling Stone and
  Time ran tribute covers with other photographs (maj. pp. 6–7). The Court
  decided nothing about the making, display or sale of the Prince Series
  (maj. p. 21).
- **The majority.** New expression, meaning or message does not by itself
  favour the user, or "transformative use" would swallow the owner's
  right to derivative works (maj. p. 28). Meaning "should be considered
  to the extent necessary to determine whether the purpose of the use is
  distinct from the original" (maj. pp. 31–32). Here the purpose, a
  portrait of Prince illustrating a magazine about Prince, was
  substantially the same, and the use was commercial (maj. pp. 24–25,
  33). Bringing out with a characteristic style a meaning "that was
  available in the photograph" is less likely to be a further purpose
  (maj. p. 33). Copying is most clearly justified when the original is
  the target, as in parody (maj. pp. 17–19).
- **The dissent.** The experts agreed the works differ in "composition,
  presentation, color palette, and media" and in meaning: Goldsmith's
  subject was Prince's identity, Warhol's "not the private person but the
  public image" (Kagan p. 9). Factor 1 asks what the copier accomplished
  (Kagan p. 13). The majority reads "character" out of the statute and
  counts the owner's market twice (Kagan pp. 19–22).

## What it can show

**Two measures of what a rendering changed, on one pair.** The majority
measures the rendering by the purpose it serves in a use: what job the
cover does for a reader of a tribute issue. The dissent measures it by
the meaning a viewer can read in it. On the same image the two measures
disagree. That makes the case a documented instance of [QUESTION-002](../questions.d/QUESTION-002.md)'s
problem: what survives a rendering depends on which relation is measured,
and here an institution had to choose one. It does not show which is
right. The Court is answering a statutory question, not a semantic one.

**[CLAIM-034](../claims.d/CLAIM-034.md).** The opinion ranks rendering goals by the source's role.
Parody and criticism, which need the original as their target, are
favoured. Adaptation, sequels and translation fall within the owner's
derivative right (maj. pp. 16, 28–29). Similarity of purpose counts
against the user. That is a legal scale on which fidelity of purpose is
a cost, not a goal, the reverse of a translator's.

**[CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md).** The other tribute covers served "the same essential
purpose" as Orange Prince with a variety of portrait photographs (maj.
n. 11). None was made from the photograph Warhol used, and no one
suggested that sharing its purpose was a wrong. People's cover was
another Goldsmith photograph, used under licence. Sharing a purpose
mattered only for the cover that copied the photograph, so the law
separates sharing a kind from being a rendering, as [CLAIM-210](../claims.d/CLAIM-210.md) does.

**What it cannot show.** It is a commercial licence between
professionals, not amateur remix, so it bears on Lessig's argument
([NOTE-1023](../notes.d/NOTE-1023.md)) only through the law's general test. Its "purpose" is
the purpose of a market use, not a communicative function in [CLAIM-220](../claims.d/CLAIM-220.md)'s
sense, and reading one as the other would be an analogy only. The images
did not survive text extraction, so the record has the opinions'
descriptions of them, not the images.
