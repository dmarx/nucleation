# THEORY-tmpp6hib: Nagel argues that the condition that we are morally assessable only for what we control, applied consistently through four kinds of luck (resultant, constitutive, circumstantial and causal), erodes nearly all moral judgment, and that this is a genuine paradox rooted in the clash between the internal view of agency and the external view of acts as events, not an error to be repaired by a refined condition

## Source

[LIT-tmp2dx6l](../literature.d/LIT-tmp2dx6l.md), Nagel, "Moral Luck", Mortal Questions (1979), ch. 3. Read in
full in [NOTE-tmpfw0h2](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpfw0h2.md) from an anthology reprint (pp. 202–212).

## What was actually shown

Nagel argues, he does not demonstrate. Ordinary excuses (involuntary
movement, physical force, ignorance) express a condition of control, and
"If the condition of control is consistently applied, it threatens to erode
most of the moral assessments we find it natural to make." He sorts the
ways the objects of judgment depend on luck into four kinds: constitutive,
circumstantial, causal and resultant. He holds that the paradox "is not a
mistake, ethical or logical", so the control condition cannot be refined.
He rejects paring action down to the will, and he rejects the compatibilist
"brazen it out", whose fault is "its failure to explain how skeptical
problems arise". His diagnosis: "something in the idea of agency is
incompatible with actions being events, or people being things".

On [QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md) the bearing is indirect. Nagel's account of how the
responsible self is "swallowed up" once its acts are viewed as events is a
standing difficulty for any answer that locates a wrong in an agent's
agency.

## What this does not say

- That moral judgment should be abandoned. Nagel notes that the judgments
  "reappear involuntarily as soon as the argument is over" (p. 208), and
  he offers no revision.
- Anything about how responsibility divides between the party who set the
  circumstances and the actor.
- A positive account of agency, which Nagel says he does not have.
