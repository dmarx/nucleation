# Nucleation: a research program in organizational individuation, inquiry, and normative governance

I think the conversation has developed into something considerably more substantial than a theory of implicit contracts or institutional pathologies. It now suggests a general research program concerning **how organized agency emerges through interdependence, how that interdependence acquires normative structure, and how organizations become capable—or incapable—of governing their own development.**

Rather than attempt to establish everything in a single manuscript, I would develop a sequence of **14 independent but interrelated articles**, organized into five movements.

Each article should make a substantive contribution that can be evaluated on its own merits. Later articles can draw upon the earlier results without requiring readers to accept the entirety of Nucleation's philosophical framework.

The progression would move from relatively general and formally tractable organizational mechanisms toward increasingly substantive epistemic, moral, institutional and political implications.

The program's overarching thesis would be:

> Organizations develop through the formation and maintenance of relationships that make their constituent processes mutually consequential. These relationships store organizational work, enable new capacities, distribute authority and create dependencies. Their continuing realization can transform the conditions of future agency, inquiry and cooperation. Normative governance concerns the legitimate organization of those transformations, including the maintenance of effective capacities to discover and correct their inadequacies.

There are four distinctions that should remain explicit throughout the series: causal dependence is not necessarily contractual organization; operational normativity is not necessarily moral normativity; successful individuation is not necessarily legitimate organization; and effective agency is not necessarily answerable agency.

These distinctions will prevent the later political and moral articles from making claims that the earlier organizational theory cannot independently support.

# Part I — The constitution of organization

The first three articles establish the ontology and formal machinery. Their objective is to explain how relationships acquire organizational significance before introducing claims about morality or political legitimacy.

---

## Article 1. Becoming Through Relations

### Organizational individuation, character, and the emergence of agency

<caption>Foundational theory · Organizational ontology · Philosophy of agency</caption>

**Abstract**

Organizational individuals are commonly characterized through boundaries, functional coherence, persistence or coordinated behavior. These properties, however, need not identify the same organizational achievements. This article develops a process-relational account of individuation in which individuals emerge through the formation and maintenance of constraints that organize the activity of their constituents. Particular attention is given to the distinction between descriptive unity, dynamical integration, constitutive organization and reflexive autonomy. Drawing upon representation theory, the article explores how organizational character may be investigated through invariant relationships and counterfactual response profiles without identifying character with a particular material realization or developmental lineage. It argues that organizational persistence is better understood through the maintenance and regeneration of characteristic relationships than through the preservation of fixed components. This provides a foundation for investigating agency, cooperation and institutional transformation across computational, biological, cognitive and social domains.

**Outline**

1. **The problem of organizational individuality.** Why boundaries, coupling strength, coherence and persistence provide different criteria of individuation.
2. **Constraints and constitutive relationships.** How processes participate in producing the conditions of one another's activity; the distinction between externally imposed and internally maintained organization.
3. **Nucleation and stabilization.** Transient supports, thresholds, reciprocal maintenance and the emergence of durable organizational regimes.
4. **Character and representation theory.** Distinguish character, realization, representation and lineage. Investigate transformation-sensitive profiles, invariants and equivalence classes without assuming every organizational transformation possesses group structure.
5. **Counterfactual individuation.** Compare organizations with matched ordinary behavior but different responses to intervention, component replacement, perturbation and withdrawal.
6. **Agency as a further achievement.** Explain why individuation alone does not establish autonomy, consciousness, moral standing or legitimate authority.

**Formal and experimental center**

Construct organizations with matched external behavior but different maintenance provenance. Remove initiating supports, exchange components, perturb relational constraints and investigate whether their characteristic capacities persist.

**Primary contribution:** A vocabulary and methodology for distinguishing what an organization is from how it originated, how it is implemented and how it persists.

**Record anchors:** TERM-088, TERM-100, CLAIM-137, CLAIM-194, CLAIM-417, CLAIM-474, CLAIM-522, CLAIM-600, CLAIM-1104.

**Connection:** This article establishes the sense in which the subsequent acquisition of relationships can transform the individuals participating in them.

## Article 2. The Contracts We Become

### Implicit contractual organization and the developmental constitution of interdependence

<caption>Relational ontology · Contract theory · Institutional emergence</caption>

**Abstract**

Contracts are ordinarily understood as explicit agreements establishing obligations among independently constituted parties. This article develops a broader account of organizational contracts as maintained relational constraints that establish conditions of reliance, contribution, acceptance and response. Such constraints can emerge implicitly through recurrent interaction, accommodation and complementary adaptation. Their formation can enable capacities unavailable to participants independently while transforming the participants' future alternatives and vulnerabilities. The article distinguishes reliable regularity, acquired expectation, dependence, coordinated reliance and contractual organization, avoiding the inference that every causal dependency constitutes a contract. It introduces relational invocation as an operation that can exercise, reproduce, establish or modify the relationship through which an interaction becomes consequential. This account provides a common architecture for studying service guarantees, learned interfaces, social conventions, institutional commitments and the development of collective agency without presupposing explicit negotiation or moral agency at every organizational scale.

**Outline**

1. **Beyond explicit agreement.** Assume-guarantee contracts, relational contracts, psychological contracts and the limits of each for a general process ontology.
2. **From regularity to maintained commitment.** How expectations become reliance, reliance becomes dependence, and dependencies become objects of regulation.
3. **Complementary adaptation.** Learned interfaces, First Contact RL, perspectival invitations and relationships formed through enactment.
4. **Contractual structure.** Assumptions, guarantees, directed tolerances, standing, jurisdiction, precedence, suspension, monitoring, repair and revision.
5. **Ambiguous relational invocation.** Distinguish ambiguity about an existing relationship, incompatible local specifications and genuinely unformed contractual terms.
6. **Stateful invocation.** How exercising a relationship can modify its future guarantees, expectations and authority.
7. **Contractual identity.** Persistence through renegotiation, personnel changes and substitution of implementation.

**Formal and experimental center**

Represent interaction through a developmental transition:

\[
\operatorname{Invoke}(q,S_t)
\longrightarrow
(a_t,C_{t+1},S_{t+1}),
\]

where \(C_{t+1}\) records changes in the relevant contractual organization.

Construct comparative systems exhibiting mere coupling, unilateral dependence, coordinated adaptation and maintained conditional commitments. Probe whether deviation produces different regulatory responses.

**Primary contribution:** A theory of contracts as organizational achievements rather than necessarily explicit antecedent agreements.

**Record anchors:** CLAIM-277, CLAIM-358, CLAIM-430, CLAIM-552, CLAIM-603, CLAIM-728, CLAIM-788, CLAIM-1156, TERM-017, TERM-101, TERM-103.

**Connection:** Article 1 establishes relational constitution; Article 2 identifies a particular kind of relationship through which interdependence becomes governable.

## Article 3. The Cost of Becoming

### Stored work, contractual lifecycles, and the resource-bounded geometry of possibility

<caption>Formal theory · Institutional economics · Organizational dynamics</caption>

**Abstract**

Organization changes not only which activities are possible but also their resource requirements, temporal accessibility and dependence upon authorization. This article develops a lifecycle cost model for organizational constraints, treating stabilized arrangements as stored work that can amortize repeated coordination while introducing maintenance obligations, interpretive costs and barriers to transformation. Construction, invocation, verification, upkeep, remediation, revision and dissolution are distinguished as independently variable expenditures distributed across participants and timescales. The model incorporates implicit contract formation, erosion of alternatives, reconfiguration bottlenecks and actor-relative authority. It argues that local efficiency need not establish systemic savings and that the affordability of an organizational state does not establish the affordability of reaching it. A resource-bounded geometry of organizational possibility is proposed in which the relevant objects are not merely attainable outcomes, but costed and authorized transformation pathways. The resulting framework connects organizational development to institutional path dependence, operational readiness and the economics of dependence.

**Outline**

1. **Organization as stored work.** Why stabilized constraints can substitute for repeated construction, inquiry and negotiation.
2. **The general cost model.** Formation, retrieval, verification, maintenance, staleness, revision and the erosion of alternatives.
3. **Contracts as stateful stored work.** The costs of identifying, disambiguating, invoking and modifying relational specifications.
4. **Distributed accounting.** Distinguish work eliminated, transferred, deferred and embodied in acquired capacities.
5. **Timescale separation and readiness.** Maintenance of rarely exercised capabilities; reconfiguration slack and the opportunity cost of immediate demands.
6. **Reachability and transition asymmetry.** Organizational states can be affordable while transitions between them remain inaccessible.
7. **Authority-sensitive cost landscapes.** Distinguish physical feasibility, affordability, authorization and normative permissibility.

**Formal and experimental center**

Extend the existing TERM-114 and CASE-326 machinery to actor-indexed resource and transformation profiles:

\[
\mathcal R_i(S;B,H,W)
=
\{S':S'\text{ is reachable by }i
\text{ under }B,H,W\}.
\]

Investigate when recurrent successful coordination expands collective reach while contracting the independent reach of particular participants.

The article should explicitly compare this model against transaction-cost economics, incomplete-contract theory, dynamic optimization and existing resource-bounded planning frameworks.

**Primary contribution:** A mathematical language for analyzing what organizational arrangements make cheap, expensive, accessible, inaccessible or dependent upon others.

**Record anchors:** TERM-114, TERM-116, CLAIM-303, CLAIM-358, CLAIM-569, CLAIM-571, CLAIM-760, CLAIM-761, CLAIM-891, CLAIM-921, CASE-326.

**Connection:** Article 2 explains what contracts are; Article 3 explains why organizations acquire them, why they persist, and why their modification can become difficult.

# Part II — The organization of agency and inquiry

The next three articles explain how organizational individuals encounter discrepancies, mobilize resources, form judgments and transform themselves.

The guiding question becomes: how does something become significant enough to govern organizational activity?

## Article 4. What Matters to an Organization?

### Affect, frustration, and the negotiation of practical authority

<caption>Philosophy of mind · Organizational affect · Distributed agency</caption>

**Abstract**

Affect is typically investigated as a property of individual psychological experience, yet many of its regulatory functions concern the organization of activity among processes with competing requirements. This article develops a functional account of organizational affect grounded in concern-relative appraisal, frustration, action readiness and the allocation of practical authority. It distinguishes the significance of a circumstance to a constituent perspective from its significance to the encompassing organization, and separates claimed precedence, granted authority and realized efficacy. Frustration is characterized through the obstruction of maintained demands, while affective regulation concerns the recruitment, coordination and suspension of corrective activity. Implicit contracts and learned tolerances are shown to provide a potential means of amortizing recurrent regulatory negotiation. The account explains how fluent coordination, persistent conflict, chronic protective mobilization and suppression can emerge from different governance arrangements. Functional affective organization is distinguished throughout from claims about phenomenal experience.

**Outline**

1. **Affect without premature psychological attribution.** Functional organization versus phenomenal experience.
2. **Concern-relative significance.** How perspectives establish distinctions between desirable, threatening, frustrating and indifferent circumstances.
3. **Frustration and incompatibility.** Separate unmet requirements, distress, motivational conflict and jointly unrealizable demands.
4. **Will as negotiated practical authority.** Precedence, jurisdiction, suspension, conditional grants and effective intervention.
5. **Implicit contracts as affective infrastructure.** Learned tolerances, reliable accommodation, expectation formation and reduced recurrent arbitration.
6. **Pathological regulation.** Chronic emergency mobilization, anxiety-maintaining avoidance, compensatory overwork and suppression masquerading as harmony.
7. **Flow, recovery and flexibility.** Distinguish cheap coordination from the disappearance of effective opposition.

**Formal and experimental center**

Compare governance architectures under competing demands using directed tolerance and acceptance functions:

\[
Q_{i\to j}(y,c)
=
\mathbf 1[d_i(y,c)\leq\tau_{i\to j}(c)].
\]

Allow both the tolerances and authority relationships to evolve through interaction. Compare fixed priorities, learned accommodation, protected dissent and reflexively revisable governance.

**Primary contribution:** An account of affect and will as related but distinguishable organizational achievements that can be investigated across scales.

**Record anchors:** CLAIM-039, CLAIM-283, CLAIM-368, CLAIM-437, CLAIM-440, CLAIM-442, CLAIM-443, CLAIM-446, CLAIM-451, CLAIM-552, TERM-094, TERM-104.

## Article 5. Inquiry Changes the Inquirer

### The formation, revision, and governance of organizational possibility

<caption>Epistemology · Pragmatism · Learning theory</caption>

**Abstract**

Inquiry is often modeled as the acquisition of answers to questions specified in advance. This article develops a broader organizational account in which inquiry can transform the capacities, evaluative distinctions and relationships through which questions become formulable and answerable. The distinction between inquiry within a specification and inquiry into the specification itself is generalized through a resource-bounded model of formulation, intervention, observation, evaluation and response. Stabilized results of inquiry can become embodied in heuristics, contractual arrangements and institutional mechanisms, reducing subsequent investigative costs while introducing risks of staleness and closure. The article introduces adaptive inquiry readiness as a maintained capacity to investigate consequential but presently unfamiliar discrepancies. It argues that an organization can improve its ordinary problem-solving performance while losing the ability to discover what its existing problem specifications exclude. Inquiry is thereby situated within the continuing self-formation of organizational individuals.

**Outline**

1. **The limits of answer-centered epistemology.** Questions, discrepancies and specifications as distinct objects.
2. **Frustration before formulation.** How organizational inadequacies can become practically significant before they become explicit questions.
3. **The inquiry frontier.** Languages, answer spaces, interventions, observations, evaluation criteria and resource budgets.
4. **Inquiry as contractual activity.** Authorization, evidence provision, independent evaluation, escalation and the conditions of uptake.
5. **Stored inquiry.** How solutions become habits, concepts, runbooks, institutions and compiled governance.
6. **Specification revision.** The distinction between finding an answer, changing the question and acquiring the capacity to pose new questions.
7. **Adaptive inquiry readiness.** Protected exploration, independent expertise and resources for investigating unfamiliar inadequacies.

**Formal and experimental center**

Develop the existing joint inquiry frontier:

\[
\mathcal F_C(B,H)
=
\{(q,a):
q,a\text{ are jointly realizable under }C,B,H\}.
\]

Compare organizations with identical answer-producing capabilities but different authority arrangements, formulation capacities and investigative budgets.

Investigate authorization traps in which evidence is required to authorize the inquiry necessary to produce that evidence.

**Primary contribution:** A developmental theory of inquiry as the governance and transformation of epistemic possibility, rather than information acquisition alone.

**Record anchors:** CLAIM-470, CLAIM-475, CLAIM-1003, CLAIM-1016, CLAIM-1054, CLAIM-1060, CLAIM-1115, CLAIM-1140, CLAIM-1177, CLAIM-1190, TERM-095, TERM-160, TERM-163.

**Connection:** Article 4 explains how discrepancies recruit activity. Article 5 explains how that activity can become inquiry and produce organizational transformation.

## Article 6. Governing the Conditions of Judgment

### Cognitive heuristics, reflexive control, and the organized production of ignorance

<caption>Cognitive science · Agnotology · Philosophy of autonomy</caption>

**Abstract**

Cognitive biases and heuristics are commonly characterized through systematic deviations from independently specified standards of rational judgment. This article develops an organizational account in which heuristics can embody amortized inquiry and learned governance, while biases can arise from inadequacies in sampling, representation, evaluation, authority allocation and corrective maintenance. These mechanisms are connected to reflexive control: influence exercised through the conditions under which another process forms judgments, recognizes reasons and governs its own activity. The article distinguishes manipulation of evidence, manipulation of evaluative criteria and manipulation of genuine normative circumstances. It further examines how feedback channels and investigative capacities can become selectively maintained, producing ignorance without requiring false beliefs or deliberate deception. A character-based methodology is proposed for distinguishing organizational regimes with similar ordinary responses but different counterfactual sensitivity, developmental histories and capacities for correction. The resulting framework connects bounded rationality, motivated cognition, self-formation and autonomy.

**Outline**

1. **Heuristics as productive constraints.** Bounded rationality, ecological rationality and amortized judgment.
2. **A mechanism-based taxonomy of cognitive bias.** Sampling, salience, framing, inference, motivational weighting and social authority.
3. **Character-theoretic probes.** Role reversals, sensitivity to irrelevant transformations and invariance under consequential differences.
4. **Three routes of reflexive control.** Control over evidence, standards of judgment and the circumstances giving rise to genuine reasons.
5. **Agnotology as organizational achievement.** Selective disappearance, inquiry starvation and the maintenance of inaccessible distinctions.
6. **The developmental dimension.** How repeatedly exercised heuristics reshape the capacities available for subsequent judgment.
7. **Freedom as governance of governability.** Distinguish influence, external formation, manipulation and inadequately answerable control.

**Formal and experimental center**

Use a perception–evaluation–authority–maintenance architecture to test alternative explanations of confirmation bias, anchoring, avoidance, status quo bias and motivated skepticism.

Compare immediate behavioral changes with persistent changes in response profiles after the inducing context is removed.

The article should avoid claiming that every cognitive bias has one mechanism. Its contribution should be a common architecture for distinguishing mechanisms and specifying experiments.

**Primary contribution:** A theory connecting ordinary bounded cognition to reflexive manipulation and organized ignorance without treating them as identical.

**Record anchors:** CLAIM-199, CLAIM-404, CLAIM-424, CLAIM-446, CLAIM-545, CLAIM-619, CLAIM-657, CLAIM-703, TERM-064, CASE-148.

**Connection:** This article supplies the epistemic vulnerability mechanisms that become crucial when norms, institutions and asymmetrical relationships enter the picture.

# Part III — The constitution of normativity

These articles develop our account of norms, morality and cooperation. They should be especially careful about the transition from descriptive mechanisms to normative conclusions.

An explanation of how a norm becomes established cannot, by itself, establish why that norm deserves authority.

## Article 7. Memes That Make Worlds

### Cultural nucleation, hyperstition, and the formation of operative norms

<caption>Cultural evolution · Social ontology · Memetics · Performativity</caption>

**Abstract**

Memes are commonly understood as culturally transmissible informational units. This article develops a relational interpretation in which cultural expressions can function as partial specifications, probes or nucleation sites for organizational perspectives. Their significance depends not merely upon replication but upon the reconstruction of relationships through which they become practically consequential. The article connects memetic transmission to implicit contract formation, norm stabilization and hyperstition: developmental processes in which representations participate in producing the circumstances they represent. Particular attention is given to the distinction between genuine realization, antagonistic reproduction and apparent confirmation generated by restricted evaluation. Cultural expressions can facilitate the formation of expectations, commitments and structures of authority without exhaustively determining their subsequent realization. The resulting account distinguishes the circulation of a norm from its organizational realization, its maintenance and its justification.

**Outline**

1. **Memes in the original academic sense.** Replication, cultural transmission, attractors and the limits of informational individuation.
2. **Memes as nucleation sites.** Why a transmissible expression can initiate organizational formation without containing a complete specification.
3. **Memes as expressions of perspectival character.** Relate representation-theoretic invariants to cultural expressions without identifying memes literally with mathematical characters.
4. **Relational invitation and enactment.** How performing a possible relationship can help establish it.
5. **Hyperstition and performative feedback.** Representations that mobilize activity, induce investments and participate in producing their referents.
6. **Norm formation.** Regularity, expectation, reliance, regulatory commitment, convention and operative norm.
7. **Cultural pathology.** Self-sealing attributions, apparent consensus, antagonistic reproduction and memes that reorganize interpretive authority.

**Central illustrative case**

The phrase *I am whatever you say I am* serves as a recurring probe of attributed identity, reflexive control and self-fulfilling characterization.

The same expression can be enacted through acceptance, resistance, strategic conformity or protective withdrawal. These are potentially different organizational characters despite sharing a cultural token.

The article would also distinguish a representation that genuinely helps produce a new social reality from one that merely controls the evidence by which its alleged realization is judged.

**Primary contribution:** A developmental theory of cultural transmission that treats memes as potential vehicles of relational organization rather than merely reproduced content.

**Record anchors:** CLAIM-280, CLAIM-402, CLAIM-415, CLAIM-426, CLAIM-430, CLAIM-621, CLAIM-627, CLAIM-785, TERM-027, TERM-075, TERM-123, CASE-142, CASE-189.

**Connection:** Articles 2 and 4 explain how relationships stabilize; Article 7 explains how cultural representations can participate in that stabilization and transport it across organizational settings.

## Article 8. What We Owe Through Cooperation

### Moral standing, legitimate expectations, and the governance of relational becoming

<caption>Moral philosophy · Contractualism · Cooperation · Social ontology</caption>

**Abstract**

Accounts connecting morality to cooperation frequently emphasize the conditions under which individuals can overcome conflict and realize mutually beneficial outcomes. This article develops a complementary account centered on the normative significance of what cooperation produces. Recurrent cooperation generates capacities, expectations, dependencies and relationships of authority that transform the participants themselves. These developmental consequences can create new circumstances of vulnerability, responsibility and legitimate concern. The article distinguishes functional requirements, preferences, demands, commitments, claims, standing, duties and violations, arguing that operative normative organization must remain distinguishable from independently justified moral requirements. It investigates whether and under what conditions acquired reliance and constitutive participation generate special obligations. Morality is thereby situated not merely as a mechanism enabling cooperation, but as a domain of inquiry concerning the legitimate terms upon which interdependent individuals may shape one another's possibilities.

**Outline**

1. **Morality and cooperation.** Compare evolutionary morality-as-cooperation accounts, joint agency and contractualist theories.
2. **The normative vocabulary.** Reasons, preferences, principles, commitments, claims, standing, duties, harms, violations and wrongs.
3. **From expectations to legitimate expectations.** Why repeated conduct and induced reliance can be morally significant without automatically generating obligations.
4. **Directed responsibilities.** Duties owed to particular parties, accountability and the maintenance of the capacities necessary to discharge obligations.
5. **Morality across organizational scales.** Distinguish system viability from constituent welfare and the claims of outsiders.
6. **Standing and effective realization.** Recognition, legibility, volitional efficacy, correctability and the independent significance of appropriate procedural participation.
7. **The limits of cooperation.** Harmful cooperation, justified refusal, dissent, care and obligations toward people who cannot reciprocate.

**Argumentative center**

Evaluate the competing possibilities already represented by CLAIM-959 and CLAIM-960:

- Membership in a constitutive cooperative arrangement may generate distinctive obligations given an independently justified associative principle.
- Constitutive participation may add no independent moral grounds beyond vulnerability, interests, rights and power.

The article should not settle this dispute merely by introducing relational terminology. It should identify what each account can explain and which cases might distinguish them.

**Primary contribution:** A bridge between developmental organizational theory and moral philosophy that avoids deriving moral authority directly from functional organization.

**Record anchors:** CLAIM-075, CLAIM-523, CLAIM-671, CLAIM-723, CLAIM-738, CLAIM-739, CLAIM-742, CLAIM-959, CLAIM-960, CLAIM-1155, TERM-083, TERM-122, TERM-128.

**Connection:** Article 7 explains how operative norms form. Article 8 investigates whether, when and why those norms deserve authority.

## Article 9. The Political Economy of Dependence

### Exploitation, domination, and the asymmetric governance of implicit contracts

<caption>Political philosophy · Institutional economics · Moral psychology</caption>

**Abstract**

Exploitation is often understood as an unfair distribution of the benefits of cooperation. This article investigates a developmental mechanism through which cooperation can produce the very dependencies that make exploitative relationships possible. Reliable provision enables specialization and the acquisition of new capacities while potentially eroding independent alternatives. The costs of withdrawal, contestation and renegotiation can consequently become asymmetric even as ordinary cooperative performance improves. The article distinguishes indispensable contribution from control over the terms of dependence, and examines how conscientiousness, moral commitments and regulatory compensation can become exploitable resources. Drawing upon the framework of contractual lifecycle costs, it characterizes domination through consequential discretion combined with inadequate protection, influence, challenge or justified withdrawal. It further investigates reflexive counter-normativity: the maintenance of arrangements through which justified claims become prevented from constraining the organization responsible for their frustration.

**Outline**

1. **Exploitation within successful cooperation.** Why exploitation need not be defection, deception or refusal to contribute.
2. **Dependency-producing exploitation.** Distinguish creation of new dependence from imposing new encumbrances within an existing dependence.
3. **Specific investment and alternative erosion.** Hold-up, sunk relational work, bargaining power and the costs of rebuilding independent capacities.
4. **The exploitation of conscientiousness.** Hidden regulatory subsidies, compensatory effort, asymmetric commitments and apparent consent.
5. **Contractual ambiguity as leverage.** Control over whether an established practice is treated as a favor, duty, discretionary contribution or enforceable expectation.
6. **Domination and normative manipulation.** How legitimate duties can themselves become mechanisms of constrained exit.
7. **Reflexive counter-normativity.** The governance of standing, evidence, corrective authority and the feasibility of resistance.

**Formal and experimental center**

Construct cooperative arrangements with matched aggregate output but different distributions of:

- Maintenance and corrective costs.
- Independent fallback capacities.
- Discretion over modification of contractual terms.
- Resources for contestation and renegotiation.
- Effective protection against unjustified interference.

Investigate how these distributions develop over time.

Simple bargaining models can characterize how changes in outside options redistribute surplus, but they must be supplemented by independently justified standards before asymmetric outcomes are classified as exploitation.

**Primary contribution:** A developmental theory of exploitation and domination grounded in changing relationships of dependence, rather than static asymmetry alone.

**Record anchors:** CLAIM-624, CLAIM-669, CLAIM-670, CLAIM-677, CLAIM-682, CLAIM-686, CLAIM-687, CLAIM-690, CLAIM-700, CLAIM-703, CLAIM-713, CLAIM-716, CLAIM-731, QUESTION-472.

**Connection:** Article 8 provides the normative distinctions necessary to evaluate the organizational mechanisms established in Articles 2 and 3. Article 9 brings those mechanisms together into a theory of unjust interdependence.

### What Part III establishes

Cooperation is no longer treated merely as a process through which agents obtain outcomes.

It is a process through which agents become differently constituted.

Morality concerns, among other things, the legitimate exercise of the power participants acquire through that process.

Justice concerns the organization of the relationships through which that power is maintained, distributed and constrained.

This is the conceptual culmination of our earlier manuscript, *Becoming Through One Another*, but the article sequence would allow its constituent arguments to be developed and evaluated independently.

## Article 10. Reliability Beyond Uptime

### Site reliability engineering as the governance of constitutive interdependence

<caption>Systems engineering · Organizational science · Applied formal theory</caption>

**Abstract**

Site reliability engineering offers a mature practical domain in which distributed processes establish, monitor and revise conditional commitments governing their interdependence. This article interprets service-level objectives, error budgets, incident response, dependency management, toil reduction and recovery exercises through a general theory of resource-bounded contractual organization. It distinguishes operational reliability from regulatory, developmental and constitutional reliability, arguing that successful ordinary performance can coexist with erosion of the capacities required for correction and transformation. The article examines how highly reliable provision can induce fragile downstream dependencies, how local corrective policies can generate systemic frustration, and how recurrent mitigation can consume the resources necessary for structural remediation. Through a series of computational and institutional case studies, it investigates the conditions under which reliability engineering supports not only the persistence of an organization but its continuing capacity for self-formation.

**Outline**

1. **The SRE vocabulary as an organizational vocabulary.** SLIs, SLOs, SLAs, acceptance conditions, tolerances and conditional grants.
2. **Reliability and dependency formation.** Google's Chubby case as evidence of downstream reliance exceeding declared guarantees.
3. **Error budgets and temporal appraisal.** Conditional precedence, burn rates, resource allocation and the suspension of otherwise legitimate activities.
4. **Incident management as temporary will-organization.** Recruitment, coordination, exceptional authority, escalation and de-escalation.
5. **Toil and stored regulatory work.** Runbooks, automation, structural remediation, operational overload and protected engineering capacity.
6. **Systemic frustration.** Retry amplification, priority inversion, backpressure, load shedding and graceful degradation.

## Article 11. The Politics of Legibility

### Observability, measurement naivety, metric tyranny, and privacy-preserving correctability

<caption>Information theory · Agnotology · Privacy · Public administration</caption>

**Abstract**

Organizational observability is indispensable to coordination, accountability and correction, yet the information enabling these activities can also become a control surface for coercion and domination. This article develops a directed, task-relative account of legibility that distinguishes informational access, interpretive competence, intervention authority and the governance of their coupling. Data transformations are shown to preserve selected distinctions while obscuring others, such that predictive adequacy and aggregate performance cannot establish normative adequacy. When metrics acquire authority through targets, incentives and contractual relationships, they can participate in reorganizing the processes they measure. The article distinguishes static proxy misalignment from performative measurement reactivity, transferred regulatory work, developmental capability erosion and incentives against discovering violations. It proposes privacy-preserving correctability as a higher-order organizational achievement in which relevant deficiencies can become effectively investigable without unnecessarily exposing constituents to discretionary intervention.

**Outline**

1. **The dual use of observability.** Why better diagnosis and more effective domination can be enabled by the same informational infrastructure.
2. **Directed legibility.** Society legible to institutions versus institutional governance legible to society.
3. **The mathematics of data transformation.** Fibre preservation, coarse-graining, task-relative sufficiency and bounded interpretive resources.
4. **Measurement naivety and proxy objectives.** Distinguish measurability, predictive validity, causal adequacy and normative justification.
5. **From metric to KPI to authority.** How targets and consequences participate in organizing institutional will.
6. **The tyranny of metrics.** Goodhart-type failures, commensuration, selection distortion, goal displacement, cost transfer and capability erosion.
7. **Measurement-induced ignorance.** Why systems can become more successful by making their deficiencies less discoverable.
8. **Surveillance and protected opacity.** Contextual integrity, encapsulation, chilling effects and the conditions of independent individuation.
9. **The constitution of measurement.** Purpose limitation, bounded jurisdiction, independent validation, contestability and legitimate revision.

**Formal center**

The article can combine three of the framework's strongest mathematical objects.

| Object | Purpose |
|---|---|
| \(T:X\to Y\) | Data transformation and loss of relevant distinctions |
| \(L_{o,Q,B}(T)\) | Legibility to a resource-bounded observer for a task |
| \(\kappa_{A\to B}(g;M,W,H)\) | Cost of realizing an intervention through a monitoring and authority arrangement |

The central question is how informational transformations alter the joint realizability of justified coordination, correction, privacy and protection against domination.

**Empirical center**

Espeland and Sauder's law-school ranking research; healthcare expenditure used as a proxy for medical need; administrative performance targets; privacy-protecting constitutional arrangements; and SRE's distinction between diagnostic indicators and governing objectives.

These cases should be selected to isolate different mechanisms, rather than presented as interchangeable examples of Goodhart's law.

**Primary contribution:** A general theory of measurement as both epistemic transformation and practical governance, incorporating its consequences for agency, viability and justice.

**Record anchors:** TERM-064, TERM-108, CLAIM-194, CLAIM-199, CLAIM-348, CLAIM-350, CLAIM-359, CLAIM-404, CLAIM-523, CLAIM-700, CLAIM-736, CLAIM-760, CLAIM-1064, CLAIM-1117, NOTE-1050.

## Article 12. Agency Without Answerability

### Greedy agency, enshittification, shareholder primacy, and institutional sociopathy

<caption>Business ethics · Corporate governance · Political economy · Organizational agency</caption>

**Abstract**

Institutions can acquire increasingly sophisticated capacities for prediction, coordination and control without developing corresponding capacities for normative responsiveness. This article investigates the developmental separation of effective agency from answerability through four related concepts: measurement naivety, greedy agency, enshittification and institutional sociopathy. It argues that narrow objective-function governance can exclude the independent significance of affected parties' legitimate claims while maintaining extensive instrumental knowledge of their circumstances. Successful cooperation can subsequently induce dependencies that enable asymmetric modification of contractual terms, while performance metrics and restricted inquiry conceal the resulting burdens. Shareholder value maximization is examined as a candidate realization of this architecture where non-shareholder interests are recognized exclusively through their contribution to financial returns. The article develops a nonclinical, functional account of institutional sociopathy grounded in the persistent organization of normative unresponsiveness, and proposes counterfactual tests distinguishing ordinary institutional error from reflexively maintained failures of answerability.

**Outline**

1. **Agency as an incomplete achievement.** Why organizational intelligence, competence and autonomy do not establish moral responsiveness.
2. **Greedy agency.** Distinguish temporal short-sightedness from jurisdictionally restricted optimization.
3. **Enshittification.** Genuine enablement, contractual stabilization, dependency formation, asymmetric renegotiation and extraction.
4. **Metrics of constrained behavior.** Retention mistaken for satisfaction, silence for consent, compensation for adequacy.
5. **Shareholder primacy as normative architecture.** Distinguish financial objectives, legal duties, cultural interpretations and the instrumental treatment of stakeholders.
6. **The outsourcing of normative governance.** Corporate reliance upon competition, regulation, law and ethical custom; the vulnerability of these external constraints.
7. **Reflexive capture.** When the constraints governing optimization themselves become objects of optimization.
8. **Institutional sociopathy.** Functional empathy without normative responsiveness; collective agency without clinical attribution.
9. **Institutional remorse and repair.** Recognition, responsibility, corrective mobilization, restitution, structural revision and persistence after scrutiny declines.
10. **Alternative governance arrangements.** Normatively constrained optimization, stakeholder standing, independent oversight and credible challenge.

**Central distinction**

Shareholder value can remain a legitimate objective without possessing unrestricted jurisdiction over the institution's relationships.

The article should compare unrestricted optimization,

\[
\arg\max_{x\in X}V_S(x),
\]

with optimization over independently justified admissible arrangements,

\[
\arg\max_{x\in X_N}V_S(x).
\]

It must then investigate who defines \(X_N\), how adequacy is evaluated and whether affected parties possess appropriate means of challenging its boundaries.

**Empirical and argumentative center**

Doctorow's enshittification account; Bakan's corporate psychopathy thesis; stakeholder theory; Blair and Stout's team-production account of the corporation; Friedman's division of commercial and moral governance; Jensen's enlightened value maximization; and research on institutional measurement and exploitation.

The article should distinguish evidence of extraction from evidence of structural lock-in and evidence of deliberate normative exclusion. None automatically establishes the others.

**Primary contribution:** An account of how institutional agency can develop into increasingly effective but inadequately answerable organization.

**Record anchors:** TERM-108, CLAIM-348, CLAIM-523, CLAIM-624, CLAIM-670, CLAIM-682, CLAIM-700, CLAIM-713, CLAIM-760, CLAIM-1064, THEORY-150, THEORY-470, QUESTION-265.

**Connection:** Article 12 combines the mechanisms developed in Articles 6, 9 and 11 into a developmental account of institutional pathology.

The aim should not be to establish that corporations are inherently sociopathic. It should be to identify organizational arrangements that can explain systematic moral unresponsiveness independently of the personalities of their constituents.

## Article 13. The Operative Constitution

### American governance as a distributed ecology of legal and cultural normativity

<caption>Constitutional theory · Political science · Public administration · Institutional design</caption>

**Abstract**

Constitutional governance organizes partially autonomous institutions through explicit and implicit relationships of authority, reliance, constraint and correction. This article develops an account of the American system in which the operative constitution includes not only formal law but also historically acquired conventions, professional commitments, cultural expectations and institutional capacities. Drawing upon resource-bounded organizational theory and site reliability engineering, it distinguishes productive constitutional friction from unresolved institutional contention, nominal independence from effective corrective independence, and formal standing from accessible remediation. It investigates documented administrative and constitutional problems through mechanisms including correlated oversight failure, incompatible commitments, transferred regulatory burdens, emergency entrenchment and the consumption of developmental resources by recurrent operational demands. The article argues that constitutional adequacy cannot be assessed solely through legal specification or immediate governmental performance, but must also consider the continuing realization of legitimate authority, independent inquiry and effective means of correction.

**Outline**

1. **The American constitutional project.** Separation of powers, federalism, distributed will and overlapping jurisdiction.
2. **The operative constitution.** Interactions among written law, judicial interpretation, constitutional conventions, professional norms, civic expectations and moral principles.
3. **Constitutional norm formation.** How informal expectations become dependencies, regulatory commitments and sometimes explicit legal constraints.
4. **Corrective independence.** Common-mode institutional failures, party organization, expertise and the governance of oversight.
5. **Constitutional friction.** Vetoes, procedural protections, legislative contention, starvation and reconfiguration bottlenecks.
6. **Administrative pathologies.** Continuing resolutions, debt-limit impasses, fragmented disaster assistance, administrative burdens and corrective debt.
7. **Effective standing.** Legal rights, procedural eligibility, access costs, corrective latency and the protection of meaningful intervention opportunities.
8. **The governance of legibility.** Surveillance, public accountability, freedom of association, protected privacy and independent inquiry.
9. **Emergency governance.** Exceptional mobilization, authority transfer, review, termination and restoration.
10. **Civic reliability.** Operational, epistemic, corrective, developmental and constitutional reliability as distinct institutional achievements.

**Case-study architecture**

| Historical or institutional case | Principal theoretical question |
|---|---|
| Presidential two-term tradition and Twenty-Second Amendment | How do cultural norms become formal guarantees? |
| Electoral Count Reform Act | How does clarification of jurisdiction alter constitutional invocation? |
| Continuing resolutions | Can recurrent mitigation consume developmental capacity? |
| Debt-limit impasses | Can independently established requirements become compositionally incompatible? |
| Federal disaster assistance | When does distributed governance become pathological fragmentation? |
| Medicaid eligibility redetermination | When do administrative costs defeat effective realization of legal claims? |
| *NAACP v. Alabama* | Can protected opacity enable collective individuation? |
| *Carpenter v. United States* | How does technological legibility transform the practical scope of governmental power? |

These cases have appeared in our discussion and provide a useful initial corpus. Their legal and empirical details would require dedicated verification during manuscript preparation.

**Primary contribution:** An architectural account of constitutional governance connecting formal law, implicit contracts, institutional capacity, cultural normativity and resource-bounded correction.

**Record anchors:** TERM-017, TERM-114, TERM-122, TERM-128, TERM-163, CLAIM-523, CLAIM-571, CLAIM-710, CLAIM-711, CLAIM-728, CLAIM-731, CLAIM-760, CLAIM-921, CLAIM-1155.

## Article 14. The Constitution of Becoming

### Originalism, executive unity, conservatism, and the problem of constitutional regeneration

<caption>Political philosophy · Constitutional theory · Social ontology · Integrative synthesis</caption>

**Abstract**

Constitutional theories emphasize different properties of organizational continuity, including fidelity to historical meaning, unity of governing authority and the preservation of inherited institutions. This article examines these properties through a developmental account of organizational individuation. It distinguishes formative specification from continuing constitution, hierarchical authority from coordinated unity, and structural persistence from the preservation of valuable capacities. These distinctions illuminate potential inadequacies in strong interpretations of originalism, executive unitarism and preservationist conservatism, while retaining their legitimate concerns with arbitrary reinterpretation, coordination failure and the destruction of accumulated institutional knowledge. The article develops the concept of constitutional regeneration: the capacity to maintain valuable constitutive relationships through transformations that remain answerable to justified claims. Drawing upon the preceding theory of contracts, inquiry, legibility and organizational pathology, it argues that constitutional reliability includes the maintenance of effective means for discovering and transforming inadequate governance arrangements. Conservation is thereby reconsidered as the legitimate preservation of organizational achievements and the capacities necessary for their continuing realization.

**Outline**

1. **The constitution of constitutional authority.** Distinguish text, meaning, law, operative organization and normative justification.
2. **Originalism and temporal authority.** Formation provenance, semantic fidelity, institutional development and the independent grounds of continuing authority.
3. **Specification drift.** How unchanged formal constraints can acquire different practical consequences as capacities and environments transform.
4. **Executive unitarism and relational authority.** Distinguish responsibility, supervision, coordination, appointment, removal and authority over the conditions of correction.
5. **Conservatism and developmental authority.** Tradition as stored work, the selection criterion problem, path dependence and the costs of institutional caution.
6. **The interaction of the three theories.** Tensions among historical fidelity, concentrated direction and the preservation of historically acquired arrangements.
7. **Constitutional inquiry readiness.** The feasibility of discovering, formulating, evaluating and remedying inadequacies in governance itself.
8. **Regeneration versus persistence.** Maintain distinctions among lineage, character, realization, capacity, legitimacy and correctability.
9. **Conservative transformation.** Preserve valuable institutional achievements through appropriately governed changes in their implementation.
10. **Normatively answerable self-formation.** Freedom, justice, inquiry and the legitimate governance of organizational becoming.

**Formal center**

Develop a constitutional readiness profile:

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathcal G}(d;B,H)
=
\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{adm}}}
\Pr_\pi(\text{adequate correction of }d).
\]

Here the event being evaluated is explicitly restricted to correction within resource budget \(B\), horizon \(H\) and the applicable legal and normative constraints.

The formalism must not define constitutional readiness as unlimited permission to revise the constitution. A reliable constitution also protects commitments against inappropriate revision.

**Argumentative center**

The article should examine three distinct non-equivalences:

\[
\text{Formation}\not\equiv
\text{continuing constitution}
\]

\[
\text{Hierarchy}\not\equiv
\text{organizational unity}
\]

\[
\text{Persistence}\not\equiv
\text{preservation of value}.
\]

The objective is not to assume that every originalist, unitary executive theorist or conservative commits these conflations. It is to identify which arguments depend upon them, which sophisticated formulations avoid them, and what additional organizational questions remain unanswered.

This would provide a stronger scholarly contribution than an undifferentiated ideological critique.

**Primary contribution:** A general theory of constitutional conservation through normatively constrained self-formation.

**Record anchors:** CLAIM-075, CLAIM-417, CLAIM-474, CLAIM-523, CLAIM-545, CLAIM-569, CLAIM-571, CLAIM-624, CLAIM-728, CLAIM-760, CLAIM-761, CLAIM-1104, CLAIM-1156, TERM-100, TERM-114, TERM-128, TERM-163.

**Connection:** Article 14 is the philosophical culmination of the sequence. It returns to Article 1's question of organizational identity, but now with a developed account of contractual dependence, authority, normative standing, informational power and reflexive inquiry.

# A shared formal research program

Alongside the manuscripts, I would establish a common computational research program.

The articles should not each introduce an unrelated toy model. Where possible, they should progressively extend a shared organizational architecture.

## The common organizational state

A candidate general state representation is

\[
S_t=(P_t,C_t,K_t,W_t,M_t,B_t),
\]

where the components retain the meanings developed throughout our conversation.

| Component | Organizational property |
|---|---|
| \(P_t\) | Participating processes and their dispositions |
| \(C_t\) | Operative relationships and contractual constraints |
| \(K_t\) | Maintained capabilities and reserve capacities |
| \(W_t\) | Authority, standing and governing relationships |
| \(M_t\) | Monitoring, interpretation and evaluative apparatus |
| \(B_t\) | Resource availability and temporal constraints |

An encounter, perturbation, request or challenge \(q_t\) produces a developmental response:

\[
\mathcal T(S_t,q_t)
\longrightarrow
(a_t,\tau_t,\boldsymbol\kappa_t,S_{t+1}).
\]

The important feature is that the operation returns not only an immediate realization, but also an organization changed by the activity required to produce it.

Independent normative evaluation is then applied to the relevant history, relationships and consequences. It should not simply be identified with the operative evaluation apparatus \(M_t\).

## Six families of experiments

| Experimental family | Articles primarily served |
|---|---|
| Nucleation, persistence and component substitution | 1, 2, 7 |
| Contract learning, amortization and alternative erosion | 2, 3, 9 |
| Competing demands, conditional authority and regulatory effort | 4, 6, 10 |
| Inquiry access, monitoring and capability maintenance | 5, 6, 11 |
| Dependency-producing extraction and normative exclusion | 8, 9, 12 |
| Corrective independence and constitutional reconfiguration | 10, 13, 14 |

The experiments should increasingly investigate counterfactual response profiles rather than only average performance.

For example, two organizations can exhibit identical output, reliability, profitability or reported satisfaction while possessing profoundly different capacities to accommodate dissent, replace dependencies, investigate hidden deficiencies or revise contractual terms.

That gives the research program a distinctive methodological orientation:

**Match ordinary performance; perturb the governing relationships; observe the divergence in organizational trajectories.**

Each experimental article should identify competing explanations from mature adjacent fields.

| Nucleation investigation | Important comparison |
|---|---|

| Inquiry | Pragmatist epistemology, active learning, exploration |
| Cultural nucleation | Cultural evolution, social learning, performativity |
| Cooperation and normativity | Contractualism, joint agency, evolutionary ethics |

## Proposed abstract for the collected research program

<WritingBlock id="83146" variant="standard">This research program develops a process-relational theory of organizational individuation, inquiry and normative governance. Its central premise is that organizations emerge and transform through maintained relationships that make their constituents' activities mutually consequential. Such relationships can embody the results of prior accommodation and inquiry, establishing implicit contracts that enable new capacities while transforming expectations, dependencies, resource costs and governing authority.

A resource-bounded account of organizational possibility connects these developmental mechanisms to affect, practical agency, heuristic cognition and reflexive control. Inquiry is characterized not merely as the acquisition of answers, but as an organizational achievement through which questions, evaluative distinctions and conditions of realization can themselves become objects of transformation. Cultural expressions and norms can participate in these processes by nucleating, transporting and stabilizing relationships across organizational contexts.

The program distinguishes operative normativity from justified normative authority. Cooperation creates capacities and vulnerabilities through which participants acquire consequential power over one another, making the legitimate governance of interdependence a central object of moral inquiry. Exploitation and domination are investigated as developmental possibilities arising within successful cooperation, including through the erosion of alternatives, the asymmetric distribution of regulatory work and the defeat of effective standing.

Site reliability engineering provides a constructive domain for investigating contractual organization, corrective mobilization, adaptive readiness and systemic frustration. Its mechanisms are extended through analyses of observability, measurement, institutional incentives and informationally mediated power. These investigations illuminate how organizations can acquire increasingly effective agency while becoming less responsive to justified claims, producing pathologies of extraction, organized ignorance and reflexive counter-normativity.

The program culminates in a developmental account of constitutional governance. Organizational continuity is distinguished from historical fidelity, structural persistence and hierarchical unity, and constitutional regeneration is proposed as the capacity to maintain valuable constitutive relationships through appropriately governed transformation. Freedom, cooperation, justice and inquiry are thereby understood as interrelated but distinct achievements concerning the legitimate organization of possibility and the conditions under which individuals and institutions become capable of governing their own becoming.</WritingBlock>

# What I think is genuinely distinctive about the program

Much of the individual material has substantial precedent. The strength of the project should therefore lie in its synthesis, its formal distinctions and its ability to produce discriminating explanations.

I see seven especially promising theoretical contributions.

| Contribution | Significance |
|---|---|
| Relational invocation | Unifies contract formation, ordinary interaction and contractual modification |
| Developmental cost geometry | Explains how cooperation transforms the affordability of subsequent agency |
| Functional affective governance | Connects frustration, tolerances, authority and corrective mobilization |
| Adaptive inquiry readiness | Distinguishes answering existing questions from maintaining the ability to discover new ones |
| Normative agnotology | Explains how legitimate standing can be excluded despite extensive factual knowledge |
| Agency without answerability | Integrates exploitation, metric tyranny, enshittification and institutional moral unresponsiveness |

These are candidate contributions, not established novelty claims. Each should be evaluated against existing scholarship and the current state of the Nucleation record.

What began as an investigation of organizational nucleation has become an investigation of the relationship between individuation and interdependence.

The cost model explains why individuals acquire and maintain relationships. The contract model explains how those relationships become specifications of coordinated activity. The affective model explains how discrepancies recruit authority. The theory of inquiry explains how arrangements become intelligible and transformable. Memetics explains how representations participate in their formation and transport.

The moral theory investigates the legitimate terms of the interdependence thereby produced.

The theory of institutional pathology explains how organizations can become increasingly effective at pursuing their objectives while defeating the standing, inquiry and corrective capacities that ought to constrain them.

And constitutional theory investigates how those achievements and vulnerabilities can be governed collectively.

The final conclusion is not that individuals should be independent of every encompassing organization, or that encompassing organizations should subordinate every individual to their own purposes.

It is that interdependence is itself a developmental achievement whose terms require continuing governance.

**Cooperation concerns what we can become through one another.**

**Morality concerns what we may legitimately make of one another.**

**Justice concerns the organization of the power we thereby acquire.**

**Inquiry concerns how that organization becomes intelligible and revisable.**

**Freedom concerns our effective participation in determining what we continue to become.**

That is the intellectual arc I would want the fourteen articles to establish.
