# CASE-175: CECOT, 2025: people removed from the United States into Salvadoran custody despite court orders, the US saying it could not bring them back and El Salvador's president that he could not send them, until coordinated transfers out showed the two governments together could

## The case

The owner, at U7: "A good one here is the DOJ ignoring court orders not to
deport people to CECOT, then claiming they didn't have the authority to
bring them back, while CECOT claimed they didn't have the authority to
send them back".

On 15 March 2025 the United States flew Venezuelan detainees, removed
under the Alien Enemies Act, and others to El Salvador, where they were
held in the Terrorism Confinement Center (CECOT), after Judge James
Boasberg had ordered such removals halted in *J.G.G. v. Trump*. Among
those removed was Kilmar Abrego García, whose removal to El Salvador a
standing withholding order forbade. On 10 April 2025 the Supreme Court
left in place the requirement that the government "facilitate" his
release, the United States having acknowledged that the removal was
illegal ([LIT-1606](../literature.d/LIT-1606.md)). At the White House on 14 April, the Attorney General said
the matter was El Salvador's, and President Bukele said: "I don't have the
power to return him to the United States." Abrego García was returned on
6 June 2025 to face federal criminal charges, and in July 2025 the
Venezuelans held at CECOT were sent to Venezuela in an exchange of
prisoners.

The exchange added particulars the record has not checked, kept as the
exchange's (A7): that DOJ argued the oral instruction to turn the planes
around was not binding as the plaintiffs claimed; that Boasberg found
probable cause for a criminal-contempt inquiry; that when asked whether he
could release Abrego García inside El Salvador, Bukele did not claim
inability but refused; and that on 22 December 2025 Boasberg found the
United States in constructive custody of the Venezuelans, from the origin
of the arrangement, the absence of an independent Salvadoran interest in
holding them, and their release when the United States sought it.

## What it shows

- **No unilateral power is not no power** ([CLAIM-457](../claims.d/CLAIM-457.md)). The United States said it
  could not compel a foreign state; El Salvador's president said he could
  not admit a man to the United States. Both can be true while the two
  together can return him, and the June return and the July exchange show
  that they could. A7: "No single institution has unilateral control"
  does not imply "No institution, or combination of institutions, can
  remedy the situation."
- **Custody, control and responsibility, three boundaries**
  ([CLAIM-405](../claims.d/CLAIM-405.md)). Who physically held the prisoners: El Salvador. Who
  selected and sent them: the United States. Who could bring about their
  release: on the constructive-custody finding, the United States. A7:
  "These were different inquiries about the same institutional
  arrangement. A geographical boundary was insufficient to answer all of
  them."
- **Coordination without a joint agent** ([CLAIM-418](../claims.d/CLAIM-418.md)). The two
  governments need not form one agent to run one detention arrangement.
  A7: "coordination sufficient to produce an outcome does not necessarily
  imply an institutional arrangement sufficient to recognize, contest, and
  remedy that outcome" ([CLAIM-462](../claims.d/CLAIM-462.md)).
- **A forward organization without a reverse one** ([QUESTION-278](../questions.d/QUESTION-278.md)). The arrangement
  had procedures for selection, transport and custody, and none for
  undoing a transfer once a court objected. A9: "an asymmetry between the
  forward organization of action and the reverse organization of
  correction".
- **A claim of inability is evidence of a position** ([QUESTION-279](../questions.d/QUESTION-279.md)). A10: an
  assertion of inability "is evidence about the government's stated
  position. To establish actual incapacity, we would need an appropriately
  specified model of possible plans, institutional cooperation, legal
  constraints, and available resources."
- **A remedy late is a different remedy.** A7: "A right to contest removal
  before deportation, for example, is not fully restored by a much later
  opportunity to seek return," and the detention suffered is not undone by
  a later release.

## What it does not show

- **Contempt, or anyone's intent.** Whether officials defied an order was
  still before the courts when the exchange ran. A9: "Intent, causation,
  feasibility, and legal duty require separate evidence."
- **That joint ability is joint responsibility.** A8: "joint ability to
  produce an outcome does not itself imply joint legal responsibility for
  producing or correcting it." That needs a normative mapping [CLAIM-457](../claims.d/CLAIM-457.md) does not
  supply.
- **That Abrego García's return shows he could have been returned at every
  earlier moment.** A7: it "demonstrates that his physical location in
  Salvadoran custody had not made return impossible", no more.
- **Standing.** The removals, the order of 10 April, the White House
  remarks, the return and the July exchange are documented; the contempt
  proceedings and the custody ruling are the exchange's. *El-Masri v. the
  former Yugoslav Republic of Macedonia* ([LIT-1619](../literature.d/LIT-1619.md)) is the comparator A8
  proposed, for what is new here and what is the old problem of rendition.

## Note of 2026-10-29: the order and the comparator, read

The Supreme Court's order in *Noem v. Abrego Garcia*, [LIT-1606](../literature.d/LIT-1606.md), is now read
([NOTE-1376](../notes.d/NOTE-1376.md)). The case's account of it is right. Two particulars can be
added. The Court itself separated facilitating release from effectuating
return, and left the second open. And it asked the government to "share
what it can concerning the steps it has taken and the prospect of further
steps": a court asking for the evidence behind a claim of inability
before accepting it, which is [QUESTION-279](../questions.d/QUESTION-279.md)'s move.

*El-Masri*, [LIT-1619](../literature.d/LIT-1619.md), is read too ([NOTE-1391](../notes.d/NOTE-1391.md)), and it marks what is
new here. Three differences.

- **Where the routes of responsibility start.** In *El-Masri* they run from
  the sending State's own acts on its own territory: its agents held him,
  acquiesced in the CIA team's torture and authorized the flight to
  Kabul.
- **What the State is responsible for abroad.** Under Article 3 it is
  responsible for exposing him to the risk, and the Court says expressly
  that it does not adjudicate the receiving state's responsibility
  (§ 212). Only under Article 5 is the sending State responsible for the
  detention abroad itself, as part of a continuing enforced disappearance
  it facilitated (§§ 239–241).
- **The remedy.** Compensation and a finding: the applicant was by then
  free, and the Court ordered nothing about where he was.

So the question this case raises, which government could or had to undo
the transfer while the person was still held, does not arise in
*El-Masri*. That is the new part. The case is unchanged.

## Note of 2026-10-29: the remand orders, read

The district court's order of 15 April 2025 granting expedited discovery,
[LIT-1841](../literature.d/LIT-1841.md), is read ([NOTE-1417](../notes.d/NOTE-1417.md)), with the two orders it recites.
Particulars the case can take as documented:

- **The amended order of 10 April** (ECF No. 51), made hours after the
  Supreme Court's, dropped "effectuate" and required a declaration from
  someone with personal knowledge on Abrego Garcia's location and custody,
  the steps taken and the steps to come. On 11 April (ECF No. 61) the
  court found that the government had "made no meaningful effort to
  comply".
- **The order of 15 April** reads "facilitate" as taking the steps
  available toward his release from Salvadoran custody, measures that
  against the government's own past practice of facilitated returns, finds
  that no step had been shown, and opens discovery that includes who
  authorizes the detention. It does not decide whether return was
  possible.
- **The custody conflict** (footnote 3). A State Department declaration
  that he was held "pursuant to the sovereign, domestic authority of El
  Salvador" is set against a press report, quoted by the court and not
  checked here, that the United States paid six million dollars to house
  the detainees "pending the United States' decision on their long-term
  disposition". That bears on the case's line on custody and control
  ([CLAIM-405](../claims.d/CLAIM-405.md)).

The case is unchanged.
