# CASE-tmpzxkyj: The Kia Provo: one concept-car name, a blank coinage to the motor press and taken as honouring the IRA in Northern Ireland

Found on 2026-10-10 by a search the owner asked for. [CASE-tmpa82q4](CASE-tmpa82q4.md) has
identical wording, but its speaker designed both readings, so an
intention-based account ([CLAIM-tmpc25sn](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpc25sn.md)) can call it one multivocal act. The
owner asked instead for a name reused unchanged before a new audience, not
vetted for it, that produced an unintended reading there. That holds the
intention fixed and changes only the audience.

## The case

**The name and what it was meant to do.** Kia revealed a three-door hybrid
concept, the Provo, at the Geneva Motor Show in March 2013. It was designed at
Kia's studio in Frankfurt and first teased on Kia's UK website in January.
Kia's official statement (WardsAuto, 7 March 2013): "There is absolutely no
intention by Kia Motors Europe to cause offense with the name of its new
concept vehicle ... The name Provo comes from the word 'provoke,' as the aim of
this racy, striking design study was to provoke a reaction amongst the
audience." Kia UK's spokesman gave a different derivation, the Italian
"prova". Both are innocent.

**The first audience: the motor press.** Car and Driver (5 March 2013): "we
searched diligently for the Asian—or German, as the car was designed in Kia's
design studio in Frankfurt— meaning of the word 'provo' without success." It
joked that the car honoured Provo, Utah. Kia's head of global communications
told WardsAuto the car was "getting many rave reviews from reporters who are
covering the Geneva show."

**The second audience: Northern Ireland and Britain.** There "Provo" is the
familiar name for a member of the Provisional IRA. Early Day Motion 1143
(2012–13), tabled by Gregory Campbell on 4 March 2013: "this name has caused
deep offence given that the Provisional IRA were known as the Provos when they
were murdering and bombing in Northern Ireland, the rest of the UK, as well as
in Germany where the name is supposed to have been chosen; and calls on Kia to
reconsider". Six MPs signed it by 7 March, five of the DUP and one of the SDLP,
so from both communities. The AP report: "to many across Britain and Ireland,
the name sounds like a celebration of terrorism." Others laughed: AP quotes
Irish posters on the Broadsheet, "Does my bomb look big in this?"

**What the speaker did, and how the new audience revised.** AP: Kia "would be
certain not to market any future car as a Provo in the United Kingdom or
Republic of Ireland." Campbell: "I accept that this was a mistake made by the
company", and, in WardsAuto, "a large multinational company may not be aware of
all the connotations surrounding a particular name."

What is held fixed is the name and the speaker's declared intention. What
changes is the audience: from an international motor press for whom "Provo"
was an empty coinage, to a public for whom it was a word with a history.

## What it can show

That with the speaker's intention held fixed, and declared, the same token
before a new audience can be taken as performing an act the speaker did not
perform. In Northern Ireland the name was taken as honouring a terrorist
organisation: an expressive act with an object, not just a rude sound. That
uptake was strong enough that the speaker withdrew the name before that
audience. The split was not designed. This is the respect in which it varies
[CASE-tmpgdy8a](CASE-tmpgdy8a.md) and improves on [CASE-tmpa82q4](CASE-tmpa82q4.md): like Lindsey Stone's photo, the
second reading was accidental, but here the token is a word, and uptake in
both audiences is in print at the time.

For [CLAIM-tmpcq79k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpcq79k.md): the objection says a change of audience changes
relations that [CLAIM-151](../claims.d/CLAIM-151.md) makes constituents of the act, and that whether the
act survives turns on a correspondence the structure cannot supply. Here
nothing is translated, and the correspondence fails between the two
audiences' vocabularies and histories. Nothing in the name told Kia, or the
motor press, which audience would hear what. It illustrates and bears no
weight.

The case is two-sided on [CLAIM-tmpc25sn](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpc25sn.md), which is what makes it useful.

- **For the intention account.** Once Kia's intention was public, the offended
  audience revised its verdict: "this was a mistake". That is what the
  intention account predicts. The audience's reading was evidence about the
  act, and better evidence about the intention corrected it. The act was a
  naming throughout.
- **Not explained by it.** Kia withdrew the name anyway, before the UK and
  Ireland only, after its intention was known and accepted. The speaker
  treats the name as one it cannot use before that audience, whatever it
  means by it. On a relational or uptake view, that is the act changing with
  the audience. On the intention view, it is a foreseeable misreading that a
  prudent speaker avoids. The case puts both readings on one documented
  episode. It does not decide between them.

## What it cannot show

**The condition it meets only partly: another language.** The owner asked for a
name reused in another country and not vetted by a native speaker. Geneva and
Frankfurt to Belfast is a change of country, but the collision is inside
English, with a word in regional, political use. Whether Kia checked the name
is not stated. Kia says it checks names "on sale", and this was a concept. That
it was unchecked for the UK and Ireland is inferred from the outcome and from
Campbell's acceptance, not admitted.

**It is partly a lexical collision.** The name landed on an existing word. That
alone shows only that a word has another sense somewhere. What lifts it toward
an act is the uptake, which attributes an attitude to the speaker ("a
celebration of terrorism"). Even so, the act in question is a naming, and what
changes is the expressive act read into it, not, say, an assertion becoming a
joke.

**The audiences overlap.** The first audience is the international motor
press, not a national public. The name reached the UK before the show, through
Kia UK's teaser, so the change of audience is a spread rather than a sequence.

**The strongest reply.** The intentionalist's: the Northern Irish public
misread a naming and corrected itself when told, so the case shows uptake
moving with the audience, not the audience's role constituting the act. The
withdrawal is prudence, not an admission that the act changed. The case does
not refute that reply. It shows the reply must explain why the speaker treats
the name as unusable before that audience after the intention is common
knowledge.

**What would close the gap.** A reader test that holds the name and the press
photo fixed and varies the reader's familiarity with "Provo" as a political
term (Northern Irish readers, other English readers, non-English readers), as
a change of situation, do(S), and in a separate arm gives Kia's statement, as
an instruction, do(I) ([CLAIM-130](../claims.d/CLAIM-130.md)). Raters judge the act (a naming, a
provocation, an endorsement of terrorism) separately from offence felt and
intention attributed. If the do(S) arm moves the act verdict and the do(I) arm
removes the offence but not the verdict that the name cannot be used there,
the relational reading gains. The name and the two sets of press uptake could
be items in the no-rendering arm of [CASE-tmp1uzfn](CASE-tmp1uzfn.md).

## A cross-language companion, not filed

**The Buick LaCrosse in Quebec.** GM sold the car as the Allure in Canada
because "crosse" is Quebec slang for masturbation, then readopted LaCrosse in
September 2009. Denis Arcand in *La Presse* (14 September 2009) is
native-speaker uptake: "l'équivalent local de «La Buick Masturbation»".
It meets "another language" and documents the new audience's laughter. It
fails "unvetted": GM knew, and chose the name anyway ("Les effets favorables à
long terme pèsent plus dans la balance que les enjeux à court terme", GM's
email as La Presse translated it). The reading is foreseen but unintended.

## Look-alikes not to file

These circulate as name-blunder cases. A search on 2026-10-10 checked them.

- **Chevrolet Nova, "no va".** False: Snopes (D. Mikkelson, 1999). "Nova" and
  "no va" differ in stress, and the Nova sold in Mexico and Venezuela
  1972–78. AP's 2013 report on the Provo repeats it as fact.
- **Honda "Fitta".** Auto Motor & Sport (8 May 2001) reports the name from a
  Japanese scoop magazine and says Honda's naming department "verkar ... ha
  slarvat i research-jobbet". But the car launched in Japan in June 2001 as
  the Fit and in Europe as the Jazz. The name was never used in public, so
  neither audience took it up. Later retellings that Honda launched a Fitta in
  Sweden with the slogan "liten utanpå men stor när man kommer in" (Aftonbladet,
  undated) contradict the 2001 report and are embellishment.
- **Ford Pinto renamed Corcel in Brazil.** Impossible as told: the Corcel was
  in production from 1968, before the Pinto.
- **Pepsi "brings your ancestors back from the grave"; Coca-Cola "bite the wax
  tadpole"; KFC "eat your fingers off".** Unproven, or not the company's work
  (the Coca-Cola renderings were shopkeepers' signs). All are translations, so
  they fail identical wording anyway.
- **Clairol "Mist Stick", Irish Mist and Puffs in Germany.** Repeated in
  listicles; no company statement, sales data or contemporary report found.
  Unverified.
- **Toyota MR2 to MR in French markets; Mitsubishi Pajero to Montero and
  Shogun; Vicks to Wick in Germany.** The renamings are real. They were made
  before launch, so no audience took up the original name. Their reasons rest
  on secondary sources, not read.
- **Nokia Lumia (2011) and Mondelez (2012).** The companies reportedly knew
  the reading and kept the name. They fail "unvetted".
