# THEORY-593: Perceptual learning can install perceiver-specific frames that change which objects a perceiver individuates from the same input, so cognition can shape the perceptual system over time while leaving open whether any single percept is cognitively penetrated

## Source

Ivy & Mroczko-Wąsowicz (2025), *Framing Effects in Object Perception*,
[LIT-099](../literature.d/LIT-099.md), read in full in [NOTE-150](../notes.d/NOTE-150.md) (Review of Philosophy and Psychology
16:969–996).

## What was actually shown

**The definition (p. 975).** "A perceptual frame is an adaptation of the
perceptual system caused by perceptual learning and realized through
bottom-up functional processes such that sensory information is organized
in a subject-dependent way leading to idiosyncratic perceptual object
representations." Frames are defined against attention, which is
"universally accessible to any perceiver with normally functioning
capacities" (p. 974); a frame needs training. Simple objects, such as a
single dot in a lattice, are individuated by any normal perceiver, so the
thesis covers a class of objects only.

**The separation (§4, pp. 989–991).** Cognition has "a direct and
unmediated influence on perception insofar as it is through top-down
influences that perceptual frames are encoded" (p. 990). Once installed, a
frame needs no occurrent cognitive input: "the cognitive origin that
explains why a perceptual frame was implemented in the first place need not
be the same reason why that frame operates in early sensory processing".
Whether individual episodes are penetrable "remains an open question" (p.
990), and frames are compatible with penetration (fn. 2).

**The evidence offered.** Reliability-weighted cue integration with
learned priors (Ernst & Banks 2002; Helbig & Ernst 2007), prism-adaptation
after-effects, early amodal completion (initial responses about 140 ms,
differential about 240 ms), Teufel et al.'s (2018) two-tone images that
cannot be unseen, expert anticipation, and radiologists at about 200 ms per
image. The only first-party data is the authors' own eye-tracking study
(Ivy et al. 2023), cited for experts applying domain search patterns
outside their domain (p. 976).

**What could have come out otherwise.** Nothing in the paper. It runs and
proposes no test, and [NOTE-150](../notes.d/NOTE-150.md) grades its claims weak to moderate. What
would test it is in `promote_when`.

## What this does not say

- **That frames have been shown to differ from attention or imagery.** No
  cited study separates the accounts, and the argument against imagery
  rests on one study ([NOTE-150](../notes.d/NOTE-150.md), Limitations).
- **That frames operate bottom-up in any clear sense.** §3.iii sums up its
  evidence as the effect of "having applied a perceptual frame (a specific
  top-down prediction or prior knowledge)" (p. 988). On a
  predictive-processing reading, priors are applied top-down on every
  episode, and the paper does not say how that squares with its
  definition.
- **That different frames yield different objects.** The multisensory
  evidence shows different weightings and estimates, for example of bar
  height. "Different objects" is inferred.
- **That perception is not penetrable.** The paper leaves the episode-level
  question open. The seeded skim's "without penetrating any single
  percept" was corrected by [NOTE-150](../notes.d/NOTE-150.md) to "whether or not".
- **That modularists are refuted.** The verdict that Firestone & Scholl's
  formulation is "inaccurate" reads "direct, unmediated" diachronically.
  Perceptual learning is an effect they set aside as not penetration.
- **That every completion needs a frame.** The three-step argument of p.
  983 states the converse of its premise; its conclusion that completion
  needs a reason survives, but that the reason is always a frame is
  stipulated ("where there is 'prior knowledge,' there is a perceptual
  frame at work", p. 980).
