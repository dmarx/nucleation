# LIT-tmpzg9it: Essai sur le don. Forme et raison de l'échange dans les sociétés archaïques

Mauss, M. (1925). Essai sur le don. Forme et raison de l'échange dans les sociétés archaïques. *L'Année sociologique*, seconde série, t. I (1923–1924). Read in the Classiques des sciences sociales edition (2002), doi:10.1522/cla.mam.ess3, transcribed from *Sociologie et anthropologie* (PUF, 1968).

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, NOTE-tmpc9mii. Page numbers are the 2002 PDF's; quotes are Mauss's French, glossed.*

- **Obligatory gifts.** Exchange takes the form of gifts "en théorie
  volontaires, en réalité obligatoirement faits et rendus" (p. 7):
  voluntary in theory, obligatory in fact.
- **Three obligations.** Besides the duty to return, total prestation
  supposes "obligation d'en faire, d'une part, obligation d'en recevoir,
  de l'autre" (p. 18): the duty to give and the duty to receive.
- **The unreturned gift subordinates.** "accepter sans rendre ou sans
  rendre plus, c'est se subordonner, devenir client et serviteur" (p. 98):
  to accept without returning, or without returning more, is to become
  client and servant. In the potlatch the sanction is "l'esclavage pour
  dette" (p. 55), debt slavery.
- **Circuits close on the origin.** The hau "veut revenir au lieu de sa
  naissance" (p. 16), wants to return to where it was born; a kula
  valuable must be passed to a third, distant partner (p. 32). Reciprocity
  runs beyond the pair, but back to the source.
- **The modern lesson.** Charity "est encore blessante pour celui qui
  l'accepte" (p. 90), still wounds whoever accepts it; social insurance is
  owed to the worker for labour given (p. 92), yet the individual must be
  "forcé de compter sur soi plutôt que sur les autres" (p. 93), made to
  rely on himself rather than on others.

## Standing in the record

Filed with the gift-economy batch after the owner linked CLAIM-tmpqa0qu to
the gift economy of Burning Man. Mauss supplies a mechanism the claim
lacks: in a gift regime one-way supply is not neutral, since accepting
without return is subordination, so dependency is a matter of rank as well
as of lost capability. He also qualifies the claim twice: the Andamanese
exchange gifts while self-sufficient in tools (p. 26), and Mauss keeps
self-reliance beside the gift rather than replacing it. His account of
harm from one-way giving runs through status, where Ostrom et al.'s
Samaritan's dilemma (THEORY-tmph35ra) runs through effort. Filed from it:
