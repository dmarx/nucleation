# LIT-687: Responsiveness and Moral Responsibility

John Martin Fischer, "Responsiveness and Moral Responsibility", in Ferdinand
Schoeman (ed.), *Responsibility, Character, and the Emotions: New Essays in
Moral Psychology*, Cambridge University Press, pp. 81–106 (chapter 5) —
DOI-10.1017/CBO9780511625411.005. Reprinted in Fischer's *My Way: Essays on
Moral Responsibility* (Oxford University Press, 2006), which I did not
verify against a copy.

## Key takeaways

Read in the *My Way* reprint ([NOTE-tmprgzuf](../notes.d/NOTE-tmprgzuf.md)); pages are its pages.

- **What responsibility is.** An agent is morally responsible "insofar as he
  is rationally accessible to certain kinds of attitudes and activities as a
  result of performing the action" (p. 63), so he can be responsible for a
  morally "neutral" act.
- **Mechanism, not agent.** Frankfurt-type cases show an agent can be
  responsible though he could not have done otherwise. Hence "An agent is
  morally responsible for performing an action insofar as the mechanism that
  actually issues in the action is reasons-responsive" (p. 66).
- **Weak responsiveness.** Strong responsiveness is too demanding, because
  of weakness of will and culpable wrongdoing (pp. 67–68). What is required
  is that "there must exist some possible world in which there is a
  sufficient reason to do otherwise, the agent's actual mechanism operates,
  and the agent does otherwise" (p. 68).
- **History and tracing.** "Moral responsibility is a historical
  phenomenon" (p. 79), with tracing to a responsive mechanism at "some
  suitable earlier time" (p. 76). There is no ownership condition yet.
- **Semicompatibilism.** Responsibility "is compatible with causal
  determinism, even if causal determinism is incompatible with freedom to do
  otherwise" (p. 78).
- **Admitted gaps.** A seemingly insane agent weakly responsive only to a
  bizarre reason "poses a problem" (n. 11), and "same mechanism" is left
  "extremely vague" (n. 18).

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 as the origin of Fischer's reasons-responsiveness and
read on 2026-10-05, when the owner authorised the copy in Andrew M. Bailey's
collection of Fischer's papers. The reading confirms what the encyclopedia
reported ([LIT-291](LIT-291.md), supplement §B): the account was mechanism-based from the
start, rested on weak responsiveness, and had no ownership condition. Those
are the two things Fischer and Ravizza's book ([LIT-684](LIT-684.md); précis
[LIT-tmpd2o1w](LIT-tmpd2o1w.md)) changed.

**Its neighbours in the same volume.** Wolf's "Sanity and the Metaphysics of
Responsibility" ([LIT-690](LIT-690.md)) is chapter 3 and Frankfurt's "Identification and
Wholeheartedness" ([LIT-575](LIT-575.md)) is chapter 2. This paper does not discuss
either; it argues against Frankfurt's and Watson's hierarchical accounts in
general, as "purely structural and ahistorical" (p. 79).

**How it bears on [THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md).** That account says a condition stated only
in present psychology is met by a manipulated agent. This paper's historicism
is aimed at that problem, but its only historical element is tracing; the
ownership condition that answers manipulation comes in the 1998 book.
