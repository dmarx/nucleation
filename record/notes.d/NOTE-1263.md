# NOTE-1263: Future People, the Non-Identity Problem, and Person-Affecting Principles

## Contribution

Parfit's last answer to the problem he named. He keeps the No Difference
View: it makes no moral difference that a bad policy is worse for no one
(p. 123). He gives up his old defence of it, an impersonal principle, and
offers a person-affecting one instead. The work is done by two moves:
benefits can be intrinsic rather than comparative, so being caused to
exist can benefit someone; and outcomes are ranked by how much they
benefit people, whoever those people are.

## Key insight

"Worse for" and "less good for" come apart when different people exist.
A cheaper energy policy that ruins later centuries is worse for none of
the people who will live then, since without it they would not exist.
"But this policy would be much less good for these people than the more
expensive, better policy would have been for the different people who
would have existed instead" (p. 130). That is how it makes things go worse
and is wrong. The comparison is between how much good each outcome does
to whoever is in it, not between two states of one person.

## Assumptions

- **Intrinsic goodness.** Most good things in a life, such as
  friendships, are good in themselves, not by being better than an
  alternative (p. 132). So a life can benefit the person who lives it
  though never existing would not have been worse for them.
- **Stipulated cases.** Each extra year of life is an equal benefit, there
  are no other relevant differences, and indirect effects are ignored
  (p. 140). Parfit says his claims could be applied to more realistic
  cases, and does not do it.
- **Levels of well-being as rough numbers.** Lives are compared at levels
  such as 100, 50 and 1, with zero where a life stops being worth living;
  the differences are "much less precise than these numbers suggest"
  (p. 152).
- **The goodness of outcomes does not depend on what is possible to
  bring about**, nor on when or from where one compares them
  (pp. 145, 148).

## Key results

- **Narrow principles fail** (pp. 118–123). On the Narrow Telic
  Principle, one outcome cannot be worse if it would be worse for no one;
  on the Narrow Deontic, an act cannot be wrong if it would be worse for
  no one.
  - They imply that there being no future people would not be worse, so
    choosing thousand-year lives at the cost of having children would not
    be wrong. Parfit: "deeply mistaken" (p. 119).
  - Clare can conceive now a child who will live to 40, or in two months
    one who will live to 80. Most believe she ought to wait, but conceiving
    now is worse for no one. "Her child" names no particular person
    (p. 122).
  - Most choices of policy change who exists, and the effects spread "and
    would not, like ripples in a pool, diminish" (p. 122).
- **Other solutions fail** (pp. 124–129).
  - Appeals to rights, harm or wronging, (C), cannot separate Clare from
    Sarah, whose any child would live to 40 and who does no wrong. Clare's
    act is wrong only because she could have had a different child.
  - Boonin accepts the Narrow Deontic Principle and bites the bullet,
    including on a Risky Energy Policy that kills tens of thousands five
    centuries later. Parfit replies with (F): it is wrong to make things go
    much worse when another act would have no moral flaw. Demandingness is
    a different question.
- **Existential benefits and harms** (pp. 131–137). Following McMahan,
  being caused to exist with a life worth living is good for that person.
  - A life of fifty years of suffering is bad for Sam, and his never
    existing would not have been bad for him: (H), not (G), which would
    need a non-existent Sam (p. 133).
  - Harms cannot be intrinsic while benefits are essentially comparative.
    At most, reasons not to harm are stronger, by degree (p. 135).
  - Narveson's "make people happy, not make happy people" fails, since it
    is just as bad to make miserable people as to make people miserable.
  - The No Complainants Claim, that an act can be wrong only if someone is
    wronged, has "considerable psychological force" and some moral force
    (p. 136). But beneficence, giving people reasons to be grateful,
    matters as well as non-maleficence. Parfit does not return to the
    claim as promised (note 14).
- **Weak Narrow versus Wide** (pp. 137–144).
  - Weak Narrow Principle: an outcome is in one way worse if it is worse
    for people. Wide Principle: an outcome is in one way worse if it is
    less good for people, benefiting them less than the other would.
  - The Wide relation is about intrinsic goodness and is transitive. "Worse
    for people" is essentially comparative and, across different people,
    not transitive.
  - Case Two: A (Tom 60, Dick 80), B (Tom 80, Harry 60), C (Dick 60, Harry
    80). The Narrow Principle makes each worse than another, a cycle; the
    Two-Tier View, which discounts existential benefits, cycles too. The
    Wide Principle makes them equal.
  - Case Three: D (Mary 70, Kate 50), E (Kate 60, Ruth 25), F (Ruth 30, Jill
    10). The Narrow Principle ranks D below E below F, "the opposite of the
    truth" (p. 142).
  - Case Four: Tom's fifty years of pain against Harry's one extra day. The
    Narrow Principle counts only Harry and so ranks wrongly.
- **Goodness of outcomes versus rightness of acts** (pp. 144–149).
  - Against Temkin's suggestion (M) that A is worse than B when only they
    are possible but equal when C is too, goodness cannot depend on what we
    can bring about. Rightness can, as in a case of saving one stranger
    or two at the same risk.
  - Case Five: A (Jack 70, Jane 80), B (Jack 71, Sally 60). A is better.
    When Jack is seventy and about to die, we may still believe we ought to
    save him, even though that "is not the act that would make things go
    best" (p. 148).
  - So (P) and (Q), giving lesser benefits to actual or present people
    rather than greater ones to merely possible or future people, may be
    defensible about acts, like saving one's own children rather than more
    strangers. But "if we and many others often act in such ways, we and
    others may together make things go much worse ... we and our
    successors might together wreck the Earth" (p. 149).
- **Different numbers** (pp. 149–157).
  - Case Six: many at a very high quality of life versus many others
    barely worth living. Only the Wide Principle says the second is worse
    while staying person-affecting.
  - (R): the same total of benefits is in one way better shared among
    fewer (p. 151). Case Eight: one person at 100 beats ten at 10.
  - Benefits "in the collective sense" (to all) and "in the individual
    sense" (to each) come apart. The Wide Collective Principle restates
    the Impersonal Total Principle, which led him in 1984 to reject wide
    principles. His mistakes then: rejecting a strong argument only for
    an implausible conclusion, and not adding the Individual Principle
    (p. 154).
  - The Wide Dual Principle counts both. Case Ten: a million people at
    1,000 against a hundred billion at 1. Z benefits people "roughly a
    hundred times more" together and "roughly a thousand times less" each;
    the second "would do much more", so Z is worse (p. 156).
  - The last paragraph grants that this does not show the Repugnant
    Conclusion can be avoided, since a principle counting benefits to all
    could be a premise of stronger arguments for it. Parfit says he has
    "started to defend" the belief that great losses in quality cannot be
    outweighed by any sum "elsewhere" (p. 157).

## Concepts

- **non-identity problem**: when different people would exist in
  different outcomes, an outcome can be worse though worse for no one.
- **No Difference View**: that this makes no moral difference.
- **Narrow and Wide person-affecting principles**: worse *for* people
  (essentially comparative, between the same people) versus *less good
  for* people (benefiting whoever exists less).
- **existential benefit and harm**: the intrinsic good or bad, to a
  person, of being caused to exist with a given life.
- **intrinsic versus essentially comparative goodness**: goodness from
  what a thing is in itself (a weight lifter's strength) versus goodness
  that consists in relations to other things (a team's beating others).
- **Two-Tier View**: existential benefits count, but less than
  comparative ones. Parfit cites McMahan for it.
- **No Complainants Claim**: an act cannot be wrong unless someone is or
  will be wronged by it.
- **Wide Dual Person-Affecting Principle**: an outcome is better in one
  way if it benefits people more together, and in another if it benefits
  each person more.

## Connections

- **Reasons and Persons ([LIT-469](../literature.d/LIT-469.md)), Unavailable.** The paper revises its
  Part IV: the No Difference View (p. 366), the impersonal principle Q
  (p. 360) and the wide principles rejected at pp. 396–401, as Parfit
  cites them. It shows what Parfit kept and what he gave up, not what the
  book argued.
- **Meyer, SEP ([NOTE-1304](NOTE-1304.md)).** Lists this paper among works holding
  that intergenerational ethics must be impersonal or mixed. That fits
  the 1984 Parfit better than this paper, which argues for a
  person-affecting principle. Whether the Wide Principle counts as
  impersonal depends on the definition: it is about people, but not about
  particular people. Meyer's threshold notion of harm is a different
  route around the same problem, which Parfit does not discuss.
- **Narveson ([LIT-1769](../literature.d/LIT-1769.md)), Unavailable.** Quoted once, for "make people
  happy" (p. 135), and rejected.
- **Gardiner ([LIT-1681](../literature.d/LIT-1681.md)).** Parfit's cheaper energy policy and his
  closing warning, each generation favouring itself until the Earth is
  wrecked, are Gardiner's intergenerational storm from the other side.
  Gardiner explains why it happens; Parfit says each step may be
  permissible.
- **Caviola et al. ([NOTE-1267](NOTE-1267.md)).** Lay judgement counts adding happy
  people as good, which is the Wide Principle's existential benefit. With
  totals fixed it prefers higher averages, and the value of more happy
  people fades as numbers grow, close to the Dual Principle's weighting of
  benefits to each over benefits to all.
- **Personal identity and ethics ([THEORY-464](../theory.d/THEORY-464.md)).** Here identity does
  matter, in the non-identity sense: which person exists. Parfit's
  solution makes the answer not matter morally, by ranking outcomes
  without reference to who is in them.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).** The paper bears on it at two
  points. These are inferences; Parfit does not discuss motivation or the
  self, apart from noting that the No Complainants Claim has
  psychological force.
  - The beneficiaries are no one in particular. On the Wide Principle,
    what makes the cheaper policy wrong is a benefit to whoever would have
    existed, and "her child" names any person who could be called Clare's
    child (p. 122). Concern for those people cannot be concern for an
    extended self that includes them as individuals, since which
    individuals exist is what the choice decides. If the thesis reaches
    them, the self extends to a role or category ("my child", "our
    descendants", "humanity"), not to persons. That matches what Eyerman
    showed backward in time ([NOTE-1246](NOTE-1246.md)): the self an ancestral
    injury extends to is a category, not a line of descent.
  - Parfit permits what the thesis predicts. Favouring actual and present
    people over greater benefits to future people, "like" saving our own
    children over more strangers, may be right though it makes things go
    worse (pp. 148–149). That is concern scaled by nearness to the self,
    and Parfit treats it as an agent-relative permission, not as an
    error. His warning that every generation doing so could wreck the
    Earth is the cost the thesis's shape would carry across time.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md)).** Not addressed directly.
  The non-identity problem does set a limit on criteria: any criterion of
  a self extended into the far future must pick out a category, since no
  particular far-future person can be picked out in advance.
- **Batson's objection ([CLAIM-494](../claims.d/CLAIM-494.md)).** Not addressed.
- **The group entity versus a category ([TERM-096](../terms.d/TERM-096.md)).** If the thesis's
  future self is "our descendants", it is a category of members whose
  membership our choices fix, not a group entity. The term would let the
  record say so.
- **A dated note to [CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md) is proposed**, as an addition to its
  note "the thesis in time":

  > Parfit's last paper (NOTE-1263) grounds obligations to far-future
  > people in benefits to whoever will exist, since our choices decide who
  > that is. A self extended to them can then extend only to a category,
  > such as "our descendants", not to persons, as with the ancestral
  > category Eyerman describes backward in time ([NOTE-1246](NOTE-1246.md)). Parfit
  > also allows that we may rightly favour present people, as we favour
  > our own children over strangers, though it makes things go worse.
  > That is the thesis's shape treated as a permission, with its cost
  > named: if every generation does it, "we and our successors might
  > together wreck the Earth".

- **No THEORY filed.** The Wide Dual Principle is a proposal in an

## Limitations

- **Unfinished.** By the editor's note the draft lacked a conclusion and
  Parfit meant to revise it. Material was "to be added" after p. 148
  (note 19) and p. 150 (note 20). He does not return to the No
  Complainants Claim (note 14). He calls Boonin's aid example Boonin's
  "main example", which Boonin disputed too late for a revision (note 8).
- **The Repugnant Conclusion is not settled.** The Dual Principle's
  weighting of benefits to each over benefits to all is asserted for Case
  Ten ("we can plausibly believe"), and how much more they weigh is left
  open (Case Nine, p. 155).
- **Stipulated cases.** Every case has equal benefits per year and no
  other differences. The application to energy policy is asserted, not
  worked through.
- **Existential benefit is contested.** The solution depends on it, and
  the paper argues for it from intrinsic goodness without engaging the
  literature against it beyond Narveson.
- **Typos.**
  - "our having being caused to have this life" (p. 134).
  - (L) on p. 135 repeats (K) without its condition: it lacks, presumably,
    "by causing Sam to exist", which the next sentence needs.
  - "Mary's seventy years of life in E" (p. 143); Mary exists only in D.
  - Case Three's third row is labelled "F" without the colon of the
    others (p. 142).

## Open questions

- Do people who favour present over future people do so because the
  present are nearer the self, as the thesis would say, or for the No
  Complainants reason Parfit says has "psychological force"? A study that
  varied whether future beneficiaries were identifiable, while holding
  felt closeness fixed, would separate the two.
- Where did Parfit "start to defend" the claim that losses in quality
  cannot be outweighed by any sum (p. 157)? The paper does not say.
