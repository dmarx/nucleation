# NOTE-tmphtua8: Noggle — The Ethics of Manipulation

## Contribution

An encyclopedia survey of the philosophy of ordinary interpersonal
manipulation, in its 2026 revision. It separates two questions, what
manipulation is (the identification question) and why it is wrong when it
is (the evaluation question), and sets out the families of answer to each
with the main objection to each. Its own position is mostly withheld. Its
use to the record is as a map: which accounts exist, which cases break
them, and which questions the literature itself counts as open.

## Key insight

Every one-mechanism account of manipulation is broken by a familiar
tactic. Covert-influence and trickery accounts miss nagging, peer pressure
and guilt trips, which are open. Pressure accounts miss misdirection,
which applies no pressure. Non-rational-influence accounts catch graphic
safety warnings and dressing up for an interview. What is left is a
disjunction, a deeper common factor (a mistake, a mindset, a vice), or the
claim that the concept has no definition.

## Assumptions

- **Ordinary, not global, manipulation (§1.1).** The entry is about
  everyday tactics, not the "radical programming or reprogramming of all
  or most of an agent's mental states" of the free-will literature. It
  says "few people have explored the connections" between the two, naming
  Long 2014 and Todd 2013 as exceptions.
- **Influence on persons.** The scientist "manipulating variables" and
  the pilot manipulating controls are set aside as non-moralized uses
  (§3.1).
- **A non-fully-moralized concept.** With Wood, the entry prefers a
  definition that explains the term's negative connotation through some
  other feature, not "wrongful influence" by definition (§3).

## Key results

The entry argues for no theorem. Its results are the classification and
the counterexample to each family.

- **Bypassing reason (§2.1).**
  - Literal bypassing (subliminal advertising, hypnosis, conditioning,
    Cave's unconscious "non-concern motives") sets the bar "too high to
    count any of Irving's tactics as manipulation".
  - Covert influence: Ware (1981), Strudler (2005), and Susser, Roessler
    and Nissenbaum, quoted: "manipulation is hidden influence. …
    Covertly influencing someone—imposing a hidden influence—means
    influencing them in a way they aren't consciously aware of, and in a
    way they couldn't easily become aware of" (Susser et al. 2019: 4). It
    fits priming, framing, cognitive biases and gaslighting, but "Many
    manipulative influences, such as peer pressure, nagging, and guilt
    trips, do not seem covert."
  - Covert intent (Belohrad 2019; Whitfield 2020, who needs only
    "plausible deniability"): the teenager nagging for a phone has a
    "quite transparent" intent.
  - Non-rational influence (Raz: manipulation "perverts the way that
    person reaches decisions"): graphic portrayals of the dangers of
    smoking, the Golden Rule, and dressing up are non-rational and not
    obviously manipulative.
  - Detrimental effect on deliberation: Hanna ("the intentional use of
    nonrational means of influence, where these means affect the
    target's deliberation for the worse"), Gorin ("deliberately fails to
    track reasons"), Renzo ("intentionally exploiting our capacity to
    exercise our practical agency in order to undermine our efforts to
    adequately respond to our reasons for action"). Open problem: which
    of the many notions of reason.
- **Trickery (§2.2).** Manipulation induces a faulty mental state or bad
  reason, deception being the case of false belief. Noggle 1996: "Manipulative
  action is the attempt to get someone's beliefs, desires, or emotions to
  violate these norms, to fall short of these ideals." Barnhill 2014,
  Mills 1995 and Dowding are in this family. Its proponents split on
  whether the fault is by the manipulator's lights or objective.
  Counterexample: charm offensives, peer pressure, nagging and emotional
  blackmail "do not seem to involve trickery".
- **Pressure (§2.3).** Faden, Beauchamp and King, Feinberg, Kligman and
  Culver: pressure short of coercion. Most of these authors hold only
  that non-coercive pressure is "sufficient (but perhaps not necessary)".
- **Disjunctive, hybrid, non-definitional (§2.4).**
  - Rudinow 1978: "A attempts to manipulate S iff A attempts to influence
    S's behavior by means of deception or pressure or by playing on a
    supposed weakness of S." The cost of any disjunction is that it
    leaves unanswered "what makes all forms of manipulation
    manifestations of the same phenomenon".
  - Greenspan's conjunctive view fails on pure pressure and pure
    misdirection.
  - Noggle's Mistake Account (2025): pressure works through the mistake
    of akratic choice, trickery through the mistake of a faulty mental
    state. Its akrasia claim is flagged as unproven.
  - Accounts by the manipulator's mindset: Baron's vice of excess in
    influencing others; Klenk 2022, on which the manipulator influences
    "while disregarding whether that method reveals reasons".
  - Non-definitional: Ackerman's "combinatorial vagueness"; Cohen 2025, a
    cluster concept organised by the metaphor of "mechanification", whose
    features include intent "to influence in a mechanifying way",
    exploiting vulnerabilities and non-transparency, "no single subset of
    which is necessary and sufficient". Pragmatic non-definitionalists:
    Gibert, Barnhill 2022, Sunstein 2025.
- **When it is wrong (§3.1).** The ticking-bomb interrogation counts
  against manipulation being wrong by definition or absolutely. The
  entry calls "perhaps, ultimately more plausible" a view (Baron 2014) on
  which manipulation is prima facie wrong and, where not defeated, pro
  tanto wrong. Cohen 2025 holds manipulation is "not even typically bad".
- **Why it is wrong (§§3.2–3.5).**
  - Harm: beneficent manipulation still seems pro tanto wrong, so harm
    cannot be the only wrong-maker.
  - Autonomy: manipulation can enhance autonomy (the student steered into
    a class; Irving keeping Tonya from returning to an abuser). S. Buss
    2005 argues the duty not to manipulate "has relatively little to do
    with the value of autonomy", because manipulation presupposes the
    target's own choice.
  - Treating persons as things: Mills ("reasons … as causal levers");
    Renzo 2025b, on which "our practical agency is coopted by the
    manipulator and turned against itself", which covers paternalistic
    manipulation as "imposing my end on you".
  - Character (Baron), relationship terms (Greenspan), mistakes (Noggle
    2025), and Gibert's Reductive View that "the wrong of wrongful
    manipulation is nothing over and above the wrong of infringing other
    rights".
- **Persons versus situations (§4.1).** Rudinow's malingerer fools a
  police officer (psychological) and so forces a psychiatrist who is not
  fooled to admit him (situational, "gaming the system"); Riker's
  "heresthetic" is "structuring the world so you can win". The entry
  says "much work remains to be done" on what the difference is.
- **Manipulation and intent (§4.2).**
  - Baron: manipulation needs only "a combination of intent and
    recklessness", and the manipulator need not be aware of the intent.
    Manne's Joan manipulates relatives by gifts without conscious intent
    to make them feel guilty, and "people can even behave manipulatively
    despite consciously intending not to".
  - Children and some personality disorders: "it would seem
    counterintuitive for a theory of manipulation to say that children
    and persons with personality disorders are incapable of acting
    manipulatively."
  - The algorithm case: an algorithm that tries variants of a platform's
    interface and selects those that raise a target behaviour "could end
    up selecting elements of the user interface that exert influences
    that, had they been chosen deliberately by a human, we would label as
    manipulation." "Should we say that the algorithm, which lacks the
    kind of intent that a human might have, nevertheless manipulates
    users?"
  - Five answers are listed, none endorsed: (1) manipulation needs no
    intention or state of mind; (2) it needs intent, so these are not
    manipulation; (3) manipulation par excellence needs intent, and
    technologies manipulate "in a looser sense" (Pepp et al. 2022) or
    exert "manipulation-like" influence (Nyholm 2022); (4) Klenk's lack
    of concern with how one's influence operates; (5) trace it to the
    designer with a loosened requirement, so the algorithm manipulates
    when "the designer did not take sufficient care".
- **Vulnerability and oppression (§4.3).** Power asymmetry is a source of
  vulnerability; manipulation is also a tool of the subordinate (Greenspan,
  Bowers). Skwire's caution against inferring manipulation from choices
  one disapproves of.

## Claims

| id | claim | strength | support |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | No single-mechanism account (covert influence, trickery, pressure, non-rational influence) covers the clear cases | moderate | a counterexample for each, §§2.1–2.3 |
| C2 | Covertness is neither necessary (nagging, peer pressure, guilt trips) nor captured by covert intent (transparent nagging) | moderate | cases, §2.1 |
| C3 | Manipulation is not wrong by definition or absolutely; prima facie and pro tanto wrong is the more plausible view | weak | the interrogation case and Baron 2014, §3.1; stated as "perhaps" |
| C4 | Harm cannot be the only wrong-maker, and the autonomy account must handle autonomy-enhancing manipulation | moderate | beneficent and autonomy-supporting cases, §§3.2–3.3 |
| C5 | Whether manipulation requires intent, and whether a selecting algorithm can manipulate, is open | strong as a report of the field | Baron, Manne, and the five listed answers, §4.2 |
| C6 | Ordinary and global (free-will) manipulation have rarely been related | strong as a report of the field | §1.1, with two exceptions named |

## Concepts

- **Ordinary versus global manipulation.** Everyday tactics, as against
  the wholesale reprogramming of the free-will literature's manipulation
  argument.
- **Identification and evaluation questions.** What manipulation is, and
  whether and why it is wrong.
- **Covert influence view.** Manipulation is influence the target is not
  aware of and could not easily become aware of.
- **Trickery account.** Manipulation induces a faulty mental state or bad
  reason, with deception as the special case of false belief.
- **Pressure.** Imposing costs for non-compliance short of coercion.
- **Situational manipulation (heresthetic).** Changing the target's choice
  situation, not her psychology, as against psychological manipulation.
- **Principled and pragmatic non-definitionalism.** Denying that the
  concept has a definition, as against working without one.

## Connections

The entry is the standard map of the field, and the 2019 paper by Susser,
Roessler and Nissenbaum ([LIT-1002](../literature.d/LIT-1002.md)) takes its three-way division (bypassing
reason, pressure, trickery) from the 2018 version. The 2026 version in
turn presents that paper as "an especially influential version" of the
covert-influence view and states the objection to it. Coons and Weber's
*Manipulation: Theory and Practice* (2014, [LIT-1003](../literature.d/LIT-1003.md)) supplies many of the
entry's sources: Barnhill, Baron 2014, Gorin, Wood, Long, Cave, Cholbi
and Manne. For the global manipulation of the free-will literature the
entry refers to the SEP entry on arguments for incompatibilism, which is
where Pereboom's four-case argument ([LIT-1006](../literature.d/LIT-1006.md)) belongs.

## Bearing on the record

- **[QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md) understates what this entry says about intent.** The
  question says "Most of the accounts Noggle surveys … also locate it in a
  manipulator's aim." That is fair for the definitions the entry quotes
  (Hanna, Gorin, Renzo, Rudinow, Kligman and Culver, Whitfield all build in
  intent or deliberateness). But §4.2 treats the intent requirement as
  contested and asks [QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md)'s first part itself, for an algorithm
  that selects interface variants which "had they been chosen
  deliberately by a human, we would label as manipulation". It lists five
  answers, which include the two branches the question's "What would
  answer it" names: (1) no state of mind is needed, and (2) without
  intent it is something else. Baron (intent with recklessness, not
  necessarily known to the agent) and Manne (manipulation without
  conscious intent, even against it) already loosen the requirement for
  individuals. So the record holds, in this entry, a statement that the
  question is open and a list of candidate answers. It does not hold an
  account stated in terms of function or selection history: Klenk's
  carelessness and the designer-negligence option still locate the fault
  in a mind. Cohen's cluster concept, on which intent is one feature
  among several none of which is necessary, comes closest.
- **The entry's case is the [LIT-179](../literature.d/LIT-179.md) case, not the [LIT-084](../literature.d/LIT-084.md) case.** Its
  algorithm is a single designed system with a designer to trace back to.
  A network of channels with no designer, [QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md)'s second case, is
  not discussed. §4.1 on situational manipulation (Rudinow's malingerer,
  Riker's heresthetic) is the nearest material for influence routed
  through other agents and institutions.
- **For [QUESTION-071](../questions.d/QUESTION-071.md) the entry adds a caution.** The question says the
  analyses of manipulation filed with it "say what makes an influence
  manipulation, as against persuasion or coercion. The four-case argument
  assumes that." The entry is about ordinary manipulation and says
  explicitly that its relation to the global manipulation of the
  free-will literature has hardly been explored (§1.1). So it does not
  supply what the four-case argument assumes. It supplies an account of a
  different phenomenon, whose relation to Pereboom's cases is itself an
  open question the record would have to argue.
- **For [THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md) and [CLAIM-022](../claims.d/CLAIM-022.md).** Nothing here decides between present
  structure and history. Two items bear indirectly. The covert-influence
  view makes manipulation depend on whether the target could become
  aware of the influence, a relation between the agent and the causal
  history of her state. Renzo's view makes it the co-opting of the
  target's own practical agency, so the manipulated agent's deliberation
  runs normally, which fits [THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md)'s claim that present-structure
  conditions are met.
- **For [THEORY-139](../theory.d/THEORY-139.md).** Nothing on group or corporate agency.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- A survey. It reports positions and their objections and argues for
  little. The 2026 revision gives Noggle's own Mistake Account a paragraph
  and does not defend it.
- Limited to ordinary manipulation of persons. Global manipulation,
  manipulation of institutions, and manipulation by collectives are out
  of scope; the algorithm case in §4.2 is the only non-human
  manipulator considered.
- The counterexamples are intuitive cases; no empirical work is used to
  settle which tactics are manipulative.

## Open questions

- Whether a definition without intent can keep the distinction between
  manipulation and harmless non-rational influence. An account in terms
  of function or selection that classifies the entry's algorithm case and
  the dressing-up case differently would answer it.
- What the difference between psychological and situational manipulation
  is, and whether it matters morally (§4.1, stated as open).
- How ordinary manipulation relates to global manipulation (§1.1).
