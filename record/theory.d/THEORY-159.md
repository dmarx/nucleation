# THEORY-159: In a language a term's value is fixed by its relations to the coexisting terms of the same system, so terms that share a signification can differ in value

## Source

Ferdinand de Saussure (1916), *Cours de linguistique générale*, ed. Bally
and Sechehaye, [LIT-774](../literature.d/LIT-774.md); read in [NOTE-594](../notes.d/NOTE-594.md) from the third-edition
text, Part Two, chapter IV ("La valeur linguistique").

## What was actually shown

An argument with examples, not an experiment. Every value, in and out of
language, needs two things: something dissimilar it can be exchanged for
(a coin for bread; a word for an idea) and similar things it can be
compared with (other coins; other words). A word's signification is the
first; its value requires the second as well. The examples:

- French *mouton* may share a signification with English *sheep* but not
  its value, because English also has *mutton* for the meat.
- Within one language, *redouter*, *craindre* and *avoir peur* limit one
  another; "si redouter n'existait pas, tout son contenu irait à ses
  concurrents".
- The Sanskrit plural is not worth the French plural, because Sanskrit
  also has a dual.
- French *louer* covers what German splits into *mieten* and *vermieten*;
  tense and aspect systems differ in the same way.

The same holds on the sound side: what matters in a word is the phonic
differences that keep it apart from others, so phonemes are "oppositive,
relative and negative", which the book supports by free variation where
no contrast is at stake (French uvular or rolled *r*) against its
absence where one is (German *r* and *ch*; Russian *t* and *t'*).

What could have come out the other way: if terms carried their values
independently of their neighbours, adding or removing a neighbour would
leave a term's range of use unchanged. The book offers cases in which it
does not, and no test in which it might have.

## What this does not say

- **Not that a sign is nothing but differences.** The book restricts "il
  n'y a que des différences sans termes positifs" to the signified and the
  signifier taken separately, and calls their combination in a sign "un
  fait positif"; between whole signs there is opposition, not difference.
  The common summary "meaning is purely differential" drops that
  qualification.
- **Not that signification is unreal.** The book keeps signification as
  one aspect of value, and says only that it does not fix the term.
- **Not a claim about reference or truth.** The account concerns the
  relations inside a language state; it says nothing about how terms
  relate to things, which the book sets aside with the nomenclature view.
- **Not established beyond its examples.** The examples are chosen; the
  book does not consider cases of semantic stability across changes in a
  field, and the text is the editors' reconstruction from lecture notes.
- **Not a statement about distributional models.** That co-occurrence
  statistics recover word meaning is a later and different claim; this
  account may be read as its ancestor, but nothing here tests it.

## Note of 2026-10-28: what holds it back

Kept Proposed by the sixth status review. Part Two ch. IV is read in full
([NOTE-594](../notes.d/NOTE-594.md)), so reading is not the blocker. No study the record holds adds,
loses or merges a term in a language's inventory and measures how its
neighbours' use shifts, against terms outside the field. The embedding
readings that cite this account ([THEORY-182](THEORY-182.md), [THEORY-185](THEORY-185.md), [LIT-860](../literature.d/LIT-860.md)) concern
what a model learns from co-occurrence, and [THEORY-518](THEORY-518.md) concerns
distributional neighbourhoods from four hand-picked words. [ARG-033](../arguments.d/ARG-033.md) and
[ARG-034](../arguments.d/ARG-034.md) judge the record's use of this reading, not the reading.
