# NOTE-tmpx40wa: Owen et al. — detecting awareness in the vegetative state

## Contribution

Before it, functional imaging had shown "islands" of preserved cortical
response in some patients diagnosed as vegetative, but a cortical response
to a stimulus could be unconscious. This paper replaced the stimulus
response with a response to command that has no motor output: the patient
was told to perform one of two mental-imagery tasks at specific points in
the scan, and the regions each task recruits in healthy people were active
when, and only when, she was told to do it. It is a single case, and it
proposes, without doing it, that such tasks could be used to communicate.

## Key insight

The marker of awareness moved from behaviour to compliance with an
instruction, read from the brain. Comprehension alone does not count: the
authors set aside their own first result, intact speech and
ambiguity-resolution responses, as "suggestive" only, because semantic
processing can run without awareness. What counts is that the patient did
what she was asked, when she was asked, for a sustained period, with a
mental act that has no motor output and so can be produced by someone who
cannot move. The decision to cooperate is the evidence.

## Assumptions

- **Diagnosis.** The patient "fulfilled all of the criteria for a diagnosis
  of vegetative state according to international guidelines" (Royal
  College of Physicians 2003), by multidisciplinary clinical assessment.
  The details are in the SOM, not read.
- **Region specificity.** Tennis imagery reliably recruits the
  supplementary motor area (SMA), and spatial navigation imagery the
  parahippocampal gyrus (PPA), posterior parietal cortex (PPC) and lateral
  premotor cortex (PMC), in healthy people. The paper relies on this and
  on its own 12 controls.
- **Inference from compliance.** Sustained, task-specific, cue-locked
  activity cannot arise without a conscious decision to comply. This is
  stated, not argued: "her decision to cooperate with the authors by
  imagining particular tasks when asked to do so represents a clear act of
  intention, which confirmed beyond any doubt that she was consciously
  aware of herself and her surroundings" (p. 1402).
- **Asymmetry of inference.** Only positive findings count: "negative
  findings in such patients cannot be used as evidence for lack of
  awareness, because false negative findings in functional neuroimaging
  studies are common, even in healthy volunteers" (p. 1402).

## Key results

- **Patient.** "In July 2005, a 23-year-old woman sustained a severe
  traumatic brain injury as a result of a road traffic accident. Five
  months later, she remained unresponsive with preserved sleep-wake
  cycles" (p. 1402). Nothing else about her history is in the main text.
  Monti et al. (2010, [NOTE-tmpy3j64](NOTE-tmpy3j64.md)) list her as their Patient 4 with an
  interval since ictus of 6.0 months.
- **Speech (first study).** Spoken sentences against acoustically matched
  noise gave speech-specific activity bilaterally in the middle and
  superior temporal gyri, "equivalent to that observed in healthy
  volunteers" (fig. S1, not read). Sentences with ambiguous words gave an
  additional left inferior frontal response, "similar to that observed for
  normal volunteers". The authors: this "although suggestive, is not
  unequivocal evidence that a person is consciously aware" (p. 1402).
- **Imagery (second study).** Spoken instructions "to perform two mental
  imagery tasks at specific points during the scan": imagining "playing a
  game of tennis" and "visiting all of the rooms of her house, starting
  from the front door". Tennis: significant SMA activity. House: PPA, PPC
  and lateral PMC. "Her neural responses were indistinguishable from those
  observed in healthy volunteers (fig. S2) performing the same imagery
  tasks in the scanner" (p. 1402). Controls: a group of 12. Threshold:
  P < 0.05 corrected for multiple comparisons (Fig. 1 caption; the "<"
  is mis-encoded in the extracted text). The number of blocks, their
  length and the statistic for "indistinguishable" are in the SOM, not
  read; the main text gives no quantitative comparison with controls.
- **No practice.** The responses were "reproducible and robust
  task-dependent responses to command without the need for any practice
  or training" (p. 1402).
- **No communication.** No question was put and no answer decoded. The
  paper proposes that patients "diagnosed as vegetative, minimally
  conscious, or locked in, may be able to use their residual cognitive
  capabilities to communicate their thoughts to those around them by
  modulating their own neural activity" (p. 1402).

## Concepts

- **Vegetative state.** Patients "who emerge from coma appear to be awake
  but show no signs of awareness"; the diagnosis "depends crucially on
  there being no reproducible evidence of purposeful behavior in response
  to external stimulation".
- **Act of intention.** The paper's marker of awareness: deciding to do
  what one is asked when asked, here by a mental act.

## Connections

It builds on Schiff et al. (2002) on preserved "islands" of cortical
function in vegetative patients and on the Royal College of Physicians'
diagnostic guidance. Monti et al. ([LIT-tmpp8ob6](../literature.d/LIT-tmpp8ob6.md), read in [NOTE-tmpy3j64](NOTE-tmpy3j64.md))
extend it to 54 patients, include this patient as their Patient 4, and
build the yes/no procedure the paper proposes. Block ([NOTE-497](NOTE-497.md)) reports it
as "an intentional act without report". That reading is correct for what
the paper shows: there is no report, only an act on command.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-tmpxgcq2](../cases.d/CASE-tmpxgcq2.md) and the channel.** The response is a mental act with
  no motor output: she responded "through her brain activity, rather than
  through speech or movement" (p. 1402). But the act that carries it in
  the tennis task is motor imagery, and the region it is read from is the
  supplementary motor area, a motor-planning region. The "spatial" task
  also recruits lateral premotor cortex. So the evidence of awareness
  crosses a channel the patient drives, and that channel is imagined
  movement. This strengthens [CLAIM-tmp9cjyo](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp9cjyo.md)'s description of it as action
  moved inward, in the most literal sense available: the patient was told
  to imagine acting, and the readout is the motor system's preparation for
  an act not made. The thesis's reply, that it acts on the patient's own
  state and not on the world, stands: nothing outside her changed except
  through the scanner. The CASE's sentence "the output channel is mental
  action alone, with no action on the world" is right for this paper.
- **What it shows about judgement.** It shows comprehension of a spoken
  instruction, its retention, and its execution at the cued time. It does
  not show judgement in the record's sense, an interpretation reached about
  something and then used: no question was asked, and the patient chose
  only whether to comply. Owen et al.'s "beyond any doubt" is an assertion,
  and a reader citing this paper for lucid judgement without action
  ([CLAIM-tmpq9do8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpq9do8.md)) should cite Monti et al. instead, where answers were
  given.
- **The asymmetry.** The paper states its own form of it: positive
  findings confirm awareness, negative ones show nothing (p. 1402). That
  is the CASE's asymmetry from the observer's side: the evidence can only
  arrive through a channel the patient drives, so a patient who does not
  drive it is uninformative.
- **Prior history.** The patient was 23, injured five months earlier; she
  built her model while acting, as the CASE's "What it cannot show" says.
  Five months is far shorter than the years at issue in Kübler and
  Birbaumer's extinction hypothesis ([LIT-tmpxcw33](../literature.d/LIT-tmpxcw33.md)), so the case says
  nothing about whether judgement survives a long loss of contingency
  between intention and outcome.
- **No feedback.** The main text describes no feedback to the patient.
  She complied without any restored contingency between intention and
  outcome, and "without the need for any practice or training". Against
  Chaudhary et al.'s patient ([LIT-tmpzuemc](../literature.d/LIT-tmpzuemc.md)), who communicated only after
  neurofeedback, this is a response with no contingency supplied in the
  session. It is not evidence that every contingency was absent before it:
  the paper says nothing about her bedside behaviour beyond the diagnosis.
- **[CLAIM-tmpt568z](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpt568z.md), [QUESTION-tmpr5tck](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpr5tck.md).** Nothing directly. The anchor of
  the patient's own loop is not addressed.
- **The LIT.** Its summary is right as far as it goes. It should add that
  the paper asked no question and decoded no answer, that the comparison
  with controls is qualitative in the main text, and that the authors set
  aside the speech result as not evidence of awareness.
- **For the anthology.** No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- One patient. No statistics for the patient–control comparison in the
  main text; the SOM was not read.
- The inference from compliance to awareness is asserted. The paper does
  not discuss whether an instruction could trigger task-specific imagery
  activity without awareness.
- The paper itself says false negatives are common, so it cannot estimate
  how many vegetative patients are aware.
- It does not attempt communication.

## Open questions

- Whether cue-locked, task-specific activity can be produced without
  awareness. A result showing it in unaware healthy subjects (under
  anaesthesia or sleep) would remove the paper's main inference.
- For the record: whether a patient with no motor channel at all, years
  after injury, can still comply on command. That would bear on
  [CASE-tmpxgcq2](../cases.d/CASE-tmpxgcq2.md)'s extinction thread.
