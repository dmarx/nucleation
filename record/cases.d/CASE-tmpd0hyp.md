# CASE-tmpd0hyp: Identical harm, different remedial assignments: two institutions produce the same wrongful outcome through the same defective procedure with blameless decision-makers, and one can remedy it while the other cannot and a separate public authority with jurisdiction and resources can; the duty to address the wrong is the same and the corrective paths differ, assigned without inventing blame

## The case

A110 §11 of the exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afcb46d-83a8-83e9-9c85-4a0c3604405b):

> Two institutions produce the same wrongful outcome through the same defective procedure.
>
> In both, the individual decision-makers are blameless.
>
> Institution A retains effective authority and resources to remedy the violation.
>
> Institution B lacks those capabilities, but a separate public authority has jurisdiction and resources to intervene.

Held fixed: the outcome, the procedure, and the absence of blame. Varied:
who can remedy. "The question is whether we can assign remedial duties
appropriately without inventing blame. The institutional arrangements may
require different corrective paths even though the underlying obligation to
address the wrong is equivalent."

## What it can show

Fictional, it shows that remedial responsibility can be assigned where
culpability is empty in both arms, and that the assignment follows capacity
and jurisdiction, not fault: in arm B the public authority owes or may owe
the correction without having caused anything. That is the second half of
[CLAIM-tmp4o53g](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp4o53g.md) (assigning remedial duties does not establish blame) and the
owner's [CLAIM-tmpsk25g](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpsk25g.md) (correction does not wait on blame). A110 §8's
graph gives the roles: an oversight body "Investigate, adjudicate, order
relief within its authority"; a successor "May inherit continuing duties to
correct, even without fault for the original design".

It cannot show who in fact owes the remedy in arm B. That the public
authority's jurisdiction grounds a duty to intervene, rather than a
permission, is a further premise: Miller's capacity ground of remedial
responsibility, which A relays and the record has not read. It does not
build the first half of the principle, blame displacement, where a culpable
party is found and the system goes unrepaired; [CASE-151](CASE-151.md) (Wells Fargo) is
the record's documented instance of that.

## Note of 2026-11-17: Miller's capacity ground, read through a neighbour
<!-- inactive-ok-file: NOTE-tmps8s9k THEORY-tmp3xr2l LIT-tmpqsyr9 — Skimmed, new or Unavailable; the reading filed on 2026-11-18 and the unread article -->

Shaw-Young 2008, read in part ([NOTE-tmps8s9k](../notes.d/NOTE-tmps8s9k.md), [THEORY-tmp3xr2l](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp3xr2l.md)), standing in weakly for Miller 2001 ([LIT-tmpqsyr9](../literature.d/LIT-tmpqsyr9.md)).

The case says the public authority's duty to intervene rests on "Miller's capacity ground of remedial responsibility, which A relays and the record has not read". The record now holds it in Miller's own words, as Shaw-Young quotes his 2007 book: "if I am the only person walking along a river bank when a child falls in, then it is my responsibility to rescue the child" (thesis p. 29, Miller 2007 p. 103). As quoted, capacity grounds a duty, not a permission, when the agent is "uniquely in this position". Whether a public authority with jurisdiction is so placed in arm B, and how capacity is weighed against Miller's other connections ("with more direct connections generating stronger duties", Shaw-Young's paraphrase, p. 26), the reading does not settle. The premise stays a premise.
