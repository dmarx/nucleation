# NOTE-tmp2b9lt: Wenar and Cruft — Rights

## Contribution

A current, authoritative map of the analysis of rights the record's rights
claims use: the Hohfeldian incidents and their molecular combinations,
powers as second-order, the will–interest dispute, directed duties, and
three approaches to justifying rights (status-based, instrumental,
contractual).

## Key results

- **Definition** (lead): "Rights are entitlements (not) to perform certain
  actions, or (not) to be in certain states; or entitlements that others
  (not) perform certain actions or (not) be in certain states."
- **Kinds by the holder's acts** (§1): "The inalienable right to life, the
  forfeitable right to liberty, and the waivable right that a promise be
  kept." Moral rights are "grounded in moral reasons", legal rights "derive
  from the laws" of the society. Steiner (2013) "questions whether
  inalienable or unwaivable rights are logically possible".
- **Molecular rights** (§2.1): "the incidents fit together in
  characteristic ways to create complex "molecular" rights."
- **Power** (§2.1.3): "A has a power if and only if A has the ability to
  alter her own or another's Hohfeldian incidents." "Power-rights to alter
  the authority of others are, as we will see, definitive of all developed
  legal and political systems." "Ordering, promising, waiving, abandoning,
  consenting, selling, and sentencing are all examples of acts by which a
  right-holder exercises a power".
- **Immunity** (§2.1.4): "B has an immunity if and only if A lacks the
  ability to alter B's Hohfeldian incidents."
- **Function** (§2.2.2): "a will theorist asserts that the function of a
  right is to give its holder control over another's duty"; "Interest
  theorists maintain that the function of a right is to further the
  right-holder's interests." "Scanlon (2003, 2013) defends the position that
  rights are constraints on the discretion of individuals or institutions
  to act", criticized by Gilbert and Wenar.
- **Directed duty** (§2.2.3): its violation "is more than just a wrong: it
  is a wronging of the being to whom the duty is owed".
- **Forst** (§6.3): "Beginning with a status-based fundamental right to
  justification, he shows how suitably idealized demands for justification
  from individuals subordinated within real relations of power can ground
  specific rights to relief from and redress for actual injustices."

## Assumptions

Hohfeld's analysis of form is taken as widely accepted (the entry says
"privilege" for the first incident); function is the contested question.

## Concepts

Privilege, claim, power, immunity and their correlatives and opposites;
molecular rights; first- and second-order rights; will and interest
theories; demand-rights; directed duty and wronging; status-based,
instrumental and contractual justification.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-tmplidg6](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmplidg6.md).** The structural half: powers alter incidents,
  including the authority of others, and are "definitive of all developed
  legal and political systems". The entry says nothing about justificatory
  priority.
- **[CLAIM-tmpnb4v4](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpnb4v4.md).** Gives "right" its standard sense, a claim correlative
  to a directed duty. Gilbert's demand-rights tie a right to standing to
  demand, not to a demand made, which is consistent with the claim. The
  entry does not discuss commitments.
- **[QUESTION-tmptinzk](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmptinzk.md).** Inalienable rights are a recognized class, defined
  by how the holder's acts can affect them; moral rights are grounded in
  moral reasons, not in law; and whether inalienable rights are logically
  possible is disputed (Steiner).
- **[LIT-tmp09f2d](../literature.d/LIT-tmp09f2d.md).** Gives a secondhand statement of Forst's right to
  justification.
- The exchange's A87 is faithful: "molecular" rights is the entry's term.
