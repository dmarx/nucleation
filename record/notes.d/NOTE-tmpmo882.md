# NOTE-tmpmo882: Cameron (2022) — "Making Out" While Driving: Relational and Efficiency Games in the Gig Economy

## Contribution

Burawoy's workplace games carried into solitary, algorithmically managed work: games built on "touchpoints" (the customer and the app) rather than coworkers, two games in one workplace, and the platform's measurement deciding whether a game can be won.

## Key results

1. Abstract: "My four-year qualitative study" finds "a more readily available mechanism of engagement—workplace games" that workers "find meaningful, can control, and 'win.'"
2. Methods: participant observation as a driver (160 hours) and rider (112 rides); 136 "interviews with sixty-three drivers" in 23 North American cities.
3. Relational game: drivers personalize service and track ratings; they "tend to follow the algorithm's other suggestions, seeing the algorithmic management system as working on their behalf". "The digital platform is thus rendered a facilitator in the relational game."
4. Efficiency game: "Because drivers believed the algorithm was working against them, they deployed tactics to counter some of the algorithm's decisions, thus reasserting control in their work"; "If drivers declined too many rides, they risked being temporarily suspended or permanently deactivated"; "At times, drivers even manipulated the algorithm"; "The digital platform is thus rendered an opponent in the efficiency game."
5. Discussion: platform changes (ratings badges) reinforced the relational game, making it likelier drivers "would follow the algorithm and continue playing"; "In both games, workers felt they had more control over humans than machines".
6. Limits: "this study does not suggest that workplace games alone can generate consent or long-term commitment"; economic dependence and lack of alternatives are other reasons drivers stay.

## Assumptions

Burawoy's game lens; drivers' accounts and the author's own driving as evidence; an active, long-tenured, mostly male sample.

## Concepts

Workplace games; "making out"; relational and efficiency games; touchpoints; organizational scaffolds; amicable and adversarial stances; gamification against organic games.

## Bearing on the record

- **QUESTION-461**: a case of an organization that depends on participants it subordinates and secures their contribution without ceding authority. It makes one game winnable through its metrics and meets the other with opacity and the threat of deactivation. Drivers' counter-tactics are real but individual.
- **CLAIM-682, CLAIM-690**: the platform alone sets assignment, fares and deactivation, the terms on which drivers act. That is a case of control over the terms of a dependence, though the paper takes no normative position.
- **CLAIM-tmp0tg7y, CLAIM-tmpyjzi8**: the relational game's extra care (gifts, emotional support) for ratings is unpaid effort the platform receives. The paper presents it as meaning the drivers make, not as exploitation.
- **CLAIM-tmpnfjln**: no bearing.
- **As a stand-in for Cameron (2024), LIT-tmpdkpdp**: same fieldwork, and its two games prefigure that paper's engagement and deviance tactics, both sustaining participation. It lacks the later paper's "constant and confined choices" and "choice-based consent", and it withholds the consent conclusion that A19 §3 attributes to the later paper.

## Limitations

A theory-generating qualitative study in one industry and region; no causal claim; the author's own experience is evidence; explicitly no claim about consent.
