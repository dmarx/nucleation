# LIT-tmpuev9s: Assessing grand narratives of economic inequality across time

Gary M. Feinman, Gabriela Cervantes Quequezana, Adam Green, Dan Lawrence, Jessica Munson, Scott Ortman, et al. (2025), *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS) 122(16), e2400698121. Series "The Global Dynamics of Economic Inequality over the Long Term"* — DOI-10.1073/pnas.2400698121

## Key takeaways

- Across 1,176 archaeological sites (47,019 houses), Gini coefficients of house size are only weakly predicted by the number of political-hierarchy levels (r² ≈ 0.22–0.27) or by polity population, and do not rise linearly or immediately with farming or urbanism. The collective-versus-autocratic character of governance explains about as much variance as hierarchy does, and the highest inequality appears at autocratic apex sites in multi-level polities.

*Seeded from the abstract and a skim, not a reading. What follows is what the work says about itself.*

Established "grand narratives" tie inequality in large societies to roughly linear trends: domestication, then population growth or step-ups in hierarchical scale, with reversals only after catastrophes or collapse. These narratives were built mostly from historical or ethnographic snapshots and pay little attention to institutions or agency. The authors use quantitative, time-anchored archaeological and ethnographic house-size data from several world regions, most of which went through urbanization and hierarchy formation. Broad temporal patterns exist, but regional inequality trends are not linear or uniform, and they are not triggered immediately or mechanically by Malthusian dynamics or growth in scale.
