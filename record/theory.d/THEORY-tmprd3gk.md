# THEORY-tmprd3gk: Institutional facts are status functions, imposed by collective acceptance on things that cannot perform the function by their physical structure alone; the status carries deontic powers that give desire-independent reasons for action, and it exists only as represented, so institutions require language; 'X counts as Y in C' is the common case of the more general 'We accept (S has power (S does A))'

## Source

John R. Searle (2006), "Social ontology: Some basic principles",
*Anthropological Theory* 6(1):12–29, [LIT-482](../literature.d/LIT-482.md), read in full in [NOTE-388](../notes.d/NOTE-388.md) in
the parallel English printing, *Papers: Revista de Sociologia* 80:51–71.
Page numbers are Papers' pages. *The Construction of Social Reality*
([LIT-485](../literature.d/LIT-485.md)), which this paper restates, is not lawfully readable and was not
read.

## What was actually shown

The paper has no theorems. Its results are distinctions and definitions,
argued informally or by example.

- **Objectivity (pp. 52–55).** Observer-relative facts are ontologically
  subjective but can be epistemically objective, and the attitudes that
  create them are themselves observer-independent. That, Searle says, is
  why social science is possible.
- **Three primitives (p. 56).** Collective intentionality, the assignment
  of function, and constitutive rules. A *social fact* is any fact
  involving the collective intentionality of two or more agents, so a wolf
  pack hunting is one. *Institutional facts* are the subclass involving
  status functions (p. 57).
- **Deontic powers (p. 59).** Accepting a status function is accepting
  rights, duties and permissions. These are "desire-independent reasons
  for action", "the glue that holds society together". Desire-based
  reasons such as wanting to be rich presuppose them (p. 66).
- **Language (pp. 60–62).** The fact exists only as represented, its
  content is deontic, the deontology persists after creation, and the
  institution must be recognised as well as its instances. The dog can see
  a man carry a ball across a line but cannot see a touchdown (p. 60).
- **The revision (pp. 63–66).** Status indicators matter in their own
  right (De Soto's squatters, whose powers "stop at the point where the
  larger society requires some official proof"). Some Y terms are
  free-standing, with no X: a corporation is created by declaration, and
  money needs only recorded values. There "the real bearer of the
  deontology is the participant". "We accept (S has power (S does A))" is
  now ranked as "a much more general account" (p. 65).
- **Scope (pp. 69–71).** Friendship is institutional "because friendship
  carries obligations". A date, a colour word or a unit of weight is not.

**What could have come out otherwise.** The paper names its own
falsifier: an institution of another structure would refute it (C7). It
offers none and finds none. The revision shows the original form already
failed for corporations and ledger money.

## What this does not say

- **That collective intentionality is irreducible.** It is a primitive
  here, "a genuine biological phenomenon", and the analysis is deferred to
  [LIT-490](../literature.d/LIT-490.md) (p. 57, fn. 3). Gilbert, Bratman and Tuomela & Miller are not
  mentioned.
- **That the three primitives suffice.** "I am going to be very brief
  because I am repeating things I have said elsewhere" (p. 56). Asserted
  and illustrated.
- **That animals lack a deontology.** Held "as far as we can tell", and if
  some primates have one, "so much the better for them" (p. 62).
- **That "X counts as Y in C" is now derivative.** The paper ranks the
  general form higher but does not say whether the old form is derivative
  or merely the common case, and a status function is still defined as
  imposed "on a person or an object" (p. 57).
- **That a corporation is an agent.** It is a deontic construction whose
  powers go to individuals. Whether it can act is a different question,
  and the paper does not ask it.
- **That the taxonomy of status functions is settled.** "I still do not
  have a taxonomy … that I find satisfactory" (p. 66).

## Connections

- **Rival: rules in equilibrium** ([THEORY-tmp22bpm](THEORY-tmp22bpm.md), [LIT-481](../literature.d/LIT-481.md)). Hindriks &
  Guala hold that a constitutive rule is a regulative rule with a name
  added, that institutions such as etiquette need none, and that
  "desire-independent reasons" is a platitude or an unargued anti-Humean
  thesis. Searle's own free-standing Y terms, with powers sent "directly to
  the individuals" and bills as "markers" of power, treat the Y term much
  as they do. He does not draw their conclusion ([NOTE-388](../notes.d/NOTE-388.md)).
- **Epstein's survey** ([LIT-487](../literature.d/LIT-487.md), [THEORY-tmpavyju](THEORY-tmpavyju.md)) reports collective
  acceptance as one theory of anchoring and "X counts as Y in C" as a frame
  principle of one narrow form.
- **Tomasello** ([LIT-125](../literature.d/LIT-125.md), [NOTE-185](../notes.d/NOTE-185.md)) locates the first felt obligation in
  children's joint commitments from about age three. That is the empirical
  side of the human/animal line Searle draws by analysis, but Searle's
  collective intentionality includes hunting wolves and Tomasello's shared
  intentionality is species-unique ([NOTE-388](../notes.d/NOTE-388.md)).
- **[QUESTION-007](../questions.d/QUESTION-007.md)** asks what grounds normativity across agents at one
  level. This account says institutional obligations are grounded in
  collectively accepted status. No `answers` edge is filed: the
  desire-independence that would make that a grounding of normativity,
  rather than a description of what people accept, is asserted here and
  disputed by the rival.
- **[THEORY-057](THEORY-057.md)** uses this paper to say Barrett's comparison of emotion
  categories with money needs collective acceptance and a status.
- **The group-mind strand** ([THEORY-043](THEORY-043.md)). A society exists as that society
  only because its members represent it; a brain's parts do not represent
  the brain. [NOTE-388](../notes.d/NOTE-388.md) notes this could be the kind of asymmetry
  [THEORY-043](THEORY-043.md) asks for, as the reader's inference, not Searle's.
