# NOTE-tmp8ceuj: Illich (1973) — Tools for Conviviality, a two-page excerpt

## Contribution

In Illich's own words: the definition of conviviality, the distinction
between tools that "work" for people and tools to work with, and the
definition of radical monopoly with its standard case.

## Key results

Page 1 unless noted; quotations as the PDF prints them.

1. "The hypothesis was that machines can replace slaves. The evidence shows that, used for this purpose, machines enslave men. Neither a dictatorial proletariat nor a leisure mass can escape the dominion of constantly expanding industrial tools."
2. "People need new tools to work with rather than tools that “work” for them."
3. "As the power of machines increases, the role of persons more and more decreases to that of mere consumers."
4. "People need not only to obtain things, they need above all the freedom to make things among which they can live"; prisoners "have no say in how things are to be made"; "They are degraded to the status of mere consumers."
5. "I consider conviviality to be individual freedom realized in personal interdependence and, as such, an intrinsic ethical value."
6. Pp. 1–2: as conviviality falls below a certain level, "no amount of industrial productivity can effectively satisfy the needs it creates among society’s members."
7. P. 2: institutional purposes that hallow productivity "are a major factor in the amorphousness and meaninglessness that plague contemporary society"; priority to "personal energy under personal control".
8. P. 2: "we need procedures to ensure that controls over the tools of society are established and governed by political process rather than by decisions by experts".
9. P. 2: ordinary monopolies "restrict the choices open to the consumer"; by contrast, "By “radical monopoly” I mean the dominance of one type of product rather than the dominance of one brand. I speak about radical monopoly when one industrial production process exercises an exclusive control over the satisfaction of a pressing need, and excludes nonindustrial activities from competition."
10. P. 2: cars shape a city, "practically ruling out locomotion on foot or by bicycle in Los Angeles"; "That motor traffic curtails the right to walk, not that more people drive Chevies than Fords, constitutes radical monopoly."
11. P. 2: "Cars create distance." They "extort tolls on the bridge over the remoteness between people that was manufactured for their sake", and velocities "force him to spend more time for the sake of travel".

## Assumptions

Personal energy and autonomous action are the measure: industrial tools
are judged by what they leave persons able to do for themselves. Tools
include institutions.

## Concepts

Conviviality; convivial reconstruction; tools to work with against tools
that "work" for people; radical monopoly (a type of product, not a
brand); personal energy under personal control; political control of
tools.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpbypt7.** Corroborates, in Illich's words, the measure and the
  infrastructural mechanism. Result 9 sets ordinary monopoly, which
  restricts consumers' choices, against radical monopoly, which excludes
  the non-industrial activity: the claim's distinction between what a
  technology lets adopters do and what it leaves non-adopters. Result
  10's "curtails the right to walk" is a non-adopter's option shrinking,
  and the city shaped around cars is infrastructural adaptation. Result
  11 adds a mechanism the claim does not list: adoption enlarges the need
  itself ("Cars create distance").
- **CLAIM-tmp6wuql.** Corroborates the distinction the claim turns on, not
  its derivation: tools to work with against tools that "work" for
  people (result 2) is capacity-first against substitutive support, and
  results 3 and 4 say what substitution costs. The excerpt has nothing on
  capacity decaying with disuse.
- **CLAIM-tmpkjv7u.** Bounds it, briefly: result 1 places the harm in the
  tools whoever controls them, a dictatorial proletariat or a leisure
  mass. Result 8 is a remedy of the third condition's kind, but by
  political control over tools, not protection against particular
  controllers.
- **CLAIM-tmpsmg1c.** Leaves it open: result 8 says who should decide about
  tools, not how heavy the burden is. Nothing on irreversibility.
- **CLAIM-tmpu2cwc.** Result 8, control of tools by political process
  rather than by experts, bears on who revises the standards; a point of
  contact, no relation.
- **The stand-in, Cayley** (THEORY-tmpxwo9k): confirmed in Illich's words on
  conviviality (result 5) and on the car as the standard case (result
  10). Not reached here: "inaccessible or unimaginable", the "second
  watershed", "counterproductivity" as a term, and the burial case. The
  qualification of CLAIM-tmpbypt7's title still rests on Cayley alone.
- **Marcuse** (THEORY-tmpoxssh): both hold that industrial production
  creates the needs it serves (result 6); Illich would meet them with
  convivial tools, Marcuse by central planning of necessities.
