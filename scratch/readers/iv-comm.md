### Bibliographic details (from the PMC text and the Europe PMC record)
Manuel Bohn, Gregor Kachel, Michael Tomasello. "Young children spontaneously recreate core properties of language in a new modality." Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences of the USA 116(51): 26072–26077. Issue date 17 Dec 2019; published online 2 Dec 2019. doi:10.1073/pnas.1904871116. PMCID PMC6926062, PMID 31792169. Bohn and Kachel contributed equally. Edited by Susan A. Gelman. Data and scripts: github.com/manuelbohn/ges3000.
### Summary
Dyads of German children, in two rooms joined by a muted video link, played a reference game: a sender had to convey which of several pictures was selected. Speech was available in training and then cut, and gesture was never suggested. Children of 4–6 (and of 3–4 with an adult partner) set up reference with iconic gestures. Partners took up each other's signs. Naive raters judged signs within a dyad more alike than signs across dyads, and later signs more abstract (an effect explained by reduced effort). Children kept the meaning of signs an adult made drift from iconic to arbitrary. When 6- and 8-year-olds had to distinguish properties or transitive relations, they composed gesture sequences, more often under higher relational complexity, and these were better understood. The sequences did not follow German word order. N = 198.
### Contribution
It is the first controlled, direct observation of the incipient steps of a new language-like system: referentiality, local conventionality, drift to the arbitrary and compositional structure, all within one session of under 30 minutes. Earlier work studied the end products (homesign, Nicaraguan Sign Language) or transmission chains of artificial languages. The paper ties its findings explicitly to Tomasello's 2008 account (ref. 7: drift to the arbitrary, conventions lifted to the group level).
### Key results (section; quote)
1. Significance: "The gestural code systems that children created ad hoc within a 30-min test session exhibited core features of natural language and emergent sign languages"
2. Results, Initiation of Communication (a developmental gradient): "Six-year-olds spontaneously invented novel referential signs." The 3-year-olds were different: "Three-year-olds, however, imitated model solutions and had to be introduced to novel meanings in a piecemeal fashion."
3. Conventionalization: "For both age groups, raters judged gestures from the same dyad to be more similar"
4. Drift to the Arbitrary (production, small effect): "The effect for abstractness ratings was rather small, and later gestures were still highly iconic."
5. Drift to the Arbitrary (comprehension, scripted adult): "Children of both ages successfully retained the meaning of a gesture when its form gradually drifted from iconic to arbitrary."
6. Grammatical Constructions (8-year-olds): "Gesture sequences were more frequent in conditions with high compared to low relational complexity"
7. Same section, comprehension: "Compositional gesture sequences were almost always understood, which illustrates how structure reduced ambiguity."
8. Same section, native language: "We found no substantial evidence for German word order"
9. Discussion (mechanism): "We found that the reduction in iconicity over time was largely explained by a decrease in production effort."
10. Discussion (convention): "Natural languages can be thought of as local conventions that have been lifted to the group level (7)."
11. Conclusion: "Through reciprocal imitation, signs and referents become linked in a conventional way."
12. Methods, Participants: "All children (n = 198) came from an ethnically homogeneous, midsized German city"
### Assumptions
- Raters' similarity and abstractness judgments are valid proxies for conventionality and arbitrariness. "Effort" is likewise rated, not measured.
- A dyad's shared signs within one session are a model of the conventions of an emerging language.
- Children were trained with speech first, so the game's common ground was set up linguistically.
- The children are a single WEIRD German sample. Experimenters prompted iconic gesture when children stalled.
### Concepts
iconic gesture; referentiality; conventionalization (local convention vs group-level convention); drift to the arbitrary; effort reduction; common ground or conversational history; holistic vs compositional gesture; relational complexity; homesign; emerging sign languages; reciprocal imitation; second-order theory of mind (speculative).
### Bearing on held entries
- LIT-473 Lewis, *Convention* (Unavailable): parallel. Dyads converge on shared signs by reciprocal imitation, an empirical case of Lewis-style coordination conventions arising without agreement. Only dyad-level ("local") conventions are shown, not group-level ones.
- LIT-771 Kirby, Cornish & Smith 2008, with THEORY-155 and THEORY-489 (iterated learning) and CLAIM-090: contrast and qualification. Here structure arises within a single dyad, from communicative pressure (relational complexity) and with no generational transmission. The authors cite transmission studies for the claim that children do not spontaneously structure artificial languages, and they predict further gains from transmission.
- THEORY-785, THEORY-172, QUESTION-209 (RSA, reference games): background or parallel. The task is a reference game. The finding that ambiguity is "outsourced" to common ground, and that compositional forms reduce it, fits informativeness-driven speaker models, but no model is fitted.
- LIT-1042 Grice "Meaning" and THEORY-444 (non-natural meaning): background. The study shows non-natural signs arising from natural (iconic) ones, but it does not test communicative intentions.
- CLAIM-489 (Rejected: significance fixed by contrasts within a system): weak bearing. Differentiation is driven by contrast sets of pictures, but reference starts iconically. Its relevance is uncertain.
- LIT-125 / THEORY-495, LIT-504 (Tomasello on obligation and morality): only distant background, through shared intentionality. No direct bearing.
### Limitations
Small effects for arbitrariness. Single sessions with dyads only; no group-level or generational test. One culture. Adult partners in the grammar studies. Measures rest on ratings. The cognitive prerequisites (second-order ToM, metalinguistic skill) are speculation, not tested. The SI, where the detail lives, was not read.
### Candidate THEORY sentence
Bohn, Kachel and Tomasello report that when speech is blocked, young children (independently at 6, with help at 4) create iconic gestural signs within one 30-minute reference game. Partners adopt these signs, so they become dyad-specific. The signs drift slightly toward the arbitrary as effort drops, and children of 6 and 8 combine them into compositional sequences more often when relations are complex. Those sequences are better understood and do not follow German word order.
- promote_when: an independent replication, in a different language community, finds (a) within-dyad sign similarity above between-dyad similarity, (b) a rated shift toward arbitrariness tied to effort, and (c) more compositional sequences under transitive than under single-property contrasts, with no native-word-order effect.

Published 2021-01-13 (received 2020-09-06). Crossref DOI 10.25189/2675-4916.2021.v2.n1.id286 resolves. The DOI printed in the PDF footer, 10.25189/2675-4916.2020.V2.N1.ID286, returns 404 at Crossref. Use the 2021 form.
How far it stands in: high for the book's core account. It states explicitly that it follows "Tomasello (2008)" and is organized as the book's argument in miniature:
- natural gestures (pointing and pantomime) as complete communicative acts (≈ ch. 1, 3);
- ape gestures and their failure in the object-choice task (≈ ch. 2);
- the cooperation model: the three motives of requesting, informing and sharing, common ground and the joint attentional frame, the Gricean communicative intention, mutual manifestness and norms, cooperative reasoning (≈ ch. 3);
- the evolution of shared intentionality in collaborative foraging (≈ ch. 5);
- the transition from pantomime to arbitrary conventions (≈ ch. 5 and 7);
- ontogeny of pointing, common ground and iconic gestures (≈ ch. 4).
It does NOT cover ch. 6 (the grammatical dimension: the grammars of requesting, informing and sharing and narrative), and it gives none of the book's chapter-level empirical detail. It is written 13 years later and cites post-2008 work (e.g. Tomasello 2014, 2018), so some framing (e.g. truth "emanating" from the informative motive) may postdate the book.
Summary: The basic pragmatic structure of human communication is independent of language and is seen "in the raw" in pointing and pantomiming. Apes gesture dyadically and imperatively and fail to grasp informative pointing. Humans communicate for three cooperative motives within common ground, overtly signalling a Gricean communicative intention that makes the act public and norm-laden; recipients reason cooperatively. This infrastructure evolved inside obligate collaborative foraging. Conventions arose "naturally": imitated iconic gestures became opaque, and so arbitrary, to learners outside the original common ground. Infants show the same infrastructure from about 12 months.
Key results / claims (printed page; quote):
1. p.3: "With natural gestures, the veil is lifted and we see pragmatics in the raw (Tomasello, 2008)."
2. p.4: "some original, non-conventional way of doing it must be available first; there must be something to conventionalize."
3. p.7: "Like pointing, then, communicating with iconic gestures also does not depend on language."
4. p.8: "Great apes do not point or gesture iconically for one another."
5. p.9: "apes do not comprehend informative pointing because their communication is not structured cooperatively"
6. p.10: "human communication just is a collaborative activity"
7. p.12: "Human cooperative communication is thus structured most basically by motives of helping (both requesting and offering help) and sharing attitudes."
8. p.14: "In direct competition, shared common ground trumps individual personal relevance every time."
9. p.16 (overt communicative intention creates): "actual social norms, whose violation is unacceptable"
10. p.17: "we call the kind of practical reasoning characteristic of human communication cooperative reasoning."
11. p.18 (ecology): "they in fact became obligate collaborators"
12. p.19 (the origin of conventions): "The move to communicative conventions is thus, paradoxically, a natural one."
13. p.25: "This is not pragmatics as leftovers, but pragmatics as the foundation"
Assumptions: Grice and relevance theory are the right frame. Pointing is primordial. The ape data (object-choice task, no conspecific pointing) generalize. Collaboration came before cooperative communication. Lewis-style conventions need a prior non-conventional channel.
Concepts: natural gestures; referential vs social intention; requestive, informative and expressive (sharing) motives; individual vs cooperative imperatives; common ground or joint attentional frame; Gricean communicative intention; mutual manifestness; cooperative reasoning; joint intentionality vs collective intentionality; obligate collaborative foraging; drift to the arbitrary; "process of the third kind" (Keller).
Limitations: An essay with no new data. Its empirical support is the author's lab's infant and ape studies (contested, e.g. on ape pointing). It is a later restatement, not the book's text, and does not cover grammar.

### Stand-in B. Tomasello (2006), "Why Don't Apes Point?"
Provenance: https://pages.ucsd.edu/~rnunez/COGS155_Readings/Tomasello_06.pdf (UCSD course reading list, COGS 155; lawful as a course page). PDF, 12 sheets, HTTP 200, 2026-11-20. Read in full. Other copies: Scribd and FlipHTML5 uploads (excluded, not fetched). The MPG.PuRe record https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_1555042_2 was not fetched, because pure.mpg.de had already shown a bot check. Duke Scholars lists a 2020 Routledge reprint, doi:10.4324/9781003135517-25.
Bibliographic details (header of the copy): Michael Tomasello, "Why Don't Apes Point?" In N. J. Enfield & S. C. Levinson (eds), *Roots of Human Sociality: Culture, Cognition and Interaction*. Oxford & New York: Berg, 2006, pp. 506–524. (MPG.PuRe, per the search snippet, gives 506–530.)
How far it stands in: moderate. It is the pre-book core of chs. 2–4 on pointing: why apes neither comprehend informative pointing nor point declaratively or informatively; the helping and sharing motives; common ground; ritualization vs imitative learning; shared intentionality as the single explanation; and pointing as the prototype of language. It has nothing on pantomime or iconic gesture, conventionalization, phylogenetic scenarios or grammar.
Summary: Apes gesture flexibly and follow gaze, yet no ape points for another ape. The chapter gives five reasons. Apes do not understand communicative intentions; lack joint attentional common ground; lack helping and sharing motives; cannot tell what is new for the other; and learn gestures by ritualization, not as bidirectional conventions. It argues that all five reflect the absence of shared intentionality, and that pointing already has language's collaborative structure, its recipient design, its motives, and a locution/illocution split.
Key results / claims (manuscript page; quote):
1. ms p.2: "And yet there is not a single reliable observation, by any scientist anywhere, of one ape pointing for another."
2. ms p.6: "they do not understand the embedded structure of informing or communicative intentions"
3. ms p.10: "may be decomposed into two basic underlying motives: helping and sharing."
4. ms p.13: "chimpanzees use and understand their gestures as one-way procedures for getting things done, not as intersubjectively shared, bidirectional coordination devices or symbols."
5. ms p.17: "uniquely human forms of communication—including both nonlinguistic and linguistic conventions—rest fundamentally on a foundation of uniquely human forms of collaborative engagement involving shared intentionality."
6. ms p.17: "many of the aspects of language that make it such a uniquely powerful form of human cognition and communication are already present in the humble act of pointing"
Assumptions: Captive and human-raised ape behaviour is the relevant contrast. The Bratman, Gilbert, Searle and Tuomela accounts of shared intentionality apply to infants. Imitation vs ritualization can be told apart.
Concepts: object-choice task; imperative, declarative and informative pointing; joint attentional frame; communicative vs simple intention; ritualization; role-reversal imitation; shared or "we" intentionality; dialogic cognitive representation.
Limitations: A pre-book manuscript with no pantomime. Its ape claims were later contested (it cites the Leavens & Hopkins captive pointing itself; Moore's PhilArchive critique was surfaced by search, not read).

### Bearing on held entries (book, through stand-ins A–B)
- LIT-1042 Grice "Meaning", THEORY-444: supports and extends. The communicative intention is central, but it is grounded in shared intentionality and cooperative motives and made "mutually manifest". Tomasello claims that natural gestures show it without convention.

- THEORY-785, THEORY-172, QUESTION-209 (RSA): contrast. Tomasello treats the reasoning as cooperative and common-ground-bound, not individual inversion of an informative speaker. His claim that common ground beats egocentric relevance is testable in the same paradigms.

- Bohn et al. 2019 (Work 1) is the experimental test of the book's ch. 5 and 7 scenario (pantomime, then conventions, then grammar).
### Candidate THEORY sentence (book, filed from stand-ins)
Tomasello argues that human communication is cooperative at base. Pointing and pantomime, used without conventions, already work by a Gricean communicative intention, three cooperative motives (requesting, informing, sharing) and common ground, all resting on a shared-intentionality infrastructure that evolved for collaboration. Arbitrary linguistic conventions and grammar were built later on top of these natural gestures. Great apes, whose gestures are dyadic, imperative and ritualized, lack this infrastructure.
- promote_when: a lawful full text of the book (or ch. 1, 3 and 5) is read and confirms the sentence as the book's own account. For its truth: evidence that great apes neither comprehend informative pointing nor point or pantomime for conspecifics, while human infants do so with common-ground-dependent interpretation by about 14 months; and that arbitrary conventions can be shown to arise from iconic gestures by imitation without agreement (cf. Work 1).
