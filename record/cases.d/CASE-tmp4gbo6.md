# CASE-tmp4gbo6: Third-country removals, 2025–2026: a district court held that people must get meaningful notice and a meaningful opportunity to raise a fear of torture before removal to a country not named in their orders, and the Supreme Court twice stayed that relief pending review, so removals continued while the right to be heard first was litigated

## The case

The owner, U13 of the exchange "Assess Depp Heard Abuse"
(https://chatgpt.com/share/6afa2a8d-2598-83ea-8d1a-d9ceb992a99c), names
"third country deportations" as an instance of executive speed outrunning
oversight. As reported (SCOTUSblog, 29 September 2026,
https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/09/supreme-court-to-hear-argument-on-trump-administrations-deportation-of-immigrants-to-third-party/,
checked 2026-11-15; the 2025 stay as read in [LIT-052](../literature.d/LIT-052.md)):

- **2025.** DHS guidance (May 2025) governed removing people to countries
  not named in their removal orders. In D.V.D. v. DHS, filed in March
  2025, Judge Brian Murphy required written notice of the specific third
  country and a meaningful chance to contest removal there for fear of
  torture. On 23 June 2025 the Supreme Court stayed his order; on 3 July it
  said the stay applied "in full", allowing eight men held in Djibouti to
  be sent to South Sudan ([LIT-052](../literature.d/LIT-052.md), read
  in [NOTE-048](../notes.d/NOTE-048.md)).
- **February 2026.** Murphy entered final judgment striking down the
  policy: before removal to a third country, people have the right to
  "meaningful notice" and a "meaningful opportunity" to contest it on the
  ground that they reasonably fear persecution or torture there.
- **September 2026.** The First Circuit largely upheld him on 18 September
  and dissolved its stay; on 29 September the Supreme Court stayed his
  ruling again and granted review, so that third-country removals continue
  until it decides. Argument is set for 9 December 2026. Justices
  Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson would have denied the stay.

## What it shows

A25 of the exchange, on this case: a person with a final removal order may
still be protected against removal "to a specific country where they face
torture or persecution", so the question can be "whether the government
may remove them to *that particular destination*". "This is the
philosophical difference between **a right to prevent a particular harm**
and **a right to seek a remedy after it occurs**." What the litigation is
about is whether the hearing comes before the act. While the stays hold,
removals proceed and the question of a prior hearing is decided, if at all,
after many of the acts it concerns.

That is [CLAIM-464](../claims.d/CLAIM-464.md)'s jurisdictional
displacement in a second instance beside CECOT
([CASE-175](CASE-175.md)): once a person is in another country's
custody, the remedies available change. The difference from CECOT is that
here no court order was disobeyed; the stays were the Supreme Court's own,
so the window for prior review was closed by review, not against it.

## What it does not show

- **That the policy is unlawful.** The Supreme Court has not ruled, and the
  government's position is that the order disrupts lawful removals of
  people with final orders. A25: "Those are genuine enforcement interests."
- **Intent.** The owner's U13 says "attempted to bypass oversight"; the
  case records the sequence, not its purpose.
- **What happened to particular people removed.**
