# NOTE-tmpebx4k: Activating the legacy motive mitigates intergenerational discounting in the climate game

## Contribution

It carries the legacy programme of Wade-Benzoni and colleagues from
individual allocation to group cooperation. Earlier work had people split
a resource between themselves now and someone later. Here six people must
together reach a threshold to protect an endowment, and the endowment goes
either to themselves tomorrow or to future generations as trees. The paper
replicates Jacquet et al.'s (2013) finding that the second version kills
cooperation, and shows that short messages built to activate the legacy
motive largely restore it.

## Key insight

The obstacle to climate cooperation is two tragedies at once: the commons
(each gains by free riding) and the horizon (each generation gains by
passing costs on). The legacy motive is offered as a bridge across the
second. The authors' account of why it works is self-interest: "the legacy
motive is a mechanism for leveraging short-term self-interest for the
long-term best interests of the collective" (p. 9).

## Assumptions

- **Intergenerational discounting = temporal + social discounting.**
  Benefits to others later are discounted for both delay and social
  distance (p. 1–2, after Wade-Benzoni 2008).
- **Three routes to the legacy motive**, taken from the programme
  (p. 2): mortality salience, the power asymmetry between generations, and
  intergenerational reciprocity (paying forward what a past generation
  gave).
- **The rational benchmark.** For a self-interested player who disregards
  the endowment to future generations, not investing ($40 kept) beats the
  cooperative equilibrium ($20 kept), so universal defection is predicted
  in the tree-planting conditions (p. 6).
- **Players believe the trees will be planted.** Each received a pledge
  signed by the chief investigator (pp. 5, 8).

## Key results

- **Design** (pp. 3–6). 180 members of the UWA campus community (92
  female, 86 male, 2 unspecified; ages 17–57, mean 21), 30 groups of six,
  10 groups per condition. $40 operating fund; each round $0 ("selfish"),
  $2 ("fair share") or $4 ("altruistic") into the climate account, which
  paid for a newspaper advertisement on climate protection. Target $120;
  if missed, each $45 endowment lost with probability .9.
  - Short-delay: saved endowments paid in cash the next day.
  - Intergenerational: saved endowments invested in 72 trees (12 per
    player) storing CO₂ for 80 years.
  - Legacy induction: as intergenerational, with a roughly 250-word
    passage before rounds 1, 4 and 7, order counterbalanced (Table 1,
    p. 4): "Leaving a Positive Legacy" (mortality salience), "The Helpless
    Nature of Future Generations" (power asymmetry), "We Are One With
    Future Generations" (reciprocity, using Second World War veterans).
- **Reaching the target** (p. 6). Short-delay 9/10, intergenerational
  2/10, legacy induction 7/10. Logistic regression, intergenerational as
  reference: short-delay β = 3.58, SE = 1.32, z = 2.72, p = .007; legacy
  induction β = 2.23, SE = 1.04, z = 2.13, p = .033.
- **Group investment** (p. 6; mean ± SEM). Short-delay $116.80 ± 8.58,
  intergenerational $77.20 ± 12.01, legacy induction $105.20 ± 9.38.
  Condition F(2, 27) = 4.07, p = .029; no effect of round, F(9, 243) =
  0.35, and no interaction, F(18, 243) = 1.02.
- **Strategies** (p. 7; counts of the 60 choices per group). Condition ×
  strategy F(4, 81) = 6.551, p < .001.
  - Short-delay: fair share 38.20, selfish 11.70, altruistic 10.10.
  - Intergenerational: selfish 28.90, fair share 23.60, altruistic 7.50.
  - Legacy induction: fair share 32.00, selfish 17.70, altruistic 10.30.

  Legacy induction did not raise altruistic choices. The authors explain
  this by conditional cooperation and inequity aversion: once everyone is
  expected to give $2 a round, no one needs to give $4 (pp. 8–9).
- **Each message** (p. 8, Fig. 5). Investment over the rounds following
  each message, against the same rounds in the intergenerational game
  ($23.16 ± 3.60): mortality salience $32.00 ± 3.45, power asymmetry
  $34.00 ± 3.86, reciprocity $39.20 ± 4.56; F(3, 36) = 2.95, p = .044.
  The messages were not compared with one another.
- **Interpretation** (p. 9). "People invest in legacies for largely—
  although not entirely—selfish reasons." A legacy offers "a little piece
  of symbolic immortality" and can be thought of as "a reputation-
  preserving mechanism" for one's standing after death. What people get by
  cooperating with the future is "the opportunity to project their personal
  life meaning and identity into the future".
- **Limitations the authors state** (p. 9). No manipulation checks, chosen
  to avoid demand; no control message without legacy content; the passages
  mix mechanisms (the reciprocity passage also builds identification with
  a past generation); a WEIRD sample.

## Concepts

- **legacy motive**: "an inherent drive for one's life to have a meaningful
  impact that continues in some symbolic form after one's physical
  existence has ceased" (p. 2).
- **intergenerational discounting**: preferring smaller benefits for
  oneself now to larger benefits for others later.
- **tragedy of the horizon**: the conflict between the interests of
  current and future generations (after Carney 2015).
- **collective-risk social dilemma**: a threshold public-goods game in
  which missing the group target risks everyone's endowment.
- **fair-share, selfish, altruistic**: investing $2, $0 or $4 in a round.

## Bearing on the record

- **The legacy question ([QUESTION-tmpruhu4](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md)).** These are inferences.
  - The authors take a side among the question's readings without testing
    it. Their legacy is a reputation kept after death, which is Pitcher's
    reading ([LIT-tmphj5mc](../literature.d/LIT-tmphj5mc.md)), interests that outlast one, as much as the
    thesis's extended self. Either way they call it self-interest.
  - The induction cannot separate the readings. It bundles three routes.
    One, "We Are One With Future Generations", with its caption "We are
    one link in a chain connecting us with past and future generations",
    is an identification message: a self extended across generations. One,
    power asymmetry, is a moral message with no self in it. Only the
    mortality message is about one's own legacy. The reciprocity message
    did best ($39.20 against $32.00 and $34.00), but the messages were not
    compared.
  - The legacy offered is collective. Two passages promise being
    remembered as a generation ("our descendants remember us as a
    generation that did not shirk their responsibility"). The paper
    describes no record of who paid for the trees. If the motive worked, it worked through a "we" that is
    remembered, not through any individual's name. That is where the
    question's temporal and social extensions of the self meet, and it is
    the same meeting Castano et al. ([LIT-tmpd9qw4](../literature.d/LIT-tmpd9qw4.md)) propose.
  - [QUESTION-tmpruhu4](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md) asks whether legacy priming raises beneficence where
    no one will know. Here no future person would know who gave, but the
    messages told players they would be remembered as a generation. So the
    study does not answer it.
- **What the living owe the future ([QUESTION-tmpmx9ha](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpmx9ha.md)).** The game makes
  Gardiner's storm concrete. Without a legacy frame, 8 of 10 groups let a
  benefit to future people fail rather than give up $20 each. The
  remedy that worked appeals to what the living value about themselves,
  which is the nearest ground to Scheffler's ([LIT-tmpl4qml](../literature.d/LIT-tmpl4qml.md)), not to the
  good of future people as such. That is an inference from the message
  content.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** The authors' interpretation is
  the thesis in time: cooperation with the future as self-interest on a
  self projected past death. But nothing measures the self, so the paper
  is evidence that legacy messages work, not that they work through an
  extended self.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md))** and **Batson's objection
  ([CLAIM-tmpybc4k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpybc4k.md)).** Not addressed.

## Limitations

- **Ten groups per condition.** The key contrast is 7 of 10 against 2 of
  10.
- **No control message and no manipulation checks**, as the authors say.
  Reading any earnest text about climate between rounds might raise
  investment.
- **The per-message test.** The three message values come from the same
  ten groups, but F(3, 36) treats the four sets as independent groups. The
  windows are of unequal length (rounds 1–3, 4–6, 7–10).
- **Stakes are modest and the future is near.** A $45 endowment converted
  to trees; the players lose only what they keep.
- **Arithmetic and reporting slips.**
  - Intergenerational investment is said to be "36% lower" than legacy
    induction (p. 6); legacy induction is 36% higher, and the
    intergenerational figure 27% lower.
  - The messages are said to raise investment by 28%, 32% and 41% over
    baseline (p. 8). Those are the baseline's shortfall as a share of each
    message's figure. Over baseline the rises are 38%, 47% and 69%.
  - The model test is printed χ²(27) = 11.66, p = .003; that p matches 2
    degrees of freedom, not 27.
  - Strategy frequencies are printed with dollar signs ("$38.20 ± 3.97");
    they are counts.
- **Citation errors.**
  - The death-priming result is attributed to Wade-Benzoni 2002 (p. 2).
  - The 2010 paper's title is given as "the intergenerational allocation
    of benefits and burdens".
  - "Millinski" for Milinski in Jacquet et al.; a stray "736" in Henrich
    et al.'s title "Most people are not WEIRD".
- **The supplementary source.** The APS release is a press summary with no
  numbers, and has "thier" for "their".

## Open questions

- Which message works, and through what? A design that gave each message
  alone, against a neutral persuasive text, with a measure of felt
  connection to future generations, would say whether identification
  carries the effect.
- Does a personal legacy (being remembered by name) move people more or
  less than a collective one (being remembered as a generation)?
