# NOTE-tmp0pm1e: Flack — Multiple time-scales and the developmental dynamics of social systems

## Contribution

A short review, with some new counts, of one captive group of pigtailed
macaques at Yerkes (1998). It sets out three stacked networks (fight
outcomes, subordination signals, and the power distribution computed from
the signals) and argues that each higher one changes more slowly than the
one below it, predicts the system better, and is what the animals use. It
names these predictive coarse-grained variables "slow variables" and
proposes that building them is the first step towards a new level of
organization.

## Key insight

A higher level appears when components start conditioning their behaviour
on a coarse-grained summary of their own interaction history, and the
summary is useful because it changes slowly. A fight outcome is noisy; a
subordination signal integrates many fights and flips only after several
reversals; power integrates many signals. The slow variable reduces
uncertainty, and the residual fast fighting keeps it revisable, so the two
timescales trade predictability against lock-in.

## Assumptions

- One captive group: 84 animals, 48 socially mature ones analysed, 156
  hours of all-occurrence sampling over 20 weeks, June to October 1998,
  with "a 4 month period during which the group was stable".
- The peaceful silent bared-teeth display is a subordination signal that
  communicates a contract to yield, as argued in Flack & de Waal (2007).
- Power is operationalized as signals received times number of
  signallers (Figure 1 caption, §6c), a simplification of the
  entropy-based measure of Flack & Krakauer (2006).
- Stationarity of the group over the study period, which is how the
  period was chosen ("no reversals in status-signalling interactions
  resulting in a change to an individual's power score").

## Key results

- **Fight network.** 761 of 2256 ordered dyads fought; directional
  consistency 90.16%; outcome direction changed 292 times; 0.384 changes
  per dyad (s.d. 0.9285), 0.003 per dyad per hour.
- **Signal network.** 704 dyads exchanged the peaceful signal; direction
  reversed twice in the four months; 0.003 changes per dyad (s.d. 0.053),
  0.00002 per dyad per hour; directional consistency 99.7% (peaceful),
  96.6% (agonistic).
- **The ratio.** "Edges in the signalling network change approximately
  165 times (roughly two orders of magnitude) more slowly than edges in
  the aggression network."
- **Power.** The distribution is not significantly different from
  log-normal (Lilliefors), heavy-tailed; individuals in the tail police
  at negligible cost, and a knockout of policing (from Flack et al. 2006,
  not this paper) destabilized the group's networks.
- **Power's timescale is not measured.** "We do not have enough data in
  the present case study to estimate the precise time-scales on which
  properties of the power distribution change."
- **Use by the animals.** "The data suggest that individuals can perceive
  and use these representations"; individuals "appear to reference the
  signal exchange, rather than fight outcomes"; for relative power, "We
  are presently exploring the heuristics the animals could be using".

## Concepts

- **Slow variable**: a coarse-grained, predictive variable that changes
  slowly because it integrates over fast microscopic dynamics; it reduces
  uncertainty only when components can detect it.
- **Subordination contract**: the peaceful silent bared-teeth display, by
  which the signaller agrees for a time to the subordinate role.
- **Power**: "the degree of consensus among group members that an
  individual can use force successfully".
- **Social niche construction**: collective construction, by edge-building,
  of aggregate social properties that feed back on the constructors.

## Connections

It builds on Flack & Krakauer (2006) on encoding power in signalling
networks, Flack, de Waal & Krakauer (2005) on policing cost, and Flack et
al. (2006) on the policing knockout, none of which the record holds. The
slow-variable notion is Flack & Krakauer (2011, *Chaos*). The record's
nearly decomposable systems (LIT-905) give a structural reason for slow
aggregates; this paper gives an informational one: slowness is useful to
the members, not only a consequence of weak coupling.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-327** takes its member-accessible slow variable from this
  paper, as A9 read it: "The animals themselves appear to use information
  encoded in these more stable relationships". The paper's own words
  match A9's "appear": that the animals read and condition on the
  variable is what "the data suggest", argued from earlier studies, and
  for relative power not yet worked out. Stage 1 of the claim
  (statistical regularity) is shown here for the signal network. Stage 2
  (member accessibility) is asserted, not shown. Stage 3 (regeneration)
  is the paper's §4 argument that fighting lowers after signals and so
  consolidates the relation; no measurement supports it here.
- **CLAIM-331** (slowness a mark, not a definition, of a level). The
  paper supports the mark and adds a condition the claim does not state:
  a slow variable founds a level only when the components can detect and
  use it (§5a). It also states the converse limit, that a slow variable
  must not change too slowly or it stops tracking fighting ability.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- **One group, one season, captive.** No replication, no second group.
- **The 165× ratio does not follow from the counts given.** Per dyad,
  0.384 / 0.003 ≈ 128; from the raw counts, (292/761) / (2/704) ≈ 135;
  per hour, 0.003 / 0.00002 = 150. Each is "roughly two orders of
  magnitude", but none is 165, and the paper does not show its
  computation.
- **Inconsistent descriptive figures.** "The mean number of agonistic
  interactions per individual ... was 2.11 (s.d. = 0.713)" cannot be a
  count per individual over the study: 761 dyads that fought at least
  once give each of 48 individuals at least 31 participations on average.
  It is probably a rate, unstated. "The maximum number of outcome changes
  observed per dyad was 11 and the minimum was 1" must refer only to
  dyads that changed, since the mean over all is 0.384.
- **Dyad counting.** "(48 × 48)–48 or 2256" counts ordered pairs; the
  text calls them dyads.
- **The stability criterion is circular for C1.** The study period was
  chosen as one with no signal reversals that changed a power score, so
  the low reversal rate of signals is partly a selection effect.
- **Use is not tested here.** No analysis in this paper conditions
  behaviour on the signal network or on power.

## Open questions

- Do the animals' choices depend on the slow variable once the fast one
  is controlled for? A test that conditions strategy use on power with
  recent fight outcomes held fixed would settle C3.
- What is the timescale of the power distribution itself, and how does it
  depend on the algorithm that computes power?
