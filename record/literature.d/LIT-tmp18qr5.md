# LIT-tmp18qr5: The Sources of Normativity

Christine M. Korsgaard (1992), "The Sources of Normativity", The Tanner
Lectures on Human Values, delivered at Clare Hall, Cambridge University, 16
and 17 November 1992; printed in *The Tanner Lectures on Human Values* 15
(1994), pp. 19–112 — <https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/korsgaard94.pdf>

## Key takeaways

- **The normative question is first-personal.** It is asked "in the heat of
  action" by someone whose confidence has been shaken, and it asks "what
  justifies the claims that morality makes on us". Substantive realism
  "refuses to answer" it: belief in intrinsically normative entities rests
  on the very confidence the question puts in doubt. Normative concepts exist
  "because human beings have normative problems".
- **Practical identity is the source of reasons and obligations.** Reflection
  opens a gap between impulse and action, so "the reflective mind cannot
  settle for perception and desire" and "needs a reason". Choosing requires
  identifying with a principle, and so a "conception of your practical
  identity": "a description under which you value yourself, a description
  under which you find your life to be worth living and your actions to be
  worth undertaking". "Your reasons express your identity, your nature; your
  obligations spring from what that identity forbids." Violating the
  identities most important to you is "to lose your integrity and so your
  identity".
- **From any identity to humanity, and to others.** Because you must value
  some practical identity in order to act at all, you must value your
  humanity, the source of all your reasons. Reasons are public, like
  meanings on Wittgenstein's private-language argument: "it takes two to
  make a reason", and in calling out your name, "I have obligated you". Pain is "the
  unreflective rejection of a threat to your identity", so animals can
  obligate us too.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-03 at the owner's request, as the read substitute for *The
Sources of Normativity* (1996, [LIT-tmpv6pq9](LIT-tmpv6pq9.md)). The book is closed, and is
filed Deferred beside this as the canonical citation. The lectures are the
book's first version: the book calls itself the "longer version" and adds
the commentaries of Cohen, Geuss, Nagel and Williams and Korsgaard's reply.
The book's chapter titles (Crossref) follow the lectures' sequence, so the
argument read here is the argument the book develops.

Tags. `ethics` comes first, because the lectures are a metaethics: an
account of what makes moral claims normative. `self-governance`, `agency`
and `personhood` follow the owner's distinction. Self-governance is because
autonomy, being "a law to yourself", is the source of obligation. Agency is
because the argument runs from the structure of reflective choice.
Personhood is because practical identity, what makes someone the person
they are for practical purposes, does the work, and Korsgaard ties integrity
to being "a person at all".

Where it bears on the record:

- **Frankfurt and the regress ([THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md)).** Korsgaard borrows Frankfurt's
  "wanton" and argues that a person "cannot be like that": someone who acts
  like a wanton "must be treating the desire of the moment as a reason", and
  that commitment "counts as a second-order volition" (Lecture III, note 4).
  So every reflective agent has a law, and which one depends on how she
  conceives her identity. This is a stopping point specified by the
  structure of reflection rather than by an attitude's order. Whether it
  escapes Velleman's dilemma is what [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md) asks, and the NOTE takes it
  up.
- **Self-constitution** ([LIT-tmpnjho3](LIT-tmpnjho3.md), [LIT-tmpstkvq](LIT-tmpstkvq.md)). Practical identity here
  becomes self-constitution in her later work. "The Normative Constitution
  of Agency" cites these lectures' Lecture 3, in its book form, for the
  claim that self-consciousness puts the agent in "a kind of relationship of
  authority over herself".
- **Personal identity.** The lectures cite her "Personal Identity and the
  Unity of Agency: A Kantian Response to Parfit" (1989) for the point that
  "the acting self cannot coherently be taken to exist just at a particular
  moment". That paper is not in the record: the author's Harvard deposit
  sits behind a bot challenge. It is the direct link between Korsgaard and
  Parfit's reductionism ([LIT-447](LIT-447.md), [LIT-469](LIT-469.md)).
- **Frankfurt's Tanner Lectures** ([LIT-293](LIT-293.md)) answer the same question from
  the side of caring and love. Korsgaard's are the Kantian answer.
