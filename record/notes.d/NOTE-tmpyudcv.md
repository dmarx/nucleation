# NOTE-tmpyudcv: Adams & Aizawa — The bounds of cognition

## Contribution

This is the first sustained philosophical reply to [LIT-097](../literature.d/LIT-097.md) and to the wider
"transcranialist" camp (Dennett, Donald, Hutchins). It moves the debate
from where cognition is to what cognition is. The bounds of cognition
"must be determined by finding the mark of the cognitive, then seeing what
sorts of processes in the world have the mark" (p. 46). It proposes two
necessary marks, and it gives the "contingent intracranialism" position a
name.

## Key insight

Grant parity, then ask whether anything outside the head actually does what
the head does. On their view nothing does: notebook marks mean what they
mean by convention, and the physics of a screen or a pencil is nothing like
the processes in a brain. Like muscle contraction and unlike some
digestion, cognition happens to stay inside the body.

## Assumptions

- **The mental is not the cognitive.** "We assume without argument that the
  mental is not the same as the cognitive" (p. 48). Paradigm cognitive
  processes are learning, remembering, sensing, perceiving and thinking.
- **Consciousness is inessential to cognition.** "We assume without
  argument that there can be cognitive processes that lack associated quale
  and are not phenomenally conscious" (p. 48).
- **Mark 1: non-derived content (§2.1).** Cognitive states "must involve
  intrinsic, non-derived content". The support is a claimed "fairly broad
  consensus" (Dretske, Fodor, Searle, Millikan) and arguments against
  Dennett's stance view and against purely syntactic parsing.
- **Mark 2: causal individuation (§2.2).** Sciences partition phenomena into
  causally homogeneous kinds, so cognition "must be discriminated on the
  basis of underlying causal processes". The analogies are Bacon on heat
  and neo-Lamarckians confusing inheritance with infection. A look-up table
  passing the Turing test, and chess programs' search, are behaviour
  without cognition.
- **Cognitive science is immature.** "Relatively little can be said about
  the difference between the cognitive and the non-cognitive" (p. 53). The
  confidence that a processing condition exists comes from what mature
  sciences have found.

## Key results

- **Parity granted (p. 46).** They "don't propose to challenge" the parity
  principle. It shows only that "being inside the brain cannot be the mark
  of the cognitive", which "seems to us to be true and obvious".
- **Contingent intracranialism (pp. 46–47, 53).** Transcranial cognition is
  "logically and perhaps nomologically possible", but as "boring contingent
  empirical fact" there is none. Cognition is like muscular contraction,
  which stays in the arm when shears are used, and unlike some digestion,
  which is transcorporeal in spiders and in coprophagous rabbits and
  chickens.
- **Tetris (§3.1).** Case (1) uses mental representations of the blocks. In
  case (2) the blocks on screen "are not representations at all ... they
  are the blocks to be fit together", and button-driven rotation is a
  circuit and a CRT, "surely not the same as any cognitive process".
  Case (2) adds decision, attention, memory and motor processing that
  case (1) lacks. Case (3) is underdescribed: it can be aligned with (1) or
  with (2), not with both.
- **Otto and Inga (§3.1).** Otto's notes have "merely derived content", so
  they "do not ... constitute beliefs or memories". Otto's retrieval uses
  motor and visual processing that Inga's lacks, and Inga uses a capacity
  Otto has lost. The differences cannot be unimportant "save by adopting a
  behavioral conception of cognition".
- **The coupling argument (§3.2).** Quoting [LIT-097](../literature.d/LIT-097.md)'s coupled-system
  passage, they reply that causal coupling does not extend a process: the
  expansion of a thermostat's bimetallic strip stays in the strip, and
  filtration stays in the kidney, though both are coupled to more. They
  grant that Clark and Chalmers "are under no illusions" about this and
  actually claim the external processes are like the internal ones. That
  claim, they say, begs the question, and is false. Without a mark of the
  cognitive, coupling threatens "pancognitivism", called "cognitive bloat"
  or "cognitive ooze".
- **Explanatory simplicity (§3.3).** Easier explanations are not better
  science ("one's plants are thirsty"). "Isomorphism is a relatively weak
  relation": melting and evaporating are isomorphic and distinct.
- **Donald and Hutchins (§§3.4–3.5).** Donald's own table of engrams versus
  exograms (Table 1) lists the processing differences that defeat his
  claim. Hutchins studies "naturally occurring computation, rather than
  cognition". Even a perfect computational duplicate of the brain's
  program would lack cognition unless its states had non-derived content.
- **No science of tool use (§4).** Functional kinds do exist in science
  (heritability, oscillators), but "there are no laws covering humans and
  their tool use over and above the laws of intracranial human cognition
  and the laws of the physical tools". Memory aids, perceptual instruments
  and computing devices are each "an unscientific motley". This is offered
  as speculation and as "our bet".
- **Not a verbal dispute (§4).** They define "cognition_cs" (non-derived
  content plus processing) and "cognition_r", the radical notion, and
  predict that only the first will yield a natural science.

## Concepts

- **mark of the cognitive** — the conditions that separate cognitive from
  non-cognitive processes. The paper offers two necessary conditions, not
  a theory.
- **non-derived (intrinsic) content** — meaning a state has not by
  convention or social practice.
- **transcranial / extracranial / transcorporeal cognition** — the labels
  for the view opposed. "Extended mind" is not their term.
- **contingent intracranialism** — cognition could extend and does not.
- **cognitive bloat ("cognitive ooze")** — the slide to pancognitivism if
  coupling suffices.
- **cognition_cs vs cognition_r** — the commonsense notion and the radical
  one.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-tmp31lxe](../theory.d/THEORY-tmp31lxe.md).** The paper takes consciousness off the table by
  assumption, so it says nothing about phenomenal group minds. Its
  natural-kind argument (C8) would, if sound, count against treating
  organised groups as cognitive systems, but it is not applied to groups,
  and List ([LIT-tmp6phwb](../literature.d/LIT-tmp6phwb.md)) grants groups agency on different grounds.
- **Mereology.** C6 is the paper's contribution to the parts question:
  coupling is not parthood. It offers no account of parthood for
  processes either.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Both marks are assumed.** The authors say so, and they say little about
  what non-derived content is.
- **The processing differences are asserted, not measured.** The paper cites
  no experiment on external memory. Rupert ([LIT-tmplqv7e](../literature.d/LIT-tmplqv7e.md)) later supplies
  the empirical version.
- **The "no science" argument is a bet.** The authors call it one.
- **The concession on p. 50** (it is unclear how much of each cognitive
  state needs non-derived content) weakens the derived-content objection
  against a hybrid like Otto-plus-notebook. Clark exploits it.

## Open questions

- Is there a non-question-begging test for non-derived content that a
  silicon replacement for a neuron passes and a notebook fails?
- Would a cognitive science of human-plus-tool systems find law-like
  regularities? The paper bets not; nobody here has measured it.
