# THEORY-tmpuehzc: Minimal selfhood is identification with the whole body, located where that body is felt to be and seen from inside it; owning a body part does not reach it, and bodily agency enables it causally without being part of it

## Source

Blanke & Metzinger (2009), [LIT-tmpnbv1d](../literature.d/LIT-tmpnbv1d.md), *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*
13(1), read in full in [NOTE-tmplqqpe](../notes.d/NOTE-tmplqqpe.md): the definitions and Box 1 (pp. 7–8),
partial against global ownership (pp. 9–10), Box 2 and the clinical cases
(pp. 9–11), full-body illusions (pp. 11–12) and the conclusion (p. 12).
Lenggenhager et al. (2007), [LIT-tmpwaa0j](../literature.d/LIT-tmpwaa0j.md), read in full with its supplement
and e-letters in [NOTE-tmphzb5j](../notes.d/NOTE-tmphzb5j.md), is the full-body illusion the paper leans
on most.

## What was actually shown

The THEORY has three parts, and they rest on evidence of different kinds.

**The three dimensions.** Blanke and Metzinger define minimal phenomenal
selfhood (MPS) as "the conscious experience of being a self", in its
simplest form (p. 7). It has three dimensions:

- self-identification with the body as a whole;
- self-location, a felt volume of space;
- a weak first-person perspective, the geometrical origin of an
  egocentric model.

"We claim that spatiotemporal self-location, self-identification and weak
1PP are necessary and sufficient for MPS" (Box 1). This is a definition,
not a finding. What the evidence shows is that the three can be disturbed
separately:

- In autoscopic hallucination a second own body is seen, but all three
  dimensions stay normal.
- In out-of-body experience all three move to an illusory, usually
  elevated body.
- In heautoscopy they can sit at either body or alternate.
- In one stimulation case, the visual perspective stayed at the body while
  self-location was felt 2 m behind it.

These come from small lesion series and case reports (Box 2, pp. 9–12).

**The full-body illusion.** In Lenggenhager et al., synchronous stroking of
a body seen 2 m ahead in a head-mounted display shifted felt self-location
toward it by about 17–25 cm (two groups of 14). Synchronous stroking also
produced reported self-identification with that body (Q3, 2.1 on a −3 to 3
scale for one's own filmed body). No one reported disembodiment or a change
of visual perspective. A mannequin worked; a rectangular object gave a
smaller, non-significant drift and no self-identification. This is the
evidence that multisensory conflict can move the first two dimensions
without the third.

**Part against whole, and agency.** The rubber hand illusion and
somatoparaphrenia "are only associated with changes in partial ownership
and do not enable the investigation of MPS" (p. 9). From the clinical
and experimental data the authors conclude that "a passive, multisensory
and globalized experience of 'owning' a body is sufficient for minimal
conscious selfhood", and that "bodily agency is a causally enabling, but not
a constitutive condition, for phenomenal selfhood" (p. 12).

## What this does not say

- **The part–whole split rests on what was not measured.** The claim that
  the rubber hand leaves MPS untouched comes from the fact that those
  studies "do not experimentally manipulate self-location,
  self-identification or the weak 1PP" (p. 9). No study is cited that
  measured them and found them unchanged. That is the first half of the
  promotion test.
- **The agency thesis rests on adults.** The patients had agency before
  their lesions, and the illusion participants had it intact but unused.
  The authors grant that agency "causally enables MPS in practically all
  humans". The evidence shows that MPS can be altered without agency at
  the time. It does not show that a system that never had agency could
  have MPS, and the further claim about "a disembodied, non-neural,
  artificial system possessing no effectors" is conceptual.
- **Box 1 is loose.** The dimensions are called necessary and sufficient
  under a heading for conditions "that are not sufficient", followed by
  "Perhaps one or more of them are not individually necessary". The text
  also allows that agency "might be a sufficient condition for MPS"
  (p. 12). The THEORY's title takes the three as the content of minimal
  selfhood. It does not take them as each strictly necessary.
- **The full-body evidence is modest and contested.** In Lenggenhager et
  al.'s study II, the own-body condition did not differ significantly
  between synchronous and asynchronous stroking. The drift for one's own
  body was not shown to differ from that for the object. In the
  e-letters, Ehrsson & Petkova argued that participants may have been
  recognising themselves in a video system, not owning a body. The design
  has no condition that separates the two.
- **The paper names no way to manipulate opacity.** It offers a graded
  form of the self-model theory's transparency claim: opacity weakens
  self-identification. Its evidence is reports of how real the experience
  is, so it does not test [THEORY-064](THEORY-064.md)'s distinctive claim.

## Connections

No relation is declared: no open question asks what the minimal self is
made of.

- **[THEORY-458](THEORY-458.md).** Gallagher's minimal self pairs ownership with agency.
  This THEORY splits ownership into part and whole, and takes agency out of
  the minimal self. The two disagree on what the minimal self is made of,
  not on any finding.
- **[NOTE-372](../notes.d/NOTE-372.md), claim C6.** Metzinger's "simplest form of self-awareness" is
  narrowed here to global ownership. The rubber hand, the best-known
  manipulation of ownership, does not reach it.
- **[THEORY-tmpef8lf](THEORY-tmpef8lf.md)** is the part-ownership finding this THEORY says does
  not reach minimal selfhood. [THEORY-tmpxdoe8](THEORY-tmpxdoe8.md) and [THEORY-tmpom3z2](THEORY-tmpom3z2.md) treat
  part and whole ownership as one inference, so they sit in tension with
  the part–whole split; neither tests it.

  ownership without agency agrees with the agency thesis from the other
  side.
- **[LIT-1186](../literature.d/LIT-1186.md).** The line Lenggenhager et al. draw between illusory
  self-location and the disembodiment of an out-of-body experience is the
