# THEORY-1963: Preschool children's wait-times on the marshmallow task depend on their evidence about whether the promised larger reward will come: children who have just seen the experimenter break promises wait about a quarter as long, so wait-times reflect a rational judgment of the environment's reliability as well as self-control

Kidd, Palmeri & Aslin (2013), [LIT-2730](../literature.d/LIT-2730.md).

## The experiment

Before the marshmallow task, an experimenter twice promised a child better
art materials and either delivered them or returned empty-handed. The
reliable group then waited a mean 722.43 s, the unreliable group 181.57 s
(W = 22.5, p < 0.0005); 9 of 14 against 1 of 14 waited the full 15
minutes (§3). The result could have come out otherwise: if wait-time
measured a stable capacity, a few minutes' evidence about one adult should
not have moved it fourfold.

## What it does not show

- **That self-control is irrelevant.** The authors say so (§4).
- **Effects beyond the session.** One experimenter, one session, one lab,
  28 children.
- **Anything about control or answerability.** The paper concerns the
  rationality of waiting given evidence; the use of the promisor's
  discretion is [CLAIM-744](../claims.d/CLAIM-744.md)'s, not the paper's.
