# NOTE-tmpqb4at: History As Cause: Columbia and Challenger

## Contribution

An official accident investigation named organizational history, not a
technical fault or individual error, as a cause of the accident, and
showed the same decision pattern recurring seventeen years apart.

## Key insight

The danger signals were seen and processed, and the processing made them
normal. Each flight that survived an anomaly became evidence that the
anomaly was safe, so the evidential standard moved with the experience.

## Assumptions

- That the two decision histories can be compared as instances of one
  organizational pattern.
- That culture, structure and political environment are causes on a par
  with the physical mechanism.
- That the Board's reconstruction from documents and testimony captures
  how decisions looked at the time.

## Key results

- **Identical trajectories** (pp. 195–196). For O-rings and foam, a first
  decision to accept rather than eliminate the deviation set a precedent.
  Anomalies that did not cause catastrophe were treated as engineering
  data justifying further flight, and were classed "in-family", within the
  experience base.
- **Mixed and weak signals** (pp. 196–197). Danger signals were followed
  by all-clear signals, and foam strikes became routine, not signals of
  danger.
- **System effects** (pp. 197–199). Budget limits imposed by political
  sponsors, the shift to a business model with schedule and cost goals
  raised to the level of safety, and leaders' "doublespeak" made
  efficiency a strong signal and safety a weak one.
- **Structure** (pp. 199–201). Flight-readiness review by consensus
  "renders no one accountable". Safety staff depended on the program they
  supervised. Engineers had to prove a flight unsafe, not safe. Dissent
  did not surface at higher levels.
- **Persistence** (pp. 198, 202). Post-Challenger changes "were undone over
  time by management actions"; flawed practices "continued for 20 years".

## Method

Comparative case history: the Board's investigation of Columbia set
against the Presidential Commission's record and Vaughan's study of Challenger,
reconstructed from documents and testimony. The chapter interprets; its
evidence is in the rest of the report and in the cited works.

## Concepts

- **normalization of deviance** — operationally, reinterpreting a
  departure from design expectations as acceptable and non-deviant. The
  chapter gives no formal definition.
- **in-family** — "a formal classification indicating that new occurrences
  of an anomaly were within the engineering experience base" (p. 196).
- **failure of foresight** — after Turner, an accident with "a long
  incubation period" in which warnings were ignored or misread (n. 1).
- **bureaucratic accountability** — "an allegiance to hierarchy,
  procedure, and following the chain of command" (p. 199).

## Bearing on the record

Source of [THEORY-tmpe60l0](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpe60l0.md).

For the owner's essay: §5.4 (protective lock-in) has here a documented
case of a regime that reproduces itself, since each survived anomaly
becomes the justification for the next flight. §6.3's extraction from
above has a case in sponsors who accepted ambitious proposals and then
imposed strict budgets, though the Board describes constraint, not
extraction. §8.4 (externalized governance) has the Board's remedy: a
safety organization with independent authority and resources, because one
dependent on the program could not correct it. The Board's observation
that "NASA has shown very little understanding of the inner workings of
its own organization" (p. 202) bears on §8's question whether an
organization can govern a will it does not know.

## Limitations

Two cases in one agency. It is an official finding, with the authority and
the incentives of one. The chapter is unsigned, and Vaughan's role in
drafting it is not established by the report. The general theory of
normalization is in her book, which the record has not read. The
reconstruction is retrospective, which the chapter itself warns can
distort how decisions looked at the time.

## Open questions

Whether normalization has a signature that can be detected before an
accident, rather than reconstructed after one; and whether an independent
safety function stays independent once the crisis that created it has
passed, which endnote 49 doubts.
