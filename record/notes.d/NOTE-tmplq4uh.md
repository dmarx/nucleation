# NOTE-tmplq4uh: Ritchie — What are groups?

## Contribution

It sets out criteria a theory of groups (teams, committees, clubs, courts) must
meet, shows that the four theories then on offer each fail at least one, and
proposes a structuralist alternative that meets all six. The criteria and the
comparison tables are the lasting part. The positive view is introduced, not
developed.

## Key insight

What distinguishes a group from its members is its structure. The chess club
and the nature club can have exactly the same members and still be two clubs,
because the members stand in different functional relations in each. Any theory
that identifies a group by its members alone (plurality, fusion, aggregate,
set) collapses them.

## Assumptions

- **Groups exist.** Supposed, not argued (p. 2).
- **The six criteria hold of all groups.** Each is supported by one ordinary
  example (p. 2), and Coincidence by the claim that a student "is in two clubs"
  (p. 3). The paper rejects the Wiggins "truism" that two things cannot be in
  one place at one time, for groups (n. 4).
- **Many members is not a criterion.** The Senate reduced to one senator, then
  refilled, is "quite natural" to call one shrinking and growing body (p. 3).
- **Intentions are optional.** Social groups differ from tables in that members
  (or authorities, as when a president creates a committee by fiat) have
  group-forming intentions; such a condition "might be added to any of the
  views" (p. 4). It is not part of the view proposed.
- **Substantive answers beat sui generis ones.** Uzquiano's view that groups are
  sui generis is set aside for space; a substantive answer is "a mark in favor"
  (n. 1).
- **Structures as in mathematical structuralism** (Shapiro, Resnik, Hellman,
  Putnam; n. 9), with node-occupation in a plural logic (Boolos; n. 12).

## Key results

- **The six criteria (p. 3).** (1) Members–Times; (2) Members–Worlds;
  (3) Existence–Times; (4) Existence–Worlds; (5) Space; (6) Coincidence: groups
  of the same basic kind can be extensionally coincident and non-identical.
  Only (6) is said to mark groups off from tables and trees, which have
  coincident non-identicals only across sorts, as in Fine's tree and wood
  (p. 4).
- **Non-singular pluralities (§2).** Fail (1) and (2), since some people are
  not identical to more or other people; meet (3)–(5); meet (6) only if
  arrangements are added, which may reify arrangements and abandon the
  pluralist's nominalism (pp. 5–6).
- **Fusions (§3, Sider).** Meet (1) by time-relative parthood and (2) with
  counterpart theory, and (3)–(5); fail (6), since fusion identity is
  extensional. The Supreme Court and a committee of exactly the Justices, with
  the same members at every time, would be one group (p. 7). Counterparts
  "qua" a sortal give one fusion several modal properties, not two groups
  (p. 8).
- **Aggregates (§4, Burge).** Fail (1) for member-components, meet (2) only with
  counterparts, fail (6) when the member-components are the same (pp. 9–10;
  n. 8 allows distinct aggregates with different member-components).
- **Sets (§5, Effingham).** A group as a set of world-indexed sets of
  time-indexed member sets meets (1) and (2); fails (5) unless some sets are
  concrete; captures (6) only "in spirit" (possibly, not necessarily,
  coincident groups differ); meets (3)–(4) only by an ad hoc stipulation; and
  faces a Benacerraf problem, since the choice of set construction is arbitrary
  (pp. 10–12).
- **Groups as realizations of structures (§6).** A structure has nodes joined by
  functional relations (pitch-ball-to, return-ball-to), some hierarchical, all
  nodes connected to all others (p. 12). Grain is left open: weighted edges, a
  vague class of structures, or vague reference of "a group" (p. 13). Nodes may
  take one occupier (Chief Justice) or several (treasurer). Some things jointly
  realize S iff each occupies a node and every node is occupied; occupying a
  node is standing in the relations it requires (p. 14). Membership at t and w:
  x occupies a node of S and is functionally related as S requires to the other
  occupiers (p. 14). Identity at t and w: same members, and the same intra- and
  inter-structural relations among them (p. 15).
- **Why groups usually have many members (p. 14).** Most group structures
  contain an irreflexive relation (return-ball-to, has-more-power-than,
  seconds-a-motion-introduced-by, collaborates-with), and realizing one needs at
  least two occupants.
- **All six criteria met (p. 15),** with a group-as-many (the occupiers at t and
  w) delivered alongside the group-as-one, answering Russell's class-as-one and
  class-as-many.

## Concepts

- **Coincidence** — two groups of the same basic kind with the same members at a
  time (or at all times) that are not identical (p. 3).
- **Structure** — nodes and edges, the edges functional relations, some
  hierarchical (directed) (p. 12).
- **Node** — a place defined wholly by intra- and inter-structural relations
  (p. 13), possibly limited to one occupier.
- **Realization** — some things jointly occupying every node (p. 14).
- **Inter-structural relations** — relations to nodes of an embedding structure,
  such as a pitcher to the opposing batter in a game, or a team to its league
  (p. 13).

  discussed. [NOTE-340](NOTE-340.md) records List's functionalist definition of a group agent

  not the belief–desire functional profile List needs. The two are compatible:
  a group could be a realized structure in her sense and an agent in his. Her
  Coincidence criterion gives a sense of "over and above the members"
  (numerical distinctness) that holds of every structured group, agent or not.

## Limitations

- The positive view is four pages. Persistence conditions are not given beyond
  "continuity of the realization" (p. 14), and the grain of structures is left
  open (p. 13).
- **Occupation is defined holistically.** The first condition on occupying a
  node is that "every node in S is occupied" (p. 14), so whether one thing
  occupies a node depends on whether all others are filled; a structure with one
  empty node has no members at all. The paper does not discuss partial
  realization (a team one player short).
- The intention condition that would make these *social* groups is optional
  (p. 4), so as stated the view is a view of structured objects in general.
- The criteria are defended by single cases; a reader who doubts Coincidence
  (Uzquiano's alternative is not discussed) loses the main argument.

## Open questions

- What are the persistence conditions of a group through changes of structure,
  not only of members?
- Does the holistic occupation condition survive vacancies, which most standing
  bodies have most of the time?
- What, if anything, links a group's structure to the group's capacity to act
  as one? The paper does not ask.
