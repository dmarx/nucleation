# NOTE-384: Frankish — Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness

## Contribution

It gives the position a name and a problem. Before it, denials of
phenomenal consciousness travelled as "eliminativism", "irrealism" or
"qualia quining". Frankish replaces these with "illusionism", sets the
"illusion problem" against the hard problem, and fixes the line between
the strong view and the weak view many physicalists already hold. The
article does not supply an illusionist theory. It argues that the
programme is worth pursuing and maps the choices a theory would face.

## Key insight

The dividing question is not whether introspection misrepresents
experience. Most physicalists agree it does. The question is whether
anything "feely" is left once the misrepresented features (ineffability,
intrinsicness, privacy, infallible access) are removed. If what remains
is only a physical property that causes representations of
phenomenality, that is quasi-phenomenality, and calling it phenomenal is
like calling stage conjuring psychokinesis. So conservative realism must
either name a residue or become illusionism (§§1.2, 2.2).

## Assumptions

- **Physical** is used broadly: properties identical with or realised in
  microphysical ones (p. 2).
- **"Experience"** is used functionally, for the direct outputs of
  sensory systems, so it is not definitional that experiences are
  phenomenally conscious (p. 2).
- **The causal closure** of the physical is assumed in the argument
  against radical realism ("as ours appears to be", p. 11).
- **A representational theory of mind**: properties must be represented
  to have cognitive, affective or motivational significance (p. 14). The
  abductive argument and the reply to acquaintance theory both depend on
  it.
- **A presumption of conservatism** in science, stronger when the
  pressure for innovation comes from introspection (p. 11). Asserted as a
  methodological principle.
- **Introspective representations are not themselves phenomenally
  conscious** (p. 3), on pain of regress; footnote 13 records Pereboom's
  alternative.

## Key results

The article is philosophical argument; its "results" are distinctions
and arguments.

- **Three approaches** (§1.1): radical realism, conservative realism,
  illusionism. The first is radical, the other two conservative.
- **Strong vs weak illusionism** (§1.2) and **quasi-phenomenal
  properties** (p. 4, "zero qualia" in Frankish 2012). Weak illusionism
  is "diet qualia"; classic qualia are "qualia max" (n. 4).
- **Analogies** (§1.3): Dennett's user illusion, Rey's projection of
  stable objects, Humphrey's "ipsundrum" after the Gregundrum,
  Pereboom's qualitative inaccuracy hypothesis, Rey's child in the
  cinema. Introspection bundles complex physical detail into a simple
  feel. The illusion is cognitively impenetrable, as perceptual illusions
  are.
- **Varieties** (§§1.3–1.5): theories differ on which sensory states
  ground the illusion, on which of their properties are
  quasi-phenomenal, and on the form of introspective access. An
  "outward-looking" illusionism puts the illusory properties on distal
  objects (Hall 2007). Illusionism is distinct from the grand-illusion
  thesis about visual richness, though change blindness supports it.
- **What it keeps** (§§1.6–1.7): consciousness in an inclusive sense; a
  functional sense of "what it is like"; a fictionalist reading of
  everyday phenomenal talk (the Lear analogy). In the functional sense
  "there is something it is like to be a zombie" (p. 10).
- **Against radical realism** (§2.1): conservatism, and epiphenomenalism
  under causal closure. Russellian panpsychism avoids it only by "huge
  profligacy" (p. 11).
- **Against conservative realism** (§2.2): the phenomenal concept
  strategy still needs concepts *of feels*, so the pressure remains.
  Reductive theories that identify phenomenality with a functional
  property explain at most why subjects would judge their experience
  qualitative: "a conflation of phenomenality with the representation of
  phenomenality" (p. 13).
- **For illusionism** (§2.3): (i) the debunking argument, after Chalmers
  1996 pp. 186–7; (ii) anomalousness is evidence of illusion, and since
  introspection cannot be checked from within, the explanatory gap and
  zombie conceivability support illusionism as much as radical realism.
  Further advantages: it lets phenomenal properties be both "magical" and
  potent, as intentional objects; and it opens an adaptive function,
  Humphrey's "magic show".
- **Replies** (§3): denying the data (§3.1, acquaintance is
  psychologically inert, needs a non-psychological subject, and posits
  "intrinsic subjectivity"); no appearance–reality gap (§3.2, true only
  weakly); who is the audience (§3.3, no inner observer, "multiple drafts
  of sensation"); representing the non-existent (§3.4, functional-role
  semantics or hybrid concepts combining a theoretical concept of the
  phenomenal with recognitional ones).

## Concepts

- **Illusionism** — the view that experiences do not have phenomenal
  properties and that the task is to explain why they seem to. Always the
  strong form in this article (p. 4).
- **Illusion problem** — explaining why experiences seem to have
  phenomenal properties, and why the illusion is so powerful (pp. 1, 21).
- **Phenomenal properties** — introspectable qualitative properties
  ("feels") that determine what it is like to have an experience (p. 2).
- **Quasi-phenomenal property** — a non-phenomenal, physical property,
  perhaps gerrymandered, that introspection typically misrepresents as
  phenomenal (p. 4).
- **Weak illusionism** — real phenomenal properties, illusorily
  represented as ineffable, intrinsic, private and infallibly known; "it
  might equally be called weak realism" (p. 4).
- **Introspective vs intrinsic subjectivity** — awareness of experience
  produced by representational mechanisms, versus a subjective dimension
  that arises "simply from our being the things we are" (p. 17). The
  first is granted, the second suspected to be a fiction of the first.
- **Representational zombie** — a creature lacking introspective
  representations of its phenomenal properties, and so without cognitive
  access to them (p. 14).

## Connections

- **Dennett.** Called the pre-eminent defender (p. 1). The user illusion
  (CE pp. 216–20, 309–14), heterophenomenology (n. 8), multiple drafts
  extended to "multiple drafts of sensation" (p. 20), the storyteller's
  authority (p. 18), and change blindness as evidence (p. 8) are all his.
  "Quining Qualia" ([LIT-433](../literature.d/LIT-433.md), [NOTE-357](NOTE-357.md)) supplies the list of marks weak
  illusionists deflate. Dennett's reply in the same issue ([LIT-434](../literature.d/LIT-434.md)) is
  filed unread.
- **Frankish 2025** ([LIT-103](../literature.d/LIT-103.md), [NOTE-143](NOTE-143.md)). That commentary assumes this
  article: its "post-Cartesian paradigm" is illusionism, and its advice
  to drop "qualia" talk is this article's §1.6 hedged the other way.
  Here Frankish allows that phenomenal terms "might" be reconceptualised
  as quasi-phenomenal ones but says that "could invite confusion", and in
  scientific work it "might be wiser" to drop them (p. 9). The 2025
  commentary makes the second option firm.
- **Chalmers.** *The Conscious Mind* is cited for the debunking
  argument, the "datum" objection and acquaintance; the 2003 phenomenal
  belief paper for acquaintance and quotational concepts. The
  meta-problem paper ([LIT-425](../literature.d/LIT-425.md), [NOTE-354](NOTE-354.md)) is the later reply to the
  programme this article launched. [NOTE-354](NOTE-354.md) records that Chalmers
  suggested the name and that §6 agrees that dissolving illusionists
  must be strong illusionists. [LIT-425](../literature.d/LIT-425.md)'s Moorean step is the "denying
  the data" objection of §3.1.
- **Schwitzgebel.** *The Weirdness of the World* ([LIT-216](../literature.d/LIT-216.md), [NOTE-188](NOTE-188.md))
  cites Frankish 2016 when sharpening the eliminativist exit from the
  United States argument.
- **Dennett 1980** ([LIT-456](../literature.d/LIT-456.md), [NOTE-371](NOTE-371.md)). Dennett's reply to
  Stich holds that "there are no such things as pains, although of course
  people do feel pain". That is §1.6's distinction between phenomenal
  properties and the experiences that are said to have them, made 36
  years earlier for pain, without the word "illusion".
- **Taylor 2024** ([LIT-461](../literature.d/LIT-461.md), [NOTE-379](NOTE-379.md)) reads Dennett as a
  real-pattern realist for whom qualia eliminativism is peripheral. This
  article reads him as the leading strong illusionist. Both can be true
  only if "consciousness is a real pattern" is read as Frankish's
  inclusive sense of consciousness (§1.6), with phenomenality excluded.

## Limitations

- **A programme, not a theory.** The article says it "will not propose a
  detailed illusionist theory" (p. 1). The illusion problem is posed,
  not solved.
- **The key challenge is a challenge.** C1 depends on no one being able
  to articulate a stable weak-illusionist residue. Frankish "doubts"
  this is possible and cites his 2012 paper; the article does not show
  it.
- **The abduction is underspecified.** "If there is even a remote
  possibility that we are mistaken ... there is a strong abductive
  inference" (p. 14) does not say how anomalousness is to be weighed
  against the cost of error about something introspection presents as
  certain.
- **The representation of phenomenality is left open** (§3.4), and it is
  the core of the illusion problem by the article's own account.
- **No empirical programme.** §4 names the method (hypotheses about
  mechanisms across the cognitive sciences) but does not propose one.

## Open questions

- Can any illusionist theory explain the *content* of the introspective
  representations: why they represent "feels", richly and determinately,
  rather than mere introspectable somethings?
- Is "multiple drafts of sensation" (p. 20) compatible with Humphrey's
  single evolved "magic show"? Frankish flags that Humphrey might not
  agree.
- Does the fictionalist reading of everyday phenomenal talk (§1.6)
  survive the fact that most speakers do not take themselves to be
  speaking within a fiction? Frankish compares them to "naïve
  theatregoers" (p. 9); Schwitzgebel's charity objection to Dennett,
  reported by Taylor ([NOTE-379](NOTE-379.md)), presses the same point.
