# NOTE-1695: Chetty, Hendren and Katz — Childhood exposure to better neighbourhoods in Moving to Opportunity, read from the NBER working paper

## Contribution

Moving to Opportunity (1994–98, five US cities, 4,604 families in
high-poverty public housing) randomly assigned families to an
experimental voucher usable for its first year only in tracts under 10%
poverty, with counselling; an unrestricted Section 8 voucher; or control.
Linking 7,340 children to federal tax records through 2012 and splitting
them at age 13 at random assignment (RA), the paper reconciles the earlier
MTO nulls, which pooled ages and measured earnings too early, with
neighbourhood effects, and gives an experimental age gradient matching
Chetty and Hendren's quasi-experimental one.

## Key insight

The benefit of a place is carried out of it by those exposed while
young, in proportion to the years of exposure, and not by adults living
in the same places. In the authors' words, "neighborhood effects operate
primarily through “developmental” effects during childhood ... rather
than contextual effects arising from spatial mismatch" (p. 39).

## Assumptions

- **Identification.** ITT rests on randomization; TOT adds that the offer
  did not affect non-takers (footnote 15).
- **Tract poverty as an index** of a correlated bundle: safety, education,
  single parenthood, and modestly school quality (p. 15).
- **Lifetime extrapolation.** A constant proportional effect over ages
  26–65, 0.5% wage growth and a 3% discount rate (p. 36); "If these gains
  persist" (p. 6).
- **The exposure reading** assumes age at RA is not confounded with
  take-up or destination.

## Key results

- **Treatment design (§I, pp. 6–7).** The vouchers "could initially (for
  the first year) only be used in census tracts with 1990 poverty rates
  below 10%"; "The experimental vouchers became regular Section 8 vouchers
  after a year"; families remained eligible "indefinitely as long their
  income was below 50 percent of the median income" in their metro area.
- **Dose (§III.A, pp. 15–16).** TOT tract poverty 22–25 points lower from
  RA to age 18 (experimental), 12–15 points (Section 8). Younger children
  got "9.8 years of childhood exposure", older ones "only 2.9 years"; and
  "some families in the MTO treatment groups moved back to higher-poverty
  areas".
- **Young children's earnings (§III.B, pp. 18–19).** ITT $1,624, TOT
  "$3,477. This is a 31% increase relative to the control group mean";
  scaled, a 10-point lower poverty rate from a young age "increases
  earnings in adulthood by about 13-15%".
- **Older children (p. 19).** "The TOT estimates for older children are
  around -$2,000 for both treatments but are not statistically
  distinguishable from 0"; the young–old difference has p = 0.02.
- **By age at measurement (p. 20).** For young movers the impact "does in
  fact rise sharply with age of income measurement"; older movers have
  "less earnings growth in their early career".
- **Gradient (§III.G, pp. 28–30).** $4,823 at birth, falling $364 a year,
  so it "reaches zero for children who are 13.25 years at RA". "There is
  little evidence of a “critical age”". But "one cannot necessarily
  interpret the interaction effects" as the causal effect of a year, since
  the experiment "did not randomize the age at which children moved".
- **Disruption (footnote 5, p. 3; p. 21).** Control families also moved
  often, so "the disruption cost must reflect the cost of moving to a
  different type of neighborhood"; ages at move are "perfectly correlated
  with their length of exposure", so "we cannot distinguish differences
  in disruption effects by age" from a fixed cost with exposure effects
  (p. 4).
- **Adults (§IV.A, pp. 31–32).** Adult TOT −$734; cumulative low-poverty
  exposure "rises substantially over time" yet the effect is "consistently
  close to zero", "with no evidence of the increasing pattern". So "it is
  the amount of exposure to better neighborhoods during childhood (rather
  than total lifetime exposure) that matters".
- **Not income (p. 32).** The findings "imply that the MTO treatment
  effects on children’s outcomes do not arise from improvements in family
  income"; the authors suggest "direct effects of neighborhood
  environments" or "parental health and stress".
- **Next generation's place (§III.E, pp. 25–26).** The adult ZIP-poverty
  ITT is "about one-third as large" as the childhood effect; vouchers
  "produce durable benefits that persist into subsequent generations",
  inferred from the grandchildren's environment, not their outcomes.
- **Fade-out and re-emergence (§VI, p. 40).** Early gains "largely faded
  away over the next decade", yet effects "re-emerge in adulthood"; older
  youth showed "positive initial impacts" on behaviour, then somewhat
  negative long-run effects.
- **Choice weakens the dose (§V, p. 38).** Section 8 vouchers "are
  frequently used to rent better housing within the same neighborhood";
  "Providing more Section 8 vouchers (or equivalent cash benefits) may have
  little effect on children’s outcomes".
- **Fiscal (pp. 36–37).** About $394 a year more income tax (TOT) and a
  tax PDV of $22,400 per family with two young children, against
  counselling of $3,783 per family taking up the voucher.
- **Multiple testing (p. 5).** Permutation p < 0.01; the hypothesis was
  pre-specified from Chetty and Hendren (2015).

## Concepts

- **Childhood exposure effect.** Benefit proportional to the years in the
  better environment before adulthood.
- **Disruption cost.** A roughly fixed cost of moving to a very different
  social environment, dominant when few years of exposure remain.
- **Developmental against contextual neighbourhood effects** (p. 39).
- **Fade-out and re-emergence.** Intermediate outcomes vanish while adult
  ones appear.
- **Dose by restriction.** The restricted voucher roughly doubled the
  change in neighbourhood against the free one.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-600](../claims.d/CLAIM-600.md) (when support is safe to withdraw).** *Supports* the unit
  clause: the return is carried by people, not places; children keep it
  to age 28 after leaving the supported household, while parents given
  the same places gain nothing. *Qualifies* "transient": the subsidy was
  standing support; what ended was exposure, at the end of childhood, so
  this is persistence after the end of exposure, not after withdrawal.
  *Supports* "persistence after withdrawal is needed": the effects were
  invisible in the earlier evaluation and emerged only in adulthood.
  *Qualifies* "the party could do the work and did it throughout": the
  effect depends on the party's developmental stage, not effort.
- **[CLAIM-604](../claims.d/CLAIM-604.md) (thresholds, hysteresis): mostly against a threshold
  reading.** "little evidence of a “critical age”" and a roughly linear
  dose; the age-13 crossover is a linear benefit meeting a fixed cost, not
  an ignition threshold. Lasting adult earnings with no further supply are
  a state change in the person; whether self-sustaining or slow reversion
  is not shown.
- **[CLAIM-597](../claims.d/CLAIM-597.md) (supplied upkeep never becomes optional): qualifies.** For
  children the environment forms a capacity that outlives exposure,
  rather than doing upkeep; for adults, support in force changed nothing
  economic, outside the claim's model.
- **[CLAIM-602](../claims.d/CLAIM-602.md) (support crowds out learning by doing): none directly.** At
  most, free choice was used to rent better housing in the same
  neighbourhood.
- **[CLAIM-603](../claims.d/CLAIM-603.md) (one-way against reciprocal supply): none directly.** The
  fiscal analysis turns the one-way supply into a return through taxes, a
  delayed reciprocity at the level of the state.
- **[CLAIM-558](../claims.d/CLAIM-558.md) (decay with disuse): none.** Fade-out and re-emergence warn
  against reading an intermediate measure's decay as a capability's loss.
- **[CLAIM-601](../claims.d/CLAIM-601.md) (a buffer as a stock of time): none directly.** Family
  income is ruled out as the channel.
- **[QUESTION-398](../questions.d/QUESTION-398.md) (independence from the founding intervention).**
  *Supports* the shape of A's measure in a developmental form: the value
  of an intervention depends on the state at which it starts. It is not a
  withdrawal test: support was never withdrawn at matched states, and age
  at move is not randomized.
- Filed: [THEORY-1508](../theory.d/THEORY-1508.md). No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **Outcomes stop at about age 28**; lifetime persistence is extrapolated
  ("If these gains persist", p. 6).
- **Not a withdrawal experiment.** The subsidy continued; exposure was
  attenuated by moves back and by control families moving out.
- **Exposure and disruption not separately identified** (pp. 4, 29), by
  the authors' own account.
- **Small samples by age**; older-child effects insignificant.
- **A bundle.** No mechanism inside the neighbourhood is identified.
- **No spillovers measured.** The calculations "ignore any spillover
  effects on prior residents of the neighborhoods" (p. 38).
- **Second-generation benefit inferred** from the grandchildren's
  environment; adult ZIP codes missing for 20.4% of children.

## Open questions

Whether the gains persist past 30 in later follow-ups, and whether a
randomized mobility programme that randomizes age at move reproduces the
gradient.
