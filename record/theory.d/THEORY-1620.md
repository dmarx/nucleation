# THEORY-1620: Schubert et al. report that most laypeople judge human extinction bad but not uniquely worse than a catastrophe killing 80%, that the share who judge it uniquely bad rises when the victims are animals or the lost future is pointed out, and in one of two general samples when the catastrophe sterilizes, and that it passes a majority only when the surviving future is described as long and utopian

## The account

Asked plainly, most people locate the badness of extinction in its victims.
"An important reason why people do not find extinction uniquely bad is that
they focus on the immediate death and suffering that the catastrophes cause
for fellow humans, rather than on the long-term consequences" (p. 1).

Concern for the lost future appears when the future is pointed out, and most
strongly when it is said to be good. A self-selected group of
existential-risk mitigators judged extinction uniquely bad unprompted.

## Evidence

- Study 1 (US, n = 183): 78.14% judged extinction bad on a binary item.
- Study 2a (UK, n = 1,251, pre-registered), share judging extinction
  uniquely bad among those ranking as expected: control 23.47%, animals
  44.34%, sterilization 46.63%, salience 50.25%, utopia 76.74%.
- Study 2b (US, n = 855, pre-registered): control 18.75%, animals 34.59%,
  sterilization 28.75% (not significant), salience 39.45%, utopia 66.88%.
- Study 2c (Oxford students): control 36.92%, utopia 76.83%. Study 3 (N = 71
  mitigators): control 85.71%, utopia 94.59%.

## What it bears on

[QUESTION-299](../questions.d/QUESTION-299.md) cites this work: "Lay judgement does not treat extinction as
uniquely bad unless the lost future is made salient (Schubert et al.,
[LIT-1748](../literature.d/LIT-1748.md))." Its note of 2026-10-29 refines it: "A reminder of the lost
future brings this to about half; only a long, utopian future makes a
majority (67–77%)." The reading bears on how far lay concern reaches.
