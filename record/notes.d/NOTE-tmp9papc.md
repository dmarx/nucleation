summary: >-
  Using the complete action and transaction log of one free-to-play mobile
  puzzle game (2.5 million users, about 20 million paid loot boxes, 96% of
  in-game revenue from loot boxes), the authors estimate a dynamic
  discrete-choice model in which loot boxes can have "functional" value
  (better items raise win probability) and "direct" value (utility from
  opening, plus state dependence). Estimating separately for "whales" (top
  spenders) and others, they find regular players value loot boxes mostly
  for gameplay and whales mostly for the direct utility. Simulations
  evaluate a loot-box ban, a paid-loot-box ban, spending caps and changes in
  game difficulty.

# NOTE-tmp9papc: Amano and Simonov (working paper) — What Makes Players Pay? An Empirical Investigation of In-Game Lotteries

## Contribution

First revealed-preference (structural) measure of why players buy loot boxes, separating gameplay complementarity from the thrill of the lottery, with policy counterfactuals.

## Key results

- Abstract: "While regular players enjoy loot boxes primarily for their gameplay complementarity, high-spenders get the vast majority of loot box value from the direct utility."
- §1: "with 90% of revenue coming from 1.5% of players"; "90% of playing comes from 31.5% of top engagement users."
- §1: "the functional mechanism accounts for almost 90% of loot boxes’ value for non-whales. In contrast, whales get 97% of loot box value from the direct mechanism."
- §8.2: a blanket ban "will reduce the utility non-whales get from playing the game by 25.4%" (§1 summary); spending caps of $100-$500 leave non-whales unaffected, since "none of these players cross the lowest simulated threshold of $100".
- §8.2, difficulty: revenue rises "from 100% to 223% if the win probability is decreased by 10 percent, and all the way to 2349% if it is halved", driven by whales, while non-whale play falls.
- §8.2: "by targeting the preferences of whales, the company will decrease the engagement of non-whales – who are most of the players"; non-whales "may increase the adoption of the game by players, including whales, e.g. because more popular games are higher in rankings of app stores."
- §8.2: "we conclude that the current game design balances the firm’s revenue and engagement objectives."
- Figure 4 note (fn 27): the utility estimates include "behavioral biases like self-control problems"; "We caution the reader from interpreting these estimates as welfare measures."

## Assumptions

Whales are defined by spending; one prototypical single-player game generalizes; the direct-utility term captures thrill and inertia together (state dependence "should be interpreted as a reduced-form parameter"); no demographics, so minors and problem gamblers cannot be separated.

## Concepts

Loot box; whales; functional (gameplay-complementarity) vs direct value; state dependence; dynamic discrete choice (Hotz-Miller, Arcidiacono-Miller); spending caps; "free" customers' value.

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** The LIT summary (A25 §3, A29 §1-2, A31 §1) is accurate on the model, the whale/non-whale contrast and the 1.5%/90% and 31.5%/90% figures. It omits the result that matters most for the record: in the authors' simulations the firm does **not** set difficulty at the whale-optimal level; it trades whale revenue against non-whale engagement.

- **CLAIM-tmpmz271** (a responsive minority can set a design parameter against the majority's preference): **qualifies.** The whales' marginal revenue response to difficulty is enormous (Table 6: overall revenue ×23.49 at half win probability) and against non-whales' preference, yet the observed design is near the engagement-preserving end. The authors' explanation, non-whale engagement feeding adoption and rankings, is consistent with the claim only if that indirect revenue is counted in "marginal revenue response"; they do not estimate it. So in this one game, revenue weight did not simply set the design.
- **CLAIM-tmpcv1pg**: **qualifies** the structural half in the same way: the heaviest spenders do not set the distribution of what everyone is offered here.
- **CASE-tmpmw3hc** (proposed loot-box study): **bears directly.** It is the closest existing study: one game, full logs, whale vs non-whale tastes, and design counterfactuals. Its design lever is stage difficulty, not the real-money cost of a standardized reward that the case proposes to track; it is cross-sectional in design (one release), so it does not test drift across releases.
- **QUESTION-tmpuftum / ARG-tmp7eevo**: not bearing directly.
