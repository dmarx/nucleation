# NOTE-1310: The Effects of Trauma, with or without PTSD, on the Transgenerational DNA Methylation Alterations in Human Offsprings

## Contribution

A short review asking whether trauma-induced DNA methylation changes pass
from parents to children in humans. It finds two parent–child studies and
three of stress in pregnancy, summarises each with its limitations, and
recommends larger, genome-wide, multi-centre studies. It is the record's
only account so far of what the Holocaust FKBP5 study (Yehuda et al.
2016) found, as distinct from what its critics say of it.

## Key insight

The offspring do not carry their parents' mark. In Yehuda et al.,
survivors had higher FKBP5 intron 7 methylation and their offspring
lower. The authors call this "perplexing" and suggest "biological
accommodation" or a "compensatory mechanism" (§2, §4). If so, what reaches
the child is a response to the parent's state, not a copy of it.

## Assumptions

- **"Transgenerational" in the loosest sense** (§1). Defined as "the
  transmission of genomic information (in this paper DNA methylation) from
  one generation to the next". Exposure in utero and exposure before
  pregnancy both count.
- **Two designs, two mechanisms** (§1). When trauma precedes pregnancy,
  "methylation changes to the mothers' DNA is a necessity for the
  transmission". When it occurs during pregnancy, the mother's change is
  not needed. The review speaks of mothers only.
- **Candidate genes of the stress axis.** Every study reviewed measures
  methylation at a glucocorticoid-related gene (NR3C1, NR3C2, FKBP5) in
  blood.

## Key results

- **Scope** (§1). PubMed from inception to March 2018. Search terms,
  inclusion criteria and a count of screened papers are not given.
- **Rwanda** (§2; Perroud et al. 2014). 25 widows pregnant during the
  genocide and their children; 25 Rwandan women pregnant at the time but
  living abroad, and their children.
  - Exposed mothers and children had more PTSD and depression.
  - Higher methylation at NR3C1 exon 1F (CpG3–CpG9); no difference at
    NR3C2.
  - Lower cortisol in exposed mothers and children. NR3C1 methylation
    correlated negatively with plasma glucocorticoid levels.
  - Limits the authors name: small sample; groups in different
    countries; no stated matching; health in pregnancy, "parenting
    style", alcohol, tobacco and diet not controlled.
- **Holocaust** (§2; Yehuda et al. 2016). 32 survivors and their 22
  offspring; 8 control subjects and their 9 offspring. Blood FKBP5
  methylation and cortisol.
  - FKBP5 intron 7 methylation "significantly higher" in survivors and
    "significantly lower" in their offspring.
  - Limits named: sample size; survivors' "extreme starvation", which
    "could have also contributed to the effect seen".
  - "If further replicated in a larger study, these findings could change
    the way our field conceptualizes trauma and PTSD."
- **Stress in pregnancy** (§3).
  - Congo (Mulligan et al. 2012): in 25 mother–newborn dyads, prenatal
    stress correlated with newborn NR3C1 promoter methylation and with
    low birth weight. War stress accounted for 35% of birth-weight
    variance (r = −0.62, p = 0.0009); rape alone for 31%.
  - Intimate partner violence (Radtke et al. 2011): violence during
    pregnancy was associated with NR3C1 methylation in children aged
    10–19; the mother's own methylation was not associated with it. 25
    participants; violence recalled retrospectively.
  - Rodney and Mulligan 2014 (Congo) is cited in §4 for the same
    association in newborns.
- **Discussion** (§4). The authors say the studies "suggest that children
  of parents who had suffered from extreme trauma have methylation
  modifications", and that "this PTSD risk can be passed from generation
  to generation". They cite Agouti diet studies in mice as support.
  Weaknesses: few studies, small samples, possible publication bias,
  unmeasured confounds, cross-sectional designs.
- **Recommendations** (§4, §5). Larger studies with controls; record
  whether trauma preceded or occurred in pregnancy, and the trimester;
  distinguish chronic from acute trauma; move from candidate genes to
  well-powered genome-wide studies, as the NIMH genomics workgroup
  advises; share data across centres.

## Concepts

- **transgenerational epigenetic transmission** (the authors'): passing
  DNA methylation states from one generation to the next without sequence
  change, in utero exposure included.
- **biological accommodation**: the authors' gloss on offspring
  methylation opposite to the parent's, a compensating response.
- **NR3C1**: the glucocorticoid receptor gene. **FKBP5**: a moderator of
  glucocorticoid receptor activity.

## Connections

- **Yehuda et al. 2016 ([LIT-1692](../literature.d/LIT-1692.md))**, the original. This review gives
  its sample, tissue, gene region, direction of effects and one named
  confound. Its title says "intergenerational"; the review's says
  "transgenerational".
- **Bird ([NOTE-1264](NOTE-1264.md))** calls the study "a sample of only 32 people".
  By this review's figures, 32 is the number of survivors; the study had
  71 people in all, 31 of them offspring.
- **Heard and Martienssen ([NOTE-1254](NOTE-1254.md)).** By their rule, every study
  here is intergenerational. The Rwandan, Congolese and partner-violence
  children were exposed in utero. Survivors' children, if conceived after
  the war, are F1 of exposed parents.
- **Horsthemke ([NOTE-1265](NOTE-1265.md))** asks that genetic effects be excluded by
  haplotype. Nothing here says whether FKBP5 genotype was considered.
- **The Dutch Hunger Winter ([LIT-1751](../literature.d/LIT-1751.md)).** The review raises the
  survivors' starvation as a confound; the record's womb route already
  links famine to methylation.
- **Yehuda and Lehrner ([LIT-1715](../literature.d/LIT-1715.md))** is the clinical side of the same
  group; not cited here.

## Bearing on the record

- **The question ([QUESTION-297](../questions.d/QUESTION-297.md)).** Four points.
  - *Where it sits.* The record files Yehuda et al. under the germline
    route. On this account it is an intergenerational, parent-to-child
    study in blood. Nothing in it reaches a gamete, and nothing excludes
    the womb (for mothers), care, or shared hardship. It belongs with
    the "parenting" and "womb" routes as much as the germline one.
  - *What passes is not a copy.* Offspring methylation went the other way
    from the parents'. That fits a child's physiology adjusting to a
    parent's state, by any route, better than a mark inherited unchanged.
    This is an inference from the direction, which the review reports
    without analysis.
  - *The human molecular evidence, as of 2018, is small.* Two parent–child
    studies (25 + 25 pairs; 32 + 22 against 8 + 9) and in-utero studies of
    25 each, with a third cited without numbers. All are cross-sectional
    and candidate-gene.
  - *No study measured the social routes.* "Parenting style" is listed
    as an uncontrolled confound in Rwanda; the Holocaust study's named
    confound is starvation. Neither measured care, silence, or
    identification with the group's history. The question's social
    routes are unexamined here, not excluded.
- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).** Not touched. What these studies
  measure is a physiological state in blood, not any identification with
  an ancestor. No note on the claim is proposed.

## Limitations

- **Not the 2016 paper.** CpG sites, statistics, effect sizes, ages, sex
  of the exposed parent, who the controls were, genotype, cortisol
  relationships and the offspring's own trauma and PTSD are not here.
- **A thin review.** Five studies, no search strategy, no quality
  assessment, no tables. Its conclusion ("accumulating evidence") is
  warmer than its own list of weaknesses.
- **Loose use of "transgenerational"**, against the original's own word,
  "intergenerational", and against the record's sceptical readings.
- **The Agouti citation** is given as support for "robust impact on
  stress-related disorders"; it concerns diet and coat colour in mice.
- **Errors.**
  - "The genocide took place in 1995" (§2); the Rwandan genocide was in
    1994.
  - The rationale for metabolic syndrome in survivors' offspring (§4)
    cites a study titled "Maternal exposure to the holocaust and health
    complaints in offspring"; whether it reports metabolic syndrome is
    not checked here.
- **Typos.** "Offsprings" (title); "Radtke al" for "Radtke et al." (§3);
  "promotor" (§3); "thought the reviewed studies" for "though" (§3);
  "methylation changes ... is a necessity" (§1); "dietary supplements ...
  was associated" (§4); "the environmental transgenerational effects ...
  has" (§4); "not necessary PTSD alone" (§4); "Lockwood, Su, Hao, and
  Rutten declares" (Conflicts of Interest).

## Open questions

- Was FKBP5 genotype controlled in Yehuda et al.? Horsthemke's one
  feasible human step is the haplotype test.
- Were survivors' offspring conceived after the war, and did the effect
  differ by which parent was exposed? Both decide which route the study
  can speak to.
- Does offspring methylation track the offspring's own childhood
  experience of a survivor parent? If it does, the parenting route is the
  likelier carrier.
