# LIT-tmp6qp14: The Architecture of Complexity

Herbert A. Simon (1962), "The Architecture of Complexity", *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society* 106(6): 467–482 (read 26 April 1962; issue of 12 December 1962) — <https://www.jstor.org/stable/985254>

## Key takeaways

*Read in full; see [NOTE-tmpi3yt0](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpi3yt0.md).*

- **Hierarchy without a boss.** A hierarchy is any system "composed of
  interrelated subsystems", each in turn hierarchic. Simon widens the word
  explicitly to "systems in which there is no relation of subordination
  among subsystems"; "even in human organizations, the formal hierarchy
  exists only on paper".
- **Defined by interaction, not by place.** Social hierarchies are found "by
  observing who interacts with whom", and the two views are "reconciled by
  defining hierarchy in terms of intensity of interaction". Nerve cells and
  telephone wires "permit very specific strong interactions at great
  distances".
- **Stable intermediate forms.** Hora's ten-part subassemblies survive
  interruption and Tempus's thousand-part watches do not. The time to
  evolve a complex form "depends critically on the numbers and distribution
  of potential intermediate stable forms".
- **Near decomposability.** Short-run behaviour of each subsystem is
  approximately independent of the others; in the long run each depends on
  the others "in only an aggregate way". This is proved for nearly
  decomposable matrices in Simon & Ando (1961), cited, not shown.
- **Frequency separation.** "The higher frequency dynamics are associated
  with the subsystems, the lower frequency dynamics with the larger
  systems". In organizations, both the duration of executives' interactions
  and "the average interval between interactions is greater at higher than
  at lower levels". This is stated as probable, not measured.
