# NOTE-tmplslae: Citron — Technological Due Process

## Contribution

Three moves. Automated systems collapse the distinction between
adjudication and rulemaking (Londoner and Bi-Metallic). The translation of
policy into code is itself unauthorized rulemaking. And the cost structure
of correcting such systems (a high fixed cost with a recurring benefit)
breaks the Mathews calculus. On these Citron builds substitute procedures.

## Key insight

The rule a person is decided under is now the code, and it was made by
programmers, outside the procedures that legitimate rules, while the
procedures that protect individuals assume that a human applied a known
rule to found facts.

## Assumptions

- The twentieth-century settlement (due process for adjudication,
  participatory rulemaking for policy) is the baseline whose norms are to
  be restored.
- The systems in view are mid-2000s rules engines, data-matching and
  data-mining systems; machine learning is not.
- Automation bias, as the human-factors literature finds it, carries over
  to caseworkers and hearing officers.

## Key results

- **Thesis** (abstract, p. 1249): "This century's automated decision
  making systems combine individual adjudications with rulemaking while
  adhering to the procedural safeguards of neither." And: "Code, not
  rules, determines the outcomes of adjudications."
- **Inadvertent rulemaking** (p. 1254): "Although programmers building
  automated systems may not intend to engage in rulemaking, they in fact
  do so."; (p. 1288) "Such inadvertent rulemaking by programmers
  constitutes a troubling delegation of legislative power."
- **The collapse** (p. 1253): "Computer programs seamlessly combine
  rulemaking and individual adjudications without the critical procedural
  protections owed either of them."
- **Mixed systems** (p. 1272): "Automation bias effectively turns a
  computer program's suggested answer into a trusted final decision." …
  "In this respect, little meaningful difference exists between a mixed
  system and its fully automated counterpart."
- **The cost of challenge** (p. 1285): "Thus, any Mathews analysis that
  focuses on a single adjudication will capture all of the costs of
  improved process but only a tiny fraction of its benefits."
- **Process before deprivation** (p. 1288): "Because agency officials
  ultimately control the administration of automated decision systems,
  pre-deprivation process is surely possible."
- **Review** (p. 1299): "Because code is hard to unearth, and because of
  automation bias, encoded policy tends to receive even more powerful
  deference than that provided under Chevron."
- **Policy bent toward code** (p. 1255): "Agencies may be increasingly
  inclined to adopt policies involving simple questions and answers that
  are easy to translate into code".
- **What not to automate** (p. 1304): "Policies that explicitly or
  implicitly require the exercise of human discretion cannot be
  automated."
- **Remedies** (pp. 1305–1310): "At a minimum, automated systems should
  generate audit trails that record the facts and rules supporting their
  decisions." (p. 1305); "agencies should require hearing officers to
  explain, in detail, their reliance on an automated system's decision."
  (p. 1307); "First, vendors should release systems' source codes to the
  public." (p. 1308).
- **Conclusion** (p. 1313): today's systems "become devices for error
  propagation themselves".

## Concepts

Technological due process; adjudication versus rulemaking
(Londoner/Bi-Metallic); automated and mixed systems; automation bias;
encoded or distorted policy; de facto delegation to programmers; audit
trails; fixed versus variable costs in Mathews balancing;
pre-deprivation process (Parratt/Zinermon); open code; testing suites as
surrogate participation; rules versus standards.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-tmpohafo](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpohafo.md)** (explanation, contestation and interruption as
  separate entitlements). Corroborates. Citron treats notice (reasons,
  through audit trails), the hearing and pre-deprivation process as
  distinct safeguards that fail differently: inadequate notice devalues
  hearings (p. 1254); hearings exist but are hollowed by automation bias
  and the cost of decoding the system (pp. 1283–86); and process before
  deprivation is "surely possible" because the agency controls the system
  (p. 1288), the ground for the claim's third entitlement. She treats
  explanation as a precondition of contestation rather than a right that
  can be had alone.
- **[CLAIM-tmph28l6](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmph28l6.md)** (the cost of imposing a decision and the cost of
  challenging it are separate). Corroborates. Automation lowers the cost
  of deciding while challenging a decision comes to need costly decoding
  of the system; a Mathews analysis of one case captures "all of the costs
  of improved process but only a tiny fraction of its benefits" (p. 1285).
- **[CLAIM-410](../claims.d/CLAIM-410.md)** (removing a constraint changes a profile of governing
  positions). Corroborates its "transferred" case: rule-making authority
  passes to programmers by "inadvertent rulemaking", "a troubling
  delegation of legislative power" (p. 1288), beside Bovens and Zouridis
  ([LIT-tmp8l2be](../literature.d/LIT-tmp8l2be.md)).
- **[CLAIM-tmp238zy](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmp238zy.md)**. A qualified counterpoint, not filed as a relation:
  Citron's remedy for automation bias is cognitive friction placed on
  officials (training, and a duty to explain reliance, pp. 1306–07), and
  she presents it as serving the affected party's hearing. The claim's
  warning would apply if that duty were used to put errors on the
  officer.
- **[CLAIM-tmpb47ul](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpb47ul.md)** (control, obligation and blame on different
  parties). Mixed systems in which workers finalize the computer's
  decisions are an early case of nominal human responsibility without
  control.

## Verdict on the exchange's characterization

Faithful. A23: automation "can collapse adjudication and rulemaking while
undermining the procedural safeguards associated with both" is the
abstract's thesis almost word for word. "Foundational" is A's evaluation.

## Limitations

US constitutional and administrative law, with 2008 rules engines in view;
how far it carries to learned models is not addressed. The evidence is
case narratives and press or audit reports. The remedies are proposals.
The fixed-cost argument assumes a software fix carries cleanly to later
cases.
