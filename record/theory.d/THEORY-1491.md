# THEORY-1491: Neumark and Simpson's review finds that the US and European evidence on place-based policy shows gains at most while incentives are in force, often offset by relocation from nearby areas or captured in land and house values, and that the one program shown to leave gains after its funding ended, the Tennessee Valley Authority, did so through manufacturing agglomeration while its direct effect faded, with its agglomeration gains estimated to net to about zero nationally

## Source

Neumark & Simpson (2015), [LIT-2158](../literature.d/LIT-2158.md); read in [NOTE-1656](../notes.d/NOTE-1656.md) from NBER
Working Paper 20049 (April 2014, revised August 2014), not from the
published chapter. Page numbers are the working paper's.

**In whose voice.** Every result here is the authors' summary of another
study. The review is the source of the synthesis; the estimates belong to
the studies it summarizes, which were not read.

## What was actually shown

A review, not a test. What it reports:

- **The verdict** (§6, p. 73): "even the most positive evidence on their
  effectiveness does not establish that they create self-sustaining
  economic gains. That is, at best the evidence (sometimes) says that when
  place-based incentives are in effect, there are increases in economic
  activity".
- **Relocation**: UK enterprise zones, where "between 50 and 80 percent of
  enterprise zone businesses had relocated into the zones" (p. 24); US
  Empowerment Zones "to a first-order approximation, simply creating
  relocation of economic activity" (Hanson and Rohlin, p. 39); France, where
  a 300 m ring mirrors the in-zone effects "albeit with opposite sign"
  (p. 44); Texas, where results are "consistent with the positive findings
  being driven more by relocation" (p. 34).
- **Capitalization**: Texas, "a statistically significant 11 percent
  increase in median housing values" with "no change in median household
  income" (p. 34); Empowerment Zones, "The bulk of the estimated welfare
  gains come from positive effects on house prices in the zones" (p. 38).
- **The TVA** (Kline and Moretti, pp. 64–66): after 1960 "federal transfers
  to the program were negligible"; manufacturing grew faster in both
  periods, "around 10-12 percent per decade during 1940-1960, as opposed to
  3-3.5 percent from 1960-2000"; agglomeration "continued to make the TVA
  counties an attractive location for new manufacturing activity even in
  the face of depreciation of the initial infrastructure investments
  following the withdrawal of federal funding"; the direct effect was
  significant in 1940–60 and "negative but insignificant" after; and with a
  constant agglomeration elasticity "the positive agglomeration benefits
  which accrued to the TVA region offset by negative effects elsewhere."
- **The one other long-run case**, the Appalachian Regional Commission,
  shows "no statistically significant evidence of an effect over a longer
  period 1970 to 2000" (p. 66).

What could have come out otherwise: the review could have found evaluations
measuring post-incentive outcomes with persistent, non-relocated gains. It
found one program with persistent local gains, and those net out nationally.

## What this does not say

- **That support never leaves lasting gains.** The finding is about the
  evidence: most evaluations end while incentives are still paid. Absence
  of measured persistence is not measured absence.
- **That the TVA produced no national gain.** The direct effect's estimated
  benefits ($22–32.4bn) exceed the transfers ($17.3bn) (p. 66); it is the
  agglomeration part that nets to about zero.
- **That the summaries are right.** The TVA results are Kline and Moretti's
  (2014), read in parallel in this batch; the zone results are contested
  (Busso et al. and Ham et al. against Hanson and Reynolds–Rohlin).
- **That the published chapter says the same.** Only the 2014 draft was read.
- **Anything outside the US and Europe.**
