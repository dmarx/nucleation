# NOTE-353: True Believers: The Intentional Strategy and Why It Works

## Contribution

The essay replaces the observer-relative definition of "Intentional
Systems" ([LIT-440](../literature.d/LIT-440.md)) with a criterion meant to be objective: being a
believer is a fact about how well a strategy works on a system, not about
who uses it. It adds the Martian argument that intentional patterns are
real and missed by any purely physical predictor, the thermostat-to-person
continuum of increasingly constrained semantics, and a distinction between
the easy (evolutionary) and the hard (mechanistic) answer to why the
strategy works.

## Key insight

"Believes that p" is like "is a real pattern": whether it holds depends on
whether a compressed, rational-agent description predicts the system's
behaviour well, and that is a matter of fact even though only someone who
tries the description can see it. The more richly a system is tied to its
world, the fewer interpretations fit, until in practice one does; that is
all "really" believing adds.

## Assumptions

- **Rationality as the default.** One "starts with the ideal of perfect
  rationality and revises downward as circumstances dictate" (p. 64), and
  needs only the implications relevant to the moment.
- **Normal exposure gives true belief.** Exposure to x is "the normally
  sufficient condition" for true beliefs about x (pp. 61–62); false beliefs
  need "a special genealogy" made mostly of true ones (p. 62).
- **Evolution makes the bet safe** for people: we are designed "to believe
  what [we] ought to believe and want what [we] ought to want" (p. 76).
- **Prediction by others is the test.** Exclusion of the lectern rests on
  predictive gain over what "we antecedently" knew (p. 66), so the criterion
  presupposes some baseline of alternative strategies.

## Key results

The essay argues; it reports no data and proves nothing.

- **The thesis** (p. 59): belief is objective, discernible only by one who
  adopts the strategy, and confirmable only by the strategy's success.
- **How the strategy works** (pp. 61–64). Treat the object as a rational
  agent; ascribe the beliefs and desires it ought to have given "its place
  in the world and its purpose"; predict the action practical reasoning
  recommends. Derived rule: "attribute as beliefs all the truths relevant
  to the system's interests … that the system's experience to date has made
  available" (p. 62). So "true believers mainly believe truths", all but
  "say, less than ten percent" of a person's beliefs (p. 63; n. 1 separates
  belief from linguistically infected "opinion"). Language forces desires
  and convictions more specific than we had (pp. 63–64).
- **Where it works** (p. 65): people "almost all the time", most mammals,
  birds, fish, reptiles, insects, spiders, clams, the chess computer, the
  thermostat, apple trees, lightning.
- **Objective success** (pp. 66–67). The lectern is excluded for lack of
  predictive gain. Against the worry that cleverer observers would not need
  the stance: "The decision to adopt the intentional stance is free, but
  the facts about the success or failure of the stance, were one to adopt
  it, are perfectly objective."
- **Its power** (pp. 67–68): it cuts a chess position's live moves to a
  handful, and chains predictions across a whole "arc of causation" (the
  Secretary of State as a KGB agent) that no extension of physics could
  predict "under any description".
- **Martians** (pp. 68–71). Super-physicists who predict the ticker tape
  but miss the intentional patterns "would be missing something perfectly
  objective". There are "indefinitely many ways of ordering 500 shares of
  General Motors", "societal sockets" in which they are interchangeable,
  and "societal pivot points" where the outcome depends on whether people
  believe p. The Earthling's prediction, from overhearing Mrs Gardner's phone call, that a car
  will arrive within the hour carrying two people and a bottle of wine,
  would look like magic to the Martian. Since the Martians observe,
  predict and communicate, they must see themselves as intentional
  systems (p. 71; n. 3).
- **Milder realism** (pp. 71–72). In degenerate cases (self-deception,
  contradiction) there is no fact of the matter which beliefs a person has,
  but "when and why there is no fact of the matter is itself a matter of
  objective fact". Two rival interpretations can each be objectively
  present; the risk is "practically negligible" for human beings (p. 72;
  n. 4, McCarthy's cryptography analogy).
- **The thermostat continuum** (pp. 72–75). A thermostat has about half a
  dozen deinterpretable belief-like states. Enriching its sensors and
  behaviour shrinks "the class of indistinguishably satisfactory models"
  until a unique interpretation is "practically (but never in principle)
  dictated" (p. 73, citing Hayes 1979). Its belief is about this boiler
  "because it is fastened to the boiler". Representation is
  role-defined: "What makes some internal feature of a thing a
  representation could only be its role in regulating the behavior of an
  intentional system" (p. 74). "There is no magic moment"; the differences
  between thermostat and person are of degree, though so great that the
  simple case explains little about the complex one (p. 75). Twin Earth
  shows that fixing reference needs facts about embedding, for us as for
  the thermostat.
- **Why it works** (pp. 75–77). For a thermostat: it was designed to be
  so predicted, and its mechanism is easy to see. For people: evolution,
  an answer with "the virtues of truth and brevity" but "strikingly
  uninformative". Skinnerian and language-of-thought accounts are
  "doctrine". Dennett thinks "some version" of the language of thought
  "will prove correct", but warns against conflating two empirical claims:
  that the intentional pattern is real (confirmed "beyond skepticism") and
  that it is produced by an isomorphic internal pattern (reasons "not
  overwhelming"). The argument for the second is the brain's solution to
  combinatorial explosion: "what else could it be?" (p. 77). Note 6: every
  language-of-thought model so far falls to combinatorial explosion too.

## Concepts

- **Intentional strategy / intentional stance**: treat the object as a
  rational agent with the beliefs and desires it ought to have and predict
  what it ought to do (p. 61).
- **True believer**: an intentional system, "a system whose behavior is
  reliably and voluminously predictable via the intentional strategy"
  (p. 59).
- **Interpretationism**: the view that belief attribution is a matter of
  interpretation, like "has style" (pp. 58–59); Dennett combines it with
  realism.
- **Real pattern**: what the Martians miss; a regularity describable only
  from the intentional stance that supports generalisation (p. 69).
- **Opinion** (n. 1): a linguistically infected state of betting on the
  truth of a formulated sentence, as opposed to belief.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-043](../theory.d/THEORY-043.md).** C2 is substrate-neutral and silent on experience.
  It therefore supports the account's claim that agency, here belief,
  survives both directions of the bridge, if the group-level or
  agent-level stance pays. It never applies itself to a group. Its
  institutional examples (a Cabinet's response, p. 68; markets, p. 69;
  Oxford colleges, p. 65) are reached through individuals' reasons. On the
  essay's criterion a nation or a corporation would be a true believer if
  the stance applied to it as a unit predicts reliably and voluminously
  what nothing cheaper predicts; whether it does is an empirical question
  the essay does not ask, and Dennett does not say that any group is a
  believer.
- **[LIT-159](../literature.d/LIT-159.md) and [NOTE-131](NOTE-131.md).** Schwitzgebel's §6 answers Dennett's pragmatic
  objection from correspondence. That objection is about consciousness and
  this essay is about belief, so the essay does not contain it; but its
  standard, predictive gain "reliably and voluminously", is the kind of
  standard the objection applies.
- **[LIT-206](../literature.d/LIT-206.md) (Goldstein & Lederman).** Their objective interpretationism is
  this essay's C2 restated without a stance, with "sufficiently well"
  judged against rival theories. They exclude thermostats on tractability;
  Dennett includes them as degenerate cases (p. 72). The difference is the
  baseline: Dennett's lectern test asks for gain over what we already knew,
  theirs for gain over a mechanical explanation.
- **[THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md).** Nothing in the essay makes belief evidence of
  consciousness; a well-predicted machine is a believer, and the essay
  says nothing about whether it feels.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- The criterion's two adverbs, "reliably and voluminously", are not
  quantified, and the lectern exclusion depends on a baseline the essay
  leaves informal.
- The Martian argument assumes that what the intentional stance captures
  is not captured by any physical description, which is the point at
  issue; it is an intuition pump, not a proof.
- "Most beliefs are true" rests on a way of counting beliefs Dennett
  himself doubts exists (p. 63).
- What the 1981 text said is not known from this reading, so claims that
  the 1981 essay contained a given passage are not supported here.

## Open questions

- Does the group-level stance on a firm or a state predict anything that
  the stance applied to its members does not? That is the measurable form
  of the question this essay's criterion leaves open for groups.
- Where on the thermostat-to-person continuum do current language models
  sit, measured by how many interpretations their states admit? The essay
  gives the measure (shrinking classes of satisfactory models) but no way
  to count.
