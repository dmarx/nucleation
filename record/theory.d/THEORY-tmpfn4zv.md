# THEORY-tmpfn4zv: Because the returns to exploitation are surer, nearer and closer to the decision than the returns to exploration, organizational learning refines exploitation faster and tends to become self-destructive in the long run; and where an organizational code learns only from members who deviate from it, slow socialization, a mix of slow and fast learners and moderate turnover raise what the organization knows, while without turnover a changing environment degrades its knowledge to chance

## Source

March (1991), [LIT-tmpe51mm](../literature.d/LIT-tmpe51mm.md), read in [NOTE-tmpsry8p](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpsry8p.md).

## What was actually shown

March ([LIT-tmpe51mm](../literature.d/LIT-tmpe51mm.md)) argues the first half and simulates the second.

- **The trade-off, argued.** Compared with exploitation, exploration's
  returns are "systematically less certain, more remote in time, and
  organizationally more distant from the locus of action and adaption"
  (p. 73). Learning from feedback therefore favours exploitation, the
  advantages cumulate, and competence in an inferior activity can exclude
  a superior one. Balance is "a primary factor in system survival and
  prosperity" (p. 71); March gives no rule for it.
- **Mutual learning, simulated.** Members adopt the code's beliefs; the
  code adopts the majority view of members who know more than it does.
  Without outside input both converge on a shared, stable, not necessarily
  accurate belief. Equilibrium knowledge is highest when the code learns
  fast and members are socialized slowly; a mix of fast and slow learners
  beats a uniform population at the same average; moderate turnover
  improves the code because recruits, though less knowledgeable, are
  diverse.
- **Under environmental change.** Without turnover, the converged beliefs
  stay put while reality moves, and knowledge falls to chance. Moderate
  turnover sustains it, provided recruits are not chosen to fit the code.
- **Who pays.** Gains from slow learners accrue to fast ones, so no member
  has an incentive to learn slowly; under turbulence tenure is valuable to
  individuals in an organization with turnover.

What could have come out otherwise: the simulations. Slow socialization
might have lowered equilibrium knowledge, and turnover might only have
diluted it. Both results depend on the code learning only from members who
outperform it.

## What this does not say

- **Not that learning is bad.** March says the models do not "overturn the
  renaissance"; knowledge remains important.
- **Not an optimal balance.** He says determining one is "a nontrivial
  exercise".
- **Not about members choosing to leave.** Turnover is an exogenous rate,
  and recruits are random with respect to the code.
- **Not shown in real organizations.** The dynamics are properties of one
  stylized model in which nobody observes reality and members learn only
  through the code.

## Connections

- **Transient diversity ([THEORY-tmpqnfk9](THEORY-tmpqnfk9.md)) and the Zollman effect
  ([THEORY-tmpgo526](THEORY-tmpgo526.md)).** Those accounts find, in models of scientific
  communities, that diversity of pursuit must last long enough to survive
  misleading early evidence, and that dense communication can end it too
  soon. March's model reaches a parallel result for an organization: fast
  socialization ends diversity too soon for the code to learn from it. The
  mechanisms differ, a central code here and peer communication there, so
  no relation is declared.
- **Drift ([THEORY-tmpj5hlr](THEORY-tmpj5hlr.md)).** Rasmussen's drift moves practice toward a
  safety boundary under cost pressure. March's lock-in keeps belief fixed
  while the boundary moves. Both make an organization worse while its
  local adjustments seem sound; the routes differ.
- **The owner's essay.** For §5.4 it supplies lock-in without a decision to
  lock in; for §8.2, an organizational case of a community's knowledge
  depending on sustained dissent.
