# NOTE-tmpnji9e: The "What" and "Why" of Goal Pursuits: Human Needs and the Self-Determination of Behavior

## Contribution

The theoretical keystone of SDT. It argues that the concept of need, dropped
by cognitive motivation theory in favour of goals, is required, and gives
SDT's version: few, innate, psychological, universal, and defined by what
they do for well-being. It then shows how two separate lines of evidence,
on regulatory process and on goal content, are unified by need
satisfaction, and extends the account to need thwarting, the self,
evolution and culture.

## Key insight

Goals are not interchangeable once valued equally and expected equally.
"Without the concept of innate needs, all desires are equal in functional
importance if they are equal in strength" (p. 231). With it, some desires
are "linked to or catalyzed by our psychological design" and others are
"derivative or compensatory", the by-products of past thwarting, which may in
turn cause further thwarting (p. 231). The same move explains why an
unusually strong wish to control a situation is read not as a strong need
for autonomy but as a sign that the need was thwarted (p. 231).

## Assumptions

- **Organismic-dialectical metatheory** (pp. 228–229). Humans are "active,
  growth-oriented organisms ... naturally inclined toward integration of
  their psychic elements into a unified sense of self and integration of
  themselves into larger social structures". This is a postulate.
- **The set point is activity, not quiescence** (p. 229). Unlike drives,
  needs do not have to be the aim of behaviour: "children ... do not play to
  feel competent" (p. 229).
- **Functional identification of needs** (p. 228). A need is shown by
  positive consequences of its satisfaction and negative consequences of its
  thwarting. This makes "need" and "well-being" partly interdefined.
- **Individual differences are in orientations, not need strength** (pp. 230–231).
  Causality orientations and regulatory styles are outcomes of the history of
  need support (p. 231).

## Key results

The article is a review and argument; results are cited.

- **Needs against Hull and Murray (pp. 227–231).** Hull's needs are innate
  and physiological; Murray's psychological and acquired. SDT's are innate
  and psychological. Murray's abasement, acquisitiveness and dominance "are
  certainly not needs" in SDT's sense (p. 227). Psychological needs differ
  from physiological ones: thwarted, they readily produce "defenses and need
  substitutes" rather than intensified pursuit (p. 230).
- **Autonomy defined (p. 230).** "Autonomy refers to volition—the organismic
  desire to self-organize experience and behavior and to have activity be
  concordant with one's integrated sense of self." It is "often incorrectly
  equated with the ideas of internal locus of control, independence, or
  individualism".
- **Intrinsic motivation (pp. 232–234).** Two strands of definition (no
  separable consequences; based in needs) can come apart: "behaviors that
  are directed at satisfaction of these needs are not necessarily
  intrinsically motivated" (p. 232). "Perceived competence is necessary for
  any type of motivation, perceived autonomy is required for the motivation
  to be intrinsic" (p. 234). The 1999 meta-analysis ([LIT-tmp58ot3](../literature.d/LIT-tmp58ot3.md)) is said to
  have "repudiated" Eisenberger and Cameron's (1996), "whose methods and
  conclusions turned out to be fatally flawed" (p. 233).
- **Internalization (pp. 234–238).** External regulation, introjection
  ("swallowing regulations whole without digesting them", Perls, p. 235),
  identification, integration. Introjection "involves internal prods and
  pressures and is characterized by inner conflict between the demand of the
  introject and the person's lack of desire to carry it out" (p. 236).
  Integrated extrinsic motivation "does not typically become intrinsic"; it
  remains instrumental (p. 236). In Deci et al. (1994), with two or three
  facilitating factors the internalization looked integrated (behaviour
  correlated positively with valuing, enjoyment and freedom); with zero or
  one it looked introjected (negative correlations) (pp. 237–238).
- **The "why" of goals (pp. 238–242).** Studies across school, religion,
  health care, politics and the environment link autonomous regulation to
  persistence, performance and health. Causality orientations: autonomy
  orientation goes with self-actualization and with congruence among traits,
  attitudes and behaviour (Koestner et al. 1992); control orientation with
  public self-consciousness; impersonal orientation with depression (p. 241).
- **The "what" of goals (pp. 243–246).** Intrinsic versus extrinsic
  aspirations, as in [LIT-tmpckq0r](../literature.d/LIT-tmpckq0r.md). Carver and Baird (1998): autonomous
  reasons for pursuing wealth help, but the relative importance of wealth
  still predicts lower self-actualization with reasons controlled (pp. 244–245).
- **Culture (pp. 245–247).** Iyengar and Lepper's (1999) Asian-American
  children are read as showing that "the means through which autonomy is
  expressed can differ across cultures" (p. 246). Genital mutilation and the
  rule that boys should not cry are offered as values that "can at best
  become introjected or compartmentalized" (p. 246).
- **The self (pp. 247–248).** The self "begins with intrinsic activity and
  the organismic integration process". "The inherent tendency for activity,
  the integrative process, and the fundamental needs are all aspects of one's
  nascent self." Introjected regulation is "motivated by processes internal
  to the person but relatively external to the self". Higgins's "ought self"
  is an introject (p. 247).
- **Need thwarting (pp. 247–251).** Three accommodations: need substitutes
  (extrinsic aspirations; T. Kasser et al. 1995: cold, controlling mothering
  predicted teenagers' materialism, even from observer ratings at age 4);
  non-optimal regulatory styles; and "rigid behavior patterns", which "have
  the maladaptive features of tending to keep people from dealing with their
  inner experiences and of tending to persist into new situations in which
  they are not needed" (p. 250). Anorexia is the worked example (Bruch;
  Strauss & Ryan 1987). "The etiology of various forms of psychopathology
  resides primarily in developmental deprivations concerning basic
  psychological needs" (p. 247).
- **Evolution (pp. 251–255).** The needs are broad, cross-domain design
  features, against Buss's and Tooby and Cosmides's modularity. Autonomy is
  "an extension of [the] deeply evolved tendency" to integrated functioning
  in living things (p. 252), and "the means by which humans can avoid having
  their behavior easily entrained down maladaptive, even disastrous, paths"
  (p. 253; Olds's self-stimulating rats). Needs, not emotions, are proposed as
  the higher-order regulators, "because emotions themselves must be
  self-regulated for effective functioning" (p. 254).
- **Other theories (pp. 255–261).** Self-efficacy theory "does not
  distinguish between autonomous and controlled behaviors", so it treats
  "pawns to reward contingencies" as agentic (p. 256); Bandura's definition
  of autonomy as "entirely independent" agency is rejected (p. 256).
  Approach–avoidance cannot replace autonomy–control: reward seeking is
  approach yet controlled, and quitting smoking on a physician's advice can be
  autonomous avoidance (pp. 257–258). Attachment security varies within a
  person across partners as a function of need support (La Guardia et al.
  2000, p. 261).

## Concepts

- **need**: "innate psychological nutriments that are essential for ongoing
  psychological growth, integrity, and well-being" (p. 228).
- **autonomy**: volition; "the organismic desire to self-organize experience
  and behavior and to have activity be concordant with one's integrated sense
  of self" (p. 230).
- **homonomy**: integration of the individual into a larger social whole,
  paired with autonomy as the two natural tendencies (pp. 252, 262).
- **causality orientations**: autonomy, control and impersonal orientations,
  general individual differences in how behaviour is regulated (p. 240).
- **need substitute / compensatory motive**: a motive that gives "collateral
  satisfaction" without satisfying the thwarted need (p. 248).
- **intrinsic / extrinsic aspirations**: life goals more (growth,
  relationships, community) or less (wealth, fame, image) directly tied to
  need satisfaction (p. 243).
- **self**: in SDT, the integrated set of processes that begins with
  intrinsic activity and organismic integration, and is elaborated by
  integrating regulations (p. 247).

## Connections

  is the argument; that one the overview.
- **Vansteenkiste, Ryan & Soenens (2020), [LIT-tmplhywo](../literature.d/LIT-tmplhywo.md)**, which formalizes
  this paper's functional criterion into five basic and four associated
  criteria for a need.
- **Bandura (1977), [LIT-545](../literature.d/LIT-545.md), unread.** The disagreement is about whether
  efficacy expectations are the whole of agency. This paper's charge is that
  self-efficacy theory has "only one class of motivated behaviors" (p. 256).
  Whether the 1977 paper answers it cannot be checked until [LIT-545](../literature.d/LIT-545.md) is read.
- **Frijda, Ridderinkhof & Rietveld (2014), [LIT-540](../literature.d/LIT-540.md).** A real disagreement.
  Frijda holds that much emotion regulation is one state of action readiness
  checking another, with no regulator of a different kind ([NOTE-433](NOTE-433.md)). This
  paper puts needs, and "the self", above emotions: "emotions themselves must
  be self-regulated" (p. 254). Frijda's concerns, which include "goals, needs,
  values" ([NOTE-433](NOTE-433.md)), are what give events pertinence, so the two may differ
  less in mechanism than in which level they call the regulator.
- **ACT's model (Hayes et al. 2006, [LIT-555](../literature.d/LIT-555.md); Kashdan & Rottenberg 2010,
  [LIT-553](../literature.d/LIT-553.md)).** SDT's "rigid behavior patterns" that keep people "from dealing
  with their inner experiences" and persist where not needed (p. 250)
  describe what ACT calls experiential avoidance and inflexibility. The
  explanations differ: ACT traces it to verbal processes (fusion), SDT to
  thwarted needs. Neither paper cites the other.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md).** The self here is specified organismically, as the
  integrative tendency and intrinsic activity, before any endorsing attitude
  (p. 247). That is SDT's candidate for a stopping point that is not an
  attitude of a higher order. Whether it is "specified without presupposing
  the agent's participation" in Velleman's sense, and so disownable, is the
  test. The paper's answer is that the nascent self cannot be alienated from
  because it is the agent's own activity, which is close to the stipulation
  [THEORY-029](../theory.d/THEORY-029.md) records for Frankfurt's wholehearted love.
- **[THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md).** Need-thwarting histories produce introjects and substitute
  motives that a person may value and pursue wholeheartedly (wealth as a
  substitute for relatedness, p. 248). SDT therefore separates present
  endorsement from autonomy by appeal to history and to need satisfaction.
  That is a substantive, not content-neutral, criterion in the IEP's sense
  ([NOTE-266](NOTE-266.md)): some contents "are not integrateable" (p. 246).
- **[THEORY-tmppzrsd](../theory.d/THEORY-tmppzrsd.md) (emotion regulation is one motive state checking
  another).** This paper is a source against it on one point: it posits
  needs as a higher-order regulating level over emotions (p. 254). It gives
  no evidence for that level beyond the argument.
- **[THEORY-tmpdbe64](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpdbe64.md) (inflexibility as a transdiagnostic process).** The
  rigid-patterns passage (p. 250) is a rival etiology for the same pattern.
- **No ML instruction.** Nothing here belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Interdefinition.** Needs are identified by their effects on well-being,
  and well-being is "an organismic function in which the person detects the
  presence or absence of vitality, psychological flexibility, and a deep inner
  sense of wellness" (p. 242). The paper does not show how to test a need
  claim without assuming the well-being measure.
- **Mostly correlational.** Apart from the reward and internalization
  experiments, the support is cross-sectional or diary correlation.
- **The evolutionary section** offers adaptive stories, not evidence that the
  needs were selected.
- **The commentaries were not read.** Carver and Scheier's objection that
  needs are "simply another set of goals" (p. 258) is known only through this
  paper's report of it.

## Open questions

- Can "integrated" regulation be distinguished empirically from
  "identified"? The paper uses the distinction but cites no study measuring
  integration alone.
- What would show that a value is "not integrateable"? The examples
  (genital mutilation, boys not crying) are asserted, not tested.
- Are compensatory motives and rigid patterns distinguishable from ACT's
  experiential avoidance by any measure, or are they two names for one
  pattern?
