## 3. Stand-in A (read in full): Tomasello 2024, Frontiers in Developmental Psychology
- Provenance: https://sites.duke.edu/tomasellolabduke/files/2024/09/fdpys-02-1367381.pdf (Tomasello lab, Duke, which hosts the publisher's own PDF). The publisher's OA page is https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fdpys.2024.1367381/full. Kinds: lab page and publisher OA, CC BY ("This is an open-access article distributed under the terms of the Creative Commons Attribution License (CC BY)"). PDF, 11 printed pp. Read in full: abstract; §1 Types of human agency (1.1 Phylogeny, 1.2 Ontogeny, 1.3 Novel features); §2 Ontogeny (2.1–2.3); §3 The regulation of agency; §4 Conclusion; note 1. Reference list scanned only.
- Bibliographic details: Michael Tomasello, "An agency-based model of executive and metacognitive regulation", Front. Dev. Psychol. 2:1367381, published 08 March 2024, doi:10.3389/fdpys.2024.1367381. Type: Conceptual Analysis.
- Summary: Tomasello recasts executive function and metacognition as two tiers of self-regulation in a control-systems model of agency. The executive tier monitors and controls action and attention; the metacognitive tier monitors and controls the executive tier. §1.1 restates the book's phylogeny (vertebrates goal-directed, mammals intentional, great apes metacognitive/rational, humans shared). §1.2 and §2 map the same architectures onto ontogeny: go/no-go before 9 months, either/or with executive tier and joint agency at 9–12 months, metacognitive and collective agency at 3–4 years. Cognitive flexibility is recast as the coordination of perspectives, learnt first in shared agencies and then internalized.
- Contribution: a typology by four dichotomies (executive vs metacognitive workspace; monitoring vs control; reactive vs proactive; individual vs shared agency), offered as a frame for the many executive-function constructs.
- Key results (printed page; verbatim):
  - p. 2, §1.1: "The computational model for agency is cybernetic control systems such as thermostats and self-driving cars"
  - p. 2, §1.1: "Goal-directed agency evolved in the first vertebrates." / "Intentional agency evolved in the first mammals."
  - p. 3, §1.1: "Metacognitive (or rational) agency evolved in the first great apes." / "Shared agency evolved in the early humans"
  - p. 3, §1.1: "The hypothesis is that there was a gradual transition from one form of agency to another across species"
  - p. 2, intro: "executive function comprises cognitive processes that regulate attention and action, whereas metacognition regulates these executive-level cognitive processes themselves"
  - p. 3, §1.2: "Both intentional agency and joint agency emerge at 9 to 12 months of age"
  - p. 4, §1.3: what Diamond and others call “cognitive flexibility” is about the coordination of perspectives
  - p. 7, §2.3 (O'Madagain et al. 2022): "The apes were in this case metacognitively assessing their executive decision after they had already made it"
  - p. 7, §2.3: "This kind of normative self-regulation can be characterized as we > me normative self-regulation"
  - p. 9, §3: "executive and metacognitive processes are two analogous control systems operating on different material from different tiers"
- Assumptions: agency is a feedback-control architecture; higher tiers are added on lower ones over phylogeny ("subsequent forms build on already existing forms", Bonner 1988) and recapitulated in ontogeny; model species (lizard, squirrel, chimpanzee) stand in for ancestral grades; the human-unique part is shared (joint, collective) agency with normative self-regulation.
- Concepts: goal-directed, intentional, metacognitive (rational), joint, collective agency; action, executive and metacognitive tiers; executive and metacognitive workspaces; go/no-go vs either/or vs reflective decisions; reactive vs proactive inhibition; procedural metacognition (Goupil and Proust 2023); we > me normative self-regulation.
- How far it stands in: it restates the book's architecture (Ch. 1, "A Feedback Control Model of Agency") and its phylogeny (Chs. 2–5) only in summary, about one page, citing Tomasello (2022). It gives none of the book's comparative evidence for lizards, squirrels or apes beyond one ape study. Its development half belongs to the 2024 OUP sequel, not to this book. Good for the architecture and the four grades. Weak for the evidence chapters and for Ch. 6.
- Limitations: a conceptual essay with no new data. Phylogeny asserted, not argued. Some readings of infant data are contested by the author's own note (Hamlin; Goupil). The labels change: "rational" in the book becomes "metacognitive (or rational)" here, and "socially normative" becomes "shared".

## 4. Stand-in B (read in full): Tomasello 2023, Animal Cognition
- Provenance: https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10071-022-01662-0.pdf (publisher OA, CC BY 4.0: "This article is licensed under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International License"). Also PMC9876876 (the Europe PMC full-text call returned HTTP 500; not retried). PDF, 11 pp. Read in full pp. 25–34 (all sections). References scanned only.
- Bibliographic details: Michael Tomasello, "Social cognition and metacognition in great apes: a theory", Animal Cognition 26 (2023) 25–35, doi:10.1007/s10071-022-01662-0 (online 1 Aug 2022).
- Summary: a 25-year review. Great apes understand others' goals, intentions, perception and knowledge (not clearly beliefs). They monitor uncertainty (opting out, hesitation) and seek information, diagnosing what they lack. The theory, attributed to Tomasello (2022): mammals have one executive tier, great apes a second, metacognitive tier. Apes attribute to others the mental states they monitor in themselves, under the pressure of food competition over clumped fruit, perhaps helped by social learning. Five testable predictions.
- Key results (printed page; verbatim):
  - p. 30, Summary: "if great apes have a theory (or an understanding), it is a theory of agentive action"
  - p. 32, Seeking information: "For many philosophers, examining one's own knowledge critically and adjusting it in the light of new evidence, is best called rational decision making."
  - p. 32, A theory: "Executive monitoring of one’s own behavior is common among mammals."
  - p. 32, A theory: "This is accomplished with a second-order tier of executive functioning— metacognitive or reflective functioning—that supervises the first-order executive tier."
  - p. 32, A theory: "attributed the same mental states that they were monitoring in themselves to others"
  - p. 33, Conclusion, prediction 1: "Across primate species—and perhaps across nonprimate species as well—there should be a correlation between self-regulatory and social cognitive skills."
- How far it stands in: it restates the book's Ch. 4 ("Ancient Apes as Rational Agents") and the mammal–ape step of Ch. 3, with the evidence for ape metacognition and social cognition, and gives the ecological story (food competition). Nothing on lizards or on humans. The best stand-in for Ch. 4.
- Limitations: a review. The step from self-monitoring to attributing mental states is a hypothesis; the author says the ecological conditions "are unknown". Carruthers and Williams 2022 (cited) hold that apes monitor feelings, not cognitive states.

## 5. Candidate THEORY sentence
Tomasello proposes that agency is a feedback-control organization built up in grades over the human lineage: goal-directed agency (go/no-go control) in the first vertebrates; intentional agency, with an executive tier that monitors and controls action and attention, in the first mammals; rational or metacognitive agency, with a second tier that monitors and controls the executive tier, in the first great apes; and shared agency, joint and then collective, with normative self-regulation, in early humans.
- promote_when: the book itself (Chs. 1–6), or an equivalent text by Tomasello, is read and states this sequence of four grades, with the tier added at each and the clade it is assigned to, as above.
- Stand-in sentence if only the 2024 paper is used: Tomasello (2024) proposes that executive processes monitor and control action and attention from an executive tier and metacognitive processes monitor and control those processes from a second tier, two analogous control systems that he holds evolved in the first mammals and the first great apes and that emerge in children at 9–12 months and 3–4 years.
