# NOTE-xxxx: Human transgenerational responses to early-life experience: potential impact on development, health and biomedical research

## Contribution

A review and manifesto for "transgenerational epidemiology". It
summarises the human studies of grandparental and paternal exposures,
chief among them the authors' own Överkalix series, and the animal
experiments that bear on how such effects could be carried. Its aim is
practical: that birth cohorts collect parents' and grandparents' exposure
data, by life stage, and test each mode of transmission separately. Two of
its three authors, Pembrey and Bygren, are authors of the 2006 paper it
is read in place of.

## Key insight

The timing of the ancestor's exposure is the authors' argument against
culture. A grandfather's food supply mattered for his grandsons when it
fell in his "slow growth period", the years before the prepubertal growth
spurt, and not later in childhood. They take that as "compelling evidence
for some other form of transmission between generations" than cultural or
genetic inheritance alone (Conclusions from human observations to date).
Their methodological rule follows: "assuming cultural inheritance
automatically when results make genetic inheritance implausible (and vice
versa) is no longer acceptable" (Extending epidemiology).

## Assumptions

- **A broad sense of "transgenerational"** (Framing future research). It
  covers any measurable outcome in offspring of an exposure in the mother
  or father before conception, or in their parents or ancestors. The
  stricter usage is noted (ref. 18) and set aside. A fetus exposed in
  utero counts as exposed, so for a pregnant mother the outcome of
  interest is in her child's children.
- **The exposure, not a phenotype, is what passes.** The exposed or
  intermediate generation need show no phenotype, and the outcome need
  not be harmful (Framing future research).
- **Mechanism-agnostic.** "Studies in the field should not be predicated
  upon a particular mode of transmission" (Framing future research).
- **Social patterning can be "accounted for"** in the right setting, which
  would separate cultural from biological transmission (Framing future
  research). How is not said.

## Key results

- **Origin of the word** (Framing future research). "Transgenerational"
  was first used in this context in the 1980s for the transmission of
  Holocaust survivors' trauma to their unexposed offspring (Nadler et al.
  1985).
- **Överkalix** (Food supply, paternal line; Table 1). Historical records
  of harvests and food supply in northern Sweden, linked to probands born
  in set years. Five studies:
  - Bygren et al. 2001: cohort 1905 (n = 94). The paternal grandfather's
    food supply just before the prepubertal spurt was inversely associated
    with proband longevity. This defined the mid-childhood "slow growth
    period".
  - Kaati et al. 2002: three cohorts. Father's poor and mother's good
    mid-childhood food supply went with less cardiovascular death;
    paternal grandfather's good supply with more diabetes death. The
    sensitive period was confirmed in two of the three. Each diabetic
    proband had a different paternal grandfather, a point made in the
    2006 exchange with Senn ("Epigenetics or ephemeral genetics?") cited
    in Table 1.
  - Pembrey et al. 2006 (the original): cohorts 1895 (n = 107), 1905
    (n = 99) and 1920 (n = 111). Grandsons' mortality was linked only to
    the paternal grandfather's mid-childhood food supply, granddaughters'
    only to the paternal grandmother's. Rate ratios against moderate
    supply: grandsons 1.67 (good) and 0.65 (poor); granddaughters 2.13 and
    0.72. Sex stratification was suggested by early ALSPAC results. The
    mid-childhood period, not (pre)puberty, was confirmed.
  - Kaati et al. 2007: the sex-specific pattern persisted with the
    grandchild's early-life circumstances taken into account, and a
    father-to-son effect on longevity appeared.
  - Bygren et al. 2014: a sharp change in the paternal grandmother's food
    supply between 0 and 13 years went with cardiovascular death in
    granddaughters.
  Biological samples exist for none of the cohorts except a 1935 one
  (What is needed for future human studies?).
- **Other famines** (Food supply). A German discussion paper on the
  1916–1918 famine (van den Berg and Pinger 2014): third-generation males
  had better mental health scores if the paternal grandfather was exposed
  (boys at 9–12, girls at 8–10). Dutch Hunger Winter studies of the
  grandchildren of women exposed in utero gave mixed birth-weight results
  and an association with childhood adiposity.
- **ALSPAC smoking** (Smoking, paternal line). Designed to test the
  Överkalix hypothesis. The earlier the father began smoking, the greater
  his sons' BMI at 9, not his daughters'; strongest for a start before 11.
  To 17 years, those sons' excess BMI, waist and fat mass grew with age,
  significant from 13 (about 5–10 kg more fat mass). Daughters showed less
  lean mass. Maternal-line results: sons of non-smoking mothers exposed
  in utero were 61 g heavier at birth; two other cohorts found 244 g and
  45 g. Asthma findings conflict and need replication.
- **Betel nut** (Other paternal line exposures). Paternal betel-quid
  chewing, usually begun about 18, went with early metabolic syndrome in
  offspring who never chewed (Taiwan), following a mouse result.
- **Their answer to the critics** (Conclusions from human observations).
  - Multiple testing: "clear prior hypotheses (see table 1) and coherence
    between different studies/populations".
  - "Research conclusions that extend the public health paradigm need a
    higher level of evidence than normal, and further replication is
    needed."
  - The associations are "most unlikely to be due to 'cultural'
    inheritance/social patterning or genetic inheritance in isolation".
- **Sex differences** (Lessons from animal experiments). Rodent studies
  report sex-specific effects in either or both sexes, so the human sex
  differences are "not unexpected". The Överkalix pattern fits X and Y
  transmission, but some mouse sex-specific effects do not.
- **Routes other than the germline epigenome** (Figure 1; Transmission
  between generations). Maternal routes: egg cytoplasm, the womb, breast
  milk, microbiota, and a "metabolic cascade" through the next uterus.
  Paternal routes need not pass through meiosis: Sertoli–spermatid
  junctions and seminal fluid. Effects beyond the second generation need
  germline involvement.
- **Mechanisms** (A role for epigenetics; ncRNAs). Marks are erased twice
  in mammals; imprinted genes escape one wave, and a few repeat-linked
  loci escape the other. Dias and Ressler's odour study and a mouse
  prediabetes model are cited as plausible gametic mechanisms. Injected
  sperm RNA from stressed males reproduced the offspring phenotype, yet F3
  mice inherited it from F2 fathers with normal sperm miRNA (Gapp et al.
  2014). Genotype moderates transmission (MLH1 epimutations are
  genetically driven in some families).

## Concepts

- **transgenerational (broad sense)**: an outcome in offspring of a
  parent's pre-conception exposure or an ancestor's exposure, by any
  primarily non-genetic route.
- **slow growth period**: the mid-childhood years before the prepubertal
  growth spurt; the proposed exposure-sensitive window.
- **cultural transmission**: parental nurturing and "social patterning of
  all kinds"; the authors grant it "does play an important role".
- **metabolic cascade / propagational programming**: an exposure alters
  one generation's physiology, which programs the next, with no mark
  passed in a gamete.
- **transgenerational epidemiology**: life-course epidemiology extended to
  exposures in earlier generations.

## Bearing on the record

- **The question (QUESTION-tmp8bttd), germline route.** Three things.
  - *Överkalix is not a trauma case.* The exposure was year-to-year food
    supply in a grandparent's childhood. Higher grandchild mortality went
    with good supply (1.67), lower with poor (0.65). Bird's "the Swedish
    famine" misdescribes it. The question cites it as germline evidence
    for trauma; it is evidence, if at all, for transmission of a
    nutritional state.
  - *Male line is not germline.* The authors keep an open mind on
    mechanism and name paternal routes that bypass the gamete's
    epigenome: seminal fluid, Sertoli-cell contacts, a metabolic cascade.
    The question should cite Överkalix as a male-line case, not a germline
    one.
  - *What it says on culture and replication.* The authors grant that
    cultural transmission plays "an important role" and that replication
    is needed. Their case against culture is the pattern: a window in the
    grandparent's mid-childhood and sex-specific lines. They measured no
    family culture (diet, child-rearing, stories); they adjusted only for
    the grandchild's early-life circumstances. So Bird's charge stands as a charge of omission, and
    the authors' reply stands as an argument from the pattern. Neither
    side has data on family culture.
- **An inference on the cultural route.** A family habit or story would
  have to explain why a grandfather's food supply at the age before his
  growth spurt, and not later, predicts his grandson's death, and only
  down the male line. That is hard for habit and harder for identity:
  nothing in the pattern looks like remembering. If the pattern holds, it
  is the strongest human sign that something other than culture passes.
  It holds on about 100 people per cohort.
- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** Not touched. What would pass
  here is physiology, not identification with an ancestor. No note on the
  claim is proposed.

## Limitations

- **Not the 2006 paper.** Its methods, food-supply classification,
  confidence intervals, model, ALSPAC sample and discussion are not here;
  only its headline result, its cohort sizes and its rate ratios.
- **Partisan.** The authors are summarising their own studies. The
  network that wrote it met four times at the Karolinska Institute.
- **Small cohorts.** About 100 probands each; for diabetes deaths, each
  proband had a different paternal grandfather (Table 1 comment).
- **Priors partly post hoc.** Table 1's "prior research questions" grow
  from earlier Överkalix results, so the coherence argument is partly
  internal to one dataset.
- **Inconsistencies.**
  - Table 1 gives the 1905 cohort as n = 94 in the first study and
    n = 99 later.
  - Table 1 says the 2006 paper's sex stratification was "suggested by
    early ALSPAC results" citing Northstone et al. 2014, a paper eight
    years later.
  - Table 2 gives ABIS as 17 045 participants and 17 055 children.
- **Typos.** "Cochester" for Colchester (correspondence); "Aarhas" for
  Aarhus (Table 2); "only one allele is expression"; "needs to
  considered"; "aimed at recruit" (Table 2); "measureable"; "Non-genetic
  biological transmission ... spell out"; "0—13ys" (Table 1); "fo" for
  "of" in reference 32's title; reference 17's pages as "257–1"; the
  ALSPAC Study Team as "Team AS" in reference 29.

## Open questions

- Did Vågerö et al. 2018 replicate the Överkalix male-line effect? It
  came after this paper; Bird reports its failures, its title reports a
  success.
- Can a cultural route explain an effect keyed to the grandparent's age
  at exposure? A test would measure family diet and child-rearing across
  the three generations in a cohort with the same exposure data.
- Does any human trauma study meet the strict F2 rule, as Överkalix
  does for food supply?
