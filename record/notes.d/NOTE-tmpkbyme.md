# NOTE-tmpkbyme: Herd and Moynihan — Administrative Burden, the introduction

## Contribution

From the introduction alone: a framework for reading people's
encounters with the state as costs that policy imposes and could remove.
The book develops it in ten chapters on voting, abortion, the ACA,
Medicare, SNAP, Medicaid, the EITC and Social Security, ending in a
proposal for an "evidence-based approach"; none of those was read.

## Key insight

A right or benefit on paper is not one in practice: the costs of finding
out about it, proving eligibility and bearing the stigma of asking decide
who actually gets it, and those costs are set by choices that can be
changed without touching eligibility. "Administrative burdens are the
fine print of the social contract between citizens and their
government" (p. 2).

## Assumptions

The authors state four normative assumptions (pp. 13–14): programs
should be simple, accessible and respectful; burdens should be minimized
consistent with values such as cost and program integrity; judgments of
burden should be evidence based; and costs on those with limited
resources deserve special attention. They grant that some burdens are
legitimate, and sort cases into three: burdens that deter fraud at no
cost to participation, burdens that buy some value at the cost of access,
and burdens with no tangible relation to their stated goal, where
"limiting citizen access ... is the tacit goal" (pp. 12–13).

## Key results

As the introduction reports them, from the literature, not shown there:

- **Three costs** (p. 2): learning costs, compliance costs, psychological
  costs (stress, loss of autonomy, stigma). Burdens can be reduced
  (accepting a person's word) or shifted to the state (eligibility workers
  checking administrative databases).
- **Take-up** (pp. 6–7): near 100 percent for Social Security and
  Medicare; 40–60 percent for SSI, about 65 for SNAP, 30–60 for
  Unemployment Insurance, 50–70 for Medicaid, about 80 for the EITC; AFDC
  77–86 and its successor TANF 42–52.
- **Constructed** (pp. 8–11): the State Department's use of documentation
  requirements to keep German Jewish refugees out in the 1930s, with
  quotas two-thirds unfilled; return-free tax filing technically feasible
  for an estimated 40 percent of taxpayers and resisted by Intuit's
  lobbying.
- **Capacity as well as will** (pp. 11–12): states opposed to the ACA used
  procedure to deter enrollment, but low capacity also created burdens
  in governments that supported it. "Opportunities to shift
  administrative burdens away from individuals depends on a mixture of
  political will and administrative capacity", sometimes supplied by
  nonprofit or private actors.

## Concepts

- **Administrative burden** (p. 2): the learning, compliance and
  psychological costs a person meets when the state regulates private
  behaviour or structures access to public services.
- **Burden shifting** (p. 2, p. 10): moving a cost from the person to the
  state, for example by using data the state already holds.

## Connections

The introduction cites the take-up literature and the field experiments
on FAFSA help and college information packets (p. 7) without discussing
them. Its pairing of political will with administrative capacity is the
pair Hillebrecht's review ([LIT-1607](../literature.d/LIT-1607.md)) is said to use for compliance with
court rulings; that work was not reachable.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-172](../cases.d/CASE-172.md) and [TERM-055](../terms.d/TERM-055.md).** The exchange's use of the book holds on the
  introduction: it says burdens can restrict access to a benefit or a
  right with no change to formal eligibility, so that what is lost is
  the practical ability to use it. That is [TERM-055](../terms.d/TERM-055.md)'s operational
  availability carried from a resource to a right: present, known, and
  not deployable before the deadline. Whether the chapters give a measure
  of that loss (take-up is the introduction's only one) is not known
  from this reading.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- Only the 14 pages of the introduction read, of a book whose index ends after p. 337; no chapter's argument or evidence was
  checked.
- **An inconsistent figure** (p. 3): "A recent analysis estimated the
  compliance costs of filing to be about $1.2 billion, or 1.2 percent of
  GDP." The two cannot both hold: 1.2 percent of US GDP around 2017
  (about $19.5 trillion) is roughly $230 billion. One of the numbers is
  wrong; the source in note 5 was not checked.

## Open questions

- Do the chapters measure burden other than by take-up, and does any
  separate the effect of a burden from that of eligibility?
