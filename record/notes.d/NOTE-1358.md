# NOTE-1358: Lynch et al. — RefraMED trial of RO DBT for refractory depression

## Contribution

It is the first multicentre randomised trial of radically open dialectical
behaviour therapy, and the main controlled evidence the record holds for
it. RO DBT, six months of weekly individual sessions and skills classes
aimed at overcontrolled personality, added to usual care, beat usual care
alone on depression at the end of therapy and did not at the pre-registered
primary end-point five months later. The trial also reports gains on the
two process measures that RO DBT targets, and an imbalance in serious
adverse reactions that the authors put down to unequal reporting.

## Key insight

The treatment works fast and the control catches up. Both arms improve
throughout the 18 months; RO DBT's improvement comes during therapy and
then levels off, while usual care improves slowly at first and then faster.
So the difference between arms is large at 7 months and shrinks after.
What does not shrink is the gap on the measures of the targeted process:
psychological inflexibility and emotional approach coping move apart
between arms after therapy ends and stay apart. The trial can be read as
showing that RO DBT changes what it says it changes, and that this change
does not by itself hold the advantage on depression.

## Assumptions

- **Population.** Adults with a current major depressive episode by
  SCID-I, an HRSD score of at least 15, and refractory depression: chronic
  (at least two years) or treatment-resistant (two or more previous
  episodes and no response to an adequate antidepressant dose for six
  weeks in the current one). Excluded: bipolar disorder, psychosis,
  "dramatic-erratic" (cluster B) personality disorders, primary substance
  dependence, current or awaited standard DBT. The exclusions are the
  undercontrolled presentations; overcontrol was **not measured or
  required** for entry. The trial is a trial of RO DBT in refractory
  depression, not in overcontrol.
- **Setting.** Three NHS secondary-care centres already running standard
  DBT (Dorset, Hampshire, North Wales); recruitment March 2012 to April
  2015; 97% of responders White.
- **Treatment as usual** was whatever the NHS or private care offered
  except standard DBT. RO DBT participants were "strongly discouraged"
  from other psychotherapy during treatment; controls were not.
- **Analysis.** Three-level linear mixed models (time within patient
  within therapist), unbiased if data are missing at random; no multiple
  imputation. Bayesian refits with brms were done post hoc.

## Key results

From Table 1 (adjusted means; difference RO DBT − TAU, so negative favours
RO DBT on HRSD, AAQ-II, MSSI and PHQ-9):

- **HRSD, primary.** 7 months: 15.34 v. 20.73, −5.40 (95% CI −9.84 to
  −0.94), P = 0.023, effect size 1.03. 12 months (primary end-point):
  14.19 v. 16.34, −2.15, P = 0.290, effect size 0.41. 18 months: 13.79 v.
  15.48, −1.69 (−6.22 to 2.84), P = 0.424, effect size 0.32. The 12-month
  difference exceeds the two points the trial was powered to detect.
- **AAQ-II (inflexibility).** −3.37 (P = 0.096) at 7 months, −4.94
  (P = 0.040) at 12, −5.48 (P = 0.014) at 18.
- **Emotional approach coping.** +1.50 (P = 0.132) at 7 months, +3.55
  (P = 0.017) at 12, +2.98 (P = 0.012) at 18.
- **PHQ-9.** −3.45 (P = 0.041), −2.92 (P = 0.058), −3.23 (P = 0.030).
- **Suicidal ideation (MSSI) and social support (SSQ-3).** No significant
  difference at any point.
- **Remission.** Full remission was rare in both arms (RO DBT 1%, 8%, 7%;
  TAU 0%, 0%, 1% at 7, 12, 18 months). By a 17.5% fall in HRSD, 59%, 69%,
  59% against 27%, 48%, 41%. Simulated for 100 new patients at 12 months:
  32 improve by choosing RO DBT, 10 deteriorate, 58 unchanged.
- **Power.** Recruitment reached 250 of 276 (91%) and analysis 190 (76%)
  against 229 planned; the intratherapist correlation was 0.14 against the
  0.025 assumed. The best and worst therapists differed by 2.6 HRSD
  points, more than the 12-month treatment effect.
- **Post hoc Bayes.** Posterior odds that RO DBT is better: 46 at 7
  months, 5.5 at 12, 4.7 at 18.
- **Harms.** 28 serious adverse events in 24 RO DBT participants against 4
  in controls. Eight, all in the RO DBT arm, were judged possibly (5) or
  probably (3) related, among them two overdoses and a prevented suicide
  attempt. No suicides. Therapists saw RO DBT participants twice a week
  and reported 23 of their 28 events; controls were seen only at
  assessments.

## Method

A pragmatic-explanatory hybrid trial, 3 : 2 allocation, stratified dynamic
randomisation (onset age, HRSD above or below 25, personality disorder)
by the Swansea Trials Unit, with participants also randomised among
available therapists. Masked assessors; when a participant revealed the
arm during an assessment the unmasked rating was kept (17 times at 7
months, 12 at 12). Twenty-three standard DBT therapists were trained for
ten days by the treatment's developer, who supervised them and did not
treat. Fidelity was rated on the standard DBT adherence scale, there being
no RO DBT one: 221 of 273 rated tapes (81%) adherent. RO DBT participants
attended on average 22.9 of 29 individual sessions and 19.9 of 27 classes.

## Concepts

- **Overcontrol** — "excessive inhibitory control": cognitive and
  behavioural rigidity, a strong desire to control one's environment,
  restrained emotional expression, limited social interaction, and
  aloofness, mistrust and fear of criticism in close relationships. The
  paper places it in cluster A and C personality disorders.
- **RO DBT** — a "transdiagnostic" therapy that, unlike other therapies,
  encourages "social bonding through emotional expression"; the paper's
  strengths section calls it "the first treatment known to us to target
  deficits in social signalling as the main problem underlying
  overcontrolled emotional loneliness".
- **Refractory depression** — chronic or treatment-resistant, as above.

## Connections

The treatment is Lynch's, from his 2018 manual (reference 9, not
registered), with standard DBT skills lessons from Linehan's 1993 skills
manual (reference 17, not registered); the record's DOI'd statements of the
two are [LIT-1205](../literature.d/LIT-1205.md) and [LIT-1221](../literature.d/LIT-1221.md). Two earlier pilots by Lynch with older
depressed adults (2003, 2007) used an earlier form of the treatment and
gave the effect sizes (0.85, 0.71) the power calculation assumed. The
systematic review that places this trial among the RO DBT evidence is
[LIT-1224](../literature.d/LIT-1224.md). Its measure of inflexibility, the AAQ-II, is the acceptance and
commitment therapy measure, so the trial's process gain is stated in ACT's
terms.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-349](../claims.d/CLAIM-349.md).** The claim cites A42 for RO DBT's evidence being "promising
  but not definitive" and RefraMED's longer-term findings being "less
  conclusive". The reading confirms both. Two things in it bear on the
  claim more specifically. First, the trial selected on refractory
  depression with the undercontrolled presentations excluded; it did not
  measure overcontrol, so it is no evidence that overcontrol is common in
  refractory depression or that the treatment's effect runs through it.
  Second, the change that persisted was on flexibility and emotional
  expression, the two properties [CLAIM-349](../claims.d/CLAIM-349.md) makes the mark of adaptive
  regulation, while the change on depression did not. That is consistent
  with the claim's own caution, in "What it does not say", that
  flexibility is not the whole of the outcome, and it does not test the
  claim, which is about organizations. The LIT's "Why this is here" reads
  the trial as "a caution on reading overcontrol as the whole of the
  problem"; that is the record's reading, not the paper's, which
  concludes the opposite way, that RO DBT's targets are the right ones.
- **[QUESTION-045](../questions.d/QUESTION-045.md)** asks whether protective lock-in is the same mechanism
  at the individual scale. The trial gives nothing on mechanism: the
  mediators were collected and no mediation is reported.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **Underpowered at the end-point it chose**, by the authors' account:
  short recruitment, more loss to follow-up than planned, and therapist
  heterogeneity nearly six times the assumed value.
- **Unequal co-treatment.** RO DBT participants were discouraged from
  other psychotherapy; controls could seek any treatment except standard
  DBT. Usual care's late improvement may be partly that.
- **Masking.** Seventeen ratings at 7 months, the time point where the
  effect was found, were made by assessors who knew the arm (12 at 12
  months, none at 18). The paper does not say how they split between
  arms.
- **Developer and financial interest.** Six of sixteen authors have
  received royalties or fees for RO DBT; the developer trained and
  supervised the therapists and leads the paper; a co-author co-owns the
  training company.
- **Fidelity** was measured with the standard DBT scale, not one for RO
  DBT.
- **Process measures are self-report**, from participants who knew their
  arm, and the processes are the ones the therapy teaches.
- **Errors and slips found.**
  - "Throughout RO DBT participants reported significantly better
    psychological flexibility and emotional coping than controls" (the
    abstract; the Results and Discussion say the same). Table 1 has the
    7-month differences at P = 0.096 (AAQ-II) and P = 0.132 (EAC): the
    gain is significant at 12 and 18 months, not throughout.
  - Table 1 gives the 12-month HRSD interval as −6.69 to 2.28; the
    abstract and text give −2.28 to 6.59. The point estimate −2.15 sits at
    the centre of −6.59 to 2.29, so the table's 6.69 is a typo.
  - The one-tailed Fisher test for eight serious adverse reactions in 162
    participants against none in 88 is given as P = 0.004. With eight
    participants affected, P is C(162,8)/C(250,8) = 0.029; with fewer it
    is larger. 0.004 is not reproduced from the numbers given. It is still
    under 0.05.
  - References 5 and 8 are the same paper (Riso et al. 2003), and the
    second is the one cited for the core characteristics of overcontrol,
    which that paper, on cognitive aspects of chronic depression, is not
    obviously the source of.
  - The title's "mechanisms" are not reported: no mediation analysis is
    in the paper.

## Open questions

- Does the effect on depression run through the change in flexibility and
  emotional coping? The data (Zenodo record 1442883) would allow a
  mediation analysis the paper does not give.
- Does RO DBT do better in participants selected for overcontrol than in
  refractory depression at large? The trial did not measure it.
- With equal permission to seek other therapy and an RO DBT fidelity
  scale, does the 12-month difference hold at the two points the trial
  sought?

## Note of 2026-10-29: the monograph, read

The NIHR monograph, reference 13, which this note's history lists as not
read, is now read: [LIT-1838](../literature.d/LIT-1838.md), [NOTE-1421](NOTE-1421.md). It bears on three findings
here.

- **"Throughout."** The monograph's Table 8 supports "throughout" (p =
  0.010 and 0.036 at 7 months), with the same differences and narrower
  intervals. So the abstract's "throughout" follows the monograph's
  analysis of the same data, while this paper's Table 1 is the more
  conservative analysis. The mismatch within this paper stands, and the
  abstract should have matched its own table.
- **P = 0.004.** The monograph gives "p < 0.01". Neither reproduces the
  0.029 computed here, and neither says how many participants had the
  eight reactions.
- **The first open question,** whether the effect on depression runs
  through flexibility and emotional coping, is not answered. The
  monograph's mediators are sessions attended, alliance and DBT skills,
  not AAQ-II or EAC, and its instrumented estimates failed on weak
  instruments. The cross-lagged analyses it planned are not reported.
