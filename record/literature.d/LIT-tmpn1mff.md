summary: >-
  A review of the common in-group identity model applied to helping. Recategorizing two groups as one removes in-group bias in helping: in a laboratory study, help to an original out-group member rose to the in-group level; at a football stadium, White fans complied with a Black interviewer's request more often when he wore their university's hat (59% vs 36%). Commons-dilemma studies show the same for cooperation. But the form of the shared identity matters. White students helped a Black student least when he called himself a Black person or a Black Colgate student (a dual identity), and most when he called himself a Colgate student. In the second study, empathic concern partly mediated that effect on helping; attitudes did not. The authors trace the dual identity's cost to majority members projecting their group's standards onto the superordinate category, and to identity threat.
