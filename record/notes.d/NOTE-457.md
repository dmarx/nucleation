# NOTE-457: Internal Family Systems Therapy

## Contribution

Chapter 1 gives the origin story and the conceptual core of IFS, in
Schwartz's voice. It is a clinical narrative, not an argument or a study. It
shows how IFS moved structural family therapy's concepts (homeostasis,
triangulation, boundaries, hierarchy, protective symptoms) inside the
person. It also records the observation the model rests on: once parts
separate, clients show a stable, compassionate state that Schwartz calls the
Self.

## Key insight

Symptoms that look self-destructive, such as cutting, bingeing and harsh
self-criticism, are treated in IFS as the work of parts trying to protect the
person, in roles they were forced into. They behave like children in a
troubled family. Fighting them strengthens them. Curiosity about their
purpose lets them change. The person who remains when the parts step back is
already capable of leading. Treatment is a matter of letting that Self lead,
not of building a healthy ego.

## Assumptions

- **Multiplicity is normal.** "The chattering mind denotes a nonunitary,
  relational mind. Throughout the day all of us pass from one personality to
  the next." The alters of dissociative identity disorder are parts in a
  system made "more polarized and disconnected" by abuse.
- **Systems thinking applies within the person.** A system resists change
  that ignores its context (homeostasis). Protectors "have a right to vet
  the therapist". The inner and outer families "parallel" each other.
- **Clients' reports of parts are data.** Schwartz says he had "the big
  advantage of total ignorance" and trusted "what my clients were telling
  me". The chapter's evidence is clinical observation of reports.
- **The Self is innate.** It "clearly did not need to develop over time"; it
  "was always right there if our parts let it in". This is inferred from
  clients with severe childhood trauma who showed "unalloyed ego strength"
  on access to the Self.

## Key results

The chapter's case sequence:

- **Quinn.** Bulimia persisted after successful structural family therapy.
  Asked about her inner experience, she described "a confusing cacophony of
  ... 'parts'": a critic, a defender, a sad and hopeless part, and a part
  that "took over" to binge. Other clients told "remarkably similar
  stories".
- **Roxanne.** Coercing a cutting part ("I badgered it for 2 hours") was
  followed by a worse injury. Giving up and asking with curiosity revealed
  its "two-pronged job": to take her out of her body during abuse, and to
  control rage that would have endangered her. It was "frozen in the past",
  and it wanted the opposite role.
- **Cora.** Asking an angry part to "step back" while Cora spoke to her
  "pessimist" produced "a calm, caring voice". Repeated with other clients,
  the "step back" procedure yielded a state they called "who I really am,
  that's my self".
- **Backlash.** Going to vulnerable parts too fast was followed by accidents,
  fevers and migraines. Protective parts later "explained" that their
  defences had been disrupted. Hence the rule to get protectors' permission
  first.
- **Eight essentials** (pp. 21–23), listed in the summary above.

## Concepts

- **Parts**: "inner personalities", each with temperament, desires and a
  way of communicating, organised in alliances and polarities.
- **Exiles / protectors**: vulnerable parts "locked away", and parts that
  manage life or distract from pain. The chapter names "managing" and
  "distracting" roles; the terms "managers" and "firefighters" are from the
  IFS Institute pages ([LIT-579](../literature.d/LIT-579.md)).
- **Burdens**: "extreme emotions and beliefs" acquired from trauma, carried
  by a part but not part of its nature.
- **Self / Self-led**: the non-part state of calm and compassion; being
  Self-led means that parts "feel loved by the Self and trust the Self's
  leadership".
- **Blending / unblending**: a part taking over the Self, and separating
  from it.
- **Direct access**: the therapist speaking to a part directly, not through
  the client's Self.

## Connections

The chapter names its sources in family therapy: Bateson and von
Bertalanffy, Minuchin, Haley, Madanes, Satir ("I was standing on her
shoulders") and Bowen. It also names Perls's empty-chair technique as
Schwartz's "first exposure to the idea of inner conversations", and grants
that psychodynamic therapy was "absolutely right" on the past, the
unconscious, emotion and the body, and the therapeutic relationship. The
IFS Institute's "Evolution" page ([LIT-579](../literature.d/LIT-579.md)) tells the same founding case
(there "Diane") and adds the "eight Cs" of the Self.

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-051](../theory.d/THEORY-051.md)** (strengthening the self as context reduces suffering in
  ACT). IFS supplies a rival account of a nearby process. Both use a
  perspective from which thoughts and feelings are held without being
  fused with. ACT's is a learned, deictic, contentless perspective. IFS's
  Self is innate and has positive qualities. A THEORY comparing them would
  need IFS's research (chapter 19, unread) and process studies for both.
  The record holds none for IFS.
- **[THEORY-050](../theory.d/THEORY-050.md)** (psychological inflexibility as a transdiagnostic process).
  IFS's protectors and exiles amount to a transdiagnostic functional
  account: symptoms are protective strategies against painful material.
  Firefighters (bingeing, drugs, self-harm) are close to experiential
  avoidance. The vocabularies differ, and nothing here tests either.
- **[THEORY-053](../theory.d/THEORY-053.md)** (efficacy is not evidence for a theory of change). The
  chapter is a case in point. Its evidence for the Self is that clients get
  better when it is accessed, and clients report it. On [THEORY-053](../theory.d/THEORY-053.md)'s terms
  that would be evidence for the Self only if a measure of Self-access
  changed first and specifically.
- **[THEORY-044](../theory.d/THEORY-044.md).** IFS's "Self" is a fourth sense of the word beside RFT's
  three selves. It is closest to the "transcendent self" (self as context),
  but it is described with content: qualities, intentions, leadership.

It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- One chapter of twenty. The theory of the Self (ch. 3), burdens (ch. 4)
  and research (ch. 19) were not read.
- The chapter is autobiographical and clinical. Its claims about parts and
  the Self rest on clients' reports, interpreted by the developer of the
  model.
- The SAMHSA rating is reported without detail.

## Open questions

- What evidence does chapter 19 give, and does any of it test the Self as a
  mechanism and not only IFS as a package?
- Is "the Self" one construct across clients, or a family of states (calm,
  compassion, curiosity) that a measure could separate?
