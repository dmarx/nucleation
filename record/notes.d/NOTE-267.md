# NOTE-267: Personal Autonomy

## Contribution

The entry frames personal autonomy as a puzzle in the metaphysics of agency. Every agent necessarily treats her own practical judgments as authoritative, because acting requires it. Yet her authority can be "a mere formality" (§1). An account of autonomy is therefore an account of which motives are "external" to an agent who is both governor and governed. The entry surveys four families of answer (§2). It argues that none of them captures *minimal* self-government, the threshold for being accountable (§3). It then sets agent-causal incompatibilism against the neo-Kantian "practical point of view" (§4), and concludes that the self-relation "is extremely difficult to pin down" (§5). It is an argument, not a neutral survey: its thesis is that ideal self-government and threshold self-government come apart, and that the threshold keeps collapsing into the conditions of agency itself.

## Key insight

Theories of autonomy spell out *ideals* that agents realise by degrees: coherence, responsiveness to reasons, good reasoning, or freedom from determination. None of these is the *minimal* condition under which a motive's power is the agent's own. Coherence fails in both directions. A manipulated endorsement is coherent but not autonomous. A weak-willed act, or one against one's plans or values, is incoherent and still self-governed. Any attempt to locate identification in some attitude invites the question of what makes *that* attitude the agent's, which is the regress.

## Assumptions

The premises and authorities the entry works from:

- **Agency entails self-authority.** In forming an intention one must treat one's own judgment as authoritative, so the authority is "inalienable" (§1). This is asserted, and the whole entry is built on it.
- **Autonomy and accountability share a threshold.** The minimal conditions of self-governing action are the conditions an agent must satisfy to be accountable for the motivating force of her states (n. 16). The entry assumes "that only autonomous actions can be attributed to those who perform them" in a special sense (n. 22). It sets aside the Strawsonian view that responsibility has no metaphysical condition (nn. 16–17).
- **Paradigm threats are fixed points.** Brainwashing and addiction are "the favorite examples" (§1). The unwilling addict who is not even minimally self-governing is taken as data (§3).
- **Weakness of will is genuine** (§3). The argument that coherence is not necessary is conditional on this ("if weakness of will is a genuine phenomenon").

## Key results

What the entry argues, section by section.

- **§1 The problem.** An agent defers to herself whenever she acts. So how can a motive's power fail to be "a manifestation of her power to act"? There is no second, independently identifiable standpoint inside one psyche, unlike the political case of an army dictating to a president.
- **§2 Four families.**
  - **Coherentist.** An agent governs her action iff her motive coheres with a mental state representing her point of view. That state is variously identified with:
    - "her highest-order desires regarding which of her first-order desires moves her to act" (Frankfurt 1988c, i.e. the 1971 paper);
    - her evaluative judgments about which actions are most worth performing (Watson 1975; n. 2 says Watson "explicitly allies himself with Plato", whereas hierarchical accounts are Humean);
    - harmony with long-term plans (Bratman 1979, 2007);
    - "caring" (Frankfurt 1988f, 1999d; Jaworska; Shoemaker 2003);
    - character (R. Dworkin);
    - integration (Arpaly & Schroeder).

    These accounts also tie synchronic unity to diachronic unity. **Strict coherentism is "doubly internalist"**: an addict who has no objection to her addiction is autonomous, and neither the origin nor the content of the higher-order attitudes matters. Strict coherentists hold that one can act autonomously from desires "so integral to one's identity that one could not possibly will to resist them" (n. 8, Frankfurt's "volitional necessity").
  - **Reasons-responsive** (Fischer & Ravizza, Nelkin, Wolf): one does not govern oneself unless one's motives are responsive to a sufficiently wide range of reasons (n. 4 gives Wolf's "the True and the Good" and Fischer & Ravizza's "regular reasons-receptivity").
  - **Responsiveness-to-reasoning** (Christman 1991, 1993; Mele 1993, 1995): self-government is the capacity to evaluate one's motives in light of one's other attitudes and to adjust them. This family carries a weak externalism, because reasoning is norm-governed. It also invites historical conditions against indoctrination. Relational accounts (Benson; Mackenzie & Stoljar; Anderson & Honneth; n. 10: Oshana; Anderson 2003) add that others can block the self-respect or self-trust a point of view of one's own needs. Such damage can also come without anyone else's action.
  - **Incompatibilist** (Pereboom; Kane 1996; van Inwagen 1983): if our motives are determined by causes independent of us, we do not govern them. Velleman's theoretical-reasoning view of intention is noted as a variant (n. 12).
- **§3 Against each as a minimal condition.**
  - **(a) Manipulation.** A brainwashed agent can have coherent endorsements that are even highly reasons-responsive. Historical supplements have trouble distinguishing autonomy-conferring reasoning from autonomy-undermining reasoning "without implicitly appealing to the very phenomenon one is trying to explain".
  - **(b) Coherence is not necessary.** The weak-willed agent "identifies with" her motives in whatever way accountability requires. Frankfurt's adoption example (n. 14) shows that someone can defy her own contemporaneous normative verdict without defying anything deep about herself. Human reflection can distance itself "from every aspect of their own psyches—even their rational reflections". So identification "cannot be cashed out in terms of higher-order attitudes of approval and disapproval". The same holds for plans.
  - **(c) The regress.** If identification is something the agent secures, then we must ask under what conditions she identifies with the motives that move her to identify. To stop the regress there must be an attitude from which "no agent can possibly be alienated". No highest-order desire, evaluative judgment, plan or combination qualifies. The only candidate is "the desire to be a self-governing agent" (n. 15: Korsgaard for, Velleman's reservations). It fails as a criterion because it would be causally at work even when the agent fails to govern herself.
  - **(d) Reasons-responsiveness is not necessary.** Othello authors his act even if jealousy made him unable to track the reasons. Denying this requires incompatibilism.
  - **(e) Threshold collapse.** Coherentist and reasoning accounts make the unwilling addict a "passive bystander". That assimilates her to the Tourette's case, where the behaviour is not action at all. So they fail to explain intentional action that is not accountable. Buss 2012 (n. 18) offers a fifth, normative account, keyed to "identity as a representative human being".
- **§4 Agents as causes.** Agent causation (Chisholm, Clarke, O'Connor, R. Taylor) is obscure. The neo-Kantian reply (Korsgaard, Bok; n. 21: McKay, Hampshire, Velleman's "epistemic freedom", Sartre) is that from the practical point of view no fact can settle what we have reason to do, so our decisions are "up to us" even under determinism.
- **§5.** The self-relation remains hard to pin down. Note 22 lists the attributability / answerability / accountability distinctions since Watson 1996 and Shoemaker 2012.

## Concepts

- **Coherentist account.** The agent governs her action iff her motive "coheres with (is in harmony with) some mental state that represents her point of view on the action" (§2). **This is broader than usual.** It includes Frankfurt's hierarchy, Watson's evaluative judgments, Bratman's plans, caring and character. The IEP entry uses "coherentist" for Ekstrom and Bratman *as opposed to* hierarchical accounts.
- **Strict coherentism / doubly internalist.** Neither the causal history nor the reality-relation of the autonomy-conferring attitudes matters (§2).
- **Reasons-responsive vs. responsiveness-to-reasoning.** Responsiveness to the reasons there are, which is externalist, versus the capacity to evaluate and revise motives by one's own reasoning, which is weakly externalist (§2).
- **Identification.** "Perhaps the most important metaphor in the literature" (§3). It is the relation in which a motive's power counts as the agent's own. The entry argues that it cannot be reduced to higher-order approval.
- **Volitional necessity.** Frankfurt's term (n. 8) for being unable to will otherwise as a mark of where one stands.
- **Satisfaction.** Per n. 1, Frankfurt's later criterion: no "restlessness or resistance" toward one's motives.
- **Minimal vs. ideal self-government.** This is the entry's organising distinction (§3).

## Limitations

- Frankfurt's 1971 scheme is compressed into one clause, and his three decades of revision into one footnote. A reader cannot recover second-order volitions, wantons, persons, freedom of will vs. action, decisive identification or wholeheartedness from this entry.
- Watson's actual objection to Frankfurt is absent. Watson appears as a rival coherentist, not as a critic.
- The §3 arguments are informal and rest on contested intuitions (weakness of will; the adoption case; Othello). The historical-account circularity charge (C4) is one sentence.
- Bratman 1996 and 2004 and Frankfurt 1976, 1987 and 1994 are listed but not discussed. Two note-14 citations appear mis-keyed (see corrections).
- The 2018 revision's division of labour between Buss and Westlund is not stated. Westlund's own relational work (2003) is listed but not discussed. The relational material is thin: one paragraph in §2 and note 10.

## Open questions

- Is there an attitude from which no agent can be alienated? The entry's negative answer (C7) is what Frankfurt's "satisfaction" and Bratman's self-governing policies are meant to dispute. Neither reply is evaluated here.
- Can a historical condition on autonomy avoid the circularity worry (C4)? Christman's counterfactual non-repudiation test (n. 6) is the candidate. The entry does not assess it.
- Do minimal autonomy and accountability share a threshold (n. 16), or does the attributability / answerability / accountability split (n. 22) dissolve the §3 puzzle?
