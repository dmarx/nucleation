# THEORY-1461: Kline and Moretti find that the Tennessee Valley Authority's federal subsidies, peaking at about 10% of household income in 1950–55 and near zero after the TVA was made self-financing in 1959, raised both agricultural and manufacturing employment growth in its region by about 10–12 points per decade in 1940–1960, after which the agricultural gain fully reversed while manufacturing kept growing about 3 points per decade faster; but their estimate of a constant agglomeration elasticity, about 0.4–0.47 in labour demand and equal across density ranges, implies a unique steady state, so that the manufacturing gain is slow convergence rather than a big-push shift to a second equilibrium and will eventually die out

## Source

Kline & Moretti (2014), [LIT-2141](../literature.d/LIT-2141.md); read in [NOTE-1646](../notes.d/NOTE-1646.md) from NBER
w19293, revised June 2014. The typeset version of record and the Online
Appendix A1–A4 were not read. Page numbers are the working paper's.

## What was actually shown

- **Reduced form (Tables III–IV).** Against three control groups (rest of
  the US, rest of the South, counties of six proposed but unpassed
  authorities), reweighted on 38 covariates from 1920 and 1930, with a
  1900–1940 placebo that passes: in 1940–1960 "the 10-year growth rate of
  employment in both agriculture and manufacturing is 10.6 - 11.9
  percentage points larger in the TVA region" (p. 12); in 1960–2000
  agriculture −0.130 to −0.166 a decade, manufacturing +0.032 to +0.035.
- **The end of support.** "In 1959, Congress passed legislation making the
  TVA power generation system self-financing. From that year on, federal
  subsidies declined sharply" (p. 5), and "from 1959 onwards maintenance
  of the TVA capital stock was paid for by local taxpayers and local users
  of electricity" (p. 13).
- **Curvature (Tables V–VI).** In levels the instrumented agglomeration
  function is "a strikingly concave pattern"; in logs "we cannot reject a
  constant elasticity relationship in any of the IV specifications", the
  p-values "all above 0.66", estimates 0.40–0.47.
- **Verdict (pp. 24–25).** "the estimated shape of the curve implies the
  system admits a unique steady state equilibrium so that the TVA region
  will ultimately revert back to its pre-program equilibrium."
- **Echo, not switch (appendix §2).** Momentum with near-zero depreciation
  predicts 1960–2000 manufacturing growth of 0.123 against 0.132 in the
  reduced form.

What could have come out otherwise: the log spline could have shown a
higher elasticity at low density, the signature of a tipping point, or
manufacturing could have reverted with agriculture.

## What this does not say

- **That the region has reverted.** It is the model's forecast; the data
  end in 2000, with manufacturing still growing faster.
- **That there is no threshold anywhere.** The test is on all US counties'
  manufacturing, 1960–2000, under a 20-year-independence restriction, with
  standard errors of about 0.10–0.15; modest nonlinearity in logs is not
  ruled out, and the authors call nonparametric identification
  "inherently untestable without further assumptions" (footnote 25).
- **That agglomeration carried the persistence.** It is partly inferred by
  elimination; durable, locally maintained infrastructure carries part of
  it, and the depreciation assumptions (about 0.2% a year in the appendix
  fit, about 5% in footnote 15) are not reconciled.
- **Which part of the TVA mattered.** Electricity, roads, canal,
  fertilizer and schools are not separated.
- **That place-based support never pays.** The national benefit, from
  direct productivity, exceeds cost on "several unverifiable assumptions
  regarding the functioning of labor markets".
