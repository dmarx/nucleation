# NOTE-tmpsp0qw: Of Personal Identity

## Contribution

Two things that did not exist before. First, an argument from the theory of
ideas that there is no idea of a simple, persisting self. Second, a genetic
account of why we believe in one anyway: the belief is a fiction the
imagination produces from resemblance and causation among perceptions,
exactly as it does for the identity of changing objects. The Appendix adds a
third thing, rarer: an author's published statement that his own account of
what unites the mind fails, with the two principles that make it fail named.

## Key insight

The identity of a person is the same kind of thing as the identity of a
ship rebuilt plank by plank. In both, the mind passes so easily from one
related object to the next that it feels like surveying one unchanging
object, and it covers the difference by inventing something that stays the
same. Nothing in the perceptions themselves is the self; the unity is
supplied by how the imagination runs over them.

## Assumptions

- **Copy principle.** "It must be some one impression, that gives rise to
  every real idea" (1.4.6.2). Without an impression of self there is no
  idea of self.
- **Separability.** Perceptions "are different, and distinguishable, and
  separable from each other … and have no need of any thing to support
  their existence" (1.4.6.3); restated as App. 12.
- **No perceived real connexion.** "The understanding never observes any
  real connexion among objects", and causation "resolves itself into a
  customary association of ideas" (1.4.6.16). This is carried over from
  Book 1, Part 3.
- **Association.** Resemblance, contiguity and causation are "the uniting
  principles in the ideal world" (1.4.6.16). Contiguity is set aside for
  persons (1.4.6.17).
- **The question is restricted.** Personal identity "as it regards our
  thought or imagination" is the subject; identity "as it regards our
  passions or the concern we take in ourselves" is left to Book 2
  (1.4.6.5).

## Key results

- **No idea of self (1.4.6.1–3).**
  - Against "some philosophers" who say we are "intimately conscious" of a
    self, its identity and simplicity, no impression is "constant and
    invariable" through life. "Consequently there is no such idea."
  - "I never can catch myself at any time without a perception." In sound
    sleep "so long am I insensible of *myself*, and may truly be said not to
    exist".
  - Anyone who claims to perceive "something simple and continu'd" may be
    right "as well as I", but "we are essentially different in this
    particular".
- **The bundle (1.4.6.4).** "Nothing but a bundle or collection of different
  perceptions … in a perpetual flux and movement." The mind is "a kind of
  theatre", but "the comparison of the theatre must not mislead us. They are
  the successive perceptions only, that constitute the mind".
- **The mechanism of the fiction (1.4.6.6–7).** The ideas of identity (one
  invariable object) and of a succession of related objects are "perfectly
  distinct, and even contrary", but the acts of the imagination that
  consider them are "almost the same to the feeling". So we "feign" the
  continued existence of perceptions, and "a soul, and self, and substance,
  to disguise the variation". The dispute is not "merely a dispute of
  words", because the fiction comes with it.
- **The cases (1.4.6.8–14).**
  - A mass of matter: a small addition "absolutely destroys the identity of
    the whole, strictly speaking", yet we call it the same. Smallness is
    judged by proportion: a mountain does not change a planet (1.4.6.8–9).
  - Gradual change preserves identity better than sudden (1.4.6.10).
  - A ship "of which a considerable part has been chang'd by frequent
    reparations" is the same by the parts' "common end" (1.4.6.11).
  - Animals and plants add "a sympathy of parts", mutual cause and effect:
    an oak with "not one particle of matter, or figure of its parts the
    same" (1.4.6.12).
  - A church rebuilt in free-stone is "the same church" by its relation to
    the parish; we confuse numerical and specific identity, as with a
    recurring noise (1.4.6.13).
  - A river changes all its parts within a day and remains the same, because
    change is what is expected of it (1.4.6.14).
- **Application to persons (1.4.6.15–20).**
  - "The identity, which we ascribe to the mind of man, is only a fictitious
    one, and of a like kind with that which we ascribe to vegetables and
    animal bodies" (1.4.6.15).
  - Identity is "merely a quality, which we attribute" to perceptions
    "because of the union of their ideas in the imagination, when we reflect
    upon them" (1.4.6.16).
  - Memory makes past perceptions resemble present ones and so "not only
    discovers the identity, but also contributes to its production"
    (1.4.6.18).
  - Causation: the mind as "a system of different perceptions … link'd
    together by the relation of cause and effect". The soul is like "a
    republic or commonwealth", which may change its members, laws and
    constitutions and stay the same, as a person may change "his character
    and disposition" (1.4.6.19).
  - Against the memory theory: who remembers what he did "on the first of
    January 1715"? Causation lets us extend identity past memory, so memory
    "does not so much produce as discover personal identity" (1.4.6.20).
- **No answers in hard cases (1.4.6.21).** Relations "may diminish by
  insensible degrees", so there is "no just standard" for when they "acquire
  or lose a title to the name of identity". The questions "are to be
  regarded rather as grammatical than as philosophical difficulties".
- **Simplicity (1.4.6.22).** The same account, "with little or no
  variation", explains why we ascribe simplicity to the mind.
- **The Appendix (App. 10–21).**
  - App. 10: "upon a more strict review of the section concerning personal
    identity, I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must
    confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to
    render them consistent."
  - App. 11–19 restate the case against a simple self, with new arguments:
    the table and the chimney (App. 13); a mind "reduc'd even below the life
    of an oyster" to one perception gives no notion of self (App. 16);
    annihilation at death is the extinction of perceptions, so they "must be
    the same with self" (App. 17).
  - App. 20: "If perceptions are distinct existences, they form a whole only
    by being connected together. But no connexions among distinct existences
    are ever discoverable by human understanding." So "the thought alone
    finds personal identity". "Most philosophers seem inclin'd to think, that
    personal identity arises from consciousness"; but "all my hopes vanish,
    when I come to explain the principles, that unite our successive
    perceptions in our thought or consciousness".
  - App. 21: "there are two principles, which I cannot render consistent;
    nor is it in my power to renounce either of them, viz. that all our
    distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never
    perceives any real connexion among distinct existences." If perceptions
    inhered in something simple, or the mind perceived real connexions,
    "there wou'd be no difficulty". He pleads "the privilege of a sceptic",
    but does not "pronounce it absolutely insuperable".

## Concepts

- **impression / idea** — livelier and fainter perceptions; every real idea
  derives from an impression (1.4.6.2).
- **perception** — any content of the mind; each is a distinct existence.
- **bundle** — the mind as a collection of successive perceptions, with no
  owner among them (1.4.6.4).
- **fiction** — what the imagination supplies to make related succession
  pass for invariable identity: a soul, self or substance (1.4.6.6).
- **numerical / specific identity** — being one thing / being exactly alike;
  sometimes confounded (1.4.6.13).
- **imperfect identity** — the identity granted to a mass with trivial
  change, constituted by "the uninterrupted progress of the thought"
  (1.4.6.9).

## Connections

- **Locke's "Of Identity and Diversity"**, filed beside this reading. The
  section takes Locke's analogy between persons and plants and animals and
  turns it against him: it holds for both, and in both the identity is
  fictitious. "Of late years in England" (1.4.6.15) is Locke's debate. The
  argument from forgotten days (1.4.6.20) answers a memory theory that
  readers attribute to Locke. The Appendix's "most philosophers … personal
  identity arises from consciousness" is Locke's thesis, and Hume says it
  would be promising if he could explain the unity of consciousness.
- **Floridi** ([LIT-136](../literature.d/LIT-136.md), [NOTE-130](NOTE-130.md)) quotes the Appendix at length as the
  statement of the unity problem, and argues that Lockean and narrative
  accounts both inherit it.
- **Dennett** ([LIT-442](../literature.d/LIT-442.md), [NOTE-364](NOTE-364.md)) closes on Hume's failure to find a self by
  introspection, and treats the self as an abstraction posited by
  interpretation. [NOTE-364](NOTE-364.md) records that the essay does not take up
  persistence. Hume does both: the bundle answers what a self is, and the
  fiction answers why we think it persists.
- **Minsky** ([LIT-432](../literature.d/LIT-432.md), [NOTE-348](NOTE-348.md)). The Single-Self as a useful fiction over a
  society of resources is Hume's fiction with a mechanism named.
- **Clark and Chalmers** ([LIT-097](../literature.d/LIT-097.md), [NOTE-180](NOTE-180.md), its C14). They argue that a
  self of occurrent states only would be "a mere bundle", threatening "its
  deep psychological continuity". Hume's bundle is exactly that, and he
  restores continuity through causation among perceptions, not through
  standing dispositions.
- **Parfit's "Personal Identity"**, filed beside this reading, agrees that
  identity is indeterminate in hard cases (1.4.6.21) and closes on a
  quotation from Hume's "The Sceptic".

## Limitations

What is shown and what is asserted:

- **Shown, given the copy principle:** that no single constant impression
  could be the source of an idea of a self that is the same through life
  (C1). The argument is as strong as the copy principle.
- **Reported, not shown:** the introspective premise (1.4.6.3). Hume himself
  concedes another may perceive otherwise.
- **Asserted with illustrations:** the mechanism of the fiction (C3). The
  cases show how we talk about ships and churches; that the same mechanism
  produces belief in a self is argued by analogy (1.4.6.15) and by the
  "closer" argument from separability (1.4.6.16).
- **Withdrawn:** the account of what unites the perceptions (C7). The
  Appendix does not say which part of 1.4.6 fails. It says the two
  principles cannot be reconciled with any explanation of the felt
  connexion. Commentators disagree about what exactly the labyrinth is; the
  text does not settle it.
- **Deferred:** identity "as it regards our passions" (1.4.6.5), left to
  Book 2.

## Open questions

- What would a "real connexion" among perceptions be, if one were
  perceived? The Appendix names two ways out (inherence in a simple
  substance, or perceived real connexion) and takes neither.
- Is the fiction of identity itself a perception, and if so whose? The
  section's language ("we feign", "our propension") presupposes the subject
  the bundle is meant to replace. The section does not address this.

## Corrections

None to the brief's citation. One point of scope: the Appendix appeared with
vol. III in 1740, a year after the section, and the edited text on the site
folds the Appendix's instructed changes into Book 1. The page's markup
shows two in 1.4.6: an ERRATA insertion ("For what is the memory", 1.4.6.18)
and one Appendix change in 1.4.6.23 ("intellectual and natural world" for
"intellectual and moral world"). Neither touches the argument; otherwise the
text read is the 1739 text.
