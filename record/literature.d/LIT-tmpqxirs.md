# LIT-tmpqxirs: A taxonomy of technology design features that promote potentially addictive online behaviours

Maèva Flayelle, Damien Brevers, Daniel L. King, Pierre Maurage, José C.
Perales & Joël Billieux (2023), *Nature Reviews Psychology* 2(3):136–150 —
DOI-10.1038/s44159-023-00153-4

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmp1mo2x](../notes.d/NOTE-tmp1mo2x.md). Pages are the deposit's PDF
pages.*

- **The question.** How "technology design features might influence
  users’ capacity to exert control over how they engage with and use
  websites and applications, thereby amplifying uncontrolled, and perhaps
  addictive, use" (p. 1).
- **Two families.** Model-free features work through cached values:
  random-ratio rewards ("where non-monetary rewards such as ‘likes’,
  ‘retweets’ or notifications are unpredictable", p. 6) and personalized
  cues. Model-based features work through the user's computed
  comparison: overvaluation, interference with deliberation, partial goal
  fulfilment and cognitive biases (Table 2, p. 8). The two "are not
  mutually exclusive, but can operate in parallel" (p. 6).
- **Unpredictability as the model-free engine.** "the unpredictability of
  rewards, and the impossibility of reducing such unpredictability with
  experience, promote perseverative and cue-driven addiction-like
  behaviour" (p. 6). Incentive sensitization can be produced by drugs "as
  well as other reinforcers when their delivery is highly unpredictable"
  (p. 6).
- **Selection without psychology on the provider's side.** "design
  features that increase the frequency of online behaviour,
  time-on-device or spending (and therefore revenue) will be maintained,
  whereas those that do not will be abandoned" (p. 6).
- **Informational asymmetry.** Providers know their users and users do not
  know how that knowledge is used. "If user motives constitute
  vulnerabilities, these automated processes could become exploitative"
  (p. 8).
- **Weak evidence, stated.** Gaming research is "limited by its reliance
  on cross-sectional data" (p. 4). For cybersex, "empirical research is
  scarce and supporting evidence is mainly indirect" (p. 5). The authors
  call substance-use models applied to online behaviour "highly
  reductionistic" (p. 10).
