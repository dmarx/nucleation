# NOTE-827: Haggard — Sense of agency in the human brain

## Contribution

The review fixes a narrow sense of "sense of agency": the experience of
controlling a specific motor act and, through it, an external outcome. It
sets this apart from the felt capacity to act (self-efficacy) and from the
social attribution of an action to an agent. It then argues against the
prevailing view that the experience is computed only retrospectively. It
cites evidence that action selection and outcome prediction contribute
before the outcome arrives, and that this prospective part is what
psychosis loses.

## Key insight

The feeling of having made something happen is not a readout of a single
prediction-error comparator. It is assembled from signals available at
different times: premotor signals of selecting and initiating an action,
predictions of its outcome, and feedback once the outcome arrives. The
early signals can raise the feeling even when they carry no information
about actual control, and can sustain it on trials where the expected
outcome never comes.

## Assumptions

- **The construct is the instrumental, motor one.** "I use the term sense
  of agency to refer to an experience that accompanies the performance of
  a specific motor act" (p. 2). The social-science and computational uses,
  "a felt capacity to act (self-efficacy), without reference to any
  specific motor act", are set aside. Bandura 1982 is cited for the former
  (ref. 1).
- **Implicit measures stand in for the experience.** A difference in
  intentional binding between two well-chosen conditions "can potentially
  be interpreted as a difference in sense of agency". Shifts in time
  perception alone "are not diagnostic", since attention, causality, drugs
  and adaptation also move them (p. 6).
- **Voluntariness is taken as given though contested.** "The precise
  definition of a voluntary action remains controversial" (p. 4).
- **A review.** Every finding below is reported from cited studies, often
  the author's own group's. Sample sizes and effect sizes are not given in
  the text.

## Key results

- **Definition (pp. 3–5).** Agency involves ownership of the body movement
  ("my body moved") and "the cognitive experience of voluntary control
  over that body movement ('I voluntarily made it move')". Involuntary
  movements (reflexes, brain stimulation) produce the peripheral
  experiences and ownership "but not central experiences", and "are never
  accompanied by a sense of agency". "The core of sense of agency,
  therefore, is the association between a voluntary action and an
  outcome."
- **Explicit judgements are biased (p. 5).** There is "a tendency to
  overestimate one's own agency", stronger "when the outcome of an action
  is positive". Implicit and explicit measures "tend to be only weakly
  correlated across individuals", though both respond to reward,
  action–outcome interval and the actual degree of control.
- **Intentional binding (p. 6, Fig. 2).** Voluntary actions are perceived
  as shifted towards their outcomes, and outcomes towards the actions.
  The effect is measured against baselines where action or tone occurs
  alone; in the standard task the tone follows the keypress after 250 ms.
  A TMS-evoked twitch "has the opposite effect". In the interval-estimate
  variant, intervals are always under one second, and shorter estimates
  mean more agency.
- **Instrumental agency and social attribution are distinct (p. 6).** "It
  remains unclear whether social attribution of actions to agents depends
  on an antecedent ability to compute and perceive instrumental agency, or
  vice versa."
- **Action preparation (p. 7).** A stronger early readiness potential went
  with stronger binding. Temporal coincidence of a TMS twitch with ongoing
  preparation did not produce binding: "The cognitive preparation of an
  action plan needs to match the muscular movement precisely".
- **Action selection (pp. 7–9).**
  - A meta-analysis of seven studies found that anodal tDCS over left
    DLPFC enhanced binding "only in those studies in which participants
    themselves selected between alternative actions".
  - Subliminal primes that eased or hindered selection moved explicit
    agency judgements over an outcome they did not predict. "The
    contribution of action selection fluency to sense of agency is, in a
    sense, illusory", but "may be a rule of thumb that normally works".
- **The comparator model and its limits (pp. 9–11, Fig. 3).** A forward
  model predicts the sensory consequences of the motor command from an
  efference copy. A zero prediction error means "the event is caused by
  one's own action". Three objections are made:
  - The model "may successfully explain the phenomena of 'non-agency'"
    but not the positive "buzz", since it "does not generate any neural
    signal that could cause this experience".
  - It attenuates the perception of the very outcomes that are the goals
    of action.
  - People can feel agency over arbitrary outcomes "long after the action
    occurred".
- **The prospective component (pp. 11–12, Fig. 4).** When a tone followed
  the action on 50% of trials, action binding was stronger on trials
  where the tone came, a retrospective effect. On trials with no tone,
  binding was stronger in blocks where the tone was likely (75%) than
  where it was less likely (50%). So "an additional, prospective component
  of the sense of agency is present even if the outcome does not in fact
  occur".
- **Brain mechanisms (pp. 12–14).**
  - A meta-analysis of 15 neuroimaging studies found the temporoparietal
    junction, including the angular gyrus, as the correlate of
    "non-agency". The only area "consistently associated with positive
    self-agency was the anterior insula".
  - The angular gyrus was more active for low agency ratings only after
    incompatible primes, and TMS over it abolished the effect of the
    primes. "The parietal cortex may not simply match outcomes to actions
    retrospectively."
  - tDCS and theta-burst TMS over pre-SMA reduced binding.
  - The comparator-in-parietal-cortex view is doubted, because parietal
    activity depends on the result of the comparison more than on its
    process. "The key neural correlate of sense of agency might lie in
    the connectivity between frontal and prefrontal motor areas that
    initiate action, and parietal areas" (p. 14).
- **Pathology (pp. 14–16).** Conditions can be hyperagentic or
  hypoagentic. In schizophrenia, patients' action binding "depended
  entirely on the actual occurrence of the tone", whereas in healthy
  volunteers it "depended on the probability that the tone might occur".
  "Psychosis thus seemed to abolish the normal prospective element of
  agency." An earlier small study found binding stronger, not weaker, in
  patients with positive symptoms.
- **Coercion (pp. 16–17, Fig. 5).** Participants took turns giving and
  receiving shocks. Under an experimenter's instruction, the perceived
  interval between keypress and tone (synchronous with the shock) was
  longer than under free choice. The auditory N1 to the tone was reduced
  "both when the action lead to a painful shock, and when it did not".
  "Obeying instructions reduces the sense of agency, producing an
  experience closer to passive movement than to voluntary action." This
  "does not, of course, legitimate the defense of 'only obeying orders'".
- **Conclusion (p. 18).** The circuit "operates both prospectively ... as
  well as retrospectively". The post hoc inference view "has perhaps been
  overstated". "The distinctive experience of initiating a voluntary motor
  action appears to be necessary, though not sufficient, for sense of
  agency in normal circumstances."
- **Box 1 (pp. 23–24).** In law, someone "who lacks a sense of agency
  regarding an action cannot be held criminally responsible", though
  courts are "rightly skeptical". The English "loss of control" defence
  is discussed: rats with prior control over a stressor acquired
  resilience to later uncontrollable stress, so prolonged abuse might
  remove that resilience.
- **Box 2 (pp. 24–25).** Infants learn to become agents. Paralysed users
  of neuroprostheses report, anecdotally, a feeling of agency over the
  prosthesis. "The cognitive mechanisms underlying instrumental agency may
  be almost unlimited in scope."

## Concepts

- **Sense of agency.** "The experience of controlling one's own motor acts
  and, through them, the course of external events" (p. 2). Not
  self-efficacy, and not the social attribution of authorship.
- **Sense of ownership.** "The feeling of 'mineness'" of a body part or
  mental state. Present in involuntary movement, where agency is absent.
- **Instrumental control.** "The capacity to initiate an action, and thus
  bring about an intended change in the environment" (glossary).
- **Intentional binding.** The mutual compression in perceived time of a
  voluntary action and its outcome, used as an implicit measure of
  agency.
- **Prospective and retrospective agency.** Contributions available before
  the outcome (selection, initiation, prediction) and after it (feedback,
  postdiction).
- **Hyperagentic and hypoagentic.** Excessive or reduced experience of
  one's own causation and control, by analogy with hyperkinetic and
  hypokinetic movement disorders.
- **Volition.** "The process of preparing, initiating and executing an
  action under one's own control". Its traditional hallmark is that the
  agent "could have done otherwise" (glossary).

## Connections

The review builds on the comparator account of motor control (Wolpert,
Blakemore and Frith) and on Frith's account of delusions of control. It
argues against Wegner's *The Illusion of Conscious Will* (2002) on how far
agency is post hoc inference, and takes from Synofzik et al. (2008) the
point that the comparator cannot produce the positive feeling. It cites
Bandura twice. The 1982 paper is cited for the self-efficacy sense of
"agency" the review sets aside. The 2001 Annual Review paper ([LIT-1005](../literature.d/LIT-1005.md))
is cited for the claim that agency is strongest with "a strong motivation
to act, a clear action goal and a specific cortical motor command"
(p. 4). That last element is not a psychological claim Bandura's paper is
known for, and it could not be checked here.

## Bearing on the record

- **No existing THEORY holds the review's findings.** A grep of theory.d,
  claims.d and questions.d for "sense of agency", "intentional binding",
  "comparator", "efference" and "forward model" found nothing on the
  experience of agency. Two accounts are filed from this reading:
  - [THEORY-322](../theory.d/THEORY-322.md): the sense of agency draws on prospective signals, not
    only retrospective comparison (C3, C4, C5).
  - [THEORY-300](../theory.d/THEORY-300.md): coercive instruction reduces the implicit sense of
    agency (C7).
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md)** asks what report, no-report, deficit and
  architectural evidence each establish about experience. This review is
  a worked case for a narrow experience. Explicit reports are biased and
  only weakly correlated with implicit measures (C2). The implicit
  measures, though, are not diagnostic alone, only as differences
  between conditions. The schizophrenia data are deficit evidence that
  dissociates two components.
- **[CLAIM-062](../claims.d/CLAIM-062.md)** (Superseded) kept agency apart from phenomenal experience. Haggard's
  "sense of agency" is the phenomenal side of that split: an experience
  that accompanies agency, can be absent when movement occurs and present
  when control is lacking. The review supports keeping the two apart, and
  shows they can dissociate in both directions (C3, C1).
- **[QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md) and [QUESTION-071](../questions.d/QUESTION-071.md).** The coercion result (C7)
  measures an agent's experience of what she does under another's
  instruction. It bears on [QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md)'s "whose agency does the wrong
  run through": the instructed agent experiences her own act as closer to
  passive movement. It says nothing about manipulation, which is covert,
  whereas the instruction here was overt. It does not touch
  [THEORY-040](../theory.d/THEORY-040.md) or [THEORY-235](../theory.d/THEORY-235.md), whose subject is the agent's structure
  or her access to an influence, not her felt control.
- **Will-organization line.** Box 1's resilience point (prior control over
  a stressor protects against later uncontrollable stress) is reported
  from rat studies in one sentence and is not filed.
- **What the LIT's summary got wrong.** It said the review "sets
  prediction-based (comparator) accounts against retrospective,
  inferential ones". On Haggard's account the comparator model is itself
  retrospective: it computes agency after delayed feedback arrives
  (p. 11). The contrast the review draws is prospective against
  retrospective signals, and comparator against an associative,
  connectivity account. The LIT's summary is corrected.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.
  Box 2's remark on brain-machine interfaces is anecdotal and gives no
  practice.

## Limitations

- A review by an author whose group ran many of the studies reviewed. No
  effect sizes, samples or replications are given in the text, and none
  of the primary studies was read here.
- The prospective component rests on one binding design (Moore and
  Haggard 2008) and its patient extension (Voss et al. 2010).
- Implicit measures are themselves under-validated as measures of an
  experience. The review grants that shifts in time perception "are not
  diagnostic of a sense of agency" in themselves.
- The coercion result is one experiment in one paradigm, with financial
  reward attached to the shock key.
- The legal discussion is the author's framing, not evidence. He calls the
  relevance of neuroscientific evidence to the law "correctly, a topic of
  intense debate".

## Open questions

- Does social attribution of agency depend on instrumental agency, or the
  reverse? The review says this is unclear (p. 6).
- Is the reduced agency under coercion a reduced experience, or a
  reduced report of it? Interval estimates are reports of time. The N1
  result is the part that does not depend on the report.
- Does the prospective component survive outside the binding paradigm,
  for example in explicit judgements made without primes?
- What signal would carry the positive "buzz" of agency if the comparator
  outputs nothing on success? The connectivity view names a place but not
  a signal.
