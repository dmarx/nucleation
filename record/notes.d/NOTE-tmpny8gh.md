# NOTE-tmpny8gh: Galanter — Why the haves come out ahead

## Contribution

A structural, party-centred account of why a formally neutral legal
system tends to favour the "haves", in four interlocking layers: parties,
legal services, institutional facilities and rules (Figure 3). It
separates a case's tangible stake from its stake in the rules, and ranks
reforms: changing rules alone is weakest, organizing one-shotters into
something like repeat players strongest.

## Key results

- **The distinction** (p. 97): claimants with "only occasional recourse
  to the courts" against "repeat players (RP) who are engaged in many
  similar litigations over time". The two are ends of a continuum, not "a
  dichotomous pair" (pp. 97–98).
- **The RP** (p. 98): "a unit which has had and anticipates repeated
  litigation, which has low stakes in the outcome of any one case, and
  which has the resources to pursue its long-run interests." The OS's
  claims are "too large (relative to his size) or too small (relative to
  the cost of remedies) to be managed routinely and rationally."
- **Playing for rules** (pp. 100–02): "RPs can play for rules as well as
  immediate gains", so precedent comes to be "relatively skewed toward
  those favorable to RP".
- **Not the haves** (p. 103): "it is not suggested that RPs are to be
  equated with 'haves'"; RP status is one position of advantage.
- **The OS's counter** (n. 10): an OS "can burn up his capital".
- **Overload** (pp. 119–22): courts are marked by "passivity and
  overload"; overload pressures claimants to settle "(a) by causing delay
  (thereby discounting the value of recovery)" and "(b) by raising costs
  (of keeping the case alive)", and "shields existing rules from
  challenge". "Third, overload tends to protect the possessor"; there are
  "more rights and rules 'on the books' than can be vindicated or
  enforced" (p. 122).
- **Process as weapon** (nn. 75, 77): "possessor-defendants exploiting the
  full panoply of procedural devices to raise the cost to claimants"; the
  advantage may come "from the complexity, expense and uncertainty of the
  litigation process itself".
- **Delay for the weaker side** (p. 136, a note on a physician facing
  loss of licence): "Every year of delay is worth money, win or lose".

## Assumptions

An American-type society: unequal parties, courts that purport to apply
general norms, specialized intermediaries, rule-making by adjudication,
too few resources for full adjudication, imperfect penetration of rules.
The essay is "speculative and tentative" and draws on the literature, with
no new data.

## Concepts

Repeat player; one-shotter; the RP–OS continuum; tangible and rule
outcomes; playing for rules; penetration; playing the odds against
minimax; passivity; overload; protection of the possessor; the time-lag
of rules; four layers of advantage.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-tmpyo35b** (litigation as siege). Independent, qualitative
  corroboration: Galanter names the claim's two channels, delay
  "discounting the value of recovery" and cost "of keeping the case
  alive", and notes defendants who use procedure "to raise the cost to
  claimants". He gives no threshold model; the comparison of sustainable
  time with time to judgment is the exchange's.
- **CASE-tmphljcr.** Supplies the mechanisms the construction formalizes
  (delay discounts recovery, cost of keeping a case alive). It does not
  check the case's numbers.
- **QUESTION-tmp14i7s.** A route by which the capacity to litigate shapes
  the standard: RPs settle cases that would make bad precedent and
  litigate promising ones, so precedent is skewed; overload "shields
  existing rules from challenge"; RPs benefit from the time-lag of rules.
- **QUESTION-tmpcz1ui.** Delay and cost are available to either side: the
  OS "can burn up his capital", and for the physician "Every year of delay
  is worth money".
- **CLAIM-tmpa0wdv.** Rights "on the books" that cannot be vindicated fit
  the claim's valid claim with falling correctability. Galanter is
  descriptive and says nothing about normative power.
- **CLAIM-619.** Notes 75 and 77 describe a party using process to raise
  the cost of challenge.
- The exchange's characterization (A61) is faithful. The asymmetry is a
  continuum, RPs are not simply the haves, and the support for "litigation
  as siege" lies in §III rather than in the RP/OS typology the exchange
  cites.

## Limitations

Speculative by its own account; evidence from the 1960s and 1970s; American
courts. §§II, V and most of VI–VII were skimmed. The copy's OCR has
character errors.
