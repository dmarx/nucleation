# NOTE-1443: Mossio & Bich — What makes biological organisation teleological?

## Contribution

It argues that intrinsic teleology can be naturalised through
organisation, not through selection, and says which kind of circularity
does the work. Earlier organisational accounts (Kant, Bernard, Piaget,
Maturana and Varela) tied teleology to self-determination but left closure
too loose to say why the water cycle is not teleological. Building on Rosen
and on Kauffman's work–constraint cycle, it makes closure a closure of
*constraints*, acting on a thermodynamic flow at another level of
causation. That lets it separate closure from cycles of transformation, and
place dissipative structures, at most, on a minimal rung that is
teleological but not organised.

## Key insight

Circularity is "necessary but not sufficient for intrinsic teleology"
(MS p. 2). What counts is whether the system's activity "contributes to
specifying the conditions under which the circular relation as such can
occur" (MS p. 16). A river only obeys or modulates its bed. An organism
makes the constraints that make it. So teleology sits at the level of
constraints, and closure is "by construction an inter-level causal regime"
(MS p. 15).

## Assumptions

- **Telos is identified with the conditions of existence.** Biological
  systems "are what they do" (MS p. 2). The goal of an organism and its
  own existence "are one and the same thing", which is what makes the
  teleology intrinsic, against the extrinsic teleology of artefacts
  (MS p. 2, after Jonas).
- **Constraints are defined by conservation at a time scale.** Constraints
  are "local and contingent causes, which reduce the degrees of freedom of
  the dynamics on which they act (Pattee, 1973), while remaining conserved
  (at the time scale which is relevant to describe their causal action)
  with respect to those dynamics" (MS p. 14). Catalysts are constraints,
  substrates are material causes (fn 17).
- **A relational, not dynamical, description.** Following Rosen, closure
  is described relationally, "abstracting closure from time", so final
  causation does not invert the arrow of time (MS p. 13, and again p. 20).
- **Rosen's efficient causes are read as constraints.** "Organisational
  closure should be understood as a closure of constraints" (MS p. 14,
  citing Moreno & Mossio, in press). The formal definitions are not given
  here; the paper refers to Mossio et al. 2013 (fn 17).

## Key results

The paper is philosophical argument; it has no formal results.

- **Closure versus cycles (MS p. 15).** "Cycles refer to circular chains
  of transformations that, under the effects of externally determined
  constraints, can be described at one level of causation". Closure is "a
  circular causal regime in which a set of constraints described at one
  level of causation maintains itself by canalising processes and
  reactions ... at another level of causation".
- **The water cycle is not teleological (MS pp. 16–17).** Its chain of
  transformations "does not generate its own constraints", which "are
  external to the circular dynamics, and independent from them": the
  slope of the ground. The river digs its bed, but "the water cycle would
  exist even though the river did not modulate its own bed". Cycles
  "simply realise" their causal regime, so "cycles do not self-determine.
  Therefore, they are not teleological regimes."
- **Closure grounds teleology, normativity and function (MS p. 15).**
  "The set of constraints subject to closure are the set of biological
  functions."
- **Non-biological closure (MS p. 18).** Closure "requires a relatively
  high degree of complexity", and "there are no clear examples of
  non-biological organisationally closed chemical networks, apart from
  abstract theoretical and computational models as, for instance, the
  well-known autocatalytic sets". A material realisation would be "'at the
  edge' of the biological domain". Footnote 21 adds that proto-biological
  closure cannot be excluded a priori.
- **Dissipative structures (MS pp. 18–19).** Whether they self-constrain
  "has not been settled": Prigogine and Stengers, Bickhard and Ruiz-Mirazo
  say yes; Anderson and Stein, Crutchfield, and Minati and Pessa say no.
  The authors have elsewhere taken the first view ("as we did elsewhere",
  MS p. 19). If they do self-constrain, the account must "concede that
  dissipative structures realise intrinsic teleology" — but of a
  "radically different kind". They "do not realise closure; rather, they
  would be minimally teleological, insofar as they would generate a single
  macroscopic constraint (the structure itself) that, supposedly,
  maintains itself by acting on its own boundary conditions". They "are
  not organised" and "their parts have no functions" (MS p. 19).

## Concepts

- **Self-determination** — the effects of a system's activity "contribute
  to establish and maintain its own conditions of existence" (MS p. 2).
- **Self-constraint** — self-determination in which the system produces
  (some of) the constraints acting on its own activity (MS p. 19).
- **Constraint** — a local, contingent cause that reduces the degrees of
  freedom of a dynamics while staying conserved, at the relevant time
  scale, with respect to it (MS p. 14).
- **Closure** — "an organisation of mutually dependent constraints"
  (MS p. 16); "a network of mutually dependent constitutive constraints"
  (abstract).
- **Cycle** — a circular chain of transformations, under external
  constraints, describable at a single level of causation (MS p. 15).
- **Minimal teleology** — the status a dissipative structure would have
  if it self-constrains through its single macroscopic constraint (MS
  p. 19).

## Connections

It is a step in Mossio's programme. Mossio, Saborido and Moreno (2009,
[LIT-570](../literature.d/LIT-570.md)) is cited for functions as constraints under closure (MS p. 3,
p. 15), and Moreno and Mossio's *Biological Autonomy* ([LIT-536](../literature.d/LIT-536.md)), then in
press, for closure of constraints and for the division of labour (MS
pp. 10–11, 14). It does not cite Montévil and Mossio (2015, [LIT-582](../literature.d/LIT-582.md)),
which came later and gives closure formal definitions. Its treatment of
cycles (the water cycle, MS pp. 11, 16–17) runs parallel to [LIT-582](../literature.d/LIT-582.md)'s
footnote 25 on the hydrologic cycle, which [NOTE-468](NOTE-468.md) records; whether that
footnote cites this paper was not checked. On teleology
it sets itself against selected-effect accounts (Ruse, Millikan, Neander),
the genetic-program view (Jacob, Monod, Mayr), cybernetics (Wiener,
Ashby) and Maturana and Varela's rejection of teleology, and against
Toepfer's inclusion of the water cycle (MS pp. 3–10, 16).

## Bearing on the record

- **[THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md) — partly; it supports the line but not the account's
  wording of it.** What the paper says about differentiation as against
  closure, exactly:
  - It **never names differentiation as a condition**. The word does not
    occur in the text; the only occurrence is the title of Arnellos and
    Moreno (2012) in the references, cited for the complexity closure
    needs (MS p. 18). Distinct time scales among constraints are not
    mentioned either; "time scale" appears once, in the definition of a
    constraint (MS p. 14).
  - It **folds the division of labour into closure**. Closure "conveys the
    idea that self-determination results from the integrated interactions
    among a set of entities which would not exist in isolation: each
    component makes a specific and complementary contribution", and "by
    virtue of the interplay between division of labour and mutual
    dependence that it implies, closure captures a fundamental aspect of
    the idea of 'organisation'" (MS pp. 10–11). Functions likewise need
    "different yet complementary contributions" and are "the set of
    constraints subject to closure" (MS p. 15). This matches the record's
    reading of [LIT-582](../literature.d/LIT-582.md) that [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md) states ("that builds
    differentiation into closure"), and it is the authors' own words,
    a year before [LIT-582](../literature.d/LIT-582.md), not the record's inference.
  - It **denies dissipative structures closure, not merely
    differentiation**. [LIT-570](../literature.d/LIT-570.md) (2009), as [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md)'s step 2 reads it,
    granted the flame "organizational closure" and drew the line at
    differentiation. This paper instead says dissipative structures "do
    not realise closure" and would at most "generate a single macroscopic
    constraint" (MS p. 19). So by 2014 the 2009 broad closure is gone from
    the authors' vocabulary, and "one constraint versus a set of mutually
    dependent constraints" is where the line falls. That is the
    single-constraint half of [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md)'s title, stated by the authors;
    the "different time scales" half is not in this paper.
  - It **does not analyse a flame**. It quotes Bickhard's candle (fn 23)
    only as a view on self-maintenance, and it leaves open whether
    dissipative structures self-constrain at all (MS pp. 18–19). So the
    first test [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md)'s promote_when asks for (a dissipative structure
    analysed under [LIT-582](../literature.d/LIT-582.md)'s Definitions 1–4) is not met here.
  - On **whether chemical systems can realise closure**, the question for
    which [LIT-582](../literature.d/LIT-582.md) cites it: no clear non-biological example is known
    beyond abstract models such as autocatalytic sets, and a material one
    would be "at the edge" of biology (MS p. 18). That supports
    [THEORY-072](../theory.d/THEORY-072.md)'s protocell clause as a place to look, but supplies no
    analysis of one, so the promote_when's second test is not met either.

  In sum, this reading settles the question promote_when puts about the
  authors' view: they fold differentiation into closure. It does not do
  either of the two analyses the promote_when requires.

## Limitations

- **The closure of constraints is asserted, not defined, here.** The
  paper defers the formal account to Mossio et al. 2013 and to the book
  then in press (fn 17, MS p. 14). It gives no criterion for counting
  constraints or for when two of them are "mutually dependent".
- **"A single macroscopic constraint" is not argued.** That a dissipative
  structure has one constraint, and not several (a flame's temperature
  field, the convection it drives), is stated, not shown (MS p. 19).
- **The authors' own view on dissipative structures is divided.** They
  say they have argued elsewhere that dissipative structures self-constrain,
  and here present it as one side of an unsettled dispute (MS p. 19).
- **Supra-organismal closure is left open.** Symbioses and ecosystems
  "might also possibly be shown to be organisationally closed", and the
  authors have "no principled objections" (MS p. 17).

## Open questions

- Does a candle flame, analysed constraint by constraint, have one
  constraint of its own or several? The paper says one and does not
  show it.
- Does any non-biological chemical system realise closure in material
  form? The paper knows of none (MS p. 18).
- Is the division of labour a consequence of mutual dependence among
  constraints, or an extra condition? The paper treats it as implied by
  closure (MS p. 11) without proving that a closed set must divide labour.
