# THEORY-1404: Oskar and colleagues find that the evidence on procedural skill decay among emergency physicians cannot yet say how long a procedural skill lasts after competence: few studies make decay the primary outcome, almost none follow skills beyond a year, most rely on simulation and checklist scores and study trainees, and many required procedures are unstudied

## Source

Oskar et al. (2026), [LIT-2019](../literature.d/LIT-2019.md); read in [NOTE-1612](../notes.d/NOTE-1612.md).

## What was actually shown

A search of PubMed, CINAHL and EMBASE (February 2025) and reference
lists, 1349 records screened, 37 included. Counted across them:

- 30 (81%) used retention to evaluate a teaching intervention, not decay
  as the primary outcome (Theme 1);
- "Only two of the included studies (5.5%) described skill retention or
  decay beyond a one-year timeframe" (Theme 2);
- ultrasound in 43%, CPR in 30%, airway in 27%; cardiac pacing, lumbar
  puncture, orthopedic reductions, procedural sedation and vaginal
  delivery in none (Theme 3);
- nearly all assessed by simulation, mostly with checklists (Theme 4);
- about half studied trainees only and five (13.5%) attending physicians
  only (Theme 5).

These are counts that could have come out otherwise had the literature
been different; they depend on the search and the inclusion rule.

## What this does not say

- How fast emergency procedural skills decay, or how often refresher
  training is needed: it gives no rates.
- That the studies it found are sound: it did no quality or risk-of-bias
  assessment, and 22% of its items are conference abstracts.
- That decay depends on procedure type, initial learning, experience and
  practice frequency: the conclusion says so, but the synthesis does not
  test it.
- Anything about measuring capabilities by utilization, or about
  organizations.
