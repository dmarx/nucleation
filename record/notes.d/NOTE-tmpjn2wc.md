# NOTE-tmpjn2wc: 100 Flowers

## Contribution

A first-person account, in about fifteen minutes of radio, of how one
translator's view of translation changed over a long project. Hofstadter
starts by treating a poem's formal constraints as its essence. Faced with
his mother's free version, he ends by denying that translation has rules
at all. The segment gives no argument for either view. It is evidence of
what Hofstadter believed in 2014 and of the case that moved him.

## Key insight

No single rendering carries the original; a set of renderings shows it
from many sides. Hofstadter extends this from the poem to the poet: a
person is not one appearance but "millions of different things that are
united by analogy into something that we refer to as one thing". On that
view the question "is this a translation?" has no sharp answer, because
"the original" is itself a family held together by resemblance.

## Assumptions

- **The poem's identity is its form.** Held at the start, then given up.
  He rejects a 30-line version because "28 was a sacrosanct number". Later
  he praises a version of about 16 lines.
- **Words stand for concepts with halos.** The halo licenses substitution:
  jam becomes buttered bread, because "jelly" brings bread with it.
- **The facts of a poem are not privileged over its other properties.**
  Asked whether jam against ham is a factual error, he replies that "a
  fact about the poem is that it was written by somebody in French", which
  every translation already gives up.

## Key results

The segment reports a case, not results. In order:

- **The constraints.** Hofstadter lists them: three syllables a line, 28
  lines, rhymed couplets, the same first and last line, and the poet's
  name in the middle.
- **His first version.** It keeps the line count and syllable count and
  replaces "Clément" with "Douglas". He produced it with grids of
  candidate lines, about ten of which he reads aloud for one line.
- **Bob French's version.** One of the French translators of *Gödel,
  Escher, Bach*. It keeps the jam, the pale face and the poet's name, and
  has an older tone. Hofstadter rejects it for its length, 30 lines.
- **About sixty translators.** Students, colleagues, friends and his wife
  sent versions. The project became a 700-page book.
- **His mother's version.** About 16 lines, with no syllable count, no
  frame and different rhymes. His first reaction was dismay. His present
  judgement: it "has stood the test of time" and has "pizzazz" no other
  version had.
- **The conclusion.** "Let one hundred flowers bloom", and "there are no
  rules. It's all informal." Lynn Levy objects that nobody can read a
  hundred versions of every poem. He agrees, and the objection is left
  there.

## Concepts

- **form against message**: the constraints a poem satisfies (metre, line
  count, rhyme, frame, the poet's name) against what it says ("get well").
- **halo**: the set of concepts a word's concept brings with it, which a
  translator may draw on.
- **one hundred flowers**: Hofstadter's pluralism about translation. Many
  versions, each a partial view, none authoritative.

## Connections

The segment applies Hofstadter's thesis that concepts and categories are
held together by analogy. *Surfaces and Essences* ([LIT-420](../literature.d/LIT-420.md)) argues
it at book length, and his epilogue on analogy as the core of cognition
([LIT-417](../literature.d/LIT-417.md)) argues it in brief. Both are registered here and
unread, so the connection is to what those works say about themselves.
The French translation of *Gödel, Escher, Bach* ([LIT-437](../literature.d/LIT-437.md)) is what
sent him back to the poem.

## Bearing on the record

No THEORY in the record concerns translation or analogy-based
categorization, so this reading supports or contradicts none. If the
record states Hofstadter's analogy thesis as a THEORY, this interview is
an illustration of it, not evidence for it. The Chopin remark bears on
the individuation questions the record holds: it is a resemblance view of
what makes something one thing over time. There is no instruction for
machine-learning practice, so nothing here belongs in the anthology.

## Limitations

- It is an edited radio interview. The transcript is a rush transcript,
  and the audio is authoritative.
- The translations are read aloud, so the transcript's line breaks are not
  the poems'. The line counts are as Hofstadter states them.
- The book, *Le Ton beau de Marot* (1997), is not named in the
  transcript and was not read. The segment's account of the project is a
  summary of that book by its author, years later.
- The change of view is told as a story. There is no comparison of
  translations by readers, and no criterion for a good translation beyond
  Hofstadter's judgement.

## Open questions

- Does Hofstadter's book state criteria that the interview's "no rules"
  leaves out? Reading *Le Ton beau de Marot* would settle what his
  considered position is.
- Is the pluralism a claim about translation or about meaning in general?
  The Chopin analogy suggests the latter, which would make it a thesis
  about individuation that the record could test against its other
  accounts.
