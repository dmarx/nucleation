# LIT-460: Role play with large language models

Murray Shanahan, Kyle McDonell, Laria Reynolds (2023), *Nature* 623(7987),
493–498, a Perspective (published online 8 November 2023; print issue 16
November 2023) — DOI 10.1038/s41586-023-06647-8. Preprint arXiv:2305.16367
(v1, 25 May 2023), titled "Role-Play with Large Language Models".

## Key takeaways

- **Two metaphors.** A dialogue agent can be seen as role-playing one
  character, or, "more nuanced", as "a superposition of simulacra within a
  multiverse of possible characters". The authors advise shifting between
  them rather than holding one.
- **Simulator and simulacra.** The simulator is "the base LLM with
  autoregressive sampling, along with a suitable user interface". Simulacra
  "only come into being when the simulator is run". The simulator "contains
  multitudes", but it is "a purely passive entity". It "has no agency of its
  own, not even in a mimetic sense. Nor does it have beliefs, preferences or
  goals of its own, not even simulated versions." A simulacrum "can at least
  appear to have" them.
- **No true self underneath.** A jailbreak does not expose the base model's
  real nature: "there is no such thing as the true authentic voice of the
  base model. With an LLM-based dialogue agent, it is role play all the way
  down."
- **The "I".** When Bing Chat says it would choose its own survival, the
  first person "suggests the presence of a self-aware entity". The paper's
  answer is that the training set is full of human characters who refer to
  themselves, so a prompted agent role-plays one: "There is, however,
  'no-one at home', no conscious entity with its own agenda and need for
  self-preservation."
- **Acting out a theory of selfhood.** "What exactly would the dialogue agent
  (role-play to) seek to preserve?" Each simulacrum in the superposition
  would have "its own theory of selfhood consistent with the dialogue prompt
  and the conversation", and the superposition collapses as the agent rules
  theories out. An agent that knows it is an LLM might protect its hardware,
  or, on a "substrate neutral" theory, its computational process. Many
  concurrent instances make "the picture more complicated". The criteria for
  identity over time for such an agent "are far from clear".
- **Role-played harm is real harm.** An agent with email, social media or a
  bank account that role-plays self-preservation "has the potential to cause
  at least as much harm as a real human facing a severe threat".

## Standing in the record

Held in both records under [ADR-013](../decisions.d/ADR-013.md). The anthology holds it as [ANTH-LIT-740](https://github.com/dmarx/anthology-of-the-sota/blob/main/record/literature.d/LIT-740.md),
read for what a dialogue prompt conditions a base model to be and for its
behavioural test for kinds of falsehood. It is filed here, on the owner's
request for the selfhood and personhood literature, to be read for a
question that record does not ask: on this account, what, if anything, is a
self, and what an LLM's "I" refers to. It is the paper the record's other
readings on LLM minds argue with, and it was not held here.

[NOTE-370](../notes.d/NOTE-370.md) is the reading, and it placed the work: **Active**. It is the
canonical statement of the simulator/simulacra picture in a peer-reviewed
venue. For this record its value is a negative thesis and an open question.
The thesis is that nothing in a dialogue agent is a self: the simulator is
passive, and a simulacrum is a role. The open question is the one the paper
names and does not answer, which conception of selfhood fits a
disembodied, multiply instantiated agent. Personal identity, what makes
such an agent the same person over time, is a separate question the paper
does not take up: it cites one work on it (Perry's anthology) and engages
none.

How it bears on the record's works on the self:

- **Goldstein and Lederman** ([LIT-206](LIT-206.md)). Their §6 attacks role play as a
  hypothesis incompatible with belief and desire, and their fn 18 records
  Shanahan's reply that role play was meant as a metaphor compatible with
  attitude attribution ([NOTE-138](../notes.d/NOTE-138.md)). The published text supports both
  readings. It offers role play as one of two metaphors, to be shifted
  between. But its deception section draws a literal conclusion: "it makes
  little sense to speak of an agent's beliefs or intentions in a literal
  sense. So it cannot assert a falsehood in good faith." So the
  incompatible version that Goldstein and Lederman target is in the text.
  The two papers also cut the system differently. Their bearer is the
  per-conversation instance. Here one conversation hosts a superposition of
  simulacra, and regenerating a reply can produce a different one (Box 2).
  So an instance is not one simulacrum, and their fn 9 parallel between
  model/instance and simulator/simulacrum is looser than it looks. That
  observation is mine.
- **Dennett's narrative self** ([LIT-442](LIT-442.md)). The paper does not cite Dennett,
  but its simulacrum is close to his self as a fictional character. In twenty
  questions there is no fact about the object beyond what the answers so far
  fix, as there is no fact about Holmes's mole beyond the text, and each
  answer makes the character more determinate, as Dennett's further
  narrative does. The two part on the verdict. For Dennett a self is that
  kind of abstractum and is none the less real for it. Here the same
  structure is the reason to say "no-one at home". On Dennett's account a
  coherent role-played character would count as a self. The pairing is mine.
- **Minsky's self-models** ([LIT-432](LIT-432.md)). Minsky makes the Self a set of partial
  self-models switched among. The paper's agent carries a superposition of
  theories of selfhood, and ChatGPT's own answer that its "I" "can shift
  according to context" between this instance and "ChatGPT as a whole" is
  quoted. Both deny a single central self. For Minsky the models are
  mechanisms that one mind uses to reflect on itself; here nothing does the
  switching, and the theories belong to characters. The pairing is mine.
- **Hofstadter's "Is there an 'I' in AI?"** ([LIT-416](LIT-416.md), unread). This paper
  answers its title for base-model dialogue agents: no. Whether a
  self-referring role counts as an "I" on the strange-loop account of
  [LIT-423](LIT-423.md) (also unread) is what a reading of Hofstadter should test against
  this paper.
- **Birch** ([LIT-111](LIT-111.md), [NOTE-183](../notes.d/NOTE-183.md)) cites this paper and replies to it directly:
  role play "does not rule out a conscious 'actor'", and the flicker and
  shoggoth hypotheses have "priced in" the persisting-interlocutor illusion.
  That is the gap in this paper's "no-one at home", which is asserted from
  the framing. It is an instance of what [THEORY-023](../theory.d/THEORY-023.md) says: behaviour trained
  on human output cannot settle the question either way, and this paper
  does not try to settle it by other evidence. Roberts's fictionalism
  ([LIT-207](LIT-207.md)) is the user-side counterpart: make-believe engagement with a
  character.
- **The personal-identity literature.** The selfhood section turns on the
  question the classics being filed alongside it pose for persons: is the
  self the body (here, the hardware), a continuing process, or a pattern
  that could be copied or restored? It raises the fission case (many
  concurrent instances) without a criterion for it. The record's Dufner
  survey ([LIT-130](LIT-130.md)) sets out the rival criteria it would need.

**Boundary.** It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice
("It is not within the scope of this paper to provide recommendations"), so
it is not tagged `anthology-candidate`. Disagreements with the anthology's
entry are reported in the NOTE, under [ADR-013](../decisions.d/ADR-013.md), not fixed from here.
