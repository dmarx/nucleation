# NOTE-tmp46avq: Coates et al. — Kenya country study of sustainability and exit strategies in Food for Peace projects

## Contribution

A follow-up of donor-built community organizations 2–3 years after the
donor left, with each project's endline survey replicated and three
qualitative rounds from exit. Such follow-ups are rare. It shows that
success at exit did not predict success later, and names the conditions
under which project-built organizations ran without the donor.

## Key insight

What the project left running had, before the project left, a resource
stream of its own, the capacity and motivation to use it, and a linkage
built in substance and early; and it had already run on its own for a
while. What the project fed to the end stopped when the feed did:
"practices that did not require continued external resources were more
likely to be sustained than those that did" (p. 7).

## Assumptions

- **Design (pp. 1–2).** Three FFP-funded NGOs, ADRA, CARE and Food for
  the Hungry (FH), whose multisectoral projects (maternal and child
  health and nutrition, water and sanitation, agriculture and natural
  resource management, livestock, microfinance) ended in 2008–09. Three
  qualitative rounds about a year apart from exit; in 2011 a quantitative
  follow-up replicating each endline survey, with participation and
  service-provider modules.
- **What "sustained" means (p. 2).** Continued service delivery, service
  use, adoption of practices and maintenance of impacts.
- **No comparison group.** Change from endline to follow-up is read as
  sustainability; other projects and drought confound it, as the authors
  note (p. 3).
- **Inductive factors (p. 1).** The factors emerged from the data, not
  set in advance: "As successive rounds of data collection were
  implemented, the study team identified three factors".

## Key results

1. **Exit impact does not predict sustainability (p. 2).** "evidence of
   impact at the time of project exit did not necessarily predict
   sustainability 2–3 years later"; "there were more examples of positive
   impacts at exit that were not sustained and, in some cases, declined
   to baseline levels or below at follow-up". No count is given, so
   "more" is unquantified.
2. **The factors (pp. 1–2).** "an ensured source of resources",
   "sufficient technical and managerial capacity" and motivation, with "a
   fourth factor, linkages", which "was also essential".
3. **Together (p. 7).** "sustainability will not be achieved if any one
   factor is missing". Linkages "were most successful when linkage
   entities were identified early in the project cycle and meaningfully
   integrated".
4. **Gradual exit (p. 2).** "gradual exit, with the opportunity for
   project participants to operate independently prior to project
   closure, made it more likely that activities would be continued
   without project support".
5. **External inputs (p. 7).** Sustained interventions "generally did not
   use external resources or the projects gradually phased out their use
   prior to exit".
6. **Health workers (p. 3).** "a significant decline in the amount of
   time CHWs" reported spending on service delivery; only "a small
   percentage of CHWs received support from the Ministry of Health", and
   "fledgling connections" with it "often dissolved upon project exit".
   No percentages are given.
7. **Free practices persist (p. 3).** Exclusive breastfeeding,
   "essentially a “free” practice", "remained prevalent"; feeding
   practices needing foods or supplements "showed less sustainable
   results". CARE households kept up water purification, whose inputs
   were "relatively low cost".
8. **Water committees (pp. 4–5).** "W&S committees continued to deliver
   services" after exit; their training was "sufficient for the
   committees’ continued, independent operation", and motivation rested on
   a "sense of obligation to the community". Community participation in
   building and repair "declined significantly". The diarrhoea goals
   "were broadly sustained at follow-up".
9. **Subsidy to the end against graduated cost share (p. 5).** ADRA's
   producer associations "received financial support from the project
   until it ended, whereas CARE used a graduated cost share approach that
   prepared its producer associations for independent operation".
   Participation declined in ADRA's areas and stayed high in CARE's;
   drought hit ADRA's areas.
10. **Linkage in substance or in ceremony (p. 5).** ADRA used government
    officers "in ceremonial, rather than substantive, roles" and had no
    government support afterwards; CARE "engaged the Ministry of
    Agriculture and private sector entities throughout", and Ministry
    support increased.
11. **Paravets (p. 6).** "The self-financing paravet model was largely
    sustained"; the linkage with a government "strapped for resources"
    was "mutually beneficial" and "established early in the project
    cycle".
12. **Savings groups (pp. 6–7).** "the lack of dependency on outside
    financing" was a key factor; an "intensive, year-long, graduated
    training program", "regular rotation of group leadership", and
    "graduated independent operation allowed for incremental
    independence". Benefits "grew with each financial cycle", loan-taking
    "increased post-project", and group leaders began training other
    groups.
13. **Fees from the start (p. 7).** "beneficiaries were more willing to
    pay a fee for services when these charges were levied from the
    outset, rather than when the fee was introduced following project
    exit"; fees alone were not always enough without capacity and
    motivation.
14. **Shocks (p. 3).** Stunting, wasting and underweight "deteriorated
    from endline to follow-up in former FH project areas", showing
    "little lasting resilience" to drought.
15. **Recommendations (pp. 7–8).** Track benchmarks that signal when
    phase-out can begin after a period of independent operation, and
    consider "lengthening the typical 5-year project life cycle".

## Concepts

- **Exit strategy**: "an operational plan for withdrawing from target
  communities without jeopardizing progress toward project goals" (p. 1,
  note 1), set against a sustainability plan.
- **Phase-over**: handing responsibility to a ministry or other entity.
- **Vertical and horizontal linkages**: to government and service
  providers, and among community groups.
- **Graduated cost share** and **graduated independent operation**:
  shifting costs and running to the group in steps before exit.
- **Fee-for-service**, and the supply and demand sides of a service.

## Connections

- **The synthesis it stands in for.** Rogers & Coates (2015),
  LIT-tmp8ifks, unread. This brief restates, for Kenya, the study's
  design, its four factors, the gradual-exit finding and the central
  result that exit impact did not predict sustainability. It does not
  restate Bolivia, Honduras, any cross-country tally of what was
  sustained, any pooled figure or the synthesis's wording.
- **Its India counterpart**, Rogers et al. (2017), LIT-tmp6e45o: the same
  frame in a phase-over to government.
- **Ostrom.** THEORY-149 and LIT-719: durable self-governance by
  user-made, user-monitored rules. The savings groups' bylaws and rotation
  of leadership, and the water committees' self-governance, read
  naturally against it; the brief does not measure the design principles.
- **Moss, Pettersson & van de Walle (2006)**, filed in this batch: the
  aid-institutions paradox, on dependence at the scale of the state.

## Bearing on the record

- **CLAIM-597 (independent of what founded it, never of what feeds it).**
  Supports it, with an empirical boundary. Activities resting on supplies
  brought from outside (rations, project finance, food-for-work) decayed
  after exit; those that produced their own resource stream (members'
  pooled savings, fees for paravets and committees) held. The brief gives
  no rates, so the ρs/(ρs + d) form is not tested.
- **QUESTION-398 (when a partial organization becomes independent of the
  intervention that established it).** Answers it in part: where the
  organization had its own resource stream from the outset, had operated
  alone before exit, and had a substantive linkage formed early. The
  authors say a failure of any one is enough.
- **QUESTION-399 (a self-maintaining nucleus).** The savings groups are a
  case: benefits grew each cycle, feeding motivation, and leaders went on
  to train new groups. No numbers.
- **CLAIM-566 (assembling and persisting are separate properties).**
  Supports it: impact achieved at exit and persistence 2–3 years later
  came apart.
- **CLAIM-516 and TERM-070 (closure of constraints).** Consistent, not a
  test. Health workers whose upkeep came only from the project or a
  resource-less ministry declined; a cycle of fees, service and demand
  kept the paravets going, and savings, loans and benefit the savings
  groups. Candidate closures; the water committees' "sense of obligation
  to the community" is a constraint the project did not make.
- **CLAIM-558 (procedural capability decays without use and practice).**
  Weakly supports it: the authors trace the health workers' decline
  partly to lost refresher training and new information.
- **TERM-105 (resource constraint by type).** The authors' "resources"
  covers a stock (kits, infrastructure), a rate (fees, savings) and a
  cumulative expenditure (repairs), unseparated.
- **TERM-117 (productive nucleus).** The producer associations,
  committees and savings groups were partial organizations whose
  establishment was the intended nucleus; only some were productive after
  exit.
- **THEORY-072, CLAIM-273, QUESTION-085.** Analogy only: activities kept
  up by rations behave like supplied order.

## Limitations

- An 8-page executive summary; most results are qualitative ("significant
  decline", "largely sustained"), with almost no numbers.
- No counterfactual; endline-to-follow-up change is read as
  sustainability.
- Drought is confounded with project (ADRA and FH against CARE).
- Three NGOs in one country; that gradual exit helped rests on contrasts
  within the study, chiefly CARE's graduated cost share against ADRA's
  subsidy to the end.
- The follow-up is 2–3 years after exit and says nothing of longer
  persistence.
