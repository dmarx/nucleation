# NOTE-tmpn32y0: Zacks et al. — Event perception: a mind-brain perspective

## Contribution

A review that states event segmentation theory (EST) in information
processing terms, maps its components onto brain systems, and gathers
behavioural, narrative, neuropsychological and imaging evidence. It joins
the older unitization literature (Newtson's button-pressing task) and
narrative situation-model work to a single mechanism: event boundaries are
moments of transient prediction error at which the current event model is
reset.

## Key insight

"Perceptual systems continuously make predictions about what will happen
next. When transient errors in predictions arise, an event boundary is
perceived" (Abstract). An event model is "a representation of “what is
happening now,” which is robust to transient variability in the sensory
input" (A Theory of Event Segmentation). Its input is gated: closed most of
the time, open briefly when error rises. "Thus, the system alternates
between long periods of stability and brief periods of change."

## Assumptions

- **Perception is hierarchical, recurrent and cyclical**, and oriented to
  predicting the near future (the "working assumptions" of many theories).
- **Event models are working memory.** "Event models are working memory
  representations, which are implemented by transient changes in neural
  activation rather than long-term changes in synaptic weights." Event
  schemata, by contrast, are long-term knowledge that biases event models
  continuously.
- **One global reset signal per timescale**, plausibly dopamine or
  norepinephrine, rather than selective updating of parts of a model; this
  is what distinguishes EST from gating models of prefrontal cortex.
- **The perceiver is passive.** The authors list this as a limitation:
  "A second limitation is that the theory in its current form is a purely
  passive perceiver."

## Key results

A theory and review; no new data except a cited network simulation.

- **The mechanism.** Updating "consists of (1) resetting the current
  representations ... and (2) transiently increasing the influence of the
  pathway from sensory inputs to the event models". It runs on several
  timescales at once, coarse resets roughly a subset of fine ones.
- **Two control roles.** Segmentation allocates processing over time ("event
  segmentation does not itself require attention; rather, it implements a
  mechanism of attention") and updates working memory by resetting event
  models. Hence its claim to be "a core, domain-general mechanism of
  cognitive control".
- **Six implications** listed: segmentation is spontaneous and needs no
  conscious attention; it is cognitive control; it is multi-timescale;
  multimodal; it "depends on change. When the world is static, prediction
  is easy"; and it depends on prior knowledge.
- **A partial simulation** (Reynolds, Zacks & Braver, then in press): a
  network predicting body positions of an actor shows larger prediction
  error at event boundaries, and error-gated event-model units improve
  prediction without labels. Event schemata are not implemented.
- **Behavioural evidence.** Unitization is reliable within and across
  observers; coarse boundaries align with fine ones; boundaries are better
  remembered; segmenting well predicts later memory, in older adults with
  and without Alzheimer's dementia; edits and commercial breaks that respect
  boundaries help recall and those that cut across them hurt it; movement
  change and goal completion predict boundaries.
- **Narrative evidence.** Situation-model dimensions (time, space, objects,
  characters, causes, goals) predict where readers mark boundaries, more so
  as more dimensions change. In Speer & Zacks (2005) a time shift ("an hour
  later") slowed reading and lowered recognition of what came just before.
  This is the paper's direct evidence for its memory prediction: "Thus,
  information presented prior to an event boundary should be difficult to
  retrieve following that boundary."
- **Neural mapping.** Transient responses at boundaries in extrastriate
  cortex (near MT+) and right posterior frontal cortex during passive
  viewing; anterior cingulate cortex proposed for prediction error;
  midbrain neuromodulatory nuclei for the reset; lateral prefrontal cortex
  for event models and schemata (lesion, Parkinson's, schizophrenia and
  script-imaging studies).
- **The admitted misfit.** "The theory makes one prediction that does not
  square well with the available data": increased ACC activity at event
  boundaries during passive viewing has not been observed. Prefrontal cortex
  does not generally respond at boundaries during passive viewing either.

## Concepts

- **Event**: "a segment of time at a given location that is perceived by an
  observer to have a beginning and an end" (after Zacks & Tversky 2001);
  the paper's core cases are goal-directed human activity lasting seconds to
  tens of minutes.
- **Event model**: working-memory representation of what is happening now,
  stable against transient input, gated open at boundaries. The authors say
  it is "analogous to the term “situation model” used in the narrative
  comprehension literature".
- **Event schema**: long-term, learned knowledge of the sequential structure
  of kinds of activity; continuously biases event models.
- **Unitization**: the button-press task for marking boundaries, kept
  distinct from segmentation as an ongoing process.
- **Gating**: the mechanism that opens event models to sensory input when
  prediction error rises and closes it as error falls.

## Connections

It descends from Zacks & Tversky (2001), Newtson's unitization work, and
narrative situation-model theory (Zwaan & Radvansky 1998; the event
indexing model). It compares itself to adaptive resonance theory and to
prefrontal gating models (Miller & Cohen 2001; Frank, Loughry & O'Reilly
2001), differing in that error is perceptual, not reward error. It does
not mention doorways or spatial navigation tasks; the doorway studies
([LIT-1392](../literature.d/LIT-1392.md) and after) build on the same situation-model literature, and the
later ones cite EST's follow-up papers. The record's active-inference
account of conscious content ([THEORY-883](../theory.d/THEORY-883.md)) shares the prediction-error
architecture; EST makes no claim about consciousness, and says event models
"are not necessarily accessible to consciousness".

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-136](../cases.d/CASE-136.md).** The case calls EST "the theory behind" the doorway effect.
  This paper does not discuss the doorway effect; the record's
  identification of A37's "Event Segmentation Theory" with this paper is
  the record's own, and it is a fair one. But EST and the account the
  doorway authors give ([LIT-1384](../literature.d/LIT-1384.md), [LIT-1385](../literature.d/LIT-1385.md)) are not the same mechanism.
  EST predicts that what came before a boundary becomes harder to retrieve
  because the event model is reset. The doorway effect falls on the object
  being carried into the new event, which a reset of the old model should
  spare. That is why the doorway authors turn to interference between
  models. The case lists both as if one theory explained the effect.
- **[CLAIM-421](../claims.d/CLAIM-421.md) (activation interruption) and A37's reading.** A37 read the
  doorway effect through EST: the intention "may have lost accessibility
  because the current situation is being organized through a different
  event model". As a statement of EST that is fair: a reset event model no
  longer holds what it held. But EST is a theory of perceiving others'
  activity. Event models in it represent the world, not one's goals, and
  the authors name extending it to "how event models explicitly represent
  one’s own goals" as future work. An intention dropped at a boundary is an
  extension of EST, not a consequence of it.
- **[TERM-055](../terms.d/TERM-055.md).** EST's split between event models (working memory, reset at
  boundaries) and event schemata (long-term, continuous) is a mechanism for
  the difference between holding and having to hand: knowledge persists in
  schemata while the model that made it available is reset. The paper does
  not put it that way.
- **[THEORY-688](../theory.d/THEORY-688.md) (doorway options in learning).** The options framework
  segments action at subgoals; EST segments perception at prediction error.
  EST's own comparison is with prefrontal gating models, which gate on
  reward error; options are not discussed.
- **[THEORY-tmpg9wtv](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpg9wtv.md)** is filed from this reading (Proposed): the core of
  EST. The record held no account of event segmentation.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- Prediction error is never measured in people. The behavioural evidence
  links boundaries to change, goals and statistical structure, all of which
  could drive boundaries without error as the intermediary. The one direct
  test of the error mechanism is a simulation on simple motion-capture
  actions.
- Its one neural prediction checked (ACC at boundaries) failed, and the
  neural mapping is offered as tentative.
- Much of the cited evidence is from the authors' groups, and several key
  studies were in press or under review at the time.
- The memory-cost prediction rests chiefly on narrative time shifts, not on
  experienced events.
- It is a theory of a passive observer of goal-directed human activity.

## Open questions

- Would manipulating predictability while holding perceptual change fixed
  move boundaries, as C2 requires? (Later work by the same group addresses
  this; not read here.)
- Does EST predict the doorway effect's specific pattern, a cost on the
  carried object? Or does that need a second mechanism, such as the
  event horizon model's interference?
- How does an actor's own goal enter an event model, so that an intention
  could be lost at a boundary?
