# Article 9. The Political Economy of Dependence

## Cooperation, exploitation, domination, and the governance of contractual costs

**Abstract.** Exploitation is usually pictured as an unfair split of what cooperation produces. This article argues that cooperation can also produce the dependence through which a split becomes unfair, and that the dependence works on the terms through a fallback that the cooperation itself wears down. Where a capacity is learned by doing and lost with disuse, supply that does a party's work lowers the capacity it keeps. What decides this is the order in which supply and capacity are used, not the amount of supply. Where growth also needs capacity already present, such supply has a deadline after which withdrawing it no longer restores the capacity. A fallback raises a party's terms whether or not it is ever used. So, in a model in which the split is re-bargained against the current fallback, substitutive supply lowers the party's terms while the party never exits and no price, rule or exit option changes. Ending the supply before the capacity's deadline restores the terms, and ending it after does not. Where the party cannot exit, the fallback does not enter the terms at all. The article then argues, as a proposal and not a result, that what sorts a dependence is who controls the terms on which it is met. It sets beside the exit channel the costs of contesting terms, the capture of conscientious compensation, and an obligation that bars exit without pricing it. Last, it states a presumptive test for domination by an arrangement's controllers. None of this shows that any arrangement is exploitative or dominating. Exploitation and domination are judged here only against a standard declared with the judgment, and the article declares none. What it establishes is the mechanism by which an exploitative or dominating relation can be built while ordinary performance looks fine.

---

### 9.1 Introduction

Accounts of exploitation in successful cooperation usually start with a dependence already in place. Someone needs the employer, the platform or the patron, and the question is whether the terms they accept are fair. This article asks the earlier question. How does reliable provision create the dependence, and how does the dependence, once created, move the terms? It also asks a later question. What would have to be added to a dependence before it counts as exploitation or domination?

The argument goes in five steps, in the order in which they depend on each other. First, a mechanism. Supply that does a party's work lowers the capacity the party keeps, and where capacity is needed for growth there is a point of no return (§9.2). Second, a distinction. An arrangement can produce a dependency, or it can encumber one that already exists (§9.3). Third, a proposal about what sorts a dependence: control over its terms, of which an unused fallback is one part (§9.4). Fourth, a model that joins the first and third steps. In it, the terms fall as the fallback decays, and the capacity's deadline becomes a deadline for the terms (§9.5). Fifth, the other ways in which the terms of withdrawal and renegotiation can be controlled: the cost of contesting, the capture of conscientious compensation, and an obligation that bars exit (§9.6, §9.7). Then a test for when dependence becomes presumptive domination, and by whom (§9.8). Two shorter sections take up relied-upon expectations (§9.9) and the pattern the mechanisms share (§9.10). The discussion (§9.11) says what none of this establishes and states the strongest objection.

Two terms fix what the article will not conclude. Exploitation is one party's benefiting from an arrangement on terms that, "as they bear on another party's labour, resources or standing, violate a standard declared with the judgment" (TERM-123). Domination is "an agent's or role's capacity, used or not, to set or change the terms on which the party can act, exit, contest or renegotiate, where the capacity is not answerable to the party under a standard declared with the judgment" (TERM-124). Both terms are proposed, and both make the standard part of the verdict. A mechanism can show how terms come to be set by one party. It cannot show, by itself, that they are set wrongly.

### 9.2 Dependence produced by successful provision

**Supply that does the work.** Consider a party whose capacity C is learned by doing and decays with disuse, dC/dt = λP(1 − C) − μC, where P is the work the party does itself. Outside supply that does the work "first lowers the capacity kept, and the longer it does all of it the longer recovery takes once it stops, while supply that covers only what the organization cannot yet do leaves its learning untouched, so what decides dependence is the order in which supply and capacity are used, not the amount of supply" (CLAIM-602). In that model, capacity settles at "0.625 at A = 0.5 and 0.25 at A = 0.9", where A is the share of the work supplied, against 0.769 with no supply. After full substitution, demand is met again "only after 3.84, 6.38 and 11.38 units for substitution lasting 10, 20 and 40". The service is whole the entire time: "Service is whole throughout, so nothing looks wrong while the supply continues" (CLAIM-602).

**A deadline.** Suppose growth also needs capacity already present, dC/dt = λPC(1 − C) − μC. Then "C below 0.276 decays to nothing", and support that does all the work has a deadline: "after ln(0.7/0.276)/0.3 = 3.10 time units capacity has crossed the threshold, and withdrawal no longer restores it" (CLAIM-605, resting on CLAIM-602 and CLAIM-604). A slow withdrawal is worse than a fast one. From C = 0.45, under support doing 95% of the work, "any taper longer than 9.13 periods traps the party", and "A gentler withdrawal is the riskier one". Capacity-first support "needs no taper, since it withdraws itself, but it cannot lift a party already below the threshold" (CLAIM-605). A restriction that removes the doing, rather than a supply that replaces it, reaches the same kind of deadline: 1.98 time units in its model (CLAIM-950; CASE-364), where "a restriction justified by a lack of competence lowers the competence it waits for" (CLAIM-721). Every one of these numbers comes from a model with invented parameters. CLAIM-605 says so itself: "The threshold, the deadline and the longest safe taper all depend on invented parameters".

**Trajectories, not present sets.** One consequence is a rule of assessment: "support, or a technology, cannot be ranked by present capability sets and has to be assessed by the capability trajectory it produces" (CLAIM-675; "C = 0.156 against 0.7 at t = 5"). Its source draws the limit itself: the model is "not empirical evidence that a particular technology produces such decline" (CLAIM-675). Another consequence is a boundary. Where every replacement part comes from outside, an organization "can become independent of what founded it but never of what feeds it" (CLAIM-597). Dependence on upkeep and dependence on founding are different things, and the substitution mechanism is held beside that boundary (CLAIM-597, which complements CLAIM-602).

**What the field readings add.** The models are not the only evidence. In three Food for Peace projects in Kenya, followed two to three years after exit, "impact at exit did not necessarily predict sustained benefit". In the authors' reading, activities that ran on project-supplied inputs to the end declined, and those that had their own resource stream and capacity, and had worked independently for a while, held (Coates et al. 2016; THEORY-1492). The study has no comparison group and confounds drought with the project, so it corroborates the mechanism without measuring it. Ostrom and colleagues hold that "predictable aid weakens the recipient's own upkeep", on the pattern of the Samaritan's dilemma (Ostrom et al. 2002; THEORY-1480). Their evidence is "five short illustrative field cases with no counterfactual". In the other direction, a structured review of cash transfers finds that transfers without an employment focus leave prime-age adults' work and earnings little changed. The authors expect responses to differ where transfers are dependable and permanent, which is the case the substitution mechanism concerns and the one they leave untested (Baird, McKenzie and Özler 2018; THEORY-1466). A broad reading of aid as eroding upkeep therefore needs the qualifier the models already carry. It applies to supply that does the work, not to supply as such. The conditions are drawn together in one held statement. Transient support can be withdrawn safely only if it started a self-sustaining process, carried the party past any threshold, outlasted the threat, and "left the work to the party throughout". Even then, "meeting them all is not enough" (CLAIM-600).

**Across domains.** Readings in other domains show the same shape. In microbial communities, losing a costly function whose product leaks to neighbours is "favoured by ordinary individual selection in large free-living microbial populations", and the loss stops "until the remaining producers just supply the community" (Morris, Lenski and Zinser 2012; THEORY-1048). In one model of incipient endosymbiosis, mutual dependence evolves ahead of reproductive cohesion, "but only under the mapping" that the authors' own appendix qualifies (Athreya, Czuppon and Gokhale 2025; THEORY-1050). For symbioses generally, which properties each route to dependence brings about "is open" (SYNTHESIS-053). In engineering, services at Google added dependencies on the global instance of the Chubby lock service on the assumption that it would never go down, so its rare outages reached users (CASE-tmp3xxqg). The general form is that "A provider whose realized reliability exceeds its declared guarantee can lower the resilience of the organization built on it: those who depend on it stop maintaining the fallbacks" (CLAIM-tmpb1wyc). Behind it is the point that an expectation can be structural, "realized in a capacity it has stopped maintaining" (CLAIM-tmp18v1o).

The step that joins these readings is a proposal: "A capability lost because another reliably supplies it is the Black Queen's loss ... and whether it is dependency or division of labour is decided by the supply, not the loss" (CLAIM-603). It is held back in two ways. Its title should be narrowed to the organizational case it concedes, and the thesis by which it reads Moore is not held (CLAIM-603, note of 2026-11-06). The transient-support synthesis declines the strong reading: it does not say "That aid recipients are like microbes in mechanism" (SYNTHESIS-032). So the cross-domain account is this. One mechanism is demonstrated in models and corroborated in part by field readings. Kindred losses appear in several domains. The claim that they are one phenomenon is proposed. The standard that would tell destructive lock-in from productive specialization is itself an open question (QUESTION-526), and so is when a partial organization becomes independent of the intervention that established it (QUESTION-398).

**The capacity to govern.** The same mechanism reaches the capacity to correct the arrangement. Where the capacities a collective needs in order to govern an arrangement are learned by exercise, and the arrangement does the work through which they are exercised, "the arrangement can erode the capacity to govern it" (CLAIM-673, resting on CLAIM-605). This matters for §9.8. The dependent's means of challenge can decay by the same route as its means of supply.

### 9.3 Producing a dependency, and encumbering one

Two ways in which an arrangement can exploit through dependence need to be kept apart.

**Producing it.** "An arrangement can produce the dependence through which it exploits: by reliably supplying what a party then ceases to supply for itself, it creates a reliance on the provider that the party did not have before, and the provider can use that reliance to set terms the party could earlier have refused" (CLAIM-670, resting on CLAIM-603). This is the route of §9.2, with a provider who can use the result. A second route to a produced dependence is hold-up. Here the party's own relationship-specific investment, often invited by the provider, makes leaving costly, and "the provider supplies nothing new" (CLAIM-670, note of 2026-11-17). In the incomplete-contracts literature, the right to decide what a contract leaves out belongs to whoever owns the relevant assets, and allocating it moves bargaining power and so the incentive to invest (Hart 2016; THEORY-1979; Grossman and Hart 1986; THEORY-1985). That is the specific investment through which hold-up works. CLAIM-670 as a whole has no reading that supports its own route. It stands as an interpretive claim.

**Encumbering it.** "An arrangement can exploit through the access it grants: a needed good offered on terms that leave the recipient with a lasting encumbrance ... that narrows later exit or renegotiation" (CLAIM-669). Its one reading is Seamster and Charron-Chénier's "predatory inclusion", in which "the need predates the access, the encumbrance is debt, and exit is closed by bankruptcy law" (CLAIM-669).

**The baseline.** In both routes, freedom has to be measured against something. "A technology can become practically compulsory without any rule requiring it", so "the freedom an arrangement leaves is measured by what remains open to a non-adopter" (CLAIM-677). And "the present distribution of options is not a neutral baseline, and an actor cannot justify its terms by the narrowness of options it helped narrow without justification" (CLAIM-747, resting on CLAIM-670).

**Why the encumbrance is hard to see.** An observed figure can merge the two cases the distinction separates. Suppose retention is a logistic function of quality, less the alternative's value, plus the cost of switching. Then "a service of quality 80 that costs nothing to leave and one of quality 20 that costs 60 to leave keep the same 63.5 percent of their customers, so the retention figure merges satisfaction with lock-in" (CASE-tmp3so1p). The construction is stipulated. Its point is that the number an organization watches can be the same whether the parties stay because they are served or because they are held.

**The bargaining form.** In a symmetric Nash bargain, "As B's relationship-specific investment lowers o_B, B's share falls by half of each unit lost". The source draws the conclusion: "The parties may continue to consent to the terms. But what they can afford to refuse has changed". It also states the limit: "This establishes bargaining vulnerability, not by itself exploitation or injustice" (CLAIM-670, note of 2026-11-24). In that model the outside option o_B is lowered by hand. Section 9.5 lets it fall by the dynamics of §9.2.

### 9.4 Control over the terms

**The thesis.** The article's central proposal is that "the same magnitude of dependence can run either way, and what sorts it is who controls the terms on which it is met, exit, renegotiation and the conditions of supply, while the magnitude and the integration that produced it set only how much rides on it" (CLAIM-690). It rests on an older and more general claim. "Material interdependence couples parties' outcomes without fixing the perspective that organizes the coupling: the same dependence can be organized as mutual opportunity, as competitive vulnerability or as coercive leverage" (CLAIM-428). The proposal is interpretive, and its ground is a fictional contrast. The argument from it says of itself that "that magnitude never sorts a dependence is not shown" (ARG-110).

**The contrast.** Two workshops have surplus S = 10, the same dependence each way, the same integration and the same membership (CASE-264). In workshop A, the part P can withdraw and renegotiate, and the symmetric Nash solution gives P "a_P + (S − a_P − a_R)/2 = 0 + (10 − 0 − 2)/2 = 4". In workshop B, the other party R sets P's terms and P cannot leave: "R pays the least that keeps P working, r = 1, and keeps 9". The change in P's share per unit of the whole's need is 1/2 in A and 0 in B. In B, R can put P's payoff anywhere "from 1 to 8, a width of 7". The case lists what it does not show: "That the numbers are the world's", and "That B is exploitation" (CASE-264).

**Not reciprocity.** It is tempting to make mutual dependence the remedy. But "reciprocal dependence is neither sufficient for freedom nor sufficient for justice", because "mutual dependence of outcomes is not mutual control of terms" (CLAIM-603, note of 2026-11-12). The article puts control, not reciprocity, at the centre.

**A fallback that works unused.** One element of control is a fallback. "A fallback capacity can matter without being used: it keeps a party able to refuse, and so changes the terms others offer" (CLAIM-tmp2jhc1). The bargaining step is textbook. With joint value V and outside options o_A and o_B, u_B = (V − o_A + o_B)/2. So "each unit of B's outside option raises B's share by half a unit, and lowers A's by the same, whether or not B ever takes the outside option" (CLAIM-tmp2jhc1). The claim separates redundancy that keeps performance when a component fails from redundancy that keeps credible alternatives within a governing relationship. It warns that "optimizing away capacity that ordinary operation never exercises can remove a condition of non-domination". It also holds the converse as comparative statics. In a model in which specialization s raises joint production (10 + 16s) while lowering B's independent capacity (5(1 − s(1 − p)), where p protects B's fallback), "At full specialization and no protection, the split is 14.75 to 11.25 in A's favour; with full protection, 12.25 to 13.75" (CLAIM-tmp2jhc1). So it is already held that an arrangement which erodes a party's fallback lowers the party's terms. What is not held in that model is time: how the erosion unfolds, whether it has a point of no return, and whether it can be reversed. The claim's own limit also stands: a fallback secures only exit among CLAIM-690's three, so a party with a fallback is not thereby undominated. In a different literature, where people can choose their partners and have equal outside options, the stable division of gains is proportional to contribution (Baumard, André and Sperber 2013; THEORY-457). Its result is conditioned on the outside options being equal, which is the condition the cases here vary.

**Residual control.** A second element is discretion over what the terms leave out. "A relationship fair in every specified term can still leave one party's terms at the other's discretion, and that discretion is exercised, and seen, only when an uncovered contingency arises" (CLAIM-728). The economics of residual control rights corroborates the first clause (Hart 2016; THEORY-1979; Grossman and Hart 1986; THEORY-1985). Read against those works, though, the argument for the claim's timing clause fails. The readings "tell against step 3", "locating the effect of residual control ex ante, through anticipation" (ARG-210). Two revisions are open. One widens the timing, at the cost that the claim "no longer separates cleanly from an ordinary account of anticipated bargaining power". The other keeps the timing and narrows the claim to "the visible exercise of residual authority" (CLAIM-728, note of 2026-11-22). This article uses residual control only as one form that control over the terms can take, and it does not need either revision.

### 9.5 Terms that move as the fallback decays

The pieces are now in place. A fallback raises terms whether or not it is used (§9.4). An arrangement that erodes it lowers them, as comparative statics (§9.4). A capacity learned by doing decays under supply that does the work, with a deadline (§9.2). This section joins them in time (CLAIM-tmp6nzs1). The model is the author's computation, with parameters taken from CLAIM-605 and CASE-264 and three more invented for it. Its qualitative clauses follow from a short derivation, given below. The computation checks the derivation at one set of parameters and adds the shape of the fall.

**The model.** P's capacity follows CLAIM-605's model with its parameters (λ = 1, μ = 0.3, τ = 1.5). Under full substitution, C(t) = 0.7e^(−0.3t), which crosses the threshold 0.2764 at the deadline 3.098. P's capacity is its self-supply, its ability to meet its own demand alone. That is distinct from its contribution to the joint value, which is why S and R's outside option are held fixed. The bargain is CASE-264's workshop A: S = 10, o_R = 2, and P's share u_P = o_P + (S − o_P − o_R)/2. P's outside option o_P(t) is what P could secure alone from its capacity at t. It is valued forward, as w times the annuitized, discounted value of P's stand-alone output min(τC_s, 1), with C_s following the unsupported dynamics from C(t). The invented parameters are w = 4, a discount rate r = 0.25 and a horizon of 80. A second valuation, current output only (o_P = w·min(τC, 1)), is reported beside the first. The split is re-solved against the current fallback at every t. That is an assumption of the model: it supposes renegotiation. What stays fixed is every price, the bargaining rule, and P's choice to stay.

**The derivation.** (1) Under full substitution, C(t) = 0.7e^(−0.3t) and falls strictly (CLAIM-605). (2) With supply removed, the dynamics are scalar and autonomous, with a Lipschitz right-hand side. So paths from ordered starts stay ordered, and P's stand-alone output path is nondecreasing in the capacity it starts from. Any valuation that is nondecreasing in that path, as both valuations here are, makes o_P nondecreasing in C(t). (3) P's share is increasing in o_P, both under Nash and under take-it-or-leave-it. So P's terms are nonincreasing in t while P never exits. (4) Once supply ends at T, o_P depends only on C(T). If C(T) is above the threshold (T < 3.098), capacity returns to 0.7 and o_P to w. If it is below, capacity and o_P go to zero. So the terms can be restored, asymptotically, exactly when the supply ends before the deadline. (5) Where P cannot exit (CASE-264's workshop B), o_P does not enter u_P. Steps 1 and 5, and the half-unit rate, are held already. Steps 2 to 4 are the new content (CLAIM-tmp6nzs1).

**Expectation and falsifier.** The expectation, stated before the run, was this. P's share falls as C decays, and the fall concentrates at CLAIM-605's deadline, because a fallback that will recover is worth far more than one that will not. The falsifier that discriminates is a comparison with the model that has no threshold. If the fall concentrates at t ≈ 3.1 in CLAIM-605's model but not in CLAIM-602's, which has no threshold, the concentration comes from the threshold. If it concentrates in both, it comes from the pipeline.

**Results.** All figures are at r = 0.25 unless stated (CLAIM-tmp6nzs1).

*The terms move with no exit and no price, rule or exit option changed.* P's share falls from 6.000 to 4.199 over six time units, and R's rises by the same amount. Each unit of fallback lost moves half a unit to R, which is CLAIM-tmp2jhc1's threat-point property run on a fallback that the supply itself erodes.

*The fall is steepest at the deadline, and it is a step only for a patient party.* At r = 0.25 the largest fall per interval of 0.05 is in the interval centred at t = 3.075, against a deadline of 3.098. A third of the fall by t = 6 (33%) lies within half a unit of the deadline. How step-like the fall is depends on the discount rate. Within half a unit of the deadline lie 85% of the fall at r = 0.02, 73% at 0.05, 56% at 0.1 and 22% at 0.5. At r = 0.5 the steepest interval moves to t = 2.275. The expectation holds for where the fall is steepest, for r ≤ 0.25. It fails as a step except for a patient party.

*Before the deadline the terms can be restored, and after it they cannot.* Suppose the supply ends and P waits 20 units. If the supply ended at T = 2.0 or 3.0, P's fallback returns toward 4 (o_P = 4.000 and 3.916). If it ended at T = 3.5 or 5.0, the fallback stays at 0.041 and 0.003, and P's share stays near 4, which is workshop A with nothing to fall back on. In this model, CLAIM-605's deadline for withdrawal is also a deadline for the terms.

*Valuing the fallback by current output misses the deadline.* Once τC < 1, the current-output valuation leaves P less than the forward valuation does before the deadline, and more after it. The two cross where C passes the threshold. The current-output fall is steepest at the start (t = 0.225).

**Controls.** Under capacity-first supply from the kept state, capacity, fallback and share stay at 0.7, 4 and 6.000. There is nothing to stop, because in CLAIM-605's model capacity-first supply is just the unsupported dynamics. A party already below the threshold decays under it anyway, and its terms fall. Under partial substitution below CLAIM-605's a* = 0.585 (A = 0.5), capacity settles at 0.400, above the threshold, and the share at 5.535, with no cliff. Run through the same pipeline, CLAIM-602's model, which has no threshold, gives a smooth fall from 6.000 to 5.547, with a largest fall per 0.05 of 0.005 against 0.042. The falsifier is therefore not met: the concentration comes from CLAIM-605's threshold, not from the pipeline. In workshop B, where P cannot leave, P gets 1 whatever its fallback is worth, so the decay moves nothing. That holds by construction, and it is CLAIM-690's point in the dynamic case: whether the decay matters at all depends on control over exit. Under take-it-or-leave-it with exit, R offers P exactly its fallback, so P bears the whole fall, from 4.000 to 0.399, twice the Nash fall (CLAIM-tmp6nzs1).

**What the result answers, and on which reading.** The question was whether a shrinking solo reach, "not a price, moves the terms the other can offer" (LEAD-tmpeo8wq). That depends on how a decline in capacity is classified, and two readings are open (LEAD-tmpeo8wq, note of 2026-11-24). On the first, capacity is the actor's state and transition costs are fixed functions of it. Then no price changes, the terms move on either valuation (to 4.199 forward, to 4.347 on current output), and the answer is yes, for a scalar capacity. The deadline shows in the terms only on the forward valuation. On the second reading, what it costs a party to reach a configuration includes rebuilding the capacity. Then the same decline is a rise in the party's costs, and the lead's own null applies: the terms still move, but by what counts as a price. The forward valuation is not this second reading. It counts the capacity's natural recovery, not the cost of rebuilding it. The two headline results therefore rest on the first reading together with the forward valuation. Which reading is the right one is open (CLAIM-tmp6nzs1).

**What it does not show.** First, it does not show terms the party could earlier have refused. In the Nash arm, u_P = 4 + o_P/2, which is at least 4 = o_P(0). P's share never falls below what it could have had alone at the start (4.199 at t = 6, against 4.000), so P never receives terms it would have refused at t = 0. What the Nash arm models is a falling floor: "what they can afford to refuse has changed" (CLAIM-670, note of 2026-11-24). Terms P could earlier have refused appear only under take-it-or-leave-it, or in the Nash arm for w > (S − o_R)/2 = 4. At w = 4 the parameters sit exactly on that boundary (CLAIM-tmp6nzs1). CLAIM-670's title states more than this model shows. Second, it shows neither intent nor gain. The supply is exogenous, and whether R would choose to supply past the deadline once its cost of supplying is counted is an open lead (LEAD-tmpfzx2c). Third, it shows neither exploitation nor domination, since no standard is declared. What it shows is a bargaining vulnerability, produced by supply (CLAIM-670, note of 2026-11-24). Fourth, it says nothing about reach indexed by actor and authority, or about the four predicates on a transition (TERM-114; LEAD-tmpeo8wq). It is a scalar capacity and a scalar split, with numbers that are not the world's.

### 9.6 The cost of contesting

The exit channel is one way of controlling the terms. The cost of contesting them is another, and it can move separately. "The cost of imposing a decision and the cost of questioning, suspending or reversing it are separate, so an institution can lower the first while the second stays the same or rises" (CLAIM-731).

**Siege.** "an actor can make a legally recognized corrective objective unreachable for another party, with the merits, the law and that party's preferences unchanged, by raising what pursuing the objective costs, in spending or in time, above what that party can sustain until relief arrives" (CLAIM-748). Its ground is a fictional construction. In it, a claimant "nets 60 if judgment takes four months and nothing if the defendant can stretch it to seven", and "halving the claimant's runway from eight months to four cuts its chance of relief from 86 to 63 per cent" (CASE-287). Whether using such a strategy is wrongful is open (QUESTION-479). The runway has a demonstrated kin. "A savings buffer is a stock of time": where shocks must be absorbed or a household is ruined, households with the same preferences decline profitable delayed investments below a buffer that rises with the delay, "a choice that reads as impatience and is the right policy for survival by a deadline" (CLAIM-601). A claimant outlasted in court and a household that declines a delayed investment may be in the same position, each lacking the time a deeper reserve would buy; no entry states that link, and it is left open here. The buffer is held beside patience as an instrument of control: "a controller not answerable for delivery can make that party's patience a means of its control" (CLAIM-744, which CLAIM-601 complements).

**Leverage from a breach.** "the breaching party gains leverage over the resolution of its own breach" (CLAIM-716; "B accepts 25 against 20").

**Speed.** Before its run, the criterion was set: "Oversight that loses effect as speed rises, with authority and resources fixed, supports CLAIM-710 and CLAIM-464; no effect of speed defeats them" (LEAD-068). In a queueing model whose overseer keeps the same authority, information and reviewers at every speed, "at ten times the speed the share of objectionable actions stopped before irreversibility fell from 0.994 to 0.396" (CLAIM-879; CASE-316). The criterion is met in the model. The model builds in the timing condition, so it adds the loss's shape and a comparison of remedies, not evidence about institutions (LEAD-068, note of 2026-11-22).

These are costs of contesting the terms. Section 9.5 modelled the cost of leaving them. No model yet puts the two together, a claimant's runway beside a capacity that erodes under supply, so that both costs move as the supply continues (LEAD-tmp3w42f).

### 9.7 Conscientiousness, commitment and the hidden subsidy

A party's moral reliability gives a controller a further channel. It is built on one distinction. "One party can wrongfully bring about circumstances in which another incurs a genuine obligation, so that the second does right in discharging it and is still wronged", and "the obligation so created is no larger than the circumstances support" (CLAIM-703). Keller argues that a person can wrongfully place another under a genuine moral obligation, by making every alternative to what he wants morally unacceptable. He also argues that fiduciary roles expose doctors, nurses, teachers and lawyers to this "moral blackmail", because they predictably generate obligations that exceed and outlast the fiduciary duty (Keller 2016; THEORY-1917). The reading is an argument by cases, and it corroborates CLAIM-703.

**Commitment as a resource.** "A moral commitment is exploitable as a resource: because a committed party reliably acts on it, whoever controls the circumstances that trigger it can capture what its fulfilment yields and leave its costs with that party", "with the burden falling hardest on its most conscientious members" (CLAIM-713, resting on CLAIM-703). Its defeater ends: "the claim is a mechanism, not a frequency". A matched construction holds three hospitals with "the same output, budget, staff competences and commitments", and "The verdicts are stipulated" (CASE-288).

**The hidden subsidy.** Suppose conscientious compensation covers an organization's shortfall, and the organization budgets to what it observes. Then "the compensation and the quiet it buys are read as adequacy, resources are cut toward what the present configuration needs", and the reconfigurations that would remove the shortfall "pass out of reach while the present arrangement stays feasible" (CLAIM-700). The claim's links are each held: the implicit moral subsidy (CLAIM-713); quiet that is not satisfaction (CLAIM-549); and a cut toward present need that "removes exactly the transitions whose threshold lies between the old holding and the new" (CLAIM-679). What the claim adds is the coupling: the subsidy's invisibility is what feeds the budget rule. A case separates the claim from its premises. Under "a statutory nurse-to-patient ratio", "no rule budgets to what is observed, so no cut follows and the loop does not close" (CLAIM-700).

Half of this loop has been run. The study CLAIM-700 proposes is to "Construct two institutions with identical obligations and equivalent present performance", one with reconfiguration slack and one without, and introduce a failure that needs a transition. A matched-performance model does this without the compensator. In it, "every regime produces the same output until a contingency arrives, while readiness at a deadline of six periods ranges from 0.147 to 0.894" (CASE-412). Where competence decays without funded maintenance, "a budget that follows observed use has a state in which the capability is never used or funded again" whenever the base budget alone sustains competence below the level of commitment. Maintenance funded apart from use has no such state (CLAIM-921; CASE-343). A budget set to last period's peak never rises (CLAIM-577). A redesigned route is lost under that rule unless its threshold is at most the cost of holding (CLAIM-1099), and a reserve keeps every transition whose threshold it covers, where a redesign keeps only one (CLAIM-1180). So the budget-to-observed-use half of CLAIM-700, and the matched-performance contrast, are executed (CASE-412, note of 2026-11-25). The coupling has not been run: compensation masking the shortfall, so that the rule reads it as adequacy. If it is run, the claim's expectation is that, "with budgets set from observed performance, the first loses reachable reconfigurations faster than the second, at the same nominal performance". Its falsifier is "such organizations keeping or raising reserves" (CLAIM-700). One lead advances the claim by measuring a fall in complaints with working conditions fixed (LEAD-022; CASE-290). Another includes an unaccounted-subsidy regime in a multi-agent environment (LEAD-tmp5l71z). The claim's measurement link, that "The system's failure is attributed to the person who stopped compensating for it", is a further proposal (CLAIM-702). Of the readings in the account of moral exploitation, none corroborates CLAIM-700 or CLAIM-702 (SYNTHESIS-095; SYNTHESIS-099).

**What is saved, and by whom.** Cooperation saves the work of monitoring and verifying, and that saving "can be captured asymmetrically", "under the same visible harmony as a relationship that shares it" (CLAIM-tmp8bimm). A general caution bears on it: "work saved, work transferred and valuable capability expanded are three separate achievements", and the efficiency an organization appears to have depends on the boundary of the system whose costs are counted (CLAIM-760). A subsidy hidden in conscientiousness looks like work transferred and counted as saved. No entry states that reading of CLAIM-700, and the article leaves it as a suggestion.

**Value against a bar.** There are two ways in which the terms of withdrawal can be controlled, and they should not be merged. The question is posed in general form: "Can a party's ability to activate another's genuine moral obligations constitute control over the terms of that other's dependence ... and if so, what separates it from ordinary reliance on someone's conscientiousness?" (QUESTION-472). Keller's fiduciaries are evidence toward yes, for fiduciaries (QUESTION-472, note of 2026-11-17). The first half has a proposed answer. "A genuine obligation enters a cost model of exit as a condition on which transitions are permissible, not as a cost", so "a party who controls the circumstances that keep the restriction in place controls the other's exit without raising its price" (CLAIM-tmpdu9v6). Section 9.5's channel works through the value of the disagreement point: the fallback's worth, with no price changed. CLAIM-tmpdu9v6's channel works through a bar on permissibility. When the article says the costs of withdrawal are transformed, that covers the first channel. The second is not a cost at all. The second half of the question, what separates such leverage from ordinary reliance on someone's conscientiousness, has no answer yet (QUESTION-472).

### 9.8 Domination, and who holds it

**The test.** "A dependence becomes presumptively dominating by those who control it when three conditions coincide". The conditions are an important sphere; "consequential discretion over the dependent"; and that the dependent "lacks effective means of protection, influence, challenge or justified withdrawal". The test has a stated scope: "this is a test for domination by an arrangement's controllers, not for every unfreedom, and impersonal constraint that no one holds is outside it" (CLAIM-682). The three conditions together raise a presumption. None of them is a definition, and none alone is sufficient.

**The third condition needs exit to fail.** Failures of legibility, of volitional efficacy or of correctability for the dependent's claims entail a lack of influence and challenge. But "justified withdrawal lies outside those three achievements, so the domination threshold's third condition is met by failures of normative efficacy only together with a failure of exit" (CLAIM-686). The point rests on the thesis that the three achievements are needed for normative efficacy (CLAIM-523), and "the converse does not follow" (CLAIM-686). A reading of online platforms illustrates the gap. Platforms make exit and "affective" voice cheap, while "effective" voice, speech that binds by transparent process, is nearly absent under consolidated control (Frey and Schneider 2020; THEORY-1918). Hirschman's own restatement of exit and voice adds that managers may favour whichever response least threatens their hold on power (Hirschman 1974; THEORY-152). And Pettit's account of popular control makes "individualized, unconditioned and efficacious" influence the condition of legitimacy, which is one way of reading what CLAIM-682's "influence" and "challenge" would have to be (Pettit 2012; THEORY-1960).

Section 9.5 bears on the exit half, but not by settling it. In its workshop A, P can still exit at every moment. What decays is what exit is worth (o_P falls to 0.399 by t = 6). Whether a withdrawal that is open but worth almost nothing counts as a lack of "effective means of ... justified withdrawal" is a further step, and the article leaves it open. The capacity to govern can decay too (CLAIM-673), so the dependent's means of challenge and its means of exit may weaken together; no model yet runs both.

**Before the threshold.** Something is owed even before domination is presumed. "The accountability that those who control an arrangement owe to those who depend on it should rise with how indispensable the arrangement has become to them" (CLAIM-687). The open question is the other side of this: when an organization comes to depend on participants it subordinates, under what conditions does that dependence raise their effective authority over it, and under what conditions does it reproduce arrangements that keep their contributions "from becoming effective claims on it" (QUESTION-461)?

**Who holds it.** The test and the term both need a holder. TERM-124 says: "Its holder must be an agent or role; an arrangement with no controller can constrain but does not dominate in this sense". CLAIM-682 was narrowed for this reason, since some accounts call complete constraint with no one holding discretion domination (CLAIM-682, after ARG-109). This matters for any political economy of platforms. Three mechanisms need to be told apart:

- CLAIM-677's practical compulsion arises "without any rule requiring it". It may lack a holder, and a case of it may fall outside the test.
- CLAIM-tmpjnly0's erosion of the constraints on extraction "can be produced by the optimization itself, with no change in the character of those who run it". But the institution in that claim chooses its actions, and those actions change the state that disciplines it. So it holds a capacity to set or change the terms, which is TERM-124's holder. "No change in character" means no intent, and the term does not require intent. What is missing before such a case can be judged domination is a declared standard of answerability, and an examination of CLAIM-682's three conditions for some actual arrangement.
- CLAIM-652's exit-driven drift: an organization that weighs a practice by the failures its present users report "can expand the practice with no act against complaint", because "as the harmed leave, the recognized cost of the practice falls with their share" (CLAIM-652). In a toy run, the practice rises from 0.05 to 0.223 as the harmed group's share falls from 0.30 to 0.18 (CASE-246). An organization sets the practice; what the mechanism lacks is any act against complaint. Whether that organization is a holder in TERM-124's sense no entry examines. The mechanism can be read as an exit-side counterpart of §9.5, in which exit by the harmed, rather than a decaying fallback, worsens the terms for those who remain; that reading is the article's suggestion and is not tested.

So enshittification, read as domination, is not established here. The reason is not that its mechanisms lack a holder: CLAIM-tmpjnly0's has one. The reason is that no standard of answerability has been declared, and the presumption test has been applied to no platform.

### 9.9 Relied-upon expectations

Exploitation can also be tied to the expectations that cooperation leaves in place. Two readings of that connection are held, as alternatives, and the article does not choose between them. On one, "relied-upon expectations create opportunities for asymmetric relationships, which exploitation takes up" (CLAIM-tmp05a1l). On the other, "Exploitation can expose, in the sense of reveal, the relied-upon expectations an implicit contract kept tacit" (CLAIM-tmppx64t). The first reading fits this article's opening. A dependence produced by cooperation is one kind of relied-upon expectation, and an implicit contract's obligation "can be held by that reliance past the provider's original reasons for the contribution, where the provider cannot cheaply exit" (CLAIM-tmpu77ve; Article 3). The second reading fits §9.7's hidden subsidy, where what exploitation reveals is a compensation no one had counted.

### 9.10 What the mechanisms share

The mechanisms have one feature in common. In none of them does the harm show in ordinary performance. Under substitutive supply, "Service is whole throughout" (CLAIM-602), and in §9.5 the terms fall while the party stays and every price holds. In CASE-412, every regime "produces the same output until a contingency arrives". A retention figure merges satisfaction with lock-in (CASE-tmp3so1p). A provider more reliable than it promised makes the rare failure worse (CLAIM-tmpb1wyc). That pattern is itself held as an integration of the mechanisms. "reliable provision, cooperation that works and optimization that meets its objective can each produce" pathologies of organized agency, "through eroded fallbacks, captured regulatory work and standing excluded from what is optimized, so in these mechanisms the pathology can deepen while ordinary performance holds or improves, and restoring performance does not reach it" (CLAIM-tmpv5sry, resting on CLAIM-tmpb1wyc, CLAIM-670, CLAIM-tmp8bimm, CLAIM-700, CLAIM-1054 and CLAIM-tmpjnly0). The methodological consequence is the one the series' plan draws: "Match ordinary performance; perturb the governing relationships; observe the divergence in organizational trajectories." Section 9.5's reversal test is an instance, and so is CASE-412's contingency.

This is also how the article meets the demand that the mechanisms be connected to "the resource-bounded accessibility of institutional transformation". A cut toward what the present configuration needs removes the transitions whose threshold lies above it (CLAIM-679). A deadline in capacity becomes a deadline in the terms (CLAIM-tmp6nzs1). And the duty to help establish a needed arrangement is bounded by whether it can be reached "by permissible, resource-bounded transitions" (CLAIM-729). Read together, these suggest that what is reachable, and by whom, is the variable the mechanisms share; the article offers that as a reading, and no entry states it.

### 9.11 Discussion: what the argument does not establish

**No verdicts.** The argument shows how dependence is produced, how it moves the terms, and what would make it presumptively dominating. It does not show that any arrangement is exploitative or dominating, and by design it cannot. TERM-123 and TERM-124 make the standard part of the verdict, and CASE-264 and CASE-288 decline to give one. The record's position that dependence, inequality and integration are not by themselves evidence of wrongdoing is held in proposed entries (TERM-123; TERM-124; CASE-264; CLAIM-682). Three non-equivalences follow, each in its model or case. Dependence does not by itself establish domination: equal dependence can leave the part a hold on its terms or none (CASE-264), and a failure of exit is needed as well (CLAIM-686). A bargaining disadvantage does not by itself establish exploitation (CLAIM-670, note of 2026-11-24). Mutual dependence of outcomes is not mutual control of terms (CLAIM-603, note of 2026-11-12). The normative questions stay open. Which declared standard supplies the verdict (QUESTION-468)? What makes the terms of an organization's sustaining activities unjust (QUESTION-463)? Does an organized whole ground norms about its parts, and who bears them (QUESTION-460)? These are questions for philosophical argument, not for more models.

**Models, mostly.** The demonstrated results hold in models with invented parameters. The bargaining contrast is fictional, and §9.5 inherits both. The field readings corroborate the substitution mechanism and qualify its reach (THEORY-1492; THEORY-1466; THEORY-1480), but none of them measures it. The theory of the firm and of non-domination is read, in Hart, Grossman and Hart, Pettit's later book, Hirschman's restatement, and Frey and Schneider (THEORY-1979; THEORY-1985; THEORY-1960; THEORY-152; THEORY-1918). Several founding works are not read: Klein, Crawford and Alchian on appropriable rents, Hart and Moore on property rights and the firm, Pettit's *Republicanism*, and Hirschman's *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty* itself. A lead holds the reading still to be done (LEAD-133). The comparative evidence that would say how often these mechanisms produce exploitation is not gathered. The readings of platform practices are proposed and wait on a cross-platform panel (THEORY-150; THEORY-470). The leads that would collect such evidence are not run: two theatres with different labour arrangements, "to tell exploitation from dependence and from domination" (LEAD-153); withdrawal episodes in the transient-support primaries (LEAD-025); and institutions with and without effective means to revise their governing standards (LEAD-129). Two research families named for this article define the experiments that would test the developmental thesis directly: "Contract learning, amortization and alternative erosion", and "Dependency-producing extraction and normative exclusion". The first is done in part, in §9.5 and in the comparative statics of §9.4. The second is done in part in §9.7's matched-performance runs and in the exit-driven drift of §9.8. Neither has an endogenous version, in which implicit contracts form among agents and then govern them (LEAD-tmp5l71z).

**Untested mechanisms.** CLAIM-603's three-arm test, which compares capability trajectories under withdrawal, is proposed and not run (LEAD-166 for co-cultures; LEAD-197 for assisted learners). Its expectation is stated in advance: "During supply, one-way: C falls toward what the supply leaves for the recipient to do, with no stop of its own", and "where CLAIM-605's conditions hold there is a duration after which C does not recover". Its falsifier: "A one-way recipient whose C stops falling short of what the supply covers, by its own dynamics" (CLAIM-603, note of 2026-11-13). The provider's side of §9.5 is not computed (LEAD-tmpfzx2c). Without it, the model cannot say whether a provider gains by producing the dependence, which is the step CLAIM-670 takes in prose.

**The strongest objection.** The strongest objection is that the argument repackages bargaining power. On this view, "control over the terms" is just the outside option and the threat point, which bargaining theory has always had. The model of §9.5 then shows only that a party with a worse outside option gets a worse split, which no one doubted. Moreover, it shows this in a Nash arm in which P never falls below what it could have had alone at the start. The objection has force, and the article concedes two parts of it. First, the threat-point property is textbook (CLAIM-tmp2jhc1), and in the Nash arm what falls is a floor, not a set of terms P would once have refused (CLAIM-tmp6nzs1). Second, residual control, read with the economics, may collapse into anticipated bargaining power. That is exactly the cost of one of CLAIM-728's open revisions (ARG-210).

Three things survive the objection. The first is the dynamics. The threat point is static, and what §9.5 adds is that the arrangement's own supply moves it, with a point after which the terms cannot be restored by ending the supply. That point is invisible in the static model. It is also invisible in a current-output valuation of the fallback. The second is the asymmetry of control. CASE-264's workshop B, and §9.5's no-exit control, show the same dependence leaving the dependent no hold on its terms at all. That is a difference in who controls exit, not in the size of the outside option, and it is what CLAIM-690 proposes sorts a dependence. The third is the bar. An obligation that controls exit "without raising its price" (CLAIM-tmpdu9v6) is not a feature of any outside option, and bargaining power does not capture it. What the objection does establish is that the developmental thesis, as distinct from the bargaining arithmetic, rests on the proposed claims CLAIM-690 and CLAIM-670. A reader who rejects them keeps the mechanism and the model and loses the political economy.

### 9.12 Conclusion

Reliable provision can produce dependence, and the order of supply decides whether it does. Supply that does the work erodes the capacity it replaces. Where growth needs capacity already present, it passes a point after which ending the supply no longer restores what was lost. A fallback moves the terms whether or not it is used. So in a model that joins the two, substitutive supply lowers a party's terms while the party stays and every price holds, and the capacity's deadline becomes a deadline for the terms. What cooperation erodes in this way is not visible in its ordinary performance, and the same is true of the hidden subsidy, of captured regulatory work and of exit-driven drift. Whether any of this is exploitation or domination depends on who controls exit, renegotiation and the conditions of supply, and on a standard of answerability declared with the judgment. The article proposes the first and declares none of the second. What remains is the normative argument that would supply a standard, and the comparative evidence that would say how often these mechanisms do what the models show they can.
