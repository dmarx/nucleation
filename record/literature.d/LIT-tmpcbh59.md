# LIT-tmpcbh59: The Broken Connection: On Death and the Continuity of Life

Lifton, R. J. (1979). *The Broken Connection: On Death and the Continuity of Life*. New York: Simon and Schuster.

Not read. The summary is drawn from the abstract or from what the work is
standardly cited for. A reading should check it first.

## Why this is here

The source of the notion of symbolic immortality that terror management theory and the legacy research use. Its biological and creative modes are legacy as a self extended in time. For [QUESTION-tmpruhu4](../questions.d/QUESTION-tmpruhu4.md).

## Read in its place

The work cannot be read here, so the record holds it at second hand.

Sturesteps's thesis ([NOTE-tmpwnl8h](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpwnl8h.md)) gives the five modes and how they were
measured by 1991. It reports four points from the book: that the struggle
for a sense of immortality is a prime human aim, "immortality hunger" (pp.
17, 284); psychic numbing (pp. 144–145); and Lifton's claim that the
nuclear threat undermines the biological, creative and nature modes and
drives interest in experiential transcendence (p. 149). It credits the
biological, afterlife and creative modes to Rank (1930) and only nature
and experiential transcendence to Lifton (1973). Missing still: Lifton's
own account of the modes, including whether the biological mode reaches
beyond one's children to one's people, as the LIT summary says; his
psychology of death imagery; and the claim that the loss of symbolic
immortality underlies psychopathology, which the thesis attributes to
Rank, not Lifton.
