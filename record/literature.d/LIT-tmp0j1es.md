# LIT-tmp0j1es: Disappearing Polymorphs

Jack D. Dunitz and Joel Bernstein (1995), *Accounts of Chemical Research* 28(4), 193–200 — DOI-10.1021/ar00052a005

## Key takeaways

*Filed, not read.* Known here by what it is standardly cited for.

- Once a more stable polymorph has nucleated somewhere, the older form may
  become impossible to obtain again, in the same laboratory and sometimes
  elsewhere.
- The explanation offered is seeding: microscopic nuclei of the new form,
  carried on equipment, in the air or in the material, template every
  later crystallization.
- It is standardly cited for the claim that which form crystallizes
  depends on the seeds present, not only on the conditions.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-11 to ground the narrowed counter, [CLAIM-tmpas8q0](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpas8q0.md). A seed
fixes the lattice, and this is the strongest form of that point: a form
that has once been coded can seed everything after it, so that the earlier
form cannot be grown again. In the record, a framing that has been given a
code is what later turns point at. A reading should check how strongly the
paper states irreversibility, and whether it distinguishes seeding from a
change in the material itself.

Ostwald 1897 ([LIT-tmpabr1n](LIT-tmpabr1n.md), [NOTE-tmpn2e2e](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpn2e2e.md)), read on 2026-10-11, bears on
both checks. It shows seed power spreading through a laboratory as dust
and surviving washing, which is the seeding explanation applied to a
stable form seeding its own solution, not to one polymorph displacing
another. It also shows seeds that do not last: volatile seeds evaporate,
unstable ones decompose, heat sterilises. So irreversibility depends on the
seed being stable, which limits how far "a form once coded seeds
everything after it" can be pushed. Its salol experiment separates loss of
seed power from change in the substance.
