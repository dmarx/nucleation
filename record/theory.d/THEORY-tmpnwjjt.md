# THEORY-tmpnwjjt: Whether a view of mind is organicist or informationalist is a dimension orthogonal to whether it is cognitivist or embodied, and the enactive approach of The Embodied Mind is set apart from other embodied views by organicism with experiential primacy and reciprocity, not by sensorimotor emphasis

## Source

Ward (2026), "What is enactivism?", LIT-tmpldiuy, read in full in
NOTE-tmpl4pbc from the author's accepted manuscript: §§3–4 (the two axes
and Figure 1), §5 (continuity), §6 (primacy and reciprocity), and
footnotes 3, 5 and 6. The manuscript has no page numbers.

## What was actually shown

Ward separates two questions. The first, which the usual taxonomy asks,
is where the processes underlying cognition lie: "skull-bound
computations" or "brain/body/world systems". The second is what the
meaning of mental states "fundamentally depends on": informational
properties, or organizational properties distinctive of living things
(§3). He places views on both (§4, Figure 1):

- Cognitivism (Fodor, Pinker) is skull-bound and informationalist.
- Extended functionalism, wide computationalism, embodied predictive
  processing and cybernetic and sensorimotor views are embodied and
  informationalist. Of predictive processing: bodily and worldly factors
  "are important only insofar as they help implement the hierarchical
  pattern of information flow upon which cognition ultimately depends".
- Cybernetic views appeal to feedback cycles that "can just as well be
  implemented by non-living systems like autonomous drones, or suitably
  constituted and embedded LLMs": properties "characteristic rather than
  distinctive of living systems".
- Organizational approaches in biology, some basal cognition and some
  ecological psychology, and the enactive approach, are embodied and
  organicist.
- The skull-bound organicist quadrant is nearly empty. Biological
  naturalism about consciousness does not automatically belong there,
  since it tends to see biology "only as implementations of the
  computational properties that remain in the explanatory driving seat"
  (fn. 6).

The enactive approach of Varela, Thompson & Rosch is organicist because it
holds that "cognition fundamentally depends on distinctive organizational
properties of living things" (§5). It is set apart from other organicist
views by two more commitments: lived experience as primary explanandum and
evidence, and reciprocity, in which the vocabularies used to understand
ourselves reshape the experience they describe (§§1, 6). The sensorimotor
definition that usually stands for enactivism comes from a passage of The
Embodied Mind which Ward calls "a poor nutshell definition of enactivism"
out of context (fn. 3).

What could have come out otherwise: the views could have lined up, every
embodied view organicist and every cognitivist one informationalist,
making the second axis redundant. Ward's placements show they do not.

## What this does not say

- **It does not say organicism is true.** Whether enactivists are right
  that informationalism leaves a gap "is not something I aim to establish
  here" (§6). Life–mind continuity is sketched and called "complex and
  controversial" (§5).
- **It gives no criterion for "distinctive".** The axis depends on telling
  properties only living things have from properties they share with the
  inorganic. Ward gives examples, not a test.
- **The placements are approximate.** Many theorists leave their stance
  indeterminate (§4), and the weak, methodological reading of organicism
  (§3) is enough for membership.
- **It says nothing about brains in vats, development or current input.**
  The enactive challenge the exchange of 2026-10-21 drew from this paper,
  that an organization held without input need not be one that could arise
  without an embodied history, is not in it.
