# NOTE-tmpdzv60: Social Identity Theory's Self-Esteem Hypothesis: A Review and Some Suggestions for Clarification

## Contribution

The first review since Abrams and Hogg (1988) to take old and new
evidence on the self-esteem hypothesis together. It gives a taxonomy of
self-esteem, eight types from three distinctions, and uses it to sort the
evidence. It concludes that the hypothesis as stated is not supported, and
argues that the fault lies in its formulation and in measures that do not
fit it. It ends with a reformulation and a list of what a fair test would
need.

## Key insight

The self-esteem in question is a group member's, not an individual's.
Social identity theory says members discriminate to make their group
positively distinct, and so are "striving for positive self-esteem as
ingroup members, rather than as individuals" (pp. 5–6). To test it with
personal self-esteem "would be to recast social identity theory as an
individualistic theory of intergroup behavior" (p. 6). Most tests did just
that, and the motivational half failed: "intergroup discrimination leads
to an increase in self-esteem but is not motivated by a need for
self-esteem" (p. 22).

## Assumptions

- **The hypothesis is Hogg and Abrams's (1990) two corollaries** of
  Turner's (1982) account (pp. 3–4). A conclusive test examines both
  together: low self-esteem promotes discrimination *and* that
  discrimination then raises self-esteem (p. 4).
- **Status is not self-esteem.** Evidence for corollary 2 that relies on
  low-status groups is excluded, since members of low-status groups can
  hold their group in high esteem (p. 7; nine studies named as excluded).
- **Competitive versus normative discrimination.** The hypothesis applies
  only to discrimination that seeks positive distinctiveness against
  intergroup norms, not to discrimination prescribed by history or
  status (pp. 7–8). Evidence where normative discrimination was possible
  is kept but reported separately.
- **The type of self-esteem does not decide the verdict.** The taxonomy is
  used to compare, not to rule evidence in or out (fn. 5, p. 31).
- **A vote count.** Given the variety of designs, the review is
  qualitative, not quantitative (fn. 7, p. 31). Each experiment counts as
  supportive or unsupportive; effect sizes are not pooled.

## Key results

- **Eight types of self-esteem** (pp. 4–6; Table 1, p. 32). Global or
  specific (the whole self-image or one aspect), trait or state (over time
  or now), personal or social (esteem for oneself or for the shared
  self-image of an in-group). Example: specific social state is "At the
  moment, I feel good about my gender." The personal–social distinction is
  "the most important from a theoretical perspective" (p. 5).
- **The measures misfit** (pp. 6–7). Most scales measure global personal
  trait self-esteem (Rosenberg's RSES and others). The only social scale,
  Luhtanen and Crocker's Collective Self-Esteem Scale, is global and
  trait, and only its 4-item private subscale fits the theory's concept.
  "No purpose-built measures of either specific social trait or specific
  social state self-esteem exist" (p. 7).
- **Competitive discrimination** (pp. 8–15; Table 2, pp. 33–34).
  Corollary 1: 9 experiments supportive, 3 unsupportive. Corollary 2: 3
  supportive, 16 unsupportive.
  - Lemyre and Smith (1985), the best controlled test: discrimination
    predicted posttest self-esteem, but mere categorization had first
    lowered it, and discrimination raised it only back to the
    uncategorized level. Pretest self-esteem did not predict
    discrimination (p. 14).
  - Gagnon and Bourhis (1996): discrimination predicted specific social
    state self-esteem, only insofar as participants identified with the
    group (p. 11).
  - Platow et al. (1997, Expt. 2): those with high personal and low social
    self-esteem discriminated more than those high in both (pp. 11–12).
  - High personal self-esteem went with *more* discrimination in several
    studies; for instance, "the higher the subjects' level of trait
    self-esteem, the greater the difference in perceived competence"
    (Sidanius et al. 1994, as quoted, p. 13).
- **Competitive and/or normative discrimination** (pp. 15–21; Table 3,
  pp. 34–36). Corollary 1: 9 supportive, 10 unsupportive. Corollary 2: 4
  supportive, 12 unsupportive.
  - Branscombe and Wann (1994), the one study to support both corollaries
    (p. 19). High identifiers with America who saw an American boxer lose
    to a Soviet one had lower collective self-esteem and derogated
    Russians more. Among them, high derogators showed a greater rise in
    self-esteem. Low identifiers showed neither effect.
  - Long et al. (1994): high personal with low social self-esteem gave
    the most discrimination (p. 18).
- **Totals** (pp. 21–22). Corollary 1: 18 supportive, 13 unsupportive.
  Corollary 2: 7 supportive, 28 unsupportive. One of the eight full tests
  supports both. Nine studies find people with high global personal trait
  self-esteem the most discriminatory, which is "congruent with the idea
  that individuals who possess generally low self-esteem lack the
  self-confidence" to self-enhance this way (p. 22).
- **By type of measure** (Table 4, p. 37). For both corollaries, specific,
  social and state measures give a higher share of support than global,
  personal and trait ones. Corollary 1: social 4 for and 1 against,
  personal 14 and 12; state 12 and 4, trait 6 and 9. Corollary 2: specific
  6 and 6, global 1 and 22.
- **The verdict** (p. 22). The theory's explanation is motivational, so it
  "fails unless either both corollaries ... receive convincing support or
  corollary 2 receives support". "The majority of evidence does not
  support social identity theory's self-esteem hypothesis in its full and
  unqualified form."
- **The reformulation** (p. 22). (1) Successful competitive discrimination
  increases the specific state self-esteem associated with that identity.
  (2) Low specific social state self-esteem promotes competitive
  discrimination. The only two studies that measured specific social
  state self-esteem (Gagnon & Bourhis 1996; Platow et al. 1997, Expt. 2)
  both support it.
- **Two cautions for such tests** (pp. 22–23). Explicit state items ("at
  the moment, I feel good about my group") invite demand effects. And
  trait ratings of in- and out-group are "directly tapping the relative
  esteem in which subjects hold their own group" (Abrams & Hogg 1988, as
  quoted, p. 23), so a bias measure can be an esteem measure. Behavioural
  allocation measures are advised.
- **Further distinctions needed** (pp. 23–25): achieving, maintaining and
  enhancing self-esteem; threatened versus actually low self-esteem;
  successful versus unsuccessful discrimination; in-group favouritism
  versus out-group derogation; and identification versus social
  self-esteem, which current scales mix although the theory predicts
  opposite correlations with bias (positive for identification, negative
  for pretest self-esteem).
- **The theory's later course** (p. 25). Extensions of social identity
  theory either ignore the hypothesis (self-categorization theory) or
  replace it with other motives (Brewer's optimal distinctiveness; Hogg
  and Abrams's uncertainty reduction).

## Concepts

- **self-esteem hypothesis**: social identity theory's claim, in Hogg and
  Abrams's two corollaries, that discrimination raises self-esteem and that
  low self-esteem motivates it (pp. 3–4).
- **social self-esteem**: "the esteem in which [members] hold the shared
  self-image that constitutes their social psychological ingroup" (p. 5).
- **specific social state self-esteem**: esteem for one particular
  in-group, at the moment; the type the authors say the theory implies.
- **competitive versus normative discrimination**: discrimination that
  seeks positive distinctiveness against intergroup norms, versus
  discrimination the situation already prescribes (pp. 7–8).
- **categorization-depression**: the finding that mere minimal
  categorization lowers self-esteem (Lemyre & Smith 1985; p. 9).

## Connections

- **Hornsey 2008 ([NOTE-tmp3dav9](NOTE-tmp3dav9.md)) and Reimer et al. ([NOTE-tmp222eb](NOTE-tmp222eb.md)).** The
  LIT was filed to settle their disagreement. Hornsey's reading is right
  on the facts: more support for "bias raises self-esteem" than for "low
  self-esteem causes bias", and criticism for measuring personal
  self-esteem to predict a collective outcome. But "dead end" is
  Hornsey's verdict, not the review's. Rubin and Hewstone call for a
  narrower hypothesis and better measures. Reimer et al.'s "extensive
  empirical support" is not what the review found: even for corollary 1
  the count is 18 to 13, and the review's own conclusion is negative. Both
  readings call it a meta-analysis; it is a qualitative vote count
  (fn. 7). [NOTE-tmp222eb](NOTE-tmp222eb.md)'s reconciliation, that the two agree on
  corollary 1 and differ in tone, is fair, but its C2 should stay weak.
- **Self-categorization theory ([THEORY-tmpo85ke](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpo85ke.md)).** The review confirms
  the THEORY's statement that the self-esteem strand is social identity
  theory's and not part of self-categorization theory: the latter
  "ignore[s]" the hypothesis (p. 25).
- **The minimal group paradigm ([NOTE-tmpoxt60](NOTE-tmpoxt60.md)).** The review restates its
  design: participants are "not given the opportunity to award points to
  themselves", which is meant to remove self-interest (p. 3). Otten's
  finding that participants given that chance pay themselves most is the
  natural complement.
- **Identity fusion ([NOTE-tmp3g9n4](NOTE-tmp3g9n4.md)).** High personal with low social
  self-esteem gives the most discrimination (Long et al. 1994; Platow et
  al. 1997). The gloss is that low social self-esteem is "a threat to
  positive personal identity" and a motive to improve the social one
  (p. 24). That parallels fusion's finding that a challenge to the
  personal self is answered through the group. The link is the record's,
  not the authors'.
- **Leach et al. ([NOTE-tmp8tnhr](NOTE-tmp8tnhr.md)).** Their split of identification into
  self-definition and self-investment answers, ten years on, the review's
  call to separate identification from social self-esteem.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** The self-esteem hypothesis is
  social identity theory's own version of the thesis: group-serving bias
  is pursued for the esteem of a social self, "as ingroup members, rather
  than as individuals". The review is the best evidence the record holds
  on whether that motive operates. These are inferences.
  - **The benefit is found more often than the motive.** Discrimination
    tends to raise self-esteem; a need for self-esteem does not, in
    general, drive it. A gain to the self that follows group-serving
    action without motivating it does not show the action aimed at that
    self's good. In Stich's terms ([NOTE-tmpeuwqz](NOTE-tmpeuwqz.md)) it is a side effect, not
    an ultimate or instrumental aim.
  - **The motive was mostly tested at the wrong level.** Only two studies
    measured esteem for the group itself, and both support the motive. So
    the review does not refute a group-level self-interest. It shows that
    by 1998 it was nearly untested.
  - **The personal self keeps showing.** The most discriminatory people
    had high personal self-esteem, and the strongest motivated pattern is
    high personal with low social self-esteem. The group's standing seems
    to matter as a threat to a personal self that already includes it.
    That fits "larger self" (the group added to a kept personal self)
    better than "changed self".
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** The review states, for
  measures, the objection's demand. Identification scales contain esteem
  items and esteem scales contain identification items, though the theory
  predicts opposite effects for them. It proposes measuring
  identification as importance and self-esteem as evaluation (p. 24). It
  also warns that bias ratings can be esteem ratings (p. 23), so "the
  self's good" and "the group-serving act" can be one measurement. A
  thesis that cites this literature needs the two kept apart.
- **Batson's objection ([CLAIM-tmpybc4k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpybc4k.md)).** Not addressed: no helping, no
  empathy.
- **Proposed corrections to two readings.** On [NOTE-tmp3dav9](NOTE-tmp3dav9.md), C4's
  strength should say "one cited qualitative review (a vote count)", not
  "meta-analysis". On [NOTE-tmp222eb](NOTE-tmp222eb.md), the open question that names this
  review is answered: the review supports Hornsey's description, not
  "extensive empirical support". These are proposed dated notes, not
  edits.
- **Dated note.** Proposed on [CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md) (Section 3 of the draft).
- **No THEORY filed.** The hypothesis is social identity theory's and
  contested, and the record files only self-categorization theory's core.

## Limitations

- **A vote count.** Experiments are counted as for or against, with no
  effect sizes, no weighting by sample size and no test of
  heterogeneity. Some studies appear in more than one cell of the tables.
- **The classification is the authors'.** Whether a study is
  "competitive" or "normative", or counts as supportive "with
  qualifications", is a judgement, made in the text case by case.
- **1998.** Later work, including implicit measures the authors call for
  and the later meta-analysis Reimer et al. cite (Aberson, Healy & Romero
  2000), is not covered.
- **Mostly laboratory and student samples**, many minimal groups.
- **An internal inconsistency.** The text lists Crocker and Luhtanen
  (1990) among eight experiments in which high self-esteem went with more
  discrimination (p. 12), but then reports that study as finding "no
  significant relationship" (p. 13), and Table 2 does not mark it as a
  reverse finding.
- **Author's final version.** Not compared with the published text.
- **Typos and reference slips** (manuscript pages).
  - "Azjen" for Ajzen (p. 5).
  - "operationlized", in a quotation from Crocker et al. (p. 12).
  - "This lead us" (p. 15).
  - "Vanbeseleare" (p. 16) and "Nascimeto-Schulze" (p. 17).
  - "Maass & Acuri" for Arcuri (p. 20 and references).
  - "St. Claire" in the text (p. 9), "St. Clare" in the references;
    "Schiffmann" in the text, "Schiffman" in the references.
  - Rosenberg et al. (1995) is "Rosenberg, Schooler, Schoenbach, &
    Rosenberg" in the text (p. 4) and "Rosenberg, Schoenbach, Schooler, &
    Rosenberg" in the references.
  - Sachdev and Bourhis (1984) and St. Clare and Turner (1982) are both
    given as *EJSP* 12, 307–314; one of them is wrong.
  - Long et al. (1994): pages "313-219".

## Open questions

- Does the reformulated hypothesis hold? A test would measure specific
  social state self-esteem implicitly, before and after a behavioural
  allocation, with identification measured separately. The record holds
  no later test.
- What did Aberson, Healy and Romero (2000) find? It is the quantitative
  successor Reimer et al. cite and is not held.
- Is the high-personal, low-social pattern a case of the group in the
  self? A study crossing it with identity fusion would show whether those
  who discriminate most under it are the fused.
