# NOTE-tmp301qd: Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin — Rational snacking

## Contribution

An experimental manipulation, not a correlation, showing that marshmallow
wait-time depends strongly on the child's evidence about the reliability
of the adult who promises the larger reward, so that wait-time is not a
clean measure of self-control.

## Key insight

Waiting is the rational choice only if the promised reward is likely to
come; a child who has just seen the promisor break promises does well not
to wait.

## Assumptions

- Evidence about the experimenter's reliability on art supplies transfers
  to her promise about marshmallows (same person, same session).
- "Rational" means maximizing expected benefit given beliefs; the process
  is called implicit, not deliberate.
- The 15-minute cap censors wait-times, so the difference is a
  conservative estimate.
- Mood did not differ by condition (supplement, not read).

## Key results

- **Hypothesis** (§1.2): "Waiting is only the rational choice if you
  believe that a second marshmallow is likely to actually appear after a
  reasonably short delay".
- **Manipulation** (§2.2.1, unreliable arm): "I'm sorry, but I made a
  mistake. We don't have any other art supplies after all."
- **Design** (§2.2.2): the usual bell signal was omitted because it "would
  necessarily provide children with additional information about the
  experimenter's reliability".
- **Main result** (§3): "Children in the unreliable condition waited
  without eating the marshmallow for a mean duration of 3 min and 2 s (M =
  181.57 s). In contrast, children in the reliable condition waited 12 min
  and 2 s (M = 722.43 s)." Wilcoxon W = 22.5, p < 0.0005.
- **Binary result** (§3): "only 1 out of the 14 children (7.1%) waited the
  full 15 min; in the reliable condition, however, 9 out of the 14
  children (64.3%) waited."
- **Scope** (§4): "our data do not demonstrate that self-control is
  irrelevant in explaining the variance in children's wait-times"; "the
  influence of a child's beliefs about the reliability of the world is at
  least comparable to their capacity for self-control."
- **Conclusion** (§5): children's decisions to wait "are strongly
  influenced by the reliability of the environment (in this case, the
  reliability of the researcher's verbal assurances)".

## Concepts

Deficient-capacity against rational-decision hypotheses; environmental
reliability; experimenter reliability; sustained delay-of-gratification
task; wait-time.

## Bearing on the record

- **CASE-tmp07hx9** (the two arms). Confirms the documented arm: the
  design, N = 28, 12.03 minutes against about 3 minutes (the paper's §3
  gives 3 min 2 s, 181.57 s, and its §4 "3.02 min"; A's 3.03 is the
  closer rounding of 181.57 s), a ratio of 0.25. A's caption matches the
  authors' framing, with their caveat that self-control is not shown
  irrelevant. The case's "unverified until read" can now be narrowed to
  the sample: N = 28, one age band, one lab, a 15-minute cap.
- **CLAIM-tmpt091d** (the controller's reliability decides whether waiting
  is good judgment). Supports its first half, through the case it rests
  on: waiting tracks the promisor's demonstrated reliability, and the
  authors call this rational. The reliability evidence came from the same
  adult who controlled delivery. Nothing here bears on the claim's second
  half, control and answerability, which stays A's interpretation.
- **CLAIM-549** cites the case for the child's taking the marshmallow as
  "not simply evidence of insufficient discipline"; this reading supports
  that use.
- **CLAIM-344** (named in the case). No bearing: the paper does not
  discuss resolve.

## Verdict on the exchange's characterization

Faithful. A53: "children's waiting behavior responded significantly to
experimentally manipulated evidence about the reliability of the adult."

## Limitations

Fourteen children per arm, one site, a 15-minute cap, reliability
manipulated in one session by one experimenter, and no bell signal, so
the procedure differs from Mischel's. Fig. 1's caption names a "Wilcoxon
signed-rank test" where §3 names a rank-sum test for the same W = 22.5;
the between-subjects design calls for rank-sum. Michaelson and Munakata
(2016) and Michaelson et al. (2013) on social trust are close neighbours,
not held.
