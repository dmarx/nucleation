# QUESTION-tmpszc1x: When origami modules are joined along shared boundaries, which constraint structures does the coupling generate, and does eliminating the interior creases leave a relation among boundary fold angles that the modules' own crease constraints can express, or one that needs higher arity, inequalities or history, and at what cost of representation and elimination?

## Why it is a question

A (§2): "Each module has a local configuration space Q_i. Joining them
introduces compatibility conditions among their boundary configurations",
Q_global = {(q_1,…,q_n): φ_ij(q_i)=φ_ji(q_j) on shared interfaces}, and
"the physical attachment is not itself the same thing as those maps.
Attachment is a mechanism that can establish constraints whose effects are
represented by correspondence conditions." That is TERM-093's distinction
and CLAIM-471's formation, discovery and adaptation. A's question: "Can we
classify origami assemblies by the constraint structures generated by
coupling their components, rather than by the geometry of the components
considered individually?"

A (§5) then turns it into QUESTION-283's question on paper: "Local crease
constraints —(composition and elimination)→ Effective boundary
constraints. Do those effective constraints remain expressible using the
original local constraint vocabulary? Or does the boundary model need new
relations, higher-order dependencies, inequalities, or history-dependent
constraints?" And: "This is not something THEORY-1090 proves for origami
specifically. But it supplies an excellent mathematical precedent and an
experimental procedure."

The precedent is exact and small. In THEORY-1090's star, the boundary
values are (p + q, q, p), so the reduced constraint is v₁ = v₂ + v₃. A
adds that "Each pairwise projection permits every real-valued pair, while
the full three-way relation excludes most triples", and that is right: the
map (p, q) ↦ (p + q, q, p) has rank 2 on every pair of coordinates
(checked by the filer), so every pairwise shadow of the section space is
all of ℝ², and no set of pairwise conditions satisfied by the sections
cuts the plane out of ℝ³.

Origami differs from that precedent in two ways the record already notes.
Loop closure is a product of rotations about different crease axes, which
do not commute, so its closure theory "is not simply the additive scalar
cohomology" (A, §7); NOTE-tmpd0dln finds that closure constrains the
transports themselves, one level above CLAIM-495's sections. And the
relations among fold angles are already nonlinear at one vertex: in
CASE-tmpe48yq each mode relates adjacent angles through tan(ρ/2), with
inequalities from non-self-intersection. So whether elimination raises
the arity, adds inequalities or adds history is open for paper, and the
linear answer does not transfer.

The record has the abstract network version as a design: CASE-179's regime
of higher-dimensional stalks with interior units, with "what effective
boundary law an observer confined to some units can fit, and whether it is
pairwise, higher-order or history-dependent" as a readout. It has no
origami assembly.

## What would answer it

A's experiment, in its three steps: "Construct a network of local
constrained components whose internal variables can be eliminated,
producing an effective relation on its boundary. Then ask whether that
relation can be represented using the same class of local constraints as
the original network." "Next, introduce computational resource bounds:
restrict the allowed representation language, the time available for
constraint elimination, or the complexity of the process used to
reconstruct interior states." "Finally, compare what becomes possible when
the system is permitted to change its own constraint architecture." It
separates "What global constraints does composition generate? What
resources does their representation require? What processes can realize
or modify them?"

An answer would be, for a stated class of assemblies (say, networks of
degree-4 vertices sharing creases), the boundary relation after
eliminating the interior, with a proof that it is or is not expressible in
the class of single-vertex relations, and the cost of computing it. A
characterization, a sufficient condition like Hansen and Ghrist's
Theorem 4.3 for scalar stalks, would answer more.

## What would not answer it

- **One example of a complicated boundary relation.** A relation can look
  complicated and still be a composite of local ones; the question is
  expressibility in the stated vocabulary, which needs a proof of
  impossibility, as THEORY-1090 gives for sheaves.
- **A classification by the modules' geometry alone**, which is what A
  proposes to replace.
- **Universality.** A: "The experiment would not by itself establish
  universality. But it could identify a candidate invariant under
  translation across substrates", which is QUESTION-tmpkcffa's business.
