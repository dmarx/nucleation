# THEORY-tmp7h69w: A moral crumple zone, on Elish's account: when control over a highly automated system is distributed, responsibility for its failure can be misattributed to the nearest human operator, who had limited control, so that blame protects the integrity of the technological system; this is likeliest right after a publicized accident and is not a property of every automated system

Elish (2019), LIT-tmpnmwlk, read in NOTE-tmps5u6y.

## The account

Responsibility "may be misattributed to a human actor who had limited
control over the behavior of an automated or autonomous system", and "the
moral crumple zone protects the integrity of the technological system, at
the expense of the nearest human operator" (p. 40). "While control has been
effectively distributed, responsibility has not scaled accordingly"
(p. 49). Certification bounds what can count as the machine's malfunction,
leaving "the human pilot" as "the only possible malfunction" (p. 50). It is
"more than just the articulation of a scapegoat": the operator may be
partly responsible, as at Three Mile Island (pp. 44, 51).

## Conditions

Not universal (p. 50). Zones "are likely to take shape in the immediate
aftermath of a highly publicized event or accident", and where the
operator knew of the malfunction but lacked "sufficient information or
authority to take corrective actions" (p. 51). In the Therac-25 accidents,
which came to light slowly and whose operator could not know of the
malfunction, lawsuits went to the manufacturer.

## What it does not show

- **Why accountability gaps arise** (p. 50: the concept "is not a way to
  explain why these gaps occur").
- **Frequency.** The cases are narrative, drawn mostly from press accounts.
- **Anything about conscientiousness** or the moral subsidy.
