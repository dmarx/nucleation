# THEORY-1914: Tessman argues that non-negotiable moral requirements stay binding when they cannot be met, so that moral failure can be unavoidable, and, following Karnein, that a duty to an innocent dependent created by a third party's wrongdoing binds the person left to meet it though it is unfair, while a dependent who is himself the wrongdoer may forfeit the care

## Source

Tessman (2016), "Moral Failure — Response to Critics" ([LIT-2702](../literature.d/LIT-2702.md)), read as a stand-in for Moral Failure ([LIT-2672](../literature.d/LIT-2672.md)). The reading is [NOTE-2002](../notes.d/NOTE-2002.md).

## What was actually shown

Argument, not evidence. "Nonnegotiable moral requirements remain binding even when they become impossible to fulfill" (p. 1). In a coercive household (p. 14), A's wrongdoing "functions like luck" for B's obligation to their child C, and "A owes all kinds of reparations to B". The case depends on C being innocent (p. 15). "There are moral requirements that it is unfair for us to be bound by, but that nevertheless are binding on us" (p. 16).

Corroborates [CLAIM-703](../claims.d/CLAIM-703.md) (a genuine obligation created by another's wrong, with a complaint against the wrongdoer) and [CLAIM-708](../claims.d/CLAIM-708.md) (the wrongdoer answers for the predicament while the bound party still answers for her own conduct). Bears on [QUESTION-472](../questions.d/QUESTION-472.md).

## What this does not say

- That the bound party must, all things considered, meet the obligation: she may rightly put her own needs first, at the cost of unavoidable failure (p. 15).
- That the obligation binds when the dependent is the wrongdoer: then it may be forfeited (p. 15).
- How large the obligation is; nothing on limits of the kind [CLAIM-703](../claims.d/CLAIM-703.md) adds.
