# THEORY-tmpo85ke: Which self-category a person uses, what it means and who in it is most typical vary lawfully with the comparative frame of reference: a grouping of self with others becomes salient as the differences within it shrink relative to the differences between it and the rest of the frame (metacontrast) and as those differences match expectations about the category, and as it does, self-perception is depersonalized

## What the account says

A **self-category** groups self with some class of others as equivalent,
against some other class. **Personal identity** is self-categorization by
differences from other in-group members. **Social identity** is
self-categorization by shared membership against other categories. Both
are equally authentic (Turner et al. 1994, p. 454).

Which category is salient is an interaction of **perceiver readiness**
(accessibility, from experience, goals and motives) and **fit**
(p. 455). **Comparative fit** is the metacontrast principle: a collection
is more likely to be categorized as an entity to the degree that the
average differences among its members are less than the average
differences between them and the rest of the frame of reference.
**Normative fit** requires those differences to lie in the direction
background beliefs about the category predict.

From metacontrast the account derives four kinds of variation (pp.
456–458):

- *level*: more inclusive categories as the frame widens;
- *kind*: which category, by normative fit;
- *content*: the traits that make up the category;
- *internal structure*: who is most prototypical.

Salience **depersonalizes** self-perception: self and others are seen as
interchangeable exemplars of the prototype, and group phenomena such as
cohesion, influence and polarization follow (p. 455; Hornsey 2008,
pp. 208–211).

## What was actually shown

Nothing in the record shows it at first hand. Both sources report results
from the theory's originating group without presenting data:

- arts and science identities most salient under both comparative and
  normative fit (Oakes, Turner & Haslam 1991);
- more sex-stereotyping of self under intergroup than intragroup
  disagreement (Hogg & Turner 1987);
- the content of "Americans" shifting with the comparison country and
  over the Gulf War (Haslam et al. 1992);
- extremists' greater polarization reversible by their position relative
  to the majority (Haslam & Turner, in press in 1994).

Hornsey adds that in the SCT programme, high salience or identification
increased self-stereotyping and perceived in-group homogeneity
(2008, p. 213). Each of these could have come out otherwise, since
content could have stayed fixed as the frame changed. None has been read
here.

## What this does not say

- **Not that the self is generated rather than stored.** Turner et al.
  (1994, pp. 458–459) go on to "speculate" that a self-concept is defined
  by reflexive use and that a fixed self-structure may be neither
  "meaningful or necessary". That is a further thesis argued by analogy
  to category research, and it is not filed. A stored repertoire with
  context-dependent selection (Markus & Wurf's working self-concept)
  predicts much of the same variation.
- **Not that fit determines salience.** The authors call fit "part of the
  explanation but not the complete account" (p. 456). Readiness takes in
  motives and goals, so the account predicts a direction only once
  readiness is held fixed or measured.
- **Not social identity theory's motivational claims.** Positive
  distinctiveness, the self-esteem hypotheses and the socio-structural
  variables are SIT's (Hornsey 2008, pp. 206–207, 214). They are not part
  of this account, and the self-esteem strand is contested.
- **Not that variability is veridical.** The authors claim it (p. 458)
  but give no criterion.
- **Not a strict functional antagonism.** The account allows competing
  categorizations at several levels at once (Turner et al. 1994, p. 456).
  Hornsey reports a later softening (2008, p. 216).
