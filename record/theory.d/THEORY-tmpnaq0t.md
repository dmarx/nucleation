# THEORY-tmpnaq0t: The self whose governance makes action autonomous is not the self whose persistence is personal identity: governance by an essence of volitional necessities would make action authentic, not autonomous; losing a defining care costs one's grasp of who one is, not one's existence; and wholeheartedness won by extruding a desire is a defence, not health

## Source

Velleman (2002), "Identification and Identity", [LIT-292](../literature.d/LIT-292.md), read in full in
[NOTE-268](../notes.d/NOTE-268.md). Pages are *Self to Self* (2006). All claims about Frankfurt are as
Velleman reports them, with his pinpoint citations.

## What was actually shown

**The target (pp. 331–338).** On Frankfurt's late view, "a person acts
autonomously only when his volitions derive from the essential character of
his will" (NVL 132). The essence is a set of volitional necessities, willing
inabilities endorsed at every higher order, which define the person, so he
"could not alter them while remaining the same person" (ANL 138). Hence the
self must be wholehearted, with a rival desire "extruded entirely as an
outlaw" (IWWCA 170). Velleman shows this yokes autonomy to a Parfitian,
un-Lockean account of survival.

**The critique.**

- **Authenticity, not autonomy (p. 338).** Winnicott's False Self is moved
  by neither his simulated nor his essential motives, yet "his problem is one
  of excess: he is overly self-controlled". He is inauthentic but all too
  autonomous.
- **No motivational essence (pp. 338–341).** If Aulis ended Agamemnon, "we
  wouldn't have the concept of a sequel at all". Frankfurt "simply asserts"
  that abandoning a commitment ends a person, and the appeal of the claim
  may be wishful.
- **Wholeheartedness as defence (pp. 341–347).** The Rat Man was made ill not
  by ambivalence toward his father but by repressing the hatred as
  "entirely foreign and repugnant to him". So he "suffered, not from the
  disease of ambivalence, but from something like Frankfurt's cure".
- **Two senses of identification (pp. 347–353).** Ordinary identification is
  imaginative and aimed mostly at other people. Read that way, identifying
  with one of one's own motives is Sartrean bad faith. Velleman says this is
  how readers may hear Frankfurt, not what Frankfurt meant.

**The positive sketch (pp. 354–360).** "Self" expresses reflexivity and
refers to no single entity across contexts. In identity contexts one's past
and future selves are those one can pick out first-personally by memory
and anticipation. In self-regard contexts, cares can be essential to the
self-conception: losing them means not knowing for a while who one is, not
ceasing to be (p. 356). In autonomy contexts, the part one cannot regard
from outside, the understanding, is one's essential self only aspectually.
"I agree with Frankfurt that autonomous action is guided by a part of us with
which we cannot help identifying; I disagree mainly with his claim that our
identifying with a part of ourselves incorporates it into something called
the self" (p. 360).

What could have come out otherwise: Frankfurt's texts could have offered an
example of a person ended by losing a care. Velleman says they offer none,
and treats the Agamemnon passage as the attempt.

## What this does not say

- **That Frankfurt's late view is false.** The genealogical argument shows
  only that its appeal could be wishful or defensive. Velleman frames it as a
  "worry" (p. 341).
- **That Velleman's own account of autonomy is established.** He says
  "Expounding my own views is not the purpose of this essay" (p. 354), and
  how the understanding motivates is "beyond the scope" (p. 359). His view of
  personal identity is assumed from "Self to Self".
- **That the Rat Man argument is independent of Freud.** It depends on
  Freud's case interpretation and on a folk premise that mixed emotions must
  "mingle" to be digested (p. 346).
- **Anything about Frankfurt's 1971 hierarchy.** The early theory gets one
  paragraph and is deferred to "What Happens When Someone Acts?" ([LIT-295](../literature.d/LIT-295.md)).
  The regress is flagged only in a footnote (n. 10).
- **That Frankfurt "simply asserts".** This is a claim about his whole corpus
  that the record cannot check from here.
- **That autonomy and authenticity always come apart.** The Winnicott case
  may show only that they can diverge in pathology.
