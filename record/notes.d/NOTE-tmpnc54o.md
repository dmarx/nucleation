# NOTE-tmpnc54o: Ivy & Mroczko-Wąsowicz — Framing effects in perception

## Contribution

The authors propose that object perception is shaped by "perceptual frames": adaptations of the perceptual system, acquired through perceptual learning and domain training, that govern how perceptual objects are singled out from the sensory scene. Because frames work through bottom-up processes but differ between perceivers, two subjects given the same sensory input can perceive different objects. The paper supports the idea with empirical work on multisensory integration (frames help bind features), amodal completion (which needs frames whether or not mental imagery is involved) and predictive anticipation of motion. It concludes that framing effects are a form of perceptual diversity that theories of perception should accommodate.

## Skim

*Abstract, figures and selected sections, read when the work was seeded. Not enough to state its assumptions or results exactly; a `Read` note replaces this one.*

- §1: the opening case is a Bach scholar who hears the B-A-C-H motif as its own perceptual object while other listeners do not. The authors grant, for argument's sake, a conservative view on which cognition and perception are encapsulated, and argue for non-trivial cognitive effects anyway.
- §2: sets perceptual frames against top-down attention, perspective shifts and imagery. Anyone can flip a Necker cube, but some perspectives, such as an expert's, are not available to every normal perceiver.
- §3 gives the formal definition: a perceptual frame is an adaptation of the perceptual system caused by perceptual learning and realized through bottom-up functional processes, organizing sensory information subject-dependently into idiosyncratic object representations. The section then surveys empirical results on multisensory integration, amodal completion and anticipation.
- §4: frames are often installed top-down but then run bottom-up. The perceptual system as a whole is therefore open to cognitive influence even if individual percepts are not. The authors say this is compatible with intersubjective objectivity and with the mind-independence of perceived objects.
- §5: frames are "perceiver-specific structural principles of perception", and whether individual experiences are cognitively affected is left open.

## Open questions

- The frame idea is structurally a learned inductive bias. It is a possible bridge to how training shapes which "objects" learned perceptual models segment, though the paper does not discuss ML.
- Check the empirical studies in §3 (expertise, multisensory binding, amodal completion) to see how strongly they support a bottom-up realization rather than top-down attention.
- Check how the view relates to the cognitive penetrability debate (Pylyshyn, Firestone & Scholl) and to predictive processing, both of which the keywords name.
