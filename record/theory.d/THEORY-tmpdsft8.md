# THEORY-tmpdsft8: Kolmogorov holds that beside the logic of theoretical statements there is a calculus of problems, whose formulas are problems to be solved and not statements to be proved, with no notion of correctness, and whose connectives are operations on problems; that it coincides in form with Heyting's intuitionistic logic without needing intuitionist assumptions; and that intuitionistic logic's objects are really problems, since a general statement's negation and an existence statement have determinate sense only as a problem with its solution

Kolmogorov (1932), [LIT-tmpgnf38](../literature.d/LIT-tmpgnf38.md); read in [NOTE-tmpmkrpn](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpmkrpn.md).

## The account

**Two logics.** "Neben der theoretischen Logik, welche die Beweisschemata
der theoretischen Wahrheiten systematisiert, kann man die Schemata der
Lösungen von Aufgaben [...] systematisieren" (p. 58). A problem is given by
examples, not defined (p. 59): find numbers satisfying a relation, prove a
theorem false, construct a circle, and conditional problems ("supposing
…, find …"). Fully stated, a problem names its permitted means of
construction (p. 59, n. 2). A problem whose supposition is impossible is
without content, and proving that counts as its solution (p. 59).

**No truth for problems.** Proofs and solutions run parallel, but it is essential "daß jede
bewiesene Aussage *richtig* ist; für die Aufgaben hat man keinen dieser
Richtigkeit entsprechenden Begriff" (p. 61, n. 8). That I solved a problem
is a subjective fact; mathematical problems have generally valid
solutions, which any able hearer must recognize (p. 61).

**The connectives.** a ∧ b, solve both; a ∨ b, solve one; a ⊃ b, given a
solution of a, solve b; ¬a, given a solution of a, obtain a contradiction
(not: prove a unsolvable, n. 5); (x)a(x), give a general method for every x
(pp. 59–60). With eleven of Heyting's formulas and three rules postulated
as solved, the calculus coincides with Heyting's first paper (p. 63). It
does not have a ∨ ¬a, a general method that solves or refutes every
problem, and does have ¬¬(a ∨ ¬a) (p. 63).

**Why intuitionistic logic is about problems** (§2). By Weyl's principle,
the negation of a general statement over an infinite set points to no
determinate state of affairs, and Brouwer's existence statements are false
before their construction and true after. Kolmogorov splits them: there is
a problem, to find an element of K with property A, with "einen bestimmten,
von dem Zustand unserer Kenntnisse unabhängigen Sinn", and its solution,
after which "Jetzt ist unsere Aufgabe gelöst" is an empirical statement
(p. 65). Nothing is left to be an existence statement proper. So "Für eine
allgemeine Aussage ist es im allgemeinen sinnlos, ihre Negation als
bestimmte Aussage zu betrachten" (p. 65), and intuitionistic logic's
formulas get their sense as problems.

## What this does not say

- **That a proposition is a problem.** Kolmogorov keeps the two apart and
  faults Heyting for not doing so (p. 65, n. 17). In §2, excluded middle
  holds for every statement whose negation has sense (p. 65); the
  intuitionistic calculus is the problems'. The
  identification is later (the BHK interpretation, propositions as types).
- **What a problem is.** It is never defined (p. 59), and what counts as
  "a general method" is left informal.
- **That the coincidence with Heyting is proved here.** The derivations are
  omitted (p. 63).
- **Anything about revising a problem.** A problem's sense is fixed
  independently of what we know (p. 65); the calculus composes and solves
  problems and never changes one.
