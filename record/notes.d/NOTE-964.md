# NOTE-964: Monti et al. — willful modulation of brain activity in disorders of consciousness

## Contribution

It ran Owen et al.'s imagery test ([LIT-1178](../literature.d/LIT-1178.md)) as a routine assessment of
54 patients at Cambridge and Liège, giving a first estimate of how often
patients diagnosed by behaviour respond to command in the scanner: 5 of 54,
4 of 23 admitted as vegetative. It then turned the two tasks into a yes/no
code, validated it on 16 healthy controls (48 of 48 answers decoded
correctly) and used it with one patient, who answered five of six questions
correctly while no communication could be established at his bedside.

## Key insight

Two mental acts that recruit separable regions are a one-bit channel that
needs no movement and no training: think of tennis for one answer and of
walking through your home for the other. The answer is a judgement, a fact
retrieved and assessed against the question, and the imagery is only its
code, assigned by instruction. Because the questions are chosen so that the
investigators do not know the answers, a correct decoding cannot be read
into the data by the people reading it.

## Assumptions

- **Diagnosis by behaviour.** Vegetative state: "repeated examinations
  yield no evidence of a sustained, reproducible, purposeful, or voluntary
  behavioral response to visual, auditory, tactile, or noxious stimuli"
  (p. 580). Minimally conscious state: "inconsistent but reproducible signs
  of awareness, including the ability to follow commands", without
  interactive communication. Misdiagnosis runs at "approximately 40%"
  because "awareness has to be inferred from a patient's motor
  responsiveness" (p. 580).
- **Region specificity.** Motor imagery recruits the supplementary motor
  area (SMA) and spatial imagery the parahippocampal gyrus (PPA). Contrasts
  were restricted to these two atlas regions (Harvard–Oxford), cluster
  z > 2.3, corrected P < 0.05 (p. 581).
- **Positive findings imply awareness.** The tasks need "language
  comprehension, working memory, decision making, or executive function";
  "positive results, whether observed with or without corroborative
  behavioral data, do confirm that all such processes were intact and that
  the patient must have been aware" (p. 588). Negative findings are
  uninterpretable (p. 588).
- **Sample.** A "convenience sample" (p. 580), November 2005 to January
  2009. Consent from legal guardians.

## Key results

- **Tasks (p. 581).** Motor imagery: "imagine standing still on a tennis
  court and to swing an arm to 'hit the ball' back and forth to an imagined
  instructor". Spatial imagery: "imagine navigating the streets of a
  familiar city or to imagine walking from room to room in their home and
  to visualize all that they would 'see' if they were there". Two localizer
  scans, each five cycles of 30 s imagery and 30 s rest, cued by the spoken
  words "tennis", "navigation" and "relax".
- **Responders (p. 583, Table 1).** "Among the 54 patients, we identified 5
  who could willfully modulate their brain activity." All five showed SMA
  activation for motor against spatial imagery, four of the five PPA for
  spatial against motor; activity was sustained over the 30 s and locked to
  the cues. "Four of the five patients were considered to be in a
  vegetative state (including Patient 4, who has been described
  previously), and all five patients had a traumatic brain injury." Table
  1: Patients 4, 6, 22, 23 (admitted VS; Patient 6 motor only) and 54
  (admitted MCS). "No such responses were observed in any of the patients
  with nontraumatic brain injuries" (p. 585).
- **Bedside after the scans (p. 585).** Of the four admitted as vegetative,
  "some behavioral indicators of awareness could be detected in two of
  them. However, the other two patients remained behaviorally unresponsive
  at the bedside, even after the functional MRI results were known and
  despite repeated testing by a multidisciplinary team." The main text does
  not say which two.
- **Communication procedure (p. 581).** Before each scan a yes/no question
  was asked (e.g., "Do you have any brothers?"), and the participant was
  told to use one imagery for "yes" and the other for "no". "The nature of
  the questions ensured that the investigators would not know the correct
  answers before judging the functional MRI data." The cue for every answer
  period was the neutral word "answer", with "relax" for rest, given once
  per period. One question per scan; three scans per control, six for the
  patient. Decoding: mean GLM estimates in the two regions, then a
  similarity metric (in the Supplementary Appendix, not read) comparing
  each communication scan with each localizer.
- **Controls (p. 583).** 16 healthy controls, 48 questions: "the correct
  answer was determined with 100% accuracy", the answer "verified after the
  analysis".
- **Patient 23 (pp. 585, 588).** Six autobiographical questions. Five
  "closely matched" one localizer "within the bounds of normal variability",
  and "for those same five questions, the pattern produced always matched
  the factually correct answer". Examples: "Is your father's name
  Alexander?", yes, by motor imagery; "Is your father's name Thomas?", no,
  by spatial imagery (names changed in the figure for privacy). The sixth,
  the last of the session, "could not be decoded ... because virtually no
  activity was observed within the regions of interest", and "Whether the
  patient fell asleep during this question, did not hear it, simply elected
  not to answer it, or lost consciousness cannot be determined."
- **Patient 23's history (pp. 580–581, 585, 588).** Male, 22, traumatic
  injury in a traffic accident. Diagnosed in a permanent vegetative state
  at 17 months; "confirmed by a month-long specialized assessment 3.5 years
  after the injury"; scanned 5 years after it (Table 1: 60.8 months),
  "assumed to remain in a vegetative state, although extensive behavioral
  testing after the functional MRI revealed reproducible, but inconsistent,
  responses indicative of a minimally conscious state". "Despite the best
  efforts of the clinical team, it was impossible to establish any
  functional communication at the bedside." The authors: "it is not known
  whether either ability was available during earlier evaluations"; he may
  have regained function, or "may have been aware during previous
  assessments but unable to produce the necessary motor response";
  "Given that all previous assessments were based on behavioral
  observations alone, these two possibilities are indistinguishable"
  (p. 588).

## Method

1. Two localizer scans per patient (motor and spatial imagery against
   rest), GLM, contrasts limited to SMA and PPA.
2. For communication: one question before each scan; imagery code for the
   answer; neutral cue "answer".
3. Decode by comparing ROI estimates on each communication scan with each
   localizer, by a similarity metric (Supplementary Appendix).

## Concepts

- **Willful modulation.** "Voluntary, reliable, and repeatable
  blood-oxygenation-level–dependent responses in predefined
  neuroanatomical regions when prompted to perform imagery tasks"
  (p. 585).
- **Functional communication.** Here, correct yes/no answers to questions
  whose answers the investigators did not know.

## Connections

It extends Owen et al. 2006 ([LIT-1178](../literature.d/LIT-1178.md), read in [NOTE-962](NOTE-962.md)),
whose patient is Patient 4 here, and Boly et al.
2007 on the imagery paradigm. It cites Weiskopf et al. on real-time fMRI
brain–computer interfaces, but does not use real-time feedback. Its
diagnostic categories are Giacino et al.'s (2002) minimally conscious state
and the Multi-Society Task Force's vegetative state.

## Bearing on the record

- **[CASE-107](../cases.d/CASE-107.md): the CASE and the LIT run two patients together.** The
  CASE says "5 of 54 patients ... could modulate their brain activity at
  will, two of them with no voluntary behaviour detectable at the bedside,
  and the imagery could be used to answer yes or no". The LIT's "Why this
  is here" says "answers given, through mental imagery by patients with no
  detectable voluntary behaviour". The paper does not support that
  reading. Only one patient was asked questions, Patient 23, and on
  retesting he showed "reproducible, but inconsistent, responses indicative
  of a minimally conscious state" (p. 581). The two patients with no
  behaviour at the bedside were not asked questions. So the paper shows,
  separately, (a) awareness-on-command with no detectable behaviour, in two
  patients, and (b) yes/no judgement through imagery in a patient who still
  had inconsistent motor responses, though no bedside communication.
  Neither patient shows both.
- **[CLAIM-341](../claims.d/CLAIM-341.md), defeater (b).** What the paper shows is judgement
  given with no action on the world in the channel that carried it: the
  answer went out through imagery read by a scanner. It does not show
  judgement in a patient with every contingency between intention and
  outcome removed. Patient 23 kept inconsistent voluntary motor responses,
  and the two unresponsive patients were only undetected, not shown to have
  no motor channel. The authors themselves call bedside unresponsiveness a
  limit of detection: "motor function can be so impaired that bedside
  assessments ... may not reveal awareness" (p. 588). So it is evidence for
  the thesis's weaker reading (judgement runs, here, without needing
  action) and not a test of its defeater.
- **What the judgement was about.** The questions shown are autobiographical
  facts from before the injury: his father's name. They test retrieval and
  assessment of an old model, built while acting, not whether a model of
  the present is kept in touch five years into near-total immobility. That
  bears on the CASE's "What it cannot show". The other four questions are
  in the Supplementary Appendix, not read.
- **[CLAIM-337](../claims.d/CLAIM-337.md): the channel is imagined action.** The "yes" code was to
  "imagine standing still on a tennis court and to swing an arm" and is read
  from the supplementary motor area. Like Owen et al., this is motor
  imagery read from a motor region. The judgement and the channel separate
  cleanly here, which the thesis can use: the judgement (is the name right?)
  is not itself an imagined act; it is encoded into one by an arbitrary
  convention set by the investigators. The counter can still say the
  encoding is mental action. The thesis's answer, that it acts on the
  patient's own state and not the world, holds.
- **The asymmetry.** The paper exhibits it twice. Negative findings are
  uninterpretable in 49 of 54 patients (p. 588). The sixth question, which
  produced no signal, cannot be told apart from sleep, deafness, refusal or
  loss of consciousness (p. 585). Evidence of lucidity arrives only when the
  patient drives the channel.
- **[CLAIM-342](../claims.d/CLAIM-342.md), from the observers' side.** The design is an instance
  of the claim's anchor, but for the investigators' interpretation, not the
  patient's: "The nature of the questions ensured that the investigators
  would not know the correct answers before judging the functional MRI
  data" (p. 581). That is scoring not routed through the interpretation it
  tests (that the patient is aware), and it is what makes the 5-of-6 more
  than a reading of noise. For the patient's own loop the paper says
  nothing: no feedback was given, so no contingency between his intention
  and an outcome was restored in the session. Against Chaudhary et al.
  ([LIT-1200](../literature.d/LIT-1200.md)), this is communication with no neurofeedback and no
  training.
- **[QUESTION-121](../questions.d/QUESTION-121.md).** Nothing directly.
- **The LIT summary.** Correct in numbers. It should say the yes/no
  procedure was used with one patient, who was not one of the two without
  bedside behaviour, and give the 5-of-6 and 48-of-48 figures.
- **For the anthology.** No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- One communicating patient, six questions, one undecodable.
- Convenience sample; negatives uninterpretable; detection depends on
  traumatic aetiology in this sample, unexplained.
- The similarity metric and the full question list are in the
  Supplementary Appendix, not read here.
- The authors' "must have been aware" for any positive result is asserted.
- The main text does not identify the two behaviourally unresponsive
  responders or give their histories.

## Open questions

- Whether the two behaviourally unresponsive responders could answer
  questions. That would put judgement and the absence of detectable
  behaviour in one patient.
- Whether a patient's answers about the present (where they are, what
  happened today) are as accurate as about the past. That would bear on
  whether the model is kept in touch, which is [CLAIM-341](../claims.d/CLAIM-341.md)'s question,
  and not only retained.
