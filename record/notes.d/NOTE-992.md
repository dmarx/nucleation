# NOTE-992: Abdulkarim & Ehrsson — No causal link between hand position sense and limb ownership

## Contribution

Earlier work, Rohde et al. (2011) above all, showed that drift can occur
without ownership, but left open that drift might still be needed for
ownership. This study manipulates felt hand position directly during
the illusion, by moving the real hand without the participant noticing,
and finds that ownership does not follow. It is the converse
dissociation: a change in position sense with no change in ownership.

## Key insight

If the felt position of the real hand coming closer to the rubber hand
were part of what makes the rubber hand feel owned, then moving the real
hand closer should strengthen the illusion and moving it away should
weaken it. Neither happened. Ownership rides on the timing of seen and
felt touch; where the hidden hand is felt to be, within a range of
about 8 to 24 cm, does not enter.

## Assumptions

- **Mechanical displacement mimics drift.** The hand rested on Plexiglas
  sheets driven by a motor at 0.9 mm/s, "causing an 8-cm displacement to
  take approximately 90 s", aimed at no more than 0.3 deg/s at the elbow,
  below the cited threshold for sensing passive movement (p. 709). The
  authors grant that it differs from illusory drift, since moving the
  limb "likely produced increased proprioceptive inflow" (p. 717), and
  argue that the end result, a changed sense of position without felt
  movement, is the same.
- **Participants did not notice.** Statement S4 ("my real hand were
  drifting toward or away") was denied, and in Experiment 2 a
  post-experiment questionnaire asked about movement (pp. 709–710, 715–716).
- **The questionnaire measures the illusion.** Nine statements adapted
  from Botvinick and Cohen, −3 to +3: S1–S3 for the illusion, S4 for
  felt displacement, S5–S9 as "control statements for suggestibility and
  task compliance" (Table 1, p. 709). The outcome is an "illusion index",
  the mean of S1–S3 minus the mean of S4–S9. It was given "after the
  first unique repetition of each condition" (p. 710), so once per
  condition although each condition ran three times.
- **Pointing measures felt position.** Eyes closed, the left index finger
  slid along the table's edge until judged to be above the right index
  finger, from a random start (p. 710). Drift is post minus pre, "No
  corrections were thus made for the veridical hand position" (p. 710).
- **Asynchrony is a 500 ms lag.** The rubber hand was touched first and
  the real hand about 500 ms later, at one stroke per second (p. 709).

## Key results

- **Set-up (pp. 708–709, Fig. 1).** Right rubber hand on a small table;
  real right hand hidden 12.5 cm below on the displacement apparatus,
  always lateral to the rubber hand. Earplugs and headphones masked the
  motor. 90 s of brushing per trial; each condition three times.
  Experiment 1: n = 18 (9 women, mean age 29); Experiment 2: n = 18
  (8 women, mean age 28). Sample size "based on previous studies"
  (p. 711).
- **Experiment 1: toward against away (2 × 2 within subjects;
  pp. 711–713, Figs. 2–4).** The hand started 24 cm or 8 cm lateral to the
  rubber hand and ended at 16 cm. Illusion index: main effect of
  synchrony F(1,17) = 12.424, p = .003, η² = .125; no effect of direction
  F(1,17) = 1.321, p = .266; no interaction (p = .840). Synchronous against
  asynchronous: toward d = 0.577, away d = 0.620. From Fig. 3A, the
  synchronous indices are about 1.85 toward and 1.45 away, not
  significantly different. Drift: main effect of direction
  F(1,17) = 112.211, p < .001, η² = .682; no effect of synchrony
  (p = .165). From Fig. 3B, about +6 cm toward and −5 cm away, for an
  8 cm movement. Shift and illusion did not correlate significantly
  (toward r = .417, p = .085; away r = .304, p = .220).
- **Experiment 2: away against static (2 × 2; pp. 713–716, Figs. 5–8).**
  The hand started 8 cm lateral to the rubber hand and either stayed or
  moved to 16 cm. Illusion index: synchrony F(1,17) = 25.826, p < .001,
  η² = .379; displacement F(1,17) = 0.638, p = .443; no interaction
  (p = .884). Synchronous against asynchronous: static d = 1.759, away
  d = 1.232. From Fig. 6A, about 2.0 static and 2.1 away under
  synchrony. Drift: displacement F(1,17) = 251.245, p < .001, η² = .602;
  synchrony F(1,17) = 11.314, η² = .067. Static synchronous against
  asynchronous "t(17) = 1.984, p = .003 (two-tailed), d = 0.435";
  away, "t(1, 17) = 1.943, p = .057". From Fig. 6B, about +1.3 cm and
  −0.3 cm static, −4.5 cm and −5.5 cm away. In the static conditions
  illusion and shift correlated, "r = .562, p = .015" (Fig. 8); in the
  away conditions they did not (r = .123, p = .628).
- **Awareness of the movement (pp. 715–716, Fig. 9).** "11 participants
  experienced no movement"; seven reported some, but "none of them
  described a movement that could be attributed to the mechanical
  displacement". The authors read those seven reports as "most likely
  ... confabulation, task compliance, or imagination" (p. 717).
- **Conclusion (pp. 716–717).** Manipulating felt position "does not
  influence the subjective illusion, which implies that proprioceptive
  drift either happens as a consequence of the illusion or constitutes an
  independent, parallel process". Against Rohde et al.'s open
  possibility, "our results suggest that proprioceptive drift is not a
  prerequisite for the illusion". They are careful about the other
  direction: "our results merely demonstrate that it is possible to
  induce an illusion without it being accompanied by proprioceptive
  drift", and drift in other contexts "does not mean that the subjective
  rubber hand illusion cannot cause proprioceptive drift" (p. 717).
- **Models (pp. 717–718).** The result is "difficult to reconcile" with
  Botvinick and Cohen's three-way model and with Tsakiris's comparator
  model, whose posture comparison should have been affected by moving
  the hand. It fits Makin et al.'s peripersonal-space model, since the
  rubber hand stayed within reach of the hand, and "appear[s] to be
  consistent with" Samad et al.'s (2015) Bayesian causal-inference model,
  in which the illusion fades only near 30 cm. They "did not attempt to
  replicate the 'vision-only' condition", so "we cannot determine whether
  the asynchronous condition eliminates proprioceptive drift or the
  synchronous condition triggers it" (p. 718).
- **On measurement (p. 718).** "One should not rely on the proprioceptive
  drift measure alone", but drift "can be used as a valuable indirect
  measure ... if it is used with caution in well-controlled rubber hand
  illusion experiments when the real hand is immobile (or when only one
  finger is moved; see Kalckert & Ehrsson, 2012)". Skin temperature
  changes "appear to be quite difficult to reproduce".

## Concepts

- **Proprioceptive drift and shift** — drift is post minus pre pointing
  in one condition; shift is synchronous drift minus asynchronous drift,
  "a synchrony-specific measure" (p. 710).
- **Illusion index** — mean of the illusion statements minus mean of the
  control statements (p. 709).
- **Peripersonal (perihand) space** — the region around the hand within
  which visuotactile integration refers touch to a seen hand; the
  rubber hand here was "never farther than 24 cm" from the real one
  (p. 718).

## Connections

The study is built as a reply to Rohde, Di Luca and Ernst 2011
([LIT-1281](../literature.d/LIT-1281.md), [NOTE-1009](NOTE-1009.md)): they showed drift without ownership and left
open that drift could be a prerequisite of ownership; this tests that
and rejects it. It cites Holle et al. (2011), drift without ownership for
rotated hands, and Holmes and colleagues on visual capture. It reads its
result as consistent with Makin, Holmes and Ehrsson's peripersonal-space
model and Samad et al.'s Bayesian causal inference model, and against
Botvinick and Cohen's and Tsakiris's. Kalckert and Ehrsson 2012, filed in
parallel by another reader, is cited for a moving-finger variant in
which drift is still usable.

## Bearing on the record

- **[NOTE-1006](NOTE-1006.md) and [LIT-1277](../literature.d/LIT-1277.md) (Botvinick & Cohen 1998).** That note's open
  question, whether drift measures ownership, is answered in part: felt
  position can be changed by several centimetres without any change in
  ownership. The authors themselves say the result is hard to reconcile
  with Botvinick and Cohen's three-way model, in which distortion of
  position sense is how the illusion reconciles vision and touch.
- **[THEORY-765](../theory.d/THEORY-765.md) (Proposed).** It replicates the synchrony effect on
  the ownership items, twice, with named tests and effect sizes
  (d 0.58–1.76), so it partly meets the first half of `promote_when`. It
  does not meet the suggestion control: S5–S9 are labelled controls "for
  suggestibility and task compliance", which is the kind of control the
  THEORY's `promote_when` says is not enough, and expectation was not
  measured or matched. Its static condition reproduces drift toward the
  rubber hand under synchrony, but the key t-test is internally
  inconsistent (see Limitations), and without a vision-only condition it
  cannot tell, as the authors say, whether synchrony causes drift or
  asynchrony removes it. So it does not refute the drift clause either;
  that refutation comes from Rohde et al. One more thing it offers, mine
  to point out: 7 of 18 participants affirmed in a forced choice that
  their hand had moved and then described movements unlike the one the
  apparatus made.
  The authors call this "confabulation, task compliance, or imagination".
  It is a small, direct sign that forced-choice reports in this set-up
  are open to compliance, which is the worry behind the THEORY's
  suggestion control.
- **[THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md) (Proposed).** Two points, both modest. First, as with
  Rohde et al., ownership and drift came apart, so a promotion test that
  scores a model on "ownership and drift" is scoring two outputs, not
  one. Second, a common-cause model in which the spatial discrepancy
  between felt and seen hand lowers the probability of a common cause
  predicts some loss of ownership when the felt hand is moved 5–6 cm
  farther away. None was found. The authors answer this themselves: in
  Samad et al.'s model the spatial term matters only near 30 cm, beyond
  the range tested. So the result constrains the shape of such a model
  (flat spatial dependence under about 24 cm) rather than counting
  against the graded account. Gradedness and the real hand's loss of
  ownership are not tested.
- **[THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md) (Proposed).** As for Rohde et al.: drift in its
  `promote_when` is not a safe measure of ownership. This paper's own
  advice, drift only with the hand immobile and alongside other
  measures, would at least be a minimum for the cardio-visual studies.
- **[THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) (Proposed).** No bearing on whole-body
  self-identification or self-location, which were not measured.
- **[THEORY-458](../theory.d/THEORY-458.md) (Proposed).** No bearing: the hand was moved passively and
  unnoticed, and agency was not at issue. The paper notes in passing that
  drift remains usable "when only one finger is moved", which concerns
  the moving rubber hand of Kalckert and Ehrsson 2012, not this design.
- **[QUESTION-048](../questions.d/QUESTION-048.md).** The design is an intervention on the putative
  mechanism, not another correlation: change the behavioural variable
  and see whether the report follows. That is a stronger test of what a
  correlate shows than any correlation, and here the answer is that it
  does not follow. The same study then reports a correlation between the
  two in its static conditions (r = .562) and uses it to defend drift as
  a measure, which shows how a correlate can be valid as a sign and
  invalid as a mechanism.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice.

## Limitations

- **A null from 18 per experiment.** No power analysis, equivalence test
  or Bayes factor; "no causal link" in the title is stronger than "no
  significant effect". In Experiment 1 the synchronous index was
  numerically higher toward (about 1.85) than away (about 1.45), read off
  Fig. 3A, the direction a causal link would predict.
- **Within the illusion's known range.** The real hand was 8–24 cm from
  the rubber hand throughout. A null within the range where distance is
  already known to matter little is a weaker test than one crossing it.
- **Mechanical, not illusory, drift.** Moving the arm adds afferent
  signals the classic illusion lacks, as the authors grant.
- **Statistics that do not match.** The static synchronous–asynchronous
  drift test is given as "t(17) = 1.984, p = .003"; a t of 1.984 on 17
  degrees of freedom gives a two-tailed p of about .06 (my computation).
  Likewise F(1,17) = 11.314 is reported as p = .001 and F(1,17) = 0.982
  as p = .326, where I get about .004 and .34; "t(1, 17)" is malformed.
  The synchrony main effect on drift in Experiment 2 survives (p about
  .004), but the paper's one test of the classic drift effect, the
  validation of its set-up, is uncertain as printed.
- **A hint of a synchrony effect in the displaced hand.** In
  Experiment 2 the away-synchronous drift (about −4.5 cm) was about 1 cm
  less negative than away-asynchronous (about −5.5 cm), p = .057, much
  as in the static case. The discussion treats this as no difference.
- **Questionnaire once per condition,** after the first of three
  repetitions, and the awareness check in Experiment 2 only.
- **No vision-only condition,** so it cannot separate its result from
  Rohde et al.'s account of what drives drift.
- **No suggestion control** beyond the control statements.

## Open questions

- Does displacing the real hand beyond the illusion's spatial limit,
  past about 30 cm, reduce ownership? That would separate "position
  sense never matters" from "it matters only at larger distances".
- With a vision-only condition added, does the static synchronous drift
  exceed vision-only drift, contrary to Rohde et al.?
- Does ownership cause any part of drift? The authors leave this open,
  and the 1 cm synchronous residue in the displaced conditions is a place
  to look.
- How many of the reports in a rubber hand questionnaire are compliance?
  The seven unfounded movement reports suggest the question is not idle.
