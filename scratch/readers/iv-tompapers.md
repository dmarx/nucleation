### Summary
Tomasello aims to ground the sociologists' concept of social role (Parsons, Nadel, Mead, Goffman) in human psychological evolution and ontogeny. His thesis is that a participant can understand a role only inside a cooperative endeavor. The cooperation must be the specifically human kind, shared intentionality, in which partners form a joint agent "we" with a joint goal and individual roles (Bratman, Gilbert, Searle).

The paper first gives the two-step evolutionary story: joint intentionality among early humans, then collective intentionality among modern humans. It then reviews comparative experiments in which children, unlike chimpanzees, form joint goals, reverse and learn roles, protest normatively, and share the spoils equally.

It then derives two cognitive consequences of the "dual-level" structure (shared goal and attention; individual roles and perspectives):
- relational-thematic-narrative thinking: role-governed categories, argument structure in linguistic constructions, narratives;
- perspectival thinking, ending in a subjective/objective distinction that comes from triangulation in joint attention and discourse.

It also derives two social consequences:
- self-other equivalence, which grounds fairness and the first "role ideals" as normative standards, backed by joint commitment;
- concern for how "we" judge "me", which runs through impression management to moral identity, justification and guilt.

The conclusion admits a "bait and switch". Roles reduce to cooperation with shared agency. The objective-normative world is a human construction that externalizes conflicting perspectives and values.

### Contribution
It brings the Tomasello shared-intentionality programme to bear on role theory. Its one new organizing idea is the "dual-level structure", shared versus individual, which it uses to unify:
- perspectival and objective cognition,
- role-based relational concepts,
- fairness and normativity,
- moral identity.

It is a synthesis and review paper with no new data.

### Key results (section, printed page, verbatim)
1. Roles presuppose cooperation (§2, p. 4): "the notion of social role only arises in the context of cooperative endeavors".
2. Chimpanzee hunting gets the lean reading (§2.1, p. 5): "thus propose a “lean” reading of this". There is no joint goal and so there are no roles; each chimpanzee hunts for itself.
3. Origin of roles (§2.1, p. 6): roles "came into existence in human evolution with the emergence of shared intentionality". Also: "The notion of social role is thus indissociable, psychologically speaking,".
4. Comparative evidence on joint goals (§2.2, p. 7): "children are able to form a joint agency toward a joint goal in ways that chimpanzees are not". The evidence is Warneken et al. 2006 on re-engagement, Hamann et al. 2012 on persisting after one's own reward, and Melis et al. 2016 on joint commitments. Role reversal and role learning are Fletcher et al. 2012. Normative protest is Kachel et al. 2018. Equalizing after collaboration is Hamann et al. 2011 and Ulber & Tomasello 2017.
5. Role-governed concepts (§3.1, p. 9): humans create "what Markman and Stillwell (2001) call “role-governed categories”". Thinking is organized "in terms of a relational-thematic-narrative dimension" (p. 10).
6. Perspectives (§3.2, p. 12): "apes and very young human infants only imagine or track the epistemic states of others; they do not understand different perspectives on a common situation".
7. Fairness (§4.1, p. 13): "Self-other equivalence is the cognitive basis for a sense of fairness". Role ideals are "as the first, if still delimited, socially shared normative standards" (p. 13).
8. Joint commitment as collaborative self-regulation (§4.1, p. 14): "each of us agrees to join the other in judging whichever of us does not perform her role in the ideal way as normatively defective". Also: "Collaborative activities with individual roles are thus the natural home of".
9. Reputation versus morality (§4.2, p. 15): "Children's strategic impression management is not moral; it is strategic." Being able to simulate others' judgments of oneself is the foundation of conscience and guilt.
10. The admitted gap (§5, p. 16): "I have provided no evidence here that playing local roles in spontaneous collaboration with". The claim that local roles are a prerequisite for societal roles is therefore not shown.
11. Constructivist close (§5, p. 17): "the human objective-normative world derives from the way that human individuals across evolutionary and developmental time construct a kind of externalized explanation".
12. Pointer to work 2 (§4.1, p. 13): "things become especially clear at around three years of age when children's collaborative activities begin to take a normative turn (Tomasello, 2018b)".

### Assumptions
- Roles exist for a participant only if that participant represents them. The functionalist ascription of roles to bees, rivers or chimpanzees is the observer's construal.
- Bratman, Gilbert and Searle describe the psychology correctly.
- Ape–child contrasts in experimental collaboration tasks reveal species differences, not differences in task familiarity or rearing.
- The evolutionary two-step story is "speculative" (p. 5).
- The ontogenetic order runs from local roles to societal ones (admitted unproven).

### Bearings on held entries
- LIT-125 / NOTE-185 / THEORY-495 (Tomasello, obligation): **parallel/supports.** This is a shorter version of the same account. Role ideals, self-other equivalence, joint commitment as collaborative self-regulation, and guilt as repair of moral identity all reappear, with the same experiments. It adds the cognitive half (perspectives, objectivity, thematic concepts), which THEORY-495 lacks.
- LIT-504 (Natural History of Human Morality, unread): **background.** It is cited as Tomasello 2016 for the two-step story and moral identity. This paper is a partial open stand-in for its ch. 2–3 line.
- LIT-488, LIT-471 (Bratman), LIT-482 (Searle, social ontology), THEORY-625 (Searle, status functions): **background.** Tomasello adopts Bratman's shared cooperative activity, and Searle's and Gilbert's we-intentions, as the psychology. Institutions with "well-defined roles" scale up from collaboration (p. 6). That is a developmental genealogy for THEORY-625's collective acceptance, not an analysis of it.
- TERM-080 (senses of collective intention): **qualifies.** Tomasello's joint/collective split maps onto the term's participatory and institutionally constituted senses. Its participatory sense is grounded developmentally.
- THEORY-1605 / NOTE-1266 (Salice & Miyazono, group identification): **parallel.** "We" judging "me" is group identification in their sense. Tomasello does not separate the two processes they distinguish.
- THEORY-414 (institutions as correlated equilibria) and LIT-473 (Lewis, Convention): **contrast.** For Tomasello, roles and institutions need shared intentionality and normativity, not only equilibrium expectations. Coordination without a joint goal is the "lean" chimpanzee case.
- THEORY-457 (partner choice and proportional division): **parallel.** Fair division arises inside collaboration. Partner choice appears through Herrmann et al. 2019 (competing to look cooperative before a choosing observer).
- LIT-440 (Dennett, Intentional Systems): **contrast, weak.** Ascribing roles from outside ("from our external point of view", p. 16) is stance-taking. Tomasello insists that a participant's understanding differs from that.
- THEORY-060 (life story as diachronic identity), NOTE-383 (Against Narrativity): **parallel, weak.** Narrative concepts and moral identity are treated as products of the dual-level structure.
- Unmerged branch works (Tomasello 2005, 2012 interdependence, Tomasello & Vaish 2013, Evolution of Agency 2022): **parallel.** The paper cites Tomasello et al. 2005 for the lean hunt reading. The interdependence step (p. 6) is the 2012 hypothesis.

### Limitations
- A review by the theory's author. Almost all the cited experiments come from his own lab.
- The prerequisite claim is explicitly unsupported (§5).
- The cognitive claims (thematic thinking, objectivity) are argued, not tested.
- The ape contrasts rest on captive chimpanzees in human-designed tasks.
- Cultural variation is not addressed.

### Candidate THEORY sentence
Tomasello (2020) argues that a participant can understand themselves as playing a social role only inside cooperation structured by shared intentionality, a joint goal with individual roles. He holds that taking part in such dual-level collaboration, which chimpanzees do not do, is what leads young children to perspectival and objective thinking, role-based relational concepts, a sense of self-other equivalence and fairness, and a normative moral identity.
- promote_when: Evidence that within children, experience of reciprocal roles in collaboration with a joint goal predicts perspective-distinction, role-concept and fairness outcomes over and above matched non-collaborative interaction, as it does not in apes given the same experience. Also, in some population, a dissociation in which these outcomes appear without such collaborative experience would count against the claim.

### Summary (abstract only)
The Cooperation Theory treats morality as a special form of cooperation. Before age 3, children help, share, and collaborate as equally deserving partners. Around 3, a respect for normative standards appears together across four contexts: joint commitments, fairness to partners, enforcement of social norms, and guilt. Tomasello proposes a transactional causal model to explain why they appear at once. Maturing capacities for shared intentionality make new forms of cooperative interaction possible, and those interactions in turn foster and guide moral development.

### Contribution (from abstract)
It names the simultaneous emergence of normativity at about age 3 across four domains as an explanandum. It answers with a transactional model, maturation plus interaction, rather than either alone.

### Key results (abstract; Crossref text, p. 248)
- "The Cooperation Theory of moral development starts from the premise that morality is a special form of cooperation."
- "at around the age of 3, their social interactions are transformed by an emerging understanding of, and respect for, normative standards"
- "Three-year-olds become capable of making and respecting joint commitments, treating collaborative partners fairly, enforcing social norms, and feeling guilty when they violate any of these."
- "The almost simultaneous emergence of a normative attitude in all of these interactional contexts demands explanation."
- "We suggest a transactional causal model"
- I did not read the body, so I have no section-level results.

### Assumptions (from abstract)
- The four behaviours share one normative attitude.
- Their co-emergence is real, not an artefact of the ages tested.
- Shared intentionality matures.

### Concepts
Cooperation Theory of moral development; normative turn; shared intentionality; joint commitment; norm enforcement; guilt; transactional causal model.

### Bearings
- THEORY-495 / LIT-125 / NOTE-185: **parallel/supports.** THEORY-495 dates the onset of obligation to about age three inside joint commitments. This is the earlier statement of that dating, and NOTE-185 already lists the matching experiments.
- LIT-504: **background** (the 2016 book on which the Cooperation Theory rests).
- Unmerged Tomasello & Vaish 2013: **parallel.** It covers the same two-step moral ontogeny.

### Limitations
Abstract only, so nothing should be filed beyond what the abstract states.

### Candidate THEORY sentence (abstract-level only)
Tomasello (2018) holds that at about age three children's cooperative interactions take a normative turn. Joint commitment, fairness to partners, norm enforcement and guilt emerge nearly together, which he explains transactionally: maturing shared-intentionality capacities enable new cooperative interactions, and these in turn guide moral development.
- promote_when: Longitudinal evidence that the four normative behaviours emerge together within children at about age three. Their co-emergence should be predicted jointly by a shared-intentionality measure and by experience of cooperative interaction, with neither alone sufficient.

### Summary (from the poster)
The question is whether the human solution to coordination survives with an AI partner. That solution is shared agency: forming shared intentions, committing to them, and signalling goals.

The game is non-verbal. Two players each move one step at a time on a map, and a round succeeds if both reach the same goal. A new alternative goal can appear midway, which tests commitment. Every partner is described as "another player", and there are four AI partners:
- individual-utility RL ("asocial");
- joint-utility RL ("social tool");
- GPT-4.1 mini;
- GPT-4.1 mini prompted to infer the partner's goal first (LLM + ToM).

The measures are success, efficiency, commitment and signalling moves.

Study 1 (adults, N = 150): human–human pairs had the highest success, efficiency and commitment. Adults adapted to each AI. LLMs were suboptimal, though ToM prompting helped. Minimally social partners (joint RL, LLM-ToM) drew out human signalling. Commitment predicted efficiency (p < .001).

Study 2 (5–7-year-olds, N = 109, ongoing): children adapted but were less efficient with individual RL, which their lower joint commitment predicted. Commitment and signalling emerged with joint RL.

Ongoing work: a "shared-agency model" AI partner. Pilot data show adults are more committed, efficient and likely to signal with it.

### Contribution
The study operationalizes "signatures of shared agency" (commitment, signalling) in a minimal game. It shows that these signatures, and their link to efficiency, fall off with AI partners whose agency is outcome optimization. It names this the "agency gap" and draws the design conclusion that human-compatible AI needs something like shared agency, not only better outcomes.

### Key results (poster, single page; quotes verbatim)
1. Premise: "Humans evolved shared agency to solve this" coordination problem. Humans "they form shared intentions, commit to them, and use" signalling (Motivation; citing Searle 1990 and Tomasello 2022).
2. Diagnosis of current AI: it "functions more like a tool (asocial or social) than as a" "genuinely collaborative agent." (Motivation; these are two consecutive poster lines.)
3. Control: "(Every partner is described neutrally as “another player”)" (Design).
4. Adults, "Study 1: Adults (N = 150)":

   - "Commitment predict efficiency (p < .001)."

   - The efficiency and signalling bar-to-label mapping is not recoverable reliably from the text layer, so it is not reported.

6. Conclusion 1: human–human collaboration was "marked by uniquely high levels of" joint commitment and efficiency, both reduced with AI. Conclusion 3: "Breaking joint commitments" "makes collaboration harder for kids."

### Assumptions
- Persisting toward the first goal when an alternative appears measures *joint* commitment.
- Movement choices that disambiguate one's goal are communicative "signalling".
- A neutral "another player" framing equates partners' perceived agency.
- RL with a joint objective is "minimal sociality".

### Concepts
- agency gap
- shared agency
- joint commitment
- social signalling (goal transparency)
- asocial tool vs social tool vs collaborative agent
- outcome optimization vs reverse-engineered social agency
- LLM + theory-of-mind prompting

### Bearings
- Unmerged *The Evolution of Agency* (Tomasello 2022): **supports/applies.** It is cited on the poster as the source of the shared-agency premise, and it turns that premise into a test of human–AI coordination.
- THEORY-495 / LIT-125: **parallel.** Commitment is the measured mark of a joint agent. The children's cost when partners break commitment fits obligation-as-"we" pressure, but the poster does not test normativity (no protest measure).
- LIT-488 / LIT-471 / LIT-593 / LIT-567 / THEORY-076 (Bratman on intention, commitment and shared agency): **background.** The commitment measure is Bratmanian intention inertia, and the lab's 2025 paper cites Bratman 1987 for "resistance to reconsideration".
- LIT-473 (Lewis) / THEORY-414 (correlated equilibria): **contrast.** The game is a pure coordination game. An equilibrium-only agent (RL) reaches near-equal success with adults (98.9 vs 100) yet shows less commitment. Outcome-level coordination therefore does not show shared agency.
- LIT-440 (Dennett, Intentional Systems): **contrast.** The AI partners are agents only in the stance sense, "agency" as "a byproduct of optimizing predefined objectives". The authors deny that this suffices for collaboration.
- THEORY-1791 (Bansal et al., human–AI teams): **parallel.** Both concern complementarity in human–AI teams. This study measures process signatures rather than accuracy.

### Limitations
- Poster only, and Study 2 is ongoing. Children's N and results may change in the paper.
- The mapping of figure values is reconstructed from the PDF layout.
- No statistics are shown except one p-value and asterisks.
- The LLM partners are one small model.
- Commitment and signalling are operational proxies whose link to *shared* intention is assumed.
- Everything rests on a grid game and a single paradigm.

### Candidate THEORY sentence
Cheng, Gao, Tomasello and Kushnir (2026) report that in a non-verbal coordination game, human–human pairs show the highest joint commitment and coordination efficiency, and both drop when the partner is an AI optimizing an objective. Adults and 5–7-year-olds adapt to AI partners, but children pay an efficiency cost that their reduced joint commitment predicts. Minimally social AI partners (joint-utility RL, theory-of-mind-prompted LLMs) still draw out human signalling.
- promote_when: The full paper's analyses confirm higher commitment and efficiency for human–human than for each AI pairing under matched partner framing, and the children's commitment–efficiency relation holds at the final sample, with a replication in a second coordination paradigm.
