# LIT-1271: Trait phenomenological control predicts experience of mirror synaesthesia and the rubber hand illusion

Lush, P., Botan, V., Scott, R. B., Seth, A. K., Ward, J. & Dienes, Z. (2020). Trait phenomenological control predicts experience of mirror synaesthesia and the rubber hand illusion. *Nature Communications* 11, 4853. DOI 10.1038/s41467-020-18591-6.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-990](../notes.d/NOTE-990.md).*

- **The ownership reports track a suggestion trait.** In 353
  participants, each point of SWASH hypnotisability (0–5) went with about
  0.57 points more agreement (−3 to +3) with the touch, cause and
  ownership items after synchronous brushing (rs 0.26), and 0.70 points
  after asynchronous brushing. The visual "control" item, that the rubber
  hand came to resemble one's own, was predicted at least as well (rs
  0.31).
- **The drift tracks it only weakly.** Synchronous drift rose about
  0.6 cm per SWASH point (rs 0.11, R² 0.02); asynchronous drift showed no
  sensitive evidence either way. Combined with an earlier study's
  difference-score correlation, drift was related to hypnotisability (r
  0.22).
- **The standard controls do not control for suggestion.** Asynchronous
  scores and control items are predicted by the same trait, and
  participants expect the illusion under synchrony and not under
  asynchrony. A synchronous-minus-asynchronous difference is therefore no
  check against expectation unless expectations and difficulty are
  matched across conditions, which the authors say future studies must
  do.
- **The causal test failed.** Telling participants that asynchrony would
  work best did not change their expectations, so the pre-registered
  analyses could not be run. The pre-registered test that was run,
  hypnotisability against the synchrony difference in the uninstructed
  group, gave anecdotal evidence for drift and insensitive evidence for
  the questionnaire. The headline analyses were not pre-registered.
- **Mirror-sensory synaesthesia too.** Hypnotisability predicted the
  number and intensity of vicarious pain responses (R² 0.07–0.08) and
  mirror-touch responses to humans and to objects (R² 0.04–0.06).

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-20 at the owner's request for experimental work that
builds on the rubber hand illusion and bears on the record's accounts of
it. It is the study that tests the alternative the record's synchrony
THEORY, [THEORY-765](../theory.d/THEORY-765.md), most needs excluded. It measures trait
suggestibility and expectations in a large sample with named tests and
effect sizes, which is the form that THEORY's `promote_when` asks for. It
does not show that the synchrony effect survives suggestion, because the
expectation manipulation failed and the difference-score test was
inconclusive, so it neither promotes nor refutes that THEORY. It does show
that the questionnaire evidence Botvinick & Cohen 1998 ([LIT-1277](LIT-1277.md))
and Lenggenhager et al. 2007 ([LIT-1279](LIT-1279.md)) rest on is confounded with a
suggestion trait. The same applies to the cardio-visual contrasts Seth
2013 ([LIT-1263](LIT-1263.md)) reports, and Seth is a co-author here. The NOTE says what
it does to each THEORY.

It is half of a dispute the record is filing together. Lush 2020 in
Collabra, on demand characteristics in the illusion, is its companion. The
Nature Communications exchange between Ehrsson et al. 2022 and Lush & Seth
2022 contests this paper's inference, and Roseboom & Lush 2022, also in
Collabra, is filed alongside them. It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.

One of the sources of [THEORY-766](../theory.d/THEORY-766.md) (filed 2026-10-20), which restates the rubber hand synchrony effect as a difference in reports, separates drift from those reports, and leaves open whether expectation produces the difference.
