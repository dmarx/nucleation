# LIT-tmp2jrx6: Copyright's Paradox

Netanel, N. W. (2008). *Copyright's Paradox*. New York: Oxford University Press. CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 (Oxford Scholarship Online).

Not read. The summary is drawn from the publisher's description and table
of contents (as given in the Instituto Autor library record), the
chapter titles Crossref lists, and the abstract of Shyamkrishna
Balganesh's review (118 Yale L.J. 1126, 2009). A reading should check it
first.

## What the sources say it argues

- **The paradox.** Copyright is "at once an engine of free expression and
  impediment to free expression". Melville Nimmer called the conflict a
  "largely ignored paradox" in 1970; the book asks why it has since come
  to the fore, what it puts at stake and how to resolve it (publisher).
- **The digital setting.** It sets the expansion of copyright holders'
  control against the new ability to "digitally cut, paste, edit, remix,
  and distribute" recordings, films, graphics and texts (publisher).
- **The plan.** Nine chapters: an introduction (p. 3); from *Mein Kampf*
  to Google (p. 13); what freedom of speech is and how it bears on
  copyright (p. 30); copyright's expansion (p. 54); whether copyright is
  the engine of free expression (p. 81); its free speech burdens (p. 109);
  the propertarian counter-argument (p. 154); copyright and the First
  Amendment (p. 169); and remaking copyright in the First Amendment's
  image (p. 195).
- **The standard.** Taking First Amendment values as the guide, copyright
  "should be delimited by how it can best promote robust debate and
  expressive diversity" (publisher).
- **The democratic paradigm.** Balganesh reads the book as continuing
  Netanel's view of copyright as a state mechanism for the democratic
  character of civil society, set against both market-based and
  minimalist accounts.

## Why this is here

Filed at the owner's request on 2026-10-20 as contemporary work in
conversation with Lessig's *Remix* (LIT-tmpreyek) at the intersection of
property and expression. Lessig's argument that copyright now regulates
ordinary expression because it regulates copies is a copyright-against-speech
argument, and his fifth reform, a levy to decriminalize file sharing,
cites Netanel by name (NOTE-tmptp3k0, ch. 9). Netanel is the
constitutional and doctrinal version of that argument. A reading would
check whether Lessig's copy-trigger complaint holds up once speech
values, not the copy, set copyright's scope, and what the levy proposal
looks like in its own author's hands.

It bears on the record's translation line (CLAIM-002, QUESTION-002) only
at a distance: as described, it is about how far law should let one
person's expression be built on, not about what survives the building.
