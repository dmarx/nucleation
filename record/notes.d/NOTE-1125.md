# NOTE-1125: Bjørnskov, Voigt & Khesali — Unconstitutional states of emergency (2021 working paper)

## Contribution

Nine in ten constitutions carry emergency provisions, but whether
governments keep to them had not been measured across countries. The paper
builds the first cross-national list of states of emergency (SOEs) coded for
lawfulness, splits unlawfulness into declaration, conduct during the
emergency and prolongation, and asks what goes with it. It identifies "115
out of 853 analyzed SOEs as unconstitutional" (p. 3).

## Key insight

The cause matters more than the constitution. Natural disasters are
exogenous; political turmoil is "highly endogenous to government behavior"
(p. 8) and threatens the government's survival, so an executive has more
reason to overstep after turmoil. Nearly all unlawful emergencies follow
turmoil, and the authors read that as executives acting "not to further
some common good but, rather, to safeguard and possibly extend their own
power" (p. 25).

## Assumptions

- **Universe.** Hafner-Burton et al. (2011), updated to 2017, restricted to
  national-level SOEs that could be confirmed: 853 cases, 1949–2017
  (pp. 12–13).
- **Coding of unlawfulness (pp. 13–14).** Factiva and Lexis/Nexis searches
  for "state of emergency" with "unlawful"; Freedom House and other NGO
  reports; and SOE length against the constitutional maximum, coded
  unlawful when no lawful extension was found. Evaluations by courts, law
  professors, NGOs and journalists all count, so the cases "could also be
  called “suspected to be unlawful.”" (p. 14). Only three of the 115 breach
  statute rather than the constitution (p. 13, fn. 8).
- **Under-reporting acknowledged (pp. 14–16).** English-language, rich-world
  press; press freedom; digitization over time ("the vast majority of
  unlawful states of emergency took place post 1990", read as an artefact);
  long-standing SOEs in Jordan, Syria and Zimbabwe with no report of
  unlawfulness.
- **Regime type** from Cheibub et al. as updated (p. 17); emergency
  constitution from the authors' Index of Emergency Powers, cost and benefit
  components (p. 19).
- **Estimation.** Simple logits, because "there is amazingly little
  systematic structure in the data" (p. 20); endogeneity of turmoil
  conceded, and a selection correction judged infeasible (pp. 20–21).

## Key results

- **Table 1 (p. 15).** Democracies: 271 SOEs after natural disasters (8
  unlawful), 241 after domestic turmoil (41). Autocracies: 68 (6) and 245
  (56).
- **Phases (Table 2, p. 16; p. 22).** Declaration 19, conduct 65,
  prolongation 37 in Table 2 and 39 in the text, overlapping. 97 unlawful
  SOEs followed turmoil, 14 natural disasters, five man-made events
  (p. 22).
- **Duration (p. 22).** Of lawful SOEs, 63 per cent ended exactly at their
  maximum, 15 per cent earlier, 22 per cent were lawfully extended. "Lawful
  SOEs last 115 days on average while the average duration of unlawful SOEs
  is 321 days" (p < .01).
- **Full sample (Table 4, p. 23).** Democracy −0.849 (s.e. 0.228), log GDP
  per capita −0.275; presidential 0.547 and bicameral 0.398 (both p < .10),
  second-chamber approval 0.593 (p < .05). Pseudo-R² 0.047–0.070.
- **Domestic events only (Table 5).** Democracy −0.52 to −0.92; bicameral
  0.532, Cost INEP 2.688 and second-chamber approval 0.700 significant;
  investment price −0.479. Recession −0.409 and the conditions index 0.277
  are not significant in the logits. Pseudo-R² 0.013–0.039.
- **Democracies only (Table 6).** Government price level −1.184, executive
  respect for the constitution −0.622, Benefit INEP −1.801 (p < .10) and
  second-chamber approval 0.724; nationalist/populist 0.708 (s.e. 0.688),
  not significant.
- **Simple differences (Table A1).** Among domestic events, recession years
  show 30 per cent unlawful against 10 (p < .01), and constitutions with
  more grounds for emergency 25 against 16 (p < .02).

## Concepts

- **De jure/de facto gap**: government behaviour under an SOE that departs
  from the constitution's emergency provisions (abstract).
- **Three phases (pp. 5–6)**: unlawful declaration (no event, a ground not
  in the constitution, the wrong actor, formal conditions unmet); unlawful
  conduct during the SOE; unlawful prolongation. A fourth, measures that
  outlive the SOE, is named and not measured: "we have no data on this
  aspect of unlawful emergency behavior" (p. 6, fn. 2).
- **Index of Emergency Powers (INEP)**: cost components (who declares, who
  approves, how many grounds) and benefit components (power to dissolve
  parliament, suspend rights, expropriate, censor), each 0–1 (p. 19).

## Connections

It builds on the authors' earlier work: harder declaration means fewer SOEs
(2018a); more emergency benefits go with more disaster deaths (2021b); SOEs
after terrorist attacks go with more attacks (2020). It cites Lührmann and
Rooney (2020), by the author of [LIT-1485](../literature.d/LIT-1485.md), for the finding that
governments declaring an SOE move towards autocracy more often (pp. 4, 7,
25).

## Bearing on the record

- **[CLAIM-445](../claims.d/CLAIM-445.md) (protective regimes become self-maintaining).** Cited for
  the gap between emergency law and emergency practice. The reading
  supports one strand of the claim, and less of it than the citation
  implies.
  - **Supports, the strand of prolongation.** Unlawful emergencies last
    nearly three times as long, and 66 per cent of unlawful SOEs run past
    their constitutional expiry against 22 per cent of lawful ones
    lawfully extended (p. 22). Emergencies that escape their legal limits
    persist. The authors' hypothesis 5, that an executive prolongs an SOE
    to postpone review of what it did during it (p. 11), is the claim's
    epistemic and political feedbacks in one: the emergency defers the
    judgement of itself. It was not tested.
  - **Supports the claim's own caution.** Nothing here measures whether the
    threat had declined. Natural disasters, where the threat is exogenous,
    rarely yield unlawful SOEs; political turmoil, partly the government's
    own doing, usually does. That is a correlation between endogenous
    threat and unlawfulness, not the residue the claim's test asks for.
  - **Does not support** the persistence of emergency measures beyond the
    emergency, the regime becoming self-maintaining in the full sense. The
    authors "suspect that at least in some cases the measures implemented
    during an SOE survive its end and become standard practice. To
    ascertain whether this really is the case, more fine-grained data are
    needed" (p. 25).
  - **The word.** The paper uses "unlawful" and "unconstitutional"
    interchangeably (p. 13, fn. 8); the exchange's "unconstitutional" is
    the paper's title. Either is short for "suspected to be unlawful".
  - Filed as [THEORY-911](../theory.d/THEORY-911.md).
- **[CLAIM-446](../claims.d/CLAIM-446.md).** Only by analogy: the prolongation hypothesis has the
  shape of a defence that defers the evidence against it.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **A working paper, not the version of record.** The JLS article may
  differ; only the abstract was compared.
- **Coding by report.** Unlawfulness is what the press and NGOs said, or a
  duration past the constitutional maximum with no extension found; the
  authors call it subjective and expect undercounting.
- **Correlational and weak.** Pseudo-R² at most 0.07; the authors say they
  "cannot claim that our findings are fully causal" (p. 3).
- **Inconsistencies.**
  - The odds ratio from Table 4's democracy coefficient (−0.849) is about
    0.43, not "about 75 %" (p. 23).
  - The conclusion's nationalist or populist executives "far more likely"
    (p. 25) rests on an insignificant coefficient (0.708, s.e. 0.688).
  - Recession and the number of emergency grounds, in the abstract, are
    significant only as simple differences (Table A1), not in the logits.
  - Prolongation cases are 37 in Table 2 and 39 in the text.
  - "exactly two thirds of all unlawful states of emergency remain in place
    too long" (p. 25) is the 66 per cent past expiry, not the 34 per cent
    coded unlawful for prolongation.

## Open questions

- Do measures adopted under an SOE survive it? The authors' fourth phase,
  unmeasured.
- Is prolongation driven by fear of ex post review (hypothesis 5)? A
  comparison of SOEs with and without independent ex post judicial review
  of emergency conduct would test it.
- Does the JLS version repair the inconsistencies?
