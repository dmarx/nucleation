# THEORY-tmpwt63q: Whether an account of organismal goal-directedness must separate genuine teleology from mere self-organisation, such as a candle flame, depends on what the account is for: a scientific model of agency need not and perhaps cannot draw the line, while an account of how mind and value fit into nature may have to, and need not be operationalisable

## Source

Nahas & Sachs (2023), "What's at stake in the debate over naturalizing
teleology?", [LIT-192](../literature.d/LIT-192.md) (*Synthese*), read in full in [NOTE-094](../notes.d/NOTE-094.md) from the accepted
manuscript. The version of record was not seen.

## What was actually shown

An informal metatheoretical argument; no formal or empirical method.

- **Two orientations (§§3.1–3.3).** One is scientific: does an
  organism-centred account improve biology, given the limits of the Modern
  Synthesis? It needs operationalisable models, which may trade truth for
  usefulness (Potochnik, Wimsatt). The other is philosophical: does
  organismal teleology reshape the liberal-versus-scientific naturalism
  debate about mind in nature? It needs claims "broadly true", not
  "concretely useful", and "incurs no obligation to operationalize its
  terms" (§3.3).
- **The demarcation dispute, in the scientific context (§4.1).** For models,
  telling organisms from candle flames and tornadoes is "unavoidable and not
  particularly problematic". Closure of constraints, as a model, is unlikely
  ever to meet Cusimano and Sterner's objectivity criterion, and need not.
  McShea and Babcock's field theory, which applies equally to bacteria and
  heat-seeking missiles, is charged with "pernicious reification" for
  treating a model's silence on intrinsic and extrinsic purpose as a fact
  about the world.
- **In the philosophical context (§4.2).** García-Valdecasas prefers Deacon's
  teleodynamics to the organisational approach because it yields a
  beneficiary individual. On the authors' reading that preference rests on
  an Aristotelian conception of teleology, which "would need to be argued,
  rather than assumed", and not on biology.
- **The conclusion (§5).** Accounts that look like rivals may be compatible
  answers to different questions. A plurality is "tempting to suggest".

What could have come out otherwise: the two critiques and the field
theory's claim might each have held within one project. The paper shows, for
one dispute, that each crosses the two.

## What this does not say

- **That the debates in general dissolve.** Only the demarcation dispute is
  worked, in two contexts ([NOTE-094](../notes.d/NOTE-094.md), corrections).
- **Where the two projects divide.** They are called "relatively
  autonomous", but the relation is conceded to be "far more complex"
  (fn. 19), and closure could serve either role (fn. 16). How the criteria
  interact when they conflict is not said.
- **That no single account can serve both.** The authors are "admittedly
  skeptical" and leave it open (§3.3).
- **That Deacon is right for naturalism, or Walsh and the organisational
  approach for biology.** These are suggested without argument; Walsh and
  the organisational approach "remain largely untested".
- **That there are only two projects.** Normative uses of teleology (welfare,
  moral status) and individuation are not separated out.
- **Anything about artificial systems.** The paper does not discuss AI. That
  a graded, field-relative measure applies to artefacts by design, and so
  would not settle intrinsic agency, is [NOTE-094](../notes.d/NOTE-094.md)'s extrapolation.
