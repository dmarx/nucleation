# NOTE-tmpyj4e9: A comparison of minimal group induction procedures

## Contribution

A methods paper. Forty years of minimal-group research had used
induction procedures that "have never been formally evaluated" (p. 81).
It compares four of them on the same implicit and explicit measures. It
also imports a procedure from implicit-partisanship research in which
participants are never put in a group at all. The authors recommend that
procedure, and recommend against the classic one's deception.

## Key insight

Explicit favouritism is easy to produce and hard to move. Telling people
they prefer one painter, telling them to imagine being assigned, assigning
them at random, and having them memorize five names all gave about the
same explicit in-group preference. The implicit measures separated the
procedures. Studying a group's names, with no claim that one belongs to
it, tied that group to "I", "me" and "mine" more strongly than being told
one belonged to it. The authors' explanation is that "the focused study of
individuating information about one group automatically creates and
strengthens links between participants' self-concepts and the studied
group" (p. 95).

## Assumptions

- **The IAT measures association.** The identification IAT pairs the
  groups with self words (I, me, mine, my, self) and other words (other,
  their, theirs, them, they); the attitude IAT with pleasant and
  unpleasant words. Scored with the D measure (p. 86).
- **Bonus money to two others is a behavioural measure.** Participants
  rated seven splits of left-over bonus money between two other people in
  the session, one from each group. They could not pay themselves, but
  were told they "could receive money from both ingroup and outgroup
  members" (p. 90).
- **"Ingroup" means the memorized, imagined or assigned group** (note 1).
  In the memorization condition it is a group the participant was never
  told they belonged to.

## Key results

- **Experiment 1** (pp. 84–88). 44 students at a small public college in
  the eastern US; 42 analysed (13 memorization, 15 imagination, 14
  classic). All conditions heard a cover story about students divided by
  art preference.
  - Attitude IAT: overall above zero, F(1, 36) = 11.47, p = .002; varied
    by condition, F(2, 36) = 7.81, p = .002, ηp² = .30. Memorization beat
    imagination (d = .96) and classic (d = 1.60). Only memorization
    differed from zero on its own (note 2).
  - Identification IAT: overall F(1, 36) = 13.43, p = .001; by condition
    F(2, 36) = 13.69, ηp² = .43. Memorization beat imagination (d = 1.55)
    and classic (d = 2.16). Again only memorization differed from zero
    (note 3).
  - Explicit attitude: in-group favoured, Mz = .55, ηp² = .30; no
    condition effect, F(2, 36) = .08, p = .93.
  - Explicit identification: Mz = .65, ηp² = .43; no condition effect,
    F(2, 36) = .19, p = .83.
  - Correlations: implicit–explicit r = .16 (attitude) and .34
    (identification). Attitude–identification r = .66 on the IATs and .71
    on explicit measures (p. 88).
- **Experiment 2** (pp. 89–93). 74 students, 73 analysed (24
  memorization, 24 random, 25 classic). The art cover story was dropped
  from memorization and random. A manipulation check was answered
  correctly by 86%.
  - Attitude IAT: by condition F(2, 67) = 13.13, ηp² = .28. Memorization
    beat random (d = .89) and classic (d = 1.49). Memorization and random
    differed from zero; classic did not (note 5).
  - Identification IAT: by condition F(2, 67) = 11.50, ηp² = .26.
    Memorization beat random (d = 1.06) and classic (d = 1.30). Only
    memorization differed from zero (note 7).
  - Explicit attitude Mz = .66 and identification Mz = .72, neither
    varying reliably by condition (p = .10, p = .07).
  - Bonus money (Table 1). On a 1–7 scale, splits favouring the in-group
    were endorsed above the midpoint and those favouring the out-group
    below it. Maximum relative in-group advantage: classic 4.24, random
    5.13, memorization 5.29. Equal split ("min dif") did not differ from
    the midpoint, p = .11.
  - Correlations (Table 2). The in-group-preference factor of the
    allocations correlated with explicit identification r = .37
    (p < .01), explicit attitude .27, implicit attitude .24 and implicit
    identification .23 (each p < .05). Nothing correlated reliably with
    the out-group-preference factor.
  - Group colour (note 8). Participants assigned to Red gave weaker
    in-group-favouring ratings on the "own" options. Taken separately,
    Red-assigned participants did not favour the in-group significantly on
    maximum relative own, maximum own or maximum joint own. They did
    reject the out-group-favouring options.
- **Recommendations** (pp. 94–95).
  - The classic procedure is fine for explicit measures, but "a prudent
    recommendation" is to use another if implicit favouritism is the
    interest.
  - Random assignment "does not strongly instill strong identity bonds"
    on implicit measures.
  - Memorization was "the most broadly successful".
  - Implicit and explicit favouritism show "little correspondence".
  - The classic procedure's deception "should" be discouraged.

## Concepts

- **implicit partisanship**: preference for, and self-association with, a
  group created by studying its members' names (Greenwald et al. 2002).
- **identification IAT**: the speed of pairing a group with self words
  rather than other words. An implicit measure of the self–group link.
- **multiple alternative matrices (MAMs)**: seven allocation options that
  separate maximum relative in-group advantage from maximum in-group gain,
  maximum joint gain and equality (Bornstein et al. 1983).
- **minimal group induction**: any procedure that creates novel group
  membership with no history, interaction or stake.

## Connections

- **Tajfel et al. 1971 ([LIT-tmp6t6ny](../literature.d/LIT-tmp6t6ny.md))**, Unavailable. The paper describes
  the classic procedure only generically (dots or paintings, false
  feedback, allocation to others) and states no result of the original.
- **Robertson et al. ([NOTE-tmp0cdsp](NOTE-tmp0cdsp.md)).** Robertson's preregistered
  replication (n = 590) found minimal-group favouritism in moral judgment
  only as it rose with identification; the main contrast was p = .07.
  Pinter and Greenwald point the same way on a different outcome.
  Allocation favouritism correlated with explicit identification (r = .37),
  and the overall effect rested largely on one colour group. Two
  differences matter. Here the mean participant identified with the
  in-group in every condition. And this was a lab study of a few dozen
  people per cell, with identification measured before the allocations.
  Neither study
  shows that favouritism follows from identification; both show it varies
  with it.
- **Aron et al. ([LIT-tmp9rujh](../literature.d/LIT-tmp9rujh.md))** and the fusion item ([NOTE-tmp3g9n4](NOTE-tmp3g9n4.md))
  measure the self–group link by drawn overlap. The identification IAT
  measures it by association with self words. The record holds no study
  comparing them.
- **Otten (2016)** summarizes this paper accurately (see the first draft).
  She gives the authors' reading of memorization as a self-concept link,
  and calls it the same factor the self-anchoring work names.
- **Self-categorization theory ([THEORY-tmpo85ke](../theory.d/THEORY-tmpo85ke.md)).** The memorization
  effect does not come from categorization: nobody was put in a group. If
  it is a self–group link, it was made by familiarity, not by metacontrast
  or fit.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-tmpjylq8](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpjylq8.md)).** These are inferences; the paper
  does not discuss selflessness.
  - The self–group link can be made without being told one belongs.
    Forty-five seconds of studying five names tied a group to "me" and
    "mine" more strongly than being told one shared its taste in
    painting. If that is an extension of the self, the extension is cheap
    and need not be chosen. It may also be shallow: it did not predict
    allocation better than the explicit measures did.
  - Favouritism to anonymous others with no gain for oneself appeared
    under every induction. That is the behaviour the thesis explains as
    serving an extended self. Here the participant was also told they
    could receive money from both groups, so a reciprocity motive was not
    excluded.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md)).** The paper measures the
  self's extent and liking separately, which is what the objection asks
  for. The identification IAT (group with "self") and the attitude IAT
  (group with "pleasant") correlated .66 in Experiment 1 and .48 in
  Experiment 2. They are related but not the same. The explicit measures,
  "I identify with" and "I like", correlated .71 in both experiments. So at the level
  of report, the self's extent and caring nearly coincide. At the implicit
  level they come apart somewhat. Neither predicted allocation much
  better than the other.
- **Batson's objection ([CLAIM-tmpybc4k](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmpybc4k.md)).** Not addressed.
- **A dated note should be added to [CLAIM-tmprudek](../claims.d/CLAIM-tmprudek.md).** Proposed text:

  > Note of 2026-10-29: an implicit measure of the self's extent. Pinter
  > and Greenwald (2011) measured self–group association (an IAT pairing
  > the group with "I", "me", "mine") apart from liking (an IAT pairing it
  > with pleasant words). The two correlated .48 to .66, and the explicit
  > versions .71, so self-extension and caring overlap heavily but are
  > separable implicitly. Memorizing a group's names, with no membership,
  > made the self-association largest. Allocation favouritism correlated
  > with each measure at about .23 to .37, none clearly ahead.
- **No THEORY filed.** A methods comparison with small samples.
- **No ML instruction.**

## Limitations

- **Small cells**: 13 to 25 per condition, at one small public college,
  for extra credit.
- **Comparable is not equal.** "No condition effect" on explicit measures
  rests on F tests with two dozen people per cell. Experiment 2's explicit
  identification was close (p = .07).
- **The colour effect.** In Experiment 2 the allocation result depends on
  which colour participants were assigned (note 8). The authors give two
  reasons for confidence, that all 14 effects were in the predicted
  direction and nine were reliable, but do not explain it.
- **The demand argument covers one procedure.** The authors say strong
  implicit effects show minimal-group favouritism is not an artifact of
  demand (p. 95). That holds for memorization. The classic procedure
  showed explicit favouritism with no implicit effect, which leaves demand
  open for it.
- **A claim the tables do not show.** The conclusion says memorization
  produced "stronger implicit–explicit correlations" (p. 94). In
  Experiment 1 its identification correlation (.50) was below the
  classic's (.83). In Table 2 its implicit–explicit correlations are
  −.06 to .13.
- **Rounded p-values.** Many are printed only as powers of ten
  ("p = 10-5"), so exact values cannot be checked.
- **Typos and slips.**
  - "Rabbie & Horwitz, 1969" in the text (p. 81) and "Horowitz" in the
    references (p. 98).
  - "Wittenbrink & Schwartz, 2009" in the text (p. 82); "Schwarz" in the
    references.
  - "This change should affect a more rigorous comparison" for "effect"
    (p. 89).
  - Note 6 gives the main effect of group, a two-level factor, as
    "F(2, 67)" (p. 95).
  - The Crisp & Turner title reads "Can imagined interactions procedure
    positive perceptions?" for "produce" (p. 97).
  - The Hofmann et al. 2005 author list is garbled (p. 97).
  - "Pennylvania" in the biographical note (p. 98).

## Open questions

- Does the memorization link predict anything costly? The allocation here
  was other people's bonus money. A test where favouring the studied group
  costs the participant would show whether a cheap self–group link moves
  behaviour.
- Does Robertson's identification moderation hold for allocation in a
  preregistered sample, with identification measured before the outcome?
