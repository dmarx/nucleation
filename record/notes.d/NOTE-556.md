# NOTE-556: Defining Coral Bleaching as a Microbial Dysbiosis within the Coral Holobiont

## Contribution

A long review of the coral holobiont (host, Symbiodiniaceae, bacteria,
viruses, archaea, protists, endolithic algae and fungi), organised to
support one proposal. Coral bleaching should be defined as a succession of
three holobiont states, the first two "adaptive", rather than as the loss
of algae alone. It adds the "adaptive dysbiosis hypothesis" (ADH) as a name
for the first two states. It adds no data.

## Key insight

Bleaching is the visible end of a longer trajectory in a multi-partner
system. Before the algae are lost, the microbial community shifts. It may
buffer the stress by keeping functions going (nitrogen fixation,
antioxidants, pathogen exclusion), and it may make room for a different
algal partner. Bleaching is either a transient phase of reorganisation or
the point where every buffer has failed. Whether it is one or the other
depends on the stress's strength and duration.

## Assumptions

- **Eubiosis and dysbiosis.** Eubiosis is the "healthy state of the
  holobiont", dysbiosis the "unhealthy, disrupted state" (§1). The model
  then applies "dysbiosis" to stages in which the holobiont "remains
  healthy" (Figure 3 caption). So in the model the word means a departure
  from the initial microbiome, not ill health.
- **Functional redundancy confers resilience**, taken from a review of the
  human gut (Sommer et al. 2017) and applied to corals by analogy (§3).
- **Resilience at two levels.** At ecosystem level it is the capacity to
  absorb stress "and to build an alternative state, such that the system
  maintains its structure and functions". At holobiont level it is the
  host's capacity to resist or revert cellular damage, plus the capacity
  to recruit new symbionts and restore microbial function (§3).
- **Scope**: hermatypic Scleractinia.

## Key results

All cited; this review reports them, it does not produce them.

- **Bleaching routes (§1).** The host immune system activates apoptosis,
  autophagy, exocytosis, detachment or necrosis and rejects the algae.
  Alternatively, pigments are lost when thylakoids are exposed to free
  radicals. ROS production in the algae is "the most likely cause" of
  expulsion. The mechanism is "still far from being fully understood".
- **Host regulation of symbionts (§2.2).** "The coral host has the ability
  to regulate the density and diversity of their associated
  Symbiodiniaceae", through tissue compounds, deterioration or digestion of
  symbionts, and limits on nitrogen and phosphorus.
- **Bacterial regulation of symbionts (§2.3.1).** Nitrifying and
  denitrifying bacteria regulate dinoflagellate density by the nitrogen to
  phosphorus balance. The review states that anammox "constrains" the algae
  to use N₂, which as written is chemically doubtful, since
  dinoflagellates do not fix N₂ (see Corrections). Endozoicomonas
  metabolises algal DMSP.
- **Microbial change during bleaching (§2.3.1).** Diversity rises,
  Endozoicomonas falls and Vibrio rises, with virulence genes. Vibrio went
  from about 0 to 30% relative abundance in heat-bleached corals and back
  to about 0 in resilient ones (Bourne et al. 2008). Cause or consequence:
  "impossible to disentangle".
- **Buffers (§§3.1–3.2).** Cyanobacteria and plastid ratios increase under
  stress. Diazotrophs proliferate at high temperature, and their abundance
  correlates with dinoflagellate abundance. Heterotrophic feeding meets
  0–35% of needs in Porites and up to 100% in Montipora capitata during
  bleaching, and is not enough under recurrent bleaching. Durusdinium
  confers heat tolerance at a cost in growth, and corals revert to their
  original partners 2–3 years after a bleaching event.
- **Death and after (§3.3).** Under abrupt or prolonged heat the
  microbiome can no longer resist opportunists. After mortality, nitrogen
  production from dead corals can be as much as 30 times higher, which
  favours algae, and endoliths speed dissolution of the skeleton.
- **Manipulation (§4).** A consortium of putatively beneficial bacteria
  (five Pseudoalteromonas strains, Halomonas taeanensis and a Cobetia
  marina-related strain) lessened bleaching effects in Pocillopora
  damicornis (Rosado et al. 2019).

## Concepts

- **eubiosis / dysbiosis** — healthy versus disrupted holobiont state. The
  model also uses dysbiosis for any departure from the initial microbiome.
- **adaptive dysbiosis hypothesis (ADH)** — microbial restructuring under
  stress as a mechanism of environmental adaptation, adaptive "as long as
  the coral host can quickly revert back to a healthy state".
- **metagenomic plasticity** — gain or loss of microbial members and genes,
  or changes in their abundance, within a host generation.
- **shuffling / switching** — changing the proportions of resident
  Symbiodiniaceae, versus acquiring new ones from the environment.
- **traumatic dysbiosis** — irreversible symbiont loss with invasion by
  opportunists, ending in death.

## Connections

- **Goulet et al. ([LIT-709](../literature.d/LIT-709.md)).** Both treat the coral as a consortium.
  Goulet et al. warn that the Adaptive Bleaching Hypothesis became
  "coral-centric" through wording. This review takes the hypothesis as the
  basis of stage 2 and writes it in host-centred terms. Goulet et al.'s
  octocoral case, where the same symbiont-density drop came by different
  routes, supports this review's C1 and is not cited by it.
- **Hughes et al. ([LIT-711](../literature.d/LIT-711.md)).** The Figure 3 caption ends in reef phase
  shifts to algae. Hughes et al. is experimental evidence for how that
  happens at reef scale after bleaching: through the loss of herbivory, not
  through anything in the coral's microbiome. The review cites other Hughes
  papers (2003, 2010, 2017, 2018), not this one.
- **Lloyd ([LIT-160](../literature.d/LIT-160.md)).** C7 conflates roles. Transmission of microbiota is a
  reproducer question, and the review offers no interactor-level evidence.
- **Baedke ([LIT-167](../literature.d/LIT-167.md), [NOTE-148](NOTE-148.md)).** The stage-2 reorganisation resembles his
  "overcomer" (destabilising and reorganising one's own organisation),
  carried out by a holobiont. The review does not claim it is controlled by
  the holobiont, and the resemblance is the record's observation.
- **Borsboom ([LIT-547](../literature.d/LIT-547.md)) and [THEORY-058](../theory.d/THEORY-058.md).** The vocabulary of eubiosis versus
  dysbiosis as states of a network of interacting parts parallels the
  network account of disorder as an alternative stable state. The review
  claims no hysteresis, and stage 2 is explicitly reversible. No relation.

## Bearing on the record

- Source, with Goulet et al., for the theory that coral bleaching is the
  breakdown of a regulated host–symbiont exchange reached by more than one
  route ([THEORY-130](../theory.d/THEORY-130.md)). That theory takes C1 and C2 and declines C4 and
  C5, the microbial-driver claim and the three-stage definition.
- No ML instruction; nothing for the anthology. The probiotic and
  microbiome-engineering suggestions of §4 are reef management.

## Limitations

- **No new data.** A narrative review; no inclusion criteria for the
  literature.
- **Microbial causation is conceded to be unestablished**, yet the title
  defines bleaching by it.
- **The stages are not operational.** Nothing says how to tell stage 1 from
  stage 2 in a sampled coral, or how to measure "buffering capacity".
- **"Adaptive" is asserted, not tested.** No fitness comparison is cited
  between corals whose microbiome shifted and those whose did not.
- **The analogy to the human gut** (functional redundancy) is imported, not
  shown for corals.

## Open questions

- Does manipulating the microbiota before heat stress change whether, when
  or how far a coral bleaches? Rosado et al. 2019 is the start. A
  replicated experiment with microbiome-only manipulation and bleaching as
  outcome would test C4.
- Can the three stages be distinguished by a measurable signature, so that
  the model makes predictions?
- Who controls stage-2 reorganisation: host selection, symbiont
  competition or environmental availability?

## Corrections

- **Nitrogen chemistry, §2.3.1.** The review says that anammox "constrains
  the dinoflagellate algae to use dinitrogen (N₂) for photosynthesis".
  Symbiodiniaceae are not known to fix N₂; diazotrophic bacteria do, as
  the next sentences of the same section say. The passage probably means
  that the algae are limited by nitrogen when bacteria remove ammonium.
  The record does not repeat the sentence as stated.
- **"Madaptive"** in the §3.3 heading is a typo for "maladaptive".
