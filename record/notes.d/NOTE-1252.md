# NOTE-1252: A Perfect Moral Storm: Climate Change, Intergenerational Ethics and the Problem of Moral Corruption

## Contribution

It moves the ethics of climate change from what a just policy would be
to why making any policy is hard. Gardiner sets the standard model, a
tragedy of the commons between states, beside a second collective-action
problem between generations, and argues that the second dominates. The
paper's own thesis is that, even if the hard ethical questions were
answered, "we might still find it difficult to act", because the storm
"makes us extremely vulnerable to moral corruption" (p. 398).

## Key insight

The intergenerational problem cannot be solved the way a commons problem
is. Between states, "mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon" can make
cooperation individually rational. Between generations it cannot, because
"the parties do not coexist" (p. 405). Spatially scattered agents "may
actually become unified"; temporally scattered agents "cannot actually
become unified, and so may at best only act as if they were a single
agent" (p. 404). What is left is the will of each generation, and that is
what corruption works on.

## Assumptions

- **Generations as agents with interests.** The Pure Intergenerational
  Problem treats each generation as one agent that prefers its own
  outcome. Overlap of generations is set aside; it "complicates the
  picture" but does not, Gardiner says, resolve the problem (note 26).
- **States are biased toward the present.** The intergenerational storm
  appears when one drops the global model's assumption that countries
  represent their citizens "in perpetuity" (p. 404).
- **The climate science of 2001–2005.** The IPCC's 2001 synthesis and
  Archer (2005) on the lifetime of carbon dioxide.
- **The paper is a sketch.** It does not discuss the theoretical storm's
  problems "in any detail" (p. 407), and lists the corruption strategies
  without analysing most of them.

## Key results

- **Three characteristics, two readings** (pp. 399, 402). Dispersion of
  causes and effects, fragmentation of agency and institutional
  inadequacy. Read spatially they give the global storm; read temporally,
  the intergenerational storm.
- **The global storm** (pp. 399–402).
  - The Prisoner's Dilemma: (PD1) each agent prefers that all restrict
    pollution over none; (PD2) each prefers not to restrict, whatever
    others do. A tragedy of the commons is that dilemma over a common
    resource.
  - Commons problems are often resolvable, with enforceable sanctions and
    a wider context of interaction. The obstacle is the lack of global
    governance.
  - Three aggravations: scientific uncertainty about national impacts,
    which casts doubt on PD1; the roots of emissions in the energy that
    supports economies, which brings vested interests and a status quo
    bias; and skewed vulnerabilities, since the rich caused most of it and
    the poor are most exposed.
- **The lag** (pp. 402–403).
  - The IPCC's 5–200 years in the atmosphere obscures a long tail. Archer
    (2005), quoted: 17–33% of fossil carbon still in the air after 1,000
    years, 10–15% after 10,000 and 7% after 100,000; mean lifetime about
    30,000–35,000 years.
  - So climate change is resilient, backloaded and deferred. By 2000 we
    had committed to at least 0.5°C and perhaps more than 1°C beyond the
    0.6°C then observed.
- **The Pure Intergenerational Problem** (pp. 404–405), drawn from
  Gardiner 2003.
  - (PIP1) It is collectively rational for most generations to cooperate:
    "(almost) every generation" prefers all restricting to all
    overpolluting.
  - (PIP2) It is individually rational for all generations to overpollute,
    whatever the others do.
  - It is worse than the Prisoner's Dilemma in two ways. PIP1 leaves out
    the first generation, so its defection has "a domino effect" and the
    problem iterates. PIP2's cause is deeper: the parties do not coexist,
    so neither a wider context of interaction nor reciprocity is
    available.
  - Gardiner holds that "the intergenerational problem dominates the
    tragedy of the commons aspect in climate change" (p. 405).
- **Multipliers** (pp. 405–407).
  - Inaction adds to the problem rather than passing it on, and
    accelerates it: emissions had more than quadrupled since 1950 and grew
    about 2% a year, which compounds to 22% in 10 years and 35% in 15.
  - It may harm generations who would otherwise be spared, which on some
    views violates "Do No Harm".
  - It may force tragic choices. A generation pushed past a threshold may
    be permitted, in self-defence, to harm a later one, and the pattern can
    iterate.
- **The theoretical storm** (p. 407). Our best theories have "basic and
  often severe difficulties" with uncertainty, intergenerational equity,
  contingent persons, animals and nature, and climate change involves all
  of them.
- **Moral corruption** (pp. 407–409).
  - Eight strategies are listed: distraction, complacency, unreasonable
    doubt, selective attention, delusion, pandering, false witness and
    hypocrisy.
  - Selective attention: emphasizing uncertainty, high discount rates and
    lifestyle costs over scientific consensus and the Pure
    Intergenerational Problem.
  - Corruption may reach theory. The prominence of the global model may be
    "encouraged by" the intergenerational storm, since the model assumes
    states represent their citizens in perpetuity and so assumes the
    intergenerational problem away (p. 408).
  - Each generation can negotiate "weak and largely substanceless global
    accords" and herald them, while "simply exploiting its temporal
    position", without "the unpleasantness of admitting it – either to
    others, or, perhaps more importantly, to itself" (pp. 408–409).
- **Two commons, two solutions** (note 36, p. 411). If nations represent
  their citizens in perpetuity, the commons is cross-generational; if
  their current citizens, intragenerational. The two have different
  solutions, the second probably with much less mitigation, so solving one
  is no evidence of interest in the other.

## Concepts

- **perfect storm**: "an unusual convergence of independently harmful
  factors" likely to produce substantial, possibly catastrophic, outcomes
  (p. 398). The definition is Gardiner's own (note 7).
- **global, intergenerational and theoretical storms**: the spatial and
  temporal readings of the three characteristics, and our theories'
  ineptitude.
- **Pure Intergenerational Problem (PIP)**: the intergenerational
  collective-action problem between non-overlapping generations, PIP1 and
  PIP2.
- **backloading and deferral**: present climate change is caused mostly
  by past emissions, and the full effects of present emissions come later.
- **moral corruption**: the facilitation, by the storm's complexity, of
  manipulative or self-deceptive handling of the problem, so that a
  generation can serve itself while seeming to act.
- **generation-relative motivation**: the paper's term for what the global
  model relies on. Protecting future people needs "motivations that are
  not generation-relative" (note 35, p. 411).

## Connections

- **Bostrom 2013 ([NOTE-1321](NOTE-1321.md)).** The pan-generational public good, and
  his "deficit of motivation", have the same structure as the
  intergenerational storm. Gardiner adds why the deficit hides itself.
- **Hurlstone et al. ([NOTE-1279](NOTE-1279.md)).** The intergenerational game is a
  laboratory version of the Pure Intergenerational Problem: without a
  legacy frame, most groups let a benefit to later players fail. That
  paper does not cite Gardiner.
- **Meyer, SEP ([NOTE-1304](NOTE-1304.md)).** Names Gardiner and sets him aside, as
  describing a structural dynamic rather than what is owed. The paper
  agrees: it says it is not about the substance of a defensible regime
  but about "the process of making climate policy" (p. 398).
- **Parfit ([LIT-1768](../literature.d/LIT-1768.md)).** The theoretical storm names "contingent
  persons" as one of theory's difficulties (p. 407) without discussing
  the non-identity problem.
- **Scheffler ([LIT-1754](../literature.d/LIT-1754.md)), unread.** Not cited. Gardiner's account
  needs a moral motive that can be corrupted. Scheffler grounds concern for
  the future in what the living value. They meet at the question of what
  the living want for the future and why it fails to bind.

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis ([CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md)).** The paper bears on it in three
  ways. These are inferences; Gardiner does not discuss the self.
  - The standard model assumes the thesis's shape and the paper denies it
    holds. A state that represents its citizens "in perpetuity" is a self
    extended across generations, whose self-interest would cover the
    future. Gardiner's intergenerational storm begins by supposing that
    countries are instead "biased towards the interests of the current
    generation" (p. 404). In the thesis's terms the self actually at work
    is generation-relative, and concern stops where it stops.
  - That is the thesis's prediction, not a counterexample to it. As with
    Bostrom 2013 ([NOTE-1321](NOTE-1321.md)), concern reaches as far as the self's
    stake and is undersupplied beyond it. Gardiner's remedy, motivation
    that is "not generation-relative" (note 35), is what the thesis would
    have to say is either a larger self or not available.
  - Corruption needs a motive to corrupt. If the living cared for nothing
    beyond a generation-relative self, there would be nothing to hide from
    themselves. Gardiner's generation avoids "overtly selfish behaviour"
    and the "unpleasantness of admitting" its exploitation "to itself"
    (pp. 408–409). The motive deceived is moral concern for future people,
    or a self-image as someone who has it. On the second reading the self
    served is again a self-concept kept true, the sense the fusion reading
    ([NOTE-1256](NOTE-1256.md)) also needs.
- **The triviality objection ([CLAIM-492](../claims.d/CLAIM-492.md)).** The paper offers a
  structural limit on how far a self can extend in time. Agents scattered
  across generations "cannot actually become unified" and can only act "as
  if they were a single agent" (p. 404). If that is right, a self extended
  across generations exists only as an identification the living hold,
  not as a unified agent. The criterion the objection asks for would then
  have to be psychological, such as felt continuity with future
  generations, not institutional.
- **Batson's objection ([CLAIM-494](../claims.d/CLAIM-494.md)).** Not addressed.
- **A dated note to [CLAIM-491](../claims.d/CLAIM-491.md) is proposed**, as an addition to its
  note "the thesis in time":

  > Gardiner's perfect moral storm (NOTE-xxxx) shows the thesis's shape
  > inside the standard climate model, which treats states as
  > representing their citizens "in perpetuity", a self extended across
  > generations. His intergenerational storm starts from denying that this
  > holds in practice: the operative self is the present generation, and
  > concern stops there, as the thesis predicts. But his problem of moral
  > corruption needs a motive that the present must deceive "to itself",
  > either concern for future people or a self-image as caring for them.
  > The thesis can claim the second only by counting a self-concept kept
  > true as the self being served.

- **No THEORY filed.** The Pure Intergenerational Problem is argued in
  Gardiner 2003, which the record does not hold.
- **No ML instruction.**

## Limitations

- **A short programmatic paper.** The theoretical storm gets one paragraph,
  and seven of the eight corruption strategies are only named.
- **The model, not the world.** Generations are treated as unitary and
  non-overlapping. Gardiner grants that overlap complicates this (note 26)
  and defers to Gardiner 2003.
- **The science is of 2005.** The emissions figures (quadrupling since
  1950, growth of about 2% a year) and the committed warming are dated.
- **C3 and C5 are asserted.** That the intergenerational problem dominates
  is "in my view"; that theory is corrupted is "a very unpleasant
  thought".
- **Typos.**
  - "the Andreas Gail" (p. 399); "the Andrea Gail" on p. 398.
  - "under the global gap" (p. 398), presumably "cap".
  - "But it also because" (p. 402).
  - "the spatial fragmentation even considered in isolation" (p. 404).
  - "What I have in mind if this" (p. 407).
  - "Hansen and Soto 2004" (note 28); the references have Sato.
  - "Edgar Elgar Publishing" in the references (p. 412), for Edward
    Elgar.
  - Junger's book is listed as *A Perfect Storm* (p. 412); its title is
    *The Perfect Storm*.

## Open questions

- Do people who feel more continuous with future generations show less of
  the corruption Gardiner lists, or only less of the incentive? A study
  that measured felt continuity, endorsement of corrupting rationales
  (uncertainty, discounting) and support for mitigation would separate a
  generation-relative self from a corrupted moral motive.
- What does Gardiner 2003 add on overlapping generations, where some of
  the future is one's own children?
