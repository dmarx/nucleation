# NOTE-402: The Evolution of Reciprocal Altruism

## Contribution

- A model of selection for altruism between non-relatives, including
  members of different species, sustained by withdrawal of help from
  cheaters (The Model, pp. 35–39).
- Three applications: host restraint in cleaning symbioses (pp. 39–43), a
  non-reciprocal, self-interested account of bird warning calls (pp.
  43–45), and human reciprocal altruism (pp. 45–54).
- A functional analysis of the human "psychological system underlying
  human reciprocal altruism" (pp. 47–54), which is the first place the
  moral emotions are given adaptive functions in this literature.

## Key insight

Reciprocity pays only if cheating is punished by the loss of future help,
and once it pays, selection builds a regulating psychology around it: warm
emotions to motivate giving, anger to protect the giver, guilt to repair a
detected breach, and then counterfeits of each and the suspicion to catch
them. The instability is the engine: "once moralistic aggression has been
selected for to protect against cheating, selection favors sham
moralistic aggression as a new form of cheating", and so on (p. 54).

## Assumptions

- **Cost below benefit.** Each altruistic act costs the giver less than it benefits the recipient, in inclusive-fitness terms (p. 36).
- **Discrimination.** Altruists respond to a non-altruistic act by curtailing future help (case 3, p. 37).
- **Conditions** (p. 37): many altruistic situations per lifetime, repeated interaction with a small set of individuals, and symmetric exposure. Favoured by long life, low dispersal, mutual dependence, long parental care, weak dominance hierarchies, and aid in combat.
- **Genetics.** One altruism allele a₂ versus a₁ for exposition; the argument is said not to require that every altruistic act be controlled by the same locus.
- **Human section.** That reciprocal altruism was important in recent human evolution and that "the underlying emotional dispositions … have important genetic components" is *assumed*, not shown (p. 48).

## Key results

- **The model.** Random altruism is eliminated; kin-directed altruism is Hamilton's case; altruism dispensed by reference to the recipient's altruism spreads if the net benefit to altruists exceeds that to non-altruists, which holds when non-altruists are cut off (p. 37).
- **Prisoner's Dilemma (pp. 38–39).** Hamilton's communicated formulation: over 2n situations two altruists each get nR; against a non-altruist, an altruist who switches after being cheated gets S + (n − 1)P. A rare non-altruist cannot spread unless T ≫ R; a rare altruist faces a barrier that weakens as n grows.
- **Cleaning symbioses.** Hosts do not eat cleaners, signal departure and chase off threats. Evidence: Hediger's grouper raised alone for six years that let a cleaner into its mouth; cleaners' site fidelity and longevity; hosts returning to the same stations. Cleaners most dependent on cleaning are territorial, solitary and rarely eaten.
- **Warning calls.** Reciprocation is rejected as the explanation ("difficult to visualize how one would discover and discriminate against the cheater"). Instead a caller benefits because a predator that eats a neighbour is more likely to learn, specialize on, or stay near the caller's species and place.
- **Human psychology** (items numbered (1)–(10) in the text): a complex regulating system; friendship and liking; moralistic aggression; gratitude and sympathy sensitive to cost/benefit; guilt and reparative altruism, stronger after public than private transgression; mimics of each; detection of subtle cheaters; overtures to strangers; multiparty interactions, generalized altruism and rules of exchange; developmental plasticity.

## Concepts

- **Altruistic behaviour** — benefits another organism, "not closely related", at an apparent cost to the actor, both in inclusive-fitness terms (p. 35).
- **Cheating** — failure to reciprocate, "solely for convenience … no conscious intent or moral connotation is implied" (p. 36).
- **Gross vs subtle cheating** — not reciprocating, versus always giving back a little less than received (p. 46).
- **Moralistic aggression** — aggression provoked by "injustice, unfairness, and lack of reciprocity", often "out of all proportion to the offenses" (p. 49).
- **Reparative altruism** — compensation by a transgressor, motivated in part by guilt (p. 50).

## Connections

- **Hamilton 1964** ([LIT-497](../literature.d/LIT-497.md), [LIT-506](../literature.d/LIT-506.md)) is the foil: the model is built for cases where kin selection "can be ruled out", and the alarm-call section argues against Hamilton's kin explanation.
- **Axelrod & Hamilton 1981** ([LIT-498](../literature.d/LIT-498.md)) formalize the same mechanism with a continuation probability w and an ESS analysis; Hamilton had already given Trivers the PD framing.
- **Alexander 1987** ([LIT-495](../literature.d/LIT-495.md)) builds moral systems on indirect reciprocity, which Trivers's multiparty section anticipates.
- **Tomasello** ([LIT-125](../literature.d/LIT-125.md), [NOTE-185](NOTE-185.md)) argues that tit-for-tat reciprocity yields gratitude, not obligation; Trivers's guilt is the regulating emotion of exactly that reciprocity, so the two disagree on whether reciprocity is a source of moral emotion or only of liking and gratitude.
- **Morality-as-cooperation.** [NOTE-034](NOTE-034.md)'s lineage paragraph and Curry 2016 ([LIT-503](../literature.d/LIT-503.md)) take reciprocity from here. Curry 2016's exchange-domain list (trust, gratitude, punishment, revenge and forgiveness) is close to Trivers's list.

## Bearing on the record

- **Exchange domain.** Directly grounded, with its emotions.
- **Moral emotions.** The only one of the record's cooperation sources that explains guilt, gratitude and moralistic anger. A THEORY about the function of moral emotions would start here.
- **Fairness.** Trivers's "unfairness" is inequity in exchange. MAC's division domain (dividing a disputed resource) is not grounded here, and a reader should not cite this paper for it.
- **Group, hawk, dove, possession.** Not grounded. Dominance appears only as an obstacle to reciprocity, aid in combat as a reciprocated service.
- **No THEORY is indicated now.** No instruction for ML practice.

## Limitations

- The formal model is a sketch; stability against invasion is not analysed (Axelrod & Hamilton did that later).
- The human section is functional reasoning fitted to existing findings, many of them small 1960s laboratory studies of American students; no prediction is tested.
- Heritability of the emotional dispositions is assumed.

## Open questions

- Do people expect "interest" on long-overdue reciprocation, highest just after sexual maturity, as the age-dependence argument predicts (p. 46)?
- Can sham guilt and sham moralistic aggression be detected, and is detection better than chance?
