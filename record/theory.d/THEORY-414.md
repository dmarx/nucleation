# THEORY-414: Institutions are rules in equilibrium, correlated equilibria of coordination games with several equilibria that the players represent to themselves as rules, and a constitutive rule 'X counts as Y in C' is a regulative rule split by a coined term into a base rule and a status rule, so the term adds a name and cognitive economy but no new kind of fact

## Source

Frank Hindriks and Francesco Guala (2015, online 16 October 2014),
"Institutions, rules, and equilibria: a unified theory", *Journal of
Institutional Economics* 11(3):459–480, [LIT-481](../literature.d/LIT-481.md), read in full in [NOTE-395](../notes.d/NOTE-395.md)
with the authors' "Understanding institutions: replies to Aoki, Binmore,
Hodgson, Searle, Smith, and Sugden", JIE 11(3):515–522. The six
commentaries were not read.

## What was actually shown

**Against rules alone (§2).** A rule that is known and not followed (a
1799 French ban on women wearing trousers; a 65 mph limit in a 75 mph
equilibrium) shows that stating a rule does not make an institution. What
does is an incentive structure that keeps behaviour where it is.

**Against equilibria alone (§§3–4).** Mutual defection in a prisoner's
dilemma needs no coordination, and hare–hare in a stag hunt needs no
correlation, so neither is an institution. *Pararge aegeria* butterflies
play a "bourgeois" correlated equilibrium but cannot condition on new
signals or invent new equilibria, so they have no institution.
Representation is the difference. Property* is modelled as the correlated
equilibrium of hawk–dove with first occupancy as the device, expected
payoff 1.5 each when occupancy is equally likely.

**The transformation (§5).**

- [R] If one is the first to occupy a piece of land, one has the right to
  its exclusive use.
- [B] If a person first occupies a piece of land, then it is her
  property*.
- [S] If a piece of land is someone's property*, she has the right to use
  it.
- [C] = [B] and [S]: "if C then X is Y, and if Y then Z".

**The reduction (§6).** The Y term is conservative and eliminable in
Belnap's sense, and behaviour under [R] is extensionally equivalent to
behaviour under [B] and [S]. So the constitutive-rules theory is
"reducible to" rules in equilibrium. Multiple realisability of
institutional kinds corresponds to multiple equilibria. If constitutive
rules were necessary, etiquette, and in the reply driving on the right,
would not be institutions. By Lewis's account of theoretical terms,
"property" refers if the rules that introduce it are true.

**The reply.** Normativity needs no constitutive rules: "No one is allowed
to use my garden" is regulative, and a right is other people's
obligations. Searle's "desire-independent reasons" is a platitude on the
weak reading and an unargued anti-Humean thesis on the strong one. A
theory of institutions should stay neutral among theories of normativity.

**What could have come out otherwise.** The split could have failed for
the toy case, by giving a status rule that says more than [R]. It did
not. No harder case was tried.

## What this does not say

- **That every constitutive rule reduces.** The split is worked once
  ([NOTE-395](../notes.d/NOTE-395.md), C4 strong for the toy case); the general claim (C5) is
  weak–moderate. No case is given where a status is created with no
  regulative rule behind it, and Searle's chess appears only as his
  example.
- **That the transformation preserves rights.** The equivalence shown is
  of prescribed behaviour. Whether [R] and [B]+[S] confer the same rights
  is answered only in the reply, and only by one example (C9).
- **That collective acceptance has been analysed.** It is read as
  "whatever set of intentional states is needed for institutions" (§5,
  n. 14), which lets the "counts as" biconditionals hold by construction.
- **Where the human/animal line falls.** The representation condition is
  a stipulation about what "institution" should cover. The reply calls the
  line a working hypothesis.
- **That multiple realisability is multiple equilibria in general.** "At
  least in many cases, they come down to the same thing" (§6; C6 weak).
- **Anything about group agents.** The account is neutral on collective
  intentionality (n. 14). Institutions on this view are patterns of
  individual strategies, which neither require nor exclude a group-level
  subject.

## Connections

- **Rival: Searle's status functions** ([THEORY-625](THEORY-625.md), [LIT-482](../literature.d/LIT-482.md)). The two
  cannot both be right about what a constitutive rule adds and whether
  institutions need one. Neither has settled it: this paper reduces one toy
  case, and Searle's later free-standing Y terms move toward treating the Y
  term as a label for powers without conceding the reduction.
- **Epstein's survey** ([LIT-487](../literature.d/LIT-487.md), [THEORY-487](THEORY-487.md)). On the
  grounding–anchoring framework, equilibrium plus representation is an
  answer to the anchoring question, what puts the conditions for *property*
  in place. The placement is [NOTE-393](../notes.d/NOTE-393.md)'s, not either text's. Epstein's
  anchors and this paper's equilibria are rival replacements for Searle's
  single form.
- **Lewis** ([LIT-473](../literature.d/LIT-473.md), unread) supplies both the equilibrium account of
  convention, generalised here to correlated equilibria, and the account of
  theoretical terms.
- **North** ([LIT-489](../literature.d/LIT-489.md), [NOTE-392](../notes.d/NOTE-392.md)) is the rules account's exemplar. The
  objection that rules alone cannot separate effective from nominal rules
  is one his essay leaves open.
- **Tomasello** ([LIT-125](../literature.d/LIT-125.md), [THEORY-495](THEORY-495.md)). The reply lists Gilbert's joint commitment as
  one option among theories of normativity; Tomasello's "we" would be
  another. The paper takes no side.
