# LIT-1002: Technology, autonomy, and manipulation

Susser, D., Roessler, B. and Nissenbaum, H. (2019). Technology, autonomy, and manipulation. *Internet Policy Review* 8(2). DOI 10.14763/2019.2.1410.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-tmpijdk1](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpijdk1.md).*

- **The definition (p. 4).** "manipulating someone means intentionally
  and covertly influencing their decision-making, by targeting and
  exploiting their decision-making vulnerabilities." Covert means "in a
  way they aren't consciously aware of, and in a way they couldn't easily
  become aware of".
- **Openness is the line.** Persuasion and coercion are "forthright".
  Deception is one species of manipulation; playing on desires, emotions
  and cognitive biases are others. Overt temptation and guilting are
  failed attempts: "it is the attempted covertness that is central"
  (p. 5). Nudges manipulate when hidden and exploiting a bias, not when
  informational.
- **Why technology suits it (pp. 6–7).** Surveillance exposes general and
  "person-specific" vulnerabilities; platforms are "adaptive choice
  architectures"; habituated technology becomes "invisible". Not every
  dark pattern or gig-app prompt manipulates, since some are overt.
- **The harm is to autonomy (pp. 9–10).** Manipulation leads people to
  act "toward ends they haven't chosen" and "for reasons not
  authentically their own". The economic harm is derivative.
- **Beneficent manipulation still harms (pp. 10–11).** The harm is to
  "the process of decision-making, not its outcome": the target is made
  "opaque to himself". But the paper claims only that manipulation
  "always carries a harm", not that it is always wrong.
- **Scope.** Individual decisions only; manipulation of institutions and
  systems is set aside (p. 3).

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-12 at the owner's request with [QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md) and
[QUESTION-071](../questions.d/QUESTION-071.md), and read in [NOTE-tmpijdk1](../notes.d/NOTE-tmpijdk1.md). It is held as the standard
statement of the covert-influence account, which Noggle's survey
([LIT-1007](LIT-1007.md)) quotes and objects to. Its harm argument is filed as
[THEORY-tmppv6y2](../theory.d/THEORY-tmppv6y2.md), of covert influence rather than of manipulation as such.

For [QUESTION-070](../questions.d/QUESTION-070.md) it supplies the definition with the intent requirement
that the question tests. It never examines that requirement, and it
treats firms as the intending manipulators even where the influence is
chosen by an adaptive system. For [QUESTION-071](../questions.d/QUESTION-071.md) its criterion is
historical and epistemic: whether the agent was, and could easily have
become, aware of the influence. It places the harm in authenticity, the
family of the set-aside [CLAIM-016](../claims.d/CLAIM-016.md). It does not address the global
manipulation of the four-case argument.

No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing here belongs in
the anthology.
