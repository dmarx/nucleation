# NOTE-1780: Nickl, Moussaïd and Lorenz-Spreen (2025) — The evolution of online news headlines

## Contribution

A large, long-run, multi-outlet measurement that the style of news headlines has
shifted toward features associated with clickbait and click success, and that the
shift is not confined to low-quality or partisan outlets.

## Key results

- Abstract: "Many – but not all – of these features have become more prevalent over time, such as length and negativity."
- Fig. 2 caption: "For each year, headlines become longer (by approximately one fourth of a word)".
- Results, "Overall trends hold across different types of outlets": "Perhaps surprisingly, there was little difference between news sites of high and lower journalistic quality".
- Results: news headlines "shift from being more similar to the arXiv corpus to being more similar to the upworthy corpus" (on many features).
- Results, topics: the same trends hold across NYT sections (world, U.S., sports, etc.); negativity rose in news but the arXiv corpus shows "the opposite pattern, indicating a news-specific development".
- Introduction (mechanism, as framing): "This process of cultural selection requires no insight into its mechanisms on behalf of the people involved".
- Discussion: "An intuitive explanation of this trend would be that production follows demand (Munger 2024)".
- Discussion (limits): "We lack the measurements to empirically confirm possible mechanisms that explain our results."
- Discussion (remedy): "Unlike possibly stable psychological tendencies, this highly artificial environment can be redesigned."

## Assumptions

- Features linked to clicks in prior work (mostly Upworthy A/B tests) are the
  right markers of attention-adaptation; the paper does not measure clicks itself.
- Year stands in for a bundle of causes (technology, cost, competition); no
  mechanism variable is measured.
- Corpus composition is adequate after filtering (NOW sampling drift and the ABC
  dataset's quality are flagged by the authors).

## Concepts

Attention economy; clickbait features; curiosity gap and forward reference;
cultural evolution / cultural selection of traits; "unbundling of journalism";
A/B-testing of headlines; success metrics as redesignable architecture.

## Bearing on the record

**On the LIT's and the exchange's characterization.** "Links the changing characteristics of online news headlines to competition for
attention": **fair but stronger than the paper**. The paper documents the change
and frames it as adaptation to attention competition; it explicitly does not
establish the link causally, and offers cheaper production (length) as a rival.

- **[CLAIM-656](../claims.d/CLAIM-656.md)** (attention optimization favours clickbait, works against
  investigative journalism): **supports the first clause weakly, leaves the second
  open.** It shows the product (headline style) moved toward click-associated
  features across outlet quality tiers, which fits selection for attention, but
  with no mechanism measured and with a technological rival named by the authors.
  It says nothing about investigative capacity or about who pays. The finding that
  high-quality outlets shifted as much as lower-quality ones is mild evidence for
  an environment-level pressure, not an outlet-type effect, which the claim's
  selection reading predicts. Corroborates (independent; the claim does not rest
  on it).

## Limitations

Descriptive regressions on year; no click data; prior click-feature evidence comes
largely from one outlet (Upworthy); English-language only; NOW corpus sampling
drift; ABC Australia dataset of uncertain quality; supplementary material not read.
