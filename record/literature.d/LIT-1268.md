# LIT-1268: No causal link between changes in hand position sense and feeling of limb ownership in the rubber hand illusion

Abdulkarim, Z. & Ehrsson, H. H. (2016). No causal link between changes in hand position sense and feeling of limb ownership in the rubber hand illusion. *Attention, Perception, & Psychophysics* 78(2), 707–720 (online 10 November 2015). DOI 10.3758/s13414-015-1016-0.

## Key takeaways

*From the reading, [NOTE-992](../notes.d/NOTE-992.md).*

- **Felt position moved; ownership did not.** Sliding the hidden hand
  8 cm toward or away from the rubber hand shifted pointing by about
  5–6 cm in the same direction, yet the illusion index showed a main
  effect of synchrony only, with no effect of direction (p = .266) or of
  displacement against a static hand (p = .443).
- **So drift is not a cause of ownership.** The results "suggest that
  proprioceptive drift is not a prerequisite for the illusion". This closes the possibility Rohde et
  al. (2011) left open, within the distances tested.
- **The classic effect is reproduced.** With the hand still, drift was
  greater after synchronous than asynchronous brushing, and the
  difference correlated with the difference in illusion strength
  (r = .562, p = .015).
- **Drift is kept as a measure, with caution.** The authors argue it is
  "a valuable indirect measure" if the hand is immobile and it is used
  with other measures; they do not test the vision-only condition that
  carries Rohde et al.'s dissociation.
- **What the null can show.** Eighteen participants, no equivalence test
  or Bayes factor, and distances of 8–24 cm, within the illusion's known
  spatial range.

## Standing in the record

Filed on 2026-10-20 at the owner's request, among experimental works
building on the rubber hand illusion. With Rohde, Di Luca & Ernst 2011
([LIT-1281](LIT-1281.md)) it answers the open question in [NOTE-1006](../notes.d/NOTE-1006.md) and the "does
not say what the drift measures" of [THEORY-765](../theory.d/THEORY-765.md) from the other side.
Rohde et al. show drift without ownership; this study shows ownership
unchanged when felt position is changed. Together they make drift a
poor measure of ownership, though this paper defends its use in
controlled designs. Read in full: **Active**.

It neither meets nor refutes [THEORY-765](../theory.d/THEORY-765.md): it replicates the
synchrony effect on the questionnaire with named tests and effect
sizes, but uses the same kind of control items the THEORY says are not
a suggestion control. Its post-experiment questionnaire, in which 7 of
18 people reported hand movements that did not match the real one, is a
small piece of evidence on compliance and confabulation. It bears on
the drift clauses of [THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md) and [THEORY-767](../theory.d/THEORY-767.md), and only
weakly on [THEORY-769](../theory.d/THEORY-769.md)'s spatial term. Nothing whole-body was measured,
so [THEORY-768](../theory.d/THEORY-768.md) is untouched. The NOTE sets this out.

Filed in parallel by other readers: Kalckert & Ehrsson 2012, which the
authors cite for drift with one moving finger; Longo et al. 2008; and
the work on demand characteristics and suggestion (Lush 2020; Lush et
al. 2020; Ehrsson et al. 2022; Lush & Seth 2022; Roseboom & Lush 2022).
It carries no instruction for machine-learning practice.

One of the sources of [THEORY-766](../theory.d/THEORY-766.md) (filed 2026-10-20), which restates the rubber hand synchrony effect as a difference in reports, separates drift from those reports, and leaves open whether expectation produces the difference.
