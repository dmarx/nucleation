# NOTE-tmpyret1: Existential Risk Prevention as Global Priority

## Contribution

It fixes the concept of existential risk, places it on a grid of scope
and severity, and classifies it. It argues that on many theories of value
reducing it is the dominant priority. It adds the normative and
motivational analysis that the 2003 paper lacked. It also reframes
sustainability as a trajectory, not a state.

## Key insight

What makes an existential catastrophe bad is not the deaths but that it
would "destroy the future" (p. 17). Parfit's comparison of peace, a war
that kills 99% and one that kills 100% puts the point (pp. 17–18, quoting
Parfit 1984, pp. 453–454). The gap between the last two is the gap
between the history so far and all the history to come.

## Assumptions

- **Aggregative, time-neutral value** (p. 16). Lives usually have
  significant positive value, two similar lives are worth twice one, and
  value does not depend "on when it occurs or on whether it already exists
  or is yet to be brought into existence". Bostrom calls this "the
  simplest case" and does not defend it.
- **Epistemic probability**: the credence an ideally reasonable observer
  should assign on current evidence (p. 16). What cannot be known safe is
  risky in the sense relevant to decision.
- **Humanity means Earth-originating intelligent life**, not Homo sapiens
  (pp. 19–20).

## Key results

- **The size of the risk** (pp. 15–16). Estimates of 10–20% total
  existential risk this century are "fairly typical" among those who have
  examined it, and rest on subjective judgement. One informal expert poll
  gave a median 19% for extinction before 2100 (p. 28, n. 1). Natural risks
  are small; the bulk is anthropogenic and technological.
- **Meta-level uncertainty** (p. 16). If an analysis puts a catastrophe's
  probability very low, the chance the analysis is flawed may be higher,
  and most of the risk may sit there (Ord, Hillerbrand and Sandberg 2010).
- **Risk categories** (p. 17, Figure 2). Scope (personal to
  pan-generational) by severity (imperceptible to crushing). Existential
  risks are at least crushing and pan-generational.
- **The expected loss** (pp. 18–19).
  - Earth alone, a billion people for a billion years: at least 10^16
    lives. So a millionth of one percentage point of risk reduction is
    worth at least a hundred times a million lives.
  - Biological lives in the accessible universe: at least 10^34
    life-years. Emulations: at least 10^54 subjective life-years, or 10^52
    ordinary lives.
- **Maxipok** (p. 19). "Maximise the probability of an 'OK outcome', where
  an OK outcome is any outcome that avoids existential catastrophe." It
  holds "whenever we act out of an impersonal concern for humankind as a
  whole". It differs from maximin, which would have us "start partying as
  if there were no tomorrow".
- **Four classes** (pp. 19–22, Table 1). Human extinction (premature, that
  is, before technological maturity); permanent stagnation (unrecovered
  collapse, plateauing, recurrent collapse); flawed realisation
  (unconsummated, ephemeral); subsequent ruination. The last two are
  defined normatively (p. 24).
- **Capability and value** (pp. 22–23). Long-run efficiency matters more
  than early output; even a delay of several million years is "brief".
  "Even from a so-called 'person-affecting' moral perspective", one should
  judge flawed realisation by the long integral of value, since
  digital or repaired agents could live indefinitely (p. 23).
- **Other ethical perspectives** (pp. 23–24).
  - Adams (1989, pp. 472–473): a commitment to the future of humanity as
    "a vast project, or network of overlapping projects, that is generally
    shared by the human race". "We owe, I think, some loyalty to this
    project of the human future. We also owe it a respect that we would
    owe it even if we were not of the human race ourselves."
  - Preference satisfaction; democratic deliberation, "even if
    hypothetical future people are not included as stakeholders".
  - Custodial duties: "We do not want to be the failing link in the chain
    of generations" (p. 23).
  - Theological views and intuitions about genocide scaling up to omnicide.
  - It is "on no account a conceptual truth" that existential catastrophe
    is bad (p. 24).
- **Normative uncertainty and sustainability** (pp. 24–26). If unsure
  whether X is bad but sure it is not good, X is bad in the decision
  sense. Option value favours reaching a state of more wisdom,
  coordination and capability with uncorrupted values. Like a rocket in
  mid-air, humanity should seek a sustainable trajectory, not a
  sustainable state.
- **Barriers** (pp. 26–27).
  - Academic neglect: fewer Scopus papers on "human extinction" than on
    "dung beetle", "star trek" or "zinc oxalate" (Figure 6, August 2012).
  - "Mitigation of existential risk is hampered by a lack of
    understanding, but also by a deficit of motivation" (p. 26). It is a
    pan-generational public good: even a world state captures only the
    benefits to people now alive. "The quadrillions of happy people who
    may come to exist ... would be willing to pay the present generation
    astronomical sums", but the trade is impossible.
  - "Moral motivations, too, may fail to measure up": scope insensitivity,
    quoting Yudkowsky on extinction entering "a 'separate magisterium'"
    (pp. 26–27).
- **Grounds for optimism** (pp. 27–28). The ideas are new; political
  integration has widened over history; movements for peace, the
  environment and human rights might take the concern on board. Bostrom
  doubts that the prospect of extinction motivates those movements now:
  "Perhaps the amount of concern would be roughly the same even in the
  face of an iron-clad guarantee that any catastrophe would stop short of
  human extinction" (p. 29, n. 37).

## Concepts

- **existential risk**: "one that threatens the premature extinction of
  Earth-originating intelligent life or the permanent and drastic
  destruction of its potential for desirable future development"
  (p. 15).
- **maxipok**: maximise the probability of an OK outcome, one avoiding
  existential catastrophe (p. 19).
- **technological maturity**: capabilities giving productivity and control
  over nature close to the feasible maximum (p. 19).
- **permanent stagnation, flawed realisation, subsequent ruination**: the
  three non-extinction classes (Table 1).
- **sustainable trajectory**: a path that minimises existential risk,
  against a "sustainable state" one could stay in (pp. 25–26).
- **pan-generational public good**: one whose benefits fall mostly on
  future generations, who cannot pay for it (p. 26).

## Bearing on the record

- **The owner's thesis (CLAIM-tmpjylq8).** This is the longtermist text
  that says most about motivation, and what it says fits the thesis.
  These are inferences; Bostrom does not discuss the self.
  - **The impersonal ground is named as such.** Maxipok is for "an
    impersonal concern for humankind as a whole" (p. 19). Bostrom grants
    that "Unrestricted altruism is not so common" (p. 19). On his account
    the obligation outruns the motive.
  - **His diagnosis of the gap is the thesis's prediction.** Each
    producer of safety, even a world state, captures only the share that
    accrues to people now alive (p. 26). So present people would supply
    safety in proportion to their own stake, and the future's
    beneficiaries cannot pay. Bostrom frames this as a market failure. Read
    through the thesis, it says concern reaches as far as the self
    reaches, and no further.
  - **Adams splits the ground in two.** "Loyalty" to humanity's project is
    owed by its members, as a commitment continuous with "the projects of
    our own personal futures". "Respect" is owed "even if we were not of
    the human race ourselves". The first is a temporally extended
    collective self of the thesis's kind. The second is the impersonal
    ground and its hard case.
  - **Note 37 is a test the thesis needs.** If concern for catastrophe
    would be the same with a guarantee against extinction, then those
    movements' concern is for people now alive, not for the far future.
- **The triviality objection (CLAIM-tmprudek).** The "failing link in the
  chain of generations" (p. 23) suggests a criterion of extent apart from
  caring: felt membership in a lineage. It is a phrase, not a measure.
- **Proposed dated note on CLAIM-tmpjylq8**: "Bostrom (2013; NOTE of
  2026-10-29) grounds existential-risk priority in 'an impersonal concern
  for humankind as a whole' and grants that 'unrestricted altruism is not
  so common'. He explains its undersupply by the fact that people now
  alive capture only their own share of the benefit and moral sentiment is
  insensitive to scope. That is what the thesis predicts: concern reaches
  as far as the self does. Through Adams he also offers a ground that fits
  the thesis, loyalty to humanity's shared project continuous with one's
  own, beside one that does not, respect owed even by outsiders."
- **No THEORY filed.**
- **No ML instruction.** Machine superintelligence appears as a risk
  area, not as practice.

## Limitations

- **The risk estimates are subjective**, as Bostrom says (p. 15, n. 1).
- **The axiology is assumed, not argued** (p. 16), and the other
  perspectives are surveyed in a page.
- **The motivational claims are untested.** Scope insensitivity is cited
  to Yudkowsky's review; note 37 is a conjecture.
- **An understated figure.** On p. 19, a 1% chance of 10^52 lives and a
  risk reduction of a billionth of a billionth of a percentage point give
  10^30 lives in expectation; the text says "a hundred billion times as
  much as a billion human lives", 10^20. The understatement does not hurt
  the argument.
- **Typos and slips.**
  - "the potential exist for" (p. 18).
  - "a civilisation starting could reach" (p. 22).
  - Figure 2's note lacks a closing parenthesis after "numerous
    generations" (p. 17).
  - Note 10 promises "section 5", but the paper has four sections (p. 28).
  - In the references, "Accessedviewed" and "Cambrige" (p. 30); Schröder
    and Smith (2008) is given as volume 368, which appears to be 386
    (p. 30).

## Open questions

- Does concern about catastrophe track the loss of the far future or the
  deaths of people now alive? Note 37's guarantee is an experiment:
  Schubert et al. (LIT-tmpk72ci) may run a version of it.
- Is Adams' "loyalty" to the human project measurable as identification
  with humanity over time, and does it predict support for risk reduction
  apart from impersonal concern?
