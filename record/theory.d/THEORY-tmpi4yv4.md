# THEORY-tmpi4yv4: In Fuentes v. Shevin the Supreme Court held that prejudgment replevin statutes letting goods be seized from their possessor on a private party's ex parte application, without a prior opportunity to be heard, deny due process, because the hearing must come when the deprivation can still be prevented and no later hearing or damage award can undo a deprivation already made, whether or not the possessor would have prevailed

Fuentes v. Shevin, 407 U.S. 67 (1972), LIT-tmpayex1.

## The holding

The statutes "work a deprivation of property without due process of law
insofar as they deny the right to a prior opportunity to be heard before
chattels are taken from their possessor" (Part VII). The hearing "must be
granted at a time when the deprivation can still be prevented", since "no
later hearing and no damage award can undo the fact that the arbitrary
taking that was subject to the right of procedural due process has already
occurred" (Part IV). "The right to be heard does not depend upon an advance
showing that one will surely prevail at the hearing" (Part V).

## What it does not show

- A moral principle beyond state-assisted seizure: the holding is "a
  narrow one", with "truly unusual" extraordinary situations excepted.
- That the protection is the holder's consent: it is a hearing.
- Its standing in later doctrine, which was not checked; it was decided
  4–3.
