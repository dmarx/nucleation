# NOTE-tmpxbidq: Stockton et al. — Mechanisms of change in ACT

## Contribution

It updated Hayes et al.'s 2006 summary of ACT mediation studies
(LIT-555) with a systematic search, inclusion rules and a mediation-quality
checklist. It confined itself to trials against an active treatment, so
that a mediator could be tested for being ACT's own. What is true after it
is that the mediation evidence for ACT's model rests mostly on overall
flexibility and on acceptance. The other processes are thinly tested or
untested, and the 2006 complaints about method had largely gone unheeded.

## Key insight

A process that mediates an outcome in ACT and equally in the treatment it
is compared with is real but not ACT's. On that test only acceptance
stands out. Defusion and committed action work, but not specially. The
self as context, a process ACT names as one of six, has never been put to
the test.

## Assumptions

- **Mediation as evidence for mechanism.** Following Kazdin (2007), a
  mediator "may be a guide that highlights possible mechanisms of change,
  but is not necessarily a bone fide mechanism" (p. ms 4).
- **Specificity by moderated mediation.** A mediator whose path is
  moderated by treatment type is taken as specific to ACT. One that
  mediates in both arms is taken as shared with the comparison (pp. ms
  8, 16–17).
- **Inclusion (p. ms 10).** Clinical trials of ACT against a different
  active treatment, 2006 to 2015, in English and peer-reviewed, using
  mediation analysis or testing treatment type's interaction with the
  mediation path. Excluded: waitlist comparisons, overlapping arms (ACT
  against ACT plus something), mixed-approach interventions and theses.
  Searched in January 2015 in PsycINFO, Medline and Web of Science, on
  abstracts only.
- **Quality.** Rhodes and Pfaeffli's 11-item mediation checklist (low
  0–4, moderate 5–8, high 9–11), with inter-rater κ = .59 on five
  studies. Three statistical criteria were added: a modern technique
  (bootstrapping or longitudinal mediation), a reported indirect effect,
  and control for time and therapist (pp. ms 11–12).

## Key results

- **Twelve studies** from the US, Sweden, the UK and South Africa, with
  27 to 222 participants. Six used mediation analysis and six tested
  moderated mediation or mediated moderation (p. ms 12). The included
  studies, from the reference list: Arch et al. 2012; Bach et al. 2013;
  Bricker et al. 2013; Flaxman & Bond 2010; Forman et al. 2007; Forman et
  al. 2012; Hesser et al. 2013; Lundgren et al. 2008; Niles et al. 2014;
  Rost et al. 2012; Wicksell et al. 2011; Zettle et al. 2011.
- **Self as context never tested.** "The mediating variables investigated
  were limited to five of the six ACT processes ... Therefore, self as
  context has been consistently overlooked as a potential mediator"
  (p. ms 12). "There were no studies that examined the 'self-as-context'
  aspect" (p. ms 18). The authors suggest this may reflect difficulty
  measuring it, and point to the Self Experiences Questionnaire (Yu,
  Norton & McCracken 2016) and to dismantling or component studies
  (pp. ms 18, 21–22).
- **Psychological flexibility** mediated mental-health outcomes in four
  of five studies (Niles 2014; Rost 2012; Flaxman & Bond 2010; Forman
  2007). Evidence was limited for physical health and functioning and
  mixed for quality of life (p. ms 14). In the discussion it "strongly
  mediated" mental health, functioning and physical symptoms (p. ms 17).
- **Acceptance** mediated mental-health outcomes in four of five studies
  (Hesser 2013; Forman 2012; Rost 2012; Wicksell 2011), and functioning in
  two. There was some evidence for quality of life and behaviour (Arch
  2012; Bricker 2013). It was "the only process for which
  mediation/moderated mediation was found across type of outcome", and is
  called "a primary mechanism of change during ACT" (pp. ms 14, 16–17).
- **Defusion.** Of eight mediation analyses in four studies, seven found
  mediation, but four of five moderated-mediation analyses found it not
  specific to ACT. The two studies that found nothing used
  non-standardized measures, while Bach et al. (a behavioural outcome,
  rehospitalization) and Zettle et al. (a validated measure) were
  positive (pp. ms 14–15, 17). The results section and discussion state
  this differently. The results say three of four studies found no
  mediation for mental-health outcomes. The discussion says only one of
  eight analyses failed. The tables that would reconcile them are not in
  the deposit.
- **Committed action** mediated in both ACT and cognitive therapy in two
  studies, so it is not distinct to ACT. **Present-moment contact**
  (Forman 2007) and **values** (Lundgren 2008) were tested in one study
  each (p. ms 15).
- **Non-ACT processes.** Change in dysfunctional cognitions, tested in
  seven studies, did not consistently mediate. Symptom frequency, symptom
  distress, self-efficacy and pain intensity did not mediate (p. ms 15).
- **Quality (pp. ms 13, 18–19).** All twelve were "moderate quality".
  Only Lundgren et al. 2008 was adequately powered, and the rest did not
  report power. Only three studies (Hesser 2013; Arch 2012; Rost 2012)
  "ascertained whether changes in mediator variables preceded changes in
  outcome". Two studies measured the mediator once (Lundgren 2008; Zettle
  2011). Only Forman 2007 and 2012 accounted for both time and therapist.
  Reliability went unreported for five mediator and seven outcome
  measures. "Studies were over dependant on self-report measures."
- **Earlier reviews, as reported (pp. ms 4–5).** Hayes et al. 2006
  summarized eight mediation studies with weaknesses that included
  "process measures being taken after outcomes have significantly
  improved". Hayes, Villatte, Levin and Hildebrandt 2011 found about half
  of between-group differences at follow-up explained by post-treatment
  flexibility.

## Concepts

- **Mediator, mechanism, moderator** — after Kazdin (2007): a mediator
  "accounts (statistically)" for the treatment–outcome relation; a
  mechanism is the process "responsible for therapeutic change"; a
  moderator changes the relation's direction or size (p. ms 2).
- **Moderated mediation and mediated moderation** — treatment type
  moderating the mediator–outcome path only, or the treatment–mediator
  path (p. ms 8).
- **Psychological flexibility (hexaflex)** — defusion, acceptance, contact
  with the present moment, values, committed action and self as context,
  this last glossed as "development of an non-evaluative, observing self"
  (p. ms 3).

## Connections

It is the systematic successor to the mediation section of Hayes et al.
2006 (LIT-555, read in NOTE-431 from an early manuscript). It cites Levin
et al.'s 2012 meta-analysis of laboratory component studies for
acceptance's large effect against inactive conditions. It recommends the
MPFI (Rolffs, Rogge & Wilson 2016), the multidimensional flexibility
measure from Rogge's group, whose 2024 meta-analysis (LIT-543) is the one
the record's ACT accounts name. Its search ends in January 2015, so it
predates that work.

## Bearing on the record

- **THEORY-050: partly, and not what its `promote_when` asks.** The
  account's test (1) is "a first-hand reading of the 2024 meta-analysis
  ... showing that change in avoidance, fusion or flexibility precedes
  and predicts change in outcome across more than one kind of disorder".
  This is a different and earlier review. It finds flexibility and
  acceptance mediating mental-health outcomes across anxiety, depression,
  social anxiety, tinnitus, chronic pain and cancer adjustment. But it
  shows the mediator changing first in only three studies. It says the
  studies relied on self-report, and its prose does not say which
  measures were used, so it cannot show that they were not the global
  AAQ that `promote_when` excludes. It also finds defusion, the
  account's "fusion", not specific to ACT. So it supports the account's
  process claim as mediation without temporal order. That is the state
  THEORY-053 calls the right kind of evidence with the order not
  established, and THEORY-050 already records that state from Hayes et
  al. 2006. It does not meet the condition.
- **THEORY-051: not at all, and it confirms the gap.** The account waits
  on a study that changes or measures the self as context apart from
  defusion and acceptance. This review found none: "self as context has
  been consistently overlooked as a potential mediator". It suggests a
  measure (the SEQ) and dismantling designs, which is the test the
  account's `promote_when` already names. Nothing here bears for or
  against it.
- **LIT-543** stays the test both accounts name. This review is not a
  substitute for it.
- No instruction for machine-learning practice; nothing for the anthology.

## Limitations

- **The tables are missing from the copy read.** Study-by-study methods,
  measures, outcomes and quality scores (Tables 2–4, Appendix 1) were not
  seen. Claims about which measures were used cannot be checked here.
- **Narrative synthesis.** There is no pooled estimate and no count of
  null analyses beyond the prose, and the two accounts of defusion differ.
- **Narrow window and search.** 2006 to 2015 only, abstracts only, three
  databases, English only.
- **Inclusion by active comparator** answers specificity but leaves out
  waitlist trials, which the authors grant would have widened the
  evidence (p. ms 20).
- **Checklist reliability** was moderate (κ = .59), and the authors say
  its statistical item is too broad (p. ms 19).

## Open questions

- Does the self as context mediate outcome when measured apart from
  defusion and acceptance, for example with the SEQ, or isolated in a
  dismantling design?
- Does acceptance change before outcome in more than the three studies
  here, and with measures other than global self-report?
- What does Macri and Rogge's 2024 meta-analysis (LIT-543), with the MPFI
  domains, add to this?
