# NOTE-tmp2q5sj: Dennett — Reply to Professor Stich

## Contribution

A four-page reply that states, more plainly than *Brainstorms* did, what
the intentional stance concedes. Stich had argued that Dennett's two
themes, eliminative materialism and the reconciliation of persons with
science, pull apart: the stance can rescue belief and desire but not
pain. Dennett accepts the diagnosis and answers it in two ways. For
belief, he makes the idealisation explicit. For pain, he declines to give
a theory and treats the choice among options as practical.

## Key insight

The intentional stance protects our status as believers from science
because being a believer is defined by an ideal we already know we only
approximate. Nothing found inside heads can show we fail a standard we
were never thought to meet. The price is that "person" is an idealisation
too, and Dennett pays it. Pain gets no such protection, because nothing
plays the stance's role for it, so for pain he offers policy instead of
theory.

## Assumptions

- **Stich's summary is accurate** ("I have no quarrel with Stich's summary
  of my views", p. 73). The reply argues only against Stich's objections.
- **Quine's interdependence of ascriptions**: evidence for one belief
  ascription is the best evidence against a contradictory one (p. 74).
- **Pain is not a good theoretical entity** in a mature psychology, "as I
  argue" (p. 75), referring to "Why You Can't Make a Computer that Feels
  Pain" (*Brainstorms* ch. 11). Assumed here, argued there.

## Key results

- **Stich's charges** (pp. 65–73, as the reply summarises them):
  - the evolutionary grounding of rationality is "simply confused";
  - the norm of full rationality would disqualify every natural believer;
  - pains are not "legitimized" by the stance, so the eliminativist must
    deny that pain can truly be attributed, while the theory of persons
    needs states of consciousness. Stich: "Can the contradiction be
    resolved?"
- **On evolution** (pp. 73–74). Dennett admits the claim is left
  "open-ended", calls this a deliberate refusal of "premature
  precision", and points to the forthcoming "Three Kinds of Intentional
  Psychology". His reliance is minimal: belief and desire structures,
  even malignant or suicidal ones, are understood as extensions of
  control systems "selected by nature because they are benign in the
  long run".
- **On full rationality** (pp. 74–75). No one is fully rational, but
  irrationality cannot be stably described as believing p and believing
  q where p implies not-q, because the evidence for each ascription
  counts against the other. Researchers on human inference who ascribe
  contradictory beliefs uncautiously "will indeed be confused — not
  'simply', but subtly". Ordinary practice tolerates the slack ("he sort
  of believes that p") at real cost, notably in judgments of
  responsibility. A norm-free naturalistic theory of belief is "not
  ruled out" but would revise our concepts and be acceptable only if it
  kept most stance-based ascriptions.
- **The concession** (p. 75). "The conception of ourselves as intentional
  systems is granted to be an unrealizable idealization", and so is the
  concept of a person built on it, and it is this idealisation, not a
  realisable counterpart, that figures in ethics.
- **On pain** (pp. 75–76). Three options, with uses and costs:
  - (A) bite the bullet: "curiously short-sighted", but it reminds the
    questioner that something is "misbegotten about the question";
  - (B) reform: replace the incoherent concept, accepting
    counter-intuitive verdicts;
  - (C) common sense: pain is like voices, holes (Lewis and Lewis) and
    haircuts, which no mature science defines either. But not all
    perplexity about pain is "merely philosophical", so a theory is
    still needed. Let it find its terms "where it will", and call it a
    theory of pain where that helps, for instance in disputes about
    treatments or animal pain.
  The choice is "in some measure a matter of policy". The philosopher's
  contribution ends once the options are clear: "philosophical medicine
  for the philosophical puzzles and science for the rest".

## Concepts

- **Intentional system** — a system whose behaviour is predictable by
  assuming it believes and desires what it rationally should (Stich's
  gloss, accepted). Here explicitly "an unrealizable idealization".
- **"Strictly speaking"** — the register in which there are no pains
  (p. 76), set against the register of common sense in which people feel
  them. The reply's resolution is to keep both registers and assign them
  to different purposes.
- **Manifest image** — Sellars's term, used for pains as "distressingly
  familiar items" (p. 75).

## Limitations

- **A reply to a review.** It answers objections and points elsewhere for
  argument (Brainstorms ch. 11; "Three Kinds of Intentional
  Psychology").
- **The pain paradox is left standing by design.** Dennett ends where
  Stich found him, "trusting that the rest of my observations on the
  subject dissolve the air of paradox". Whether they do is not argued.
- **The evolutionary grounding is admittedly left vague** ("one of those
  disconcerting bits of soft-pedalling of which Stich rightly accuses
  me").

## Open questions

- Is "there are no such things as pains, although of course people do
  feel pain" consistent once "pain" is read in two registers, or does it
  need the later real-pattern machinery (Taylor) or the illusionist's
  separation of experiences from phenomenal properties (Frankish)?
- If irrationality cannot be stably described, what do experimental
  findings of inferential error measure? The reply says they find
  something other than "unproblematic cases of people believing
  contradictions", without saying what.
