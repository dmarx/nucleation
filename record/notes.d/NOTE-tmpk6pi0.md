# NOTE-tmpk6pi0: Cours de linguistique générale

## Contribution

The book fixes an object for linguistics and a set of principles for it.
Before it, linguistics was comparative and historical grammar with no
account of what a language is at a moment. After it, there is a defined
object (langue, a social system of signs, set apart from individual
speech), a definition of the sign as a two-sided psychic unit with an
arbitrary bond, a methodological split between the study of states
(synchrony) and of changes (diachrony), and an account of meaning in
which what a term is worth comes from its place among the other terms
rather than from what it names. Linguistics is placed inside a projected
general science of signs, sémiologie.

## Key insight

A language is a system of pure values. Neither the sounds nor the ideas
exist as units before the language divides them; the language cuts both
"amorphous masses" at once, like a sheet of paper whose recto (thought)
cannot be cut without cutting its verso (sound). So a term is not a sound
attached to a ready-made concept. It is whatever the other terms of the
system leave it: French *mouton* can share a signification with English
*sheep* without sharing its value, because English also has *mutton*.
Because the bond between sound and idea is arbitrary, nothing but these
differences can hold a term in place, and nothing outside the system
anchors it, which is also why the system can neither be reformed at will
nor protected from drift.

## Assumptions

- **The object is constructed by the point of view** (Intro. ch. III §1):
  "c'est le point de vue qui crée l'objet". Langue is chosen as the norm
  for all other manifestations of language because it alone admits an
  autonomous definition; the argument is methodological, not empirical.
- **Both sides of the sign are psychic** (Intro. ch. III §2; Part One
  ch. I §1): the acoustic image is "l'empreinte psychique" of a sound, not
  the sound. Langue is located in the receptive and coordinating part of
  the speech circuit in the brains of a community; execution (phonation)
  belongs to parole.
- **Langue exists completely only in the mass**: a sum of imprints in each
  brain, "1 + 1 + 1 + 1… = 1 (modèle collectif)", against parole's
  "(1 + 1′ + 1″ + 1‴…)" (Intro. ch. IV).
- **Spoken, not written, language is the object** (Intro. ch. VI): writing
  is a second sign system whose only reason is to represent the first.
- **A language state is an idealization** (Part Two ch. I): a span in
  which change is negligible, compared to neglecting infinitesimals; "la
  notion d'état de langue ne peut être qu'approximative".
- **The argument works on words as stand-ins for units** (Part Two ch. IV
  §1), although words are said not to coincide with the concrete units.

## Key results

Principles, with where they are argued.

- **The sign** (Part One ch. I §1). The linguistic sign unites "non une
  chose et un nom, mais un concept et une image acoustique"; renamed
  signifié and signifiant, with *signe* for the whole.
- **First principle, arbitrariness** (§2). The bond is unmotivated with
  respect to the signified ("immotivé"), not free for the speaker.
  Onomatopoeia and interjections are set aside as few, partly
  conventional (French *ouaoua*, German *wauwau*) and drawn into ordinary
  sound change. Fully arbitrary systems "réalisent mieux que les autres
  l'idéal du procédé sémiologique".
- **Second principle, linearity** (§3). The signifier unfolds in time in
  one dimension; "tout le mécanisme de la langue en dépend". Accent is
  argued not to be a simultaneous second element.
- **Immutability** (ch. II §1). The signifier is "imposé" on the
  community, "la carte forcée". Four reasons: arbitrariness leaves no
  rational norm to argue for a change; the multitude of signs; the
  complexity of the system; the inertia of a mass that uses language at
  every moment. "C'est parce que le signe est arbitraire qu'il ne connaît
  d'autre loi que celle de la tradition, et c'est parce qu'il se fonde sur
  la tradition qu'il peut être arbitraire."
- **Mutability** (ch. II §2). Time also alters signs, always as "un
  déplacement du rapport entre le signifié et le signifiant" (Latin
  *necare* "kill" > French *noyer* "drown"). A language "est radicalement
  impuissante à se défendre" against such shifts, again because of
  arbitrariness. Even an artificial language (Esperanto) would escape its
  maker once in circulation.
- **Synchrony and diachrony** (ch. III). Sciences of value, like economics,
  must separate the axis of simultaneities from the axis of successions.
  Change strikes isolated elements, never the system as such (umlaut in
  *gasti* > *Gäste*, *fōti* > *fēt*), with no aim at what the new state
  will signify; the state that results is "fortuit". The chess analogy: a
  position's values depend on the pieces' positions, one move shifts one
  piece, and a position is "affranchie de ses antécédents"; the analogy
  fails only in that the player intends the move and language does not.
- **Synchronic and diachronic "laws"** (ch. III §6). A synchronic law is
  general but not imperative (it states an arrangement, like trees in a
  quincunx); a diachronic event is imperative but not general. Sound
  laws are regular, but each is one particular event affecting a phoneme,
  not the words. Neither kind is a law in the juridical sense, and there
  is no panchronic viewpoint on particular facts (ch. III §7).
- **Units are not given** (Part Two ch. II–III). The spoken chain is a
  continuous ribbon; units appear only when sound and sense are delimited
  together (*sižlaprã*: "si je la prends" or "si je l'apprends"). Identity
  is that of the 8:45 Geneva–Paris express or a rebuilt street, not of a
  stolen coat; in such systems identity and value coincide (the knight
  replaced by any object given the same value).
- **Value** (Part Two ch. IV). Every value needs something dissimilar it
  can be exchanged for and similar things it can be compared with (a
  five-franc coin: bread, and other coins). Signification is the vertical
  link from image to concept inside the sign; value is the horizontal
  relation among signs, and the concept is "purement différentiel",
  defined "négativement par ses rapports avec les autres termes";
  "Leur plus exacte caractéristique est d'être ce que les autres ne sont
  pas." Examples: *mouton*/*sheep*–*mutton*; *redouter*, *craindre*,
  *avoir peur*; the Sanskrit dual changes the value of the plural;
  *louer* against *mieten*/*vermieten*.
- **The signifier is differential too** (§3). What counts is the phonic
  differences; phonemes are "des entités oppositives, relatives et
  négatives", shown by the free variation of French *r* against the fixed
  contrast of German *r* and *ch*. "Arbitraire et différentiel sont deux
  qualités corrélatives."
- **Only differences, but a positive sign** (§4). "Dans la langue il n'y a
  que des différences sans termes positifs", but this holds only of
  signified and signifier taken separately; their combination in a sign
  is "un fait positif", and between signs there is not difference but
  opposition. "La langue est une forme et non une substance."
- **Two kinds of relation** (Part Two ch. V–VI). Syntagmatic relations hold
  *in praesentia* along the linear chain; associative relations hold *in
  absentia* in memory (*enseignement* with *enseigner*, *armement*,
  *éducation*, *justement*). Choosing *marchons!* means choosing it against
  *marche!*, *marchez!* and against *montons!*, *mangeons!*; "l'idée
  appelle, non une forme, mais tout un système latent".
- **Absolute and relative arbitrariness** (ch. VI §3). *vingt* is
  unmotivated, *dix-neuf* relatively motivated; motivation is never
  absolute. Languages range between "lexicological" (most unmotivated;
  Chinese as the extreme) and "grammatical" (Indo-European, Sanskrit);
  French is more arbitrary than Latin (*ennemi* against *inimīcus*).
- **Grammar** (ch. VII–VIII). Morphology, syntax and lexicology are not
  natural divisions; grammar should be ordered by the syntagmatic and
  associative axes. Abstract entities (the genitive, word order) rest on
  concrete units.
- **Analogy is creation, not change** (Part Three ch. IV, skimmed):
  *honor* is formed by proportion (*ōrātōrem : ōrātor = honōrem : x*) and
  coexists with *honōs* before replacing it; it counteracts the
  disorganising effect of sound change.

The support for nearly all of these is argument and illustrative example,
not systematic evidence; the book is a reconstruction by its editors from
students' notes.

## Concepts

- **langage**: the whole heterogeneous faculty and its exercise, physical,
  physiological, psychic, individual and social.
- **langue**: the social product of that faculty and the set of conventions
  adopted by the body of speakers; "le langage moins la parole"; a system
  of signs in which only the union of sense and acoustic image is
  essential.
- **parole**: the individual act of will and intelligence that uses the
  code, with the psycho-physical mechanism that executes it.
- **signe, signifié, signifiant**: the whole, the concept, and the acoustic
  image (a psychic imprint, not the sound).
- **arbitraire**: unmotivated with respect to the signified; not chosen by
  the speaker. *Arbitraire absolu* against *arbitraire relatif*
  (relative motivation through analysable composition).
- **sémiologie**: a science, not yet existing, of "la vie des signes au
  sein de la vie sociale", of which linguistics would be a part.
- **synchronie / diachronie**: a state of the language and a phase of
  evolution; "idiosynchronique" is offered as the more exact term.
- **valeur**: what a term is worth by its relations to the other terms of
  the system, as against **signification**, the counterpart of the
  acoustic image inside one sign.
- **rapports syntagmatiques / associatifs**: relations *in praesentia*
  along the chain, and *in absentia* in memory; the associative ones are
  of indefinite number and no fixed order, except in inflectional
  paradigms, where the number is fixed.
- **unité**: a stretch of sound that is, to the exclusion of what precedes
  and follows, the signifier of a certain concept.

## Connections

The book places itself against comparative grammar from Bopp to
Schleicher, which it faults for never asking what its object was, and
credits the Neogrammarians with treating language as a product of the
collective mind. It takes from Whitney that language is an institution
and its signs conventional, and goes further: arbitrariness separates
language from every other institution, which Whitney did not see. It
defends traditional grammar, Port-Royal included, as correctly synchronic.
The economic analogy (value as exchange against the dissimilar and
comparison with the similar) is explicit.

Inside this record the nearest readings are the compositional
distributional models of meaning (LIT-273, LIT-272), in which a word's
meaning is fixed by its co-occurrence with other words. Saussure's
associative and syntagmatic relations, and value as position in a system,
are a precursor of that idea, but the line from one to the other runs
through later structuralist and distributionalist work this record does
not hold. The connection is mine, not the book's.

## Bearing on the record

- **THEORY-tmpsqomf (filed from this reading).** The record held no account
  of linguistic meaning as differential value. The thesis is filed as a
  Proposed THEORY with the book's own limits stated: it is argued from
  examples, and the book itself makes the sign as a whole positive.
- **LIT-273 and LIT-272.** See Connections. No THEORY in the record rests
  on distributional semantics, so nothing is supported or contradicted.
- **The LIT as filed.** The LIT's standard description says the value of a
  term is "purely differential". The book confines this to signified and
  signifier taken separately and calls the sign as a whole a positive
  fact, with opposition, not difference, between signs. The LIT has been
  corrected.
- **No instruction for machine-learning practice**, so nothing for the
  anthology.

## Limitations

- **Not Saussure's own text.** The first-edition preface says Bally and
  Sechehaye, who attended few of the lectures, reconstructed the book as
  "une recréation" based on the third course and students' notes, and
  arranged it "conforme à l'intention de l'auteur, même lorsque cette
  intention se devinait". How far any formulation is Saussure's cannot be
  told from this text; the published student notebooks and manuscript
  sources are other works.
- **The edition read is not the 1916 one.** See the history note.
- **Argument by example.** The principles are supported by chosen
  examples from Indo-European languages, chiefly French, German, Latin
  and Greek; no systematic test is offered, and counter-cases (sound
  symbolism beyond onomatopoeia, for instance) are dismissed as marginal.
- **The tension between arbitrariness and the system** is acknowledged but
  not resolved in the parts read: the system is where "une raison
  relative" reigns, yet it is also said to be immutable in itself.
- **A linguistics of parole** is promised and absent, as the editors say.
- **Psychological claims** (both sides of the sign are psychic; langue
  sits in the brain) are asserted, not investigated.

## Open questions

- How much of the doctrine of value, and of the strong "only differences"
  wording, belongs to Saussure rather than to the editors? Reading the
  student notebooks or the manuscript sources would answer it.
- What would count against the claim that a term's value is fixed by its
  oppositions? A case in which a term's use is unchanged when a
  neighbouring term is added or lost would; the book gives the opposite
  cases (*redouter*) and none of this kind.
- Does the linearity principle survive for signed languages, whose
  signifiers are visual and can be simultaneous? The book contrasts
  acoustic signifiers with visual signals, which "peuvent offrir des
  complications simultanées sur plusieurs dimensions", but does not
  consider a visual language.
