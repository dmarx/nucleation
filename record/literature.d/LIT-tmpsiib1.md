summary: >-
  Bogetoft & Hougaard (working paper 2001, revised 2002; J. Productivity
  Analysis 2003). Measured technical inefficiency, excess inputs or slack,
  is treated not as waste but as a rational choice: the organization values
  off-the-job profit and on-the-job slack and chooses slack under its
  control regime. Under a price-based regime it uses an allocatively
  efficient underlying plan and takes the rest of the budget as profit and
  slack; under a restriction-based regime the underlying plan is
  technically efficient and supported by endogenous prices. Observed inputs
  then constrain, by revealed preference, how it trades profit against
  slack and one kind of slack against another. Since the gains from slack
  are unknown, inefficiency cannot be concluded to be waste to be
  eliminated; which slack is valuable is posed as an open problem.

# LIT-tmpsiib1: Rational Inefficiencies

Bogetoft, P. & Hougaard, J. L. (2002). Rational Inefficiencies. Unit of Economics Working Papers 2001/14, Food and Resource Economic Institute, Royal Veterinary and Agricultural University, Copenhagen (September 2001, revised July 2002). Published as *Journal of Productivity Analysis* 20(3): 243–271 (2003), per RePEc.

## Key takeaways

*From the partial reading, NOTE-tmpb2xb9. Page numbers are the working paper's PDF pages; ligatures restored in quotes.*

- **Slack as choice.** "we see inefficiency and in particular the
  allocation of inefficiency (slack) among different inputs as the result
  of a rational choice made by that firm" (p. 3).
- **Not all slack is waste.** "Since we typically are ignorant about those
  gains and costs, we can not conclude that inefficiency as such should be
  eliminated." (p. 5).
- **The split left open.** "The distinction between valuable and
  non-valuable waste is a challenging one." (p. 5).
- **Efficient underneath.** Under price-based control the unit picks an
  allocatively efficient underlying plan (Proposition 1); under
  restriction-based control a technically efficient one with supporting
  prices (Proposition 2).
